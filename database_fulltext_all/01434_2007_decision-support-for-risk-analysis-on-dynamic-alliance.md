# Decision support for risk analysis on dynamic alliance

Yuan Li <sup>\*</sup>, Xiuwu Liao

School of Management, Xian Jiaotong University, Xian 710049, China

Available online 19 December 2004

## Abstract

Dynamic alliance has been considered a temporary organization of member enterprises formed to pool their core competencies and exploit fast changing market opportunities. Although many problems on dynamic alliance such as partner selection, operation management, information exchanges and their standards, etc. have been investigated, the risk management of dynamic alliance has not received deserved attention until now. For risk evaluation is the most important phase of risk management, so this paper is mainly concerned with the risk evaluation problem. Various kinds of risk factors that affect the operation of alliance are identified, and their levels are measured by three parameters such as risk occurrence likelihood, consequence severity and risk control degree. These parameters are expressed by trapezoidal fuzzy numbers. Furthermore, risk evaluation is regarded as a multiattribute decision-making (MADM) problem with uncertainty. A new evaluation approach based on the framework of the evidential reasoning (ER) is proposed. Finally, a numerical example is given to illustrate the application of this approach in the process of risk evaluation. © 2004 Elsevier B V All rights reserved

Keywords: Dynamic alliance; Risk evaluation; Decision-making; Evidential reasoning; Decision support system

## 1. Introduction

Dynamic alliance is also called virtual enterprise [3]. It is a temporary organization of member enterprises formed to exploit fast-changing market opportunities. According to this concept, on the one hand, member enterprises in a dynamic alliance, which are geographically distributed, will keep their independence and autonomy. On the other hand, they will provide their own core competencies in areas such as marketing, engineering and manufacturing to the virtual enterprise. When new market requirements arise and individual enterprise does not have all necessary skills and competencies to undertake these requirements independently, by combining the particular expertise of other enterprises, it is possible to create a dynamic alliance, capable of responding to the new requirements [15]. For dynamic alliance has many excellent virtues, such as flexibility and adaptability to environmental changes, cost apportionment and competencies and resources sharing, etc., which can effectively help member enterprises capture fleeting market opportunities [14], so the dynamic alliance is considered as one of the most promising paradigms for the future enterprises in many literatures [9,22].

The life cycle of a dynamic alliance consists of five major phases [15]: identification phase, formation phase, design phase, operation management phase and dissolution phase. During the first phase, one enterprise might search and recognize the business opportunities. After analyzing the internal environment, external environment and evaluating own core competency, it plans to form a new alliance to exploit the market opportunities. The main task of the second phase is to choose the most suitable partners to form dynamic alliance. The design phase specifies detailed procedures for carrying out the mission. It includes the design of new products, development of all material and information flows and the corresponding control and information systems as well as business transactions between the partners, involving negotiations, commitments, contracts, logistics and son on [15]. In the operation management phase, by scheduling and harmonizing the partners’ operational plans, member enterprises collaboratively execute the whole business process of the dynamic alliance, thereby achieving the common goal of the virtual enterprise. The dynamic alliance is a temporary organization with short-term relationships between the partners. After the specific mission is completed, the alliance smoothly dissolves. The member enterprises may search new market opportunities once more and form a new dynamic alliance with other enterprises.

While the dynamic alliance brings a lot of flexibility to us, it also implies some unavoidable risks. Compared to traditional cooperation styles, there are more uncertain factors in a dynamic alliance. For example, in the operation process of alliance, the core technologies of an enterprise will intentionally and unconsciously leak to other member partners, which are likely to become competitors. In contract design process, it is inevitable that there are some ambiguous items, which are likely to result in the profit conflicts among partners and so on. In order to operate dynamic alliance effectively and achieve anticipated goal, it is necessary to have cognizance of all kind of risk factors that influence the operation of alliance and, furthermore, take appropriate measures to prevent and control them. Such process is called risk management. Implementing risk management in dynamic alliance favors to make sure the risk responsibility of each partner, help member enterprises to avoid bad cooperative relations and make alliance operate in lower cost and risk, etc.

The risk management process of dynamic alliance consists of the following four phases (see Fig. 1):

<sup>!</sup> risk identification;

<sup>!</sup> risk measurement and evaluation;

<sup>!</sup> risk prevention and control;

<sup>!</sup> performance assessment of risk management.

Risk identification is the base of whole risk management. Its main task is that working team recognizes all kinds of risk factors by analyzing a great deal of reliable information and data. They not only include those obvious factors but also include those potential factors, which may be more difficult to identify. Working team must analyze the business activities of all alliance members and identify various risk factors in business activities and their characters. Risk measure is to determine the risk level of each risk factor on the basis of risk identification, and risk evaluation is to estimate the total risk magnitude of alliance by using various approaches and technologies such as expert scoring method, AHP method and fuzzy comprehensive evaluation and so on. It is a key phase of risk management process. Wrong risk estimation will result in that alliance loses market opportunities or undertakes unnecessary loss for neglecting risk. After risk is identified, measured and evaluated, it is necessary to take measures to reduce the risk occurrence likelihood and abate loss degree. Such process is called risk prevention and control. The last process is to examine and evaluate the performance of risk prevention and control, and further, to adjust the risk factors and modify the means of risk prevention and control in order to adapt new situation.

Situated in the environment of intense market competition, dynamic alliance organization must have sufficient cognition to various risks that influence alliance operation and implement all-round control and management for such risks. During all phases of development and operation of a dynamic alliance, the risk management plays a major role in ensuring the effective operation of alliance. How to identify, measure and evaluate risks is the basis and premise of risk management implementation. Although many problems on dynamic alliance, such as partner selection, operation management, information exchanges and their standards, etc. [19,14,15,16,22,27], have been investigated deeply, the risk management of dynamic alliance has not received deserved attention until now.

This paper is concerned with the risk measure and evaluation problem in the risk management process of dynamic alliance. In this study, the risk evaluation process is considered a typical multiattribute decisionmaking (MADM) problem with uncertainty. When dealing with such a problems, the following difficulties may be encountered: (1) how to identify various kind of risk factors that influence alliance operation; (2) how to measure the risk level of each elementary factor; (3) how to estimate the total risk magnitude of alliance. When measuring the risk level of each elementary factor, there is often inadequate data or imprecise information available. It is difficult to cope with such a problem using conventional approaches [8]. One of the main limitations associated with conventional methods such as probabilistic risk assessment is the utilization of a probability measure to evaluate uncertainty. More effort is required in defining and establishing probability distribution for each elementary risk factor, using historical data in estimating relative frequencies [19]. Since each new dynamic alliance is affected by different risk factors, accurate knowledge of relative frequencies cannot be assumed from another alliance. In this paper, we use three parameters such as risk occurrence likelihood, consequence severity and risk control degree to measure the risk level of each elementary factor and use trapezoidal fuzzy numbers to express these parameters. When estimating the total risk magnitude of alliance, the evidential reasoning (ER) approach is employed to synthesize the risk levels of all elementary factors. The ER approach [23–26] is well suited for handling the uncertain risk evaluation associated with dynamic alliance. This approach is based on the principle that it will become more likely that a given hypothesis is true if more pieces of evidence support that hypothesis [21].

The rest of this paper is organized as follows: in the subsequent section, risk factors in dynamic alliance are identified and their characteristics are described. In the third section, the ER approach is introduced. Section 4 details our research on how to measure and estimate alliance risk. Section 5 gives an example to illustrate proposed risk evaluation process. The final section concludes this paper.

## 2. The identification and description of risk factors

The identification of risk factors is an important step in the evaluation process as it attempts to structure the diverse risk factors that may affect the operation of alliance. The life cycle of a dynamic alliance is full of various risks. These risks come from many aspects such as policy, finance, market, competency, cooperation, operation and so on. Usually, they can be classified into two categories [7,12]. One category is defined as the set of all risk factors from external environment, including market risk (MR), financial risk (FR), political risk (PR) and natural risk (NR). The other one is defined as the set of all risk factors from internal environment, including competency risk (CR), relational risk (RR), investment risk (IR) and operational risk (OR; see Fig. 2). Furthermore, these risk factors can be decomposed into detailed subfactors (i.e., elementary risk factors).

## 3. MADM model based on the evidential reasoning method

Multiattribute decision-making (MADM) is defined as decision aids in evaluating and selecting alternative options, which are characterized by multiple, usually conflicting, attributes or criteria. MADM problems are common in our life, such as partner selection, contractor prequalification process and global investing risk analysis. Usually, the decision-making process of multiple attributes can be divided into three steps: (1) structuring the decision problem, (2) formulating a preference model and (3) evaluating and comparing alternatives [18]. So far, many decision-making methods have been proposed in literatures [11,13]. These approaches include MAUT, SAW, AHP, TOP-SIS, ELECTRE and PROMETHEE and so on. However, the majority of methods require certain and precise information from the decision-maker. This may not be the case in real-word decision problems since most decisions are made in the presence of some information that is uncertain, incomplete and/or missing [20]. Therefore, there is a need to develop new methods and tools for dealing with multiattribute decision-making problems under uncertainty.

Over the past two decades, considerable researches [1,4,6,10,19] have been conducted on integrating techniques from artificial intelligence (AI), such as fuzzy logic, expert system, KDD and operational research (OR), for handling uncertain information. Following this line of research, an evidential reasoning (ER) approach has been proposed [23–26]. It offers a rational, reliable way to aggregate uncertain, incomplete and vague data. In recent years, this approach has been applied to different fields such as engineering design selection, organizational selfassessment, safety and risk assessment and supplier assessment. Nowadays, ER approach is considered as one of the best approaches to deal with MADM problem with uncertainty.

The kernel of the ER approach is an evidential reasoning algorithm developed on the basis of a multiattribute decision-making framework and the evidence combination rule of the D–S theory [17]. It is different from most conventional MADM modeling methods in the following three aspects: (1) it employs a belief structure to represent an assessment as a distribution instead of as a single numerical score, which can capture various types of uncertainties such as vagueness in subjective judgments; (2) when decision-maker is not able to provide a precise judgment because of inadequacy of information available, the ER approach allows a DM to define a degree of belief of less than 1. No other MADM approaches can deal with this level of uncertainty; (3) it employs the evidential combination rule to aggregate degrees of belief rather than scores. In this way, the evidential reasoning approach can preserve the qualitative feature of subjective attribute in aggregation process.

In order to describe ER approach, suppose there is a simple two level evaluation hierarchy with a general attribute at the upper level and several associated basic attributes $A _ { 1 } , A _ { 2 } , . . . , A _ { L }$ at the lower level (see Fig. 3). Suppose the weights of the attributes are given by $\scriptstyle w = \left( w _ { 1 } , w _ { 2 } , \dotsc , w _ { L } \right)$ where $w _ { i }$ is relative weight for basic attribute and $A _ { 1 }$ is normalized so that

$$
0 \leq w _ {i} \leq 1 \text { and } \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {L} w _ {i} = 1.
$$

Weights play an important role in assessment. They may be estimated using existing methods such as expert rating method and AHP. Each basic attribute may be assessed with reference to a set of evaluation grates with different degrees of belief. The use of evaluation grades facilitates data collection and allows capture of the decision-maker’s preference, experience and intuition. The general scale of evaluation grades is defined as:

$$
H = \{H _ {1}, H _ {2}, \dots . H _ {N} \}.
$$

The assessment of an attribute $A _ { i } ,$ denoted by $S ( A _ { i } )$ can be represented using the belief structure as

$$
S (A _ {i}) = \left\{\left(H _ {1}, \beta_ {1, i}\right), \left(H _ {2}, \beta_ {2, i}\right), \dots , \left(H _ {N}, \beta_ {N, i}\right) \right\}
$$

where $\beta _ { n , i }$ denotes a degree of belief that the attribute $A _ { i }$ is assessed to the evaluation grade $H _ { n }$ and $\beta _ { n , i } { \ge } 0$ with $\textstyle \sum _ { n = 1 } ^ { N } \beta _ { n , i } { \leq } 1$ . An assessment $S ( A _ { i } )$ is complete if $\begin{array} { r } { \sum _ { n = 1 } ^ { N } \beta _ { n , i } = 1 } \end{array}$ and incomplete if $\scriptstyle \sum _ { n = 1 } ^ { N } \beta _ { n , 1 } < 1$

Let $m _ { n , i }$ be a basic probability mass representing the degree to which ith the basic attribute A supports a hypothesis that the general attribute A at $A _ { i }$ is assessed to the nth evaluation grade $H _ { n } .$ . Let $m _ { H , i }$ be a remaining probability mass unassigned to any individual grade after $A _ { i }$ has been assessed. $m _ { n , i }$ and $m _ { H , i }$ are calculated as follows:

$$
m _ {n, i} = w _ {i} \beta_ {n, i}, \quad n = 1, 2, \dots , N, i = 1, 2, \dots , L,
$$

$$
m _ {H, i} = 1 - \sum_ {n = 1} ^ {N} m _ {n, i} = 1 - w _ {i} \sum_ {n = 1} ^ {N} \beta_ {n, i}, i = 1, 2, \dots , L.
$$

To simplify the discussion, only the combination of complete assessments is given in this section. The detail description of recursive evidential reasoning algorithm capable of aggregating both complete and incomplete assessments can be found in literature [26].

Define $E _ { I ( i ) }$ as the subset of the first basic attributes as follows:

$$
E _ {I (i)} = \left\{A _ {1}, A _ {2}, \dots , A _ {i} \right\}.
$$

Let $m _ { n , I ( i ) }$ be a probability mass defined as the degree to which all the i attributes in $E _ { I ( i ) }$ support the hypothesis that A is assessed to the grade $H _ { n } . \ m _ { H , I ( i ) }$ is the remaining probability mass unassigned to individual grades after all the basic attributes in $E _ { I ( i ) }$ have been assessed. Obviously, $m _ { n , I ( 1 ) } { = } m _ { n , 1 }$ and $m _ { H , I ( 1 ) } { = } m _ { H , I } \ m _ { n , I ( L ) }$ and $m _ { H , I ( i ) }$ can be generated by aggregating all the all the basic probability assignment, using the following recursive ER algorithm [23]:

$$
\begin{array}{c} m _ {n, I (i + 1)} = K _ {I (i + 1)} (m _ {n, I (i)} m _ {n, i + 1} + m _ {n, I (i)} m _ {H, i + 1} \\ \qquad + m _ {H, I (i)} m _ {n, i + 1}), n = 1, 2, \dots , N, \end{array}
$$

$$
m _ {H, I (i + 1)} = K _ {I (i + 1)} m _ {H, I (i)} m _ {H, i + 1}
$$

$$
K_{I(i + 1)} = \left[1 - \sum_{t = 1}^{N}\sum_{\substack{j = 1\\ j\neq t}}m_{t,I(i)}m_{j,i + 1}\right]^{-1},
$$

$$
i = 1, 2, \dots L - 1.
$$

Let $\beta _ { n }$ denote a degree of belief that the general attribute A is assessed to the grade $H _ { n }$ , which is generated by combining the assessment for all the basic attributes. $\beta _ { n }$ is then calculated by

$$
\beta_ {n} = \frac {m _ {n , I (L)}}{1 - m _ {H , I (L)}} \qquad n = 1, 2, \dots , N.
$$

The aggregated assessment for attribute A can be given by the following distribution:

$$
S (A) = \{(H _ {n}, \beta_ {n}) | n = 1, 2, \dots . N \}.
$$

It has been proven in literature [26] that the above aggregation process follows following synthesis axioms:

Axiom 1. If none of the basic attributes $A _ { 1 } , A _ { 2 } ; \cdots , A _ { L }$ is assessed to $H _ { n } , A$ must not be assessed to a grade $H _ { n }$

Axiom 2. If all the basic attributes $A _ { 1 } , A _ { 2 } ; \cdots , A _ { L }$ are precisely assessed to $H _ { n }$ , A should be precisely assessed to a grade $H _ { n }$

Axiom 3. If all the basic attributes $A _ { 1 } , A _ { 2 } ; \cdots , A _ { L }$ are completely assessed to a subset of evaluation grades, then A should be completely assessed to the same subset of grades.

## 4. A decision support framework for the risk evaluation of dynamic alliance

The risk evaluation of dynamic alliance is a typical multiattribute decision-making problem. In evaluation process, there are multiple risk factors involved, and the measures of some factors are vague due to the highly subjective nature and lack of past experience. Hence, it is difficult to use traditional MADM approaches to deal such problem with uncertainty. In this respect, ER approach and fuzzy set theory show significant potential. In this section, we propose a decision support framework for the risk evaluation of dynamic alliance. Fig. 4 depicts the generic framework. ## 4.2. Use fuzzy Delphi method to determine the ratings of all parameters associated with each elementary risk factor

After the hierarchical structure of risk factors is created, the next task is to measure the risk level of each elementary factor. There are many parameters that may affect risk level of each elementary factor. These parameters include risk occurrence likelihood and consequence severity. Risk occurrence likelihood describes the frequency of risk occurrence in the life span of a dynamic alliance. Consequence severity describes the magnitude of possible loss when risk happens. In addition to these two parameters, risk control degree is also an important parameter that affects risk level of elementary factor. Risk control degree refers to the extent to which risk can be prevented and controlled. We maintain that control can reduce risk occurrence likelihood and/or undesirable consequence. Therefore, high-risk control degree can debase risk level. Due to the highly subjective nature and lack of information, it is usually difficult to measure these parameters precisely. A feasible and convenient way to describe three parameters is to use verbal expressions (i.e., linguistic variables). To estimate the risk occurrence likelihood, for example, one may often use such variables as <sup>b</sup>definite<sup>Q</sup>, <sup>b</sup>highly likely<sup>Q</sup>, <sup>b</sup>reasonably likely<sup>Q</sup>, <sup>b</sup>likely<sup>Q</sup>, <sup>b</sup>reasonably unlikely<sup>Q</sup>, <sup>b</sup>unlikely<sup>Q</sup> and <sup>b</sup>highly unlikely<sup>Q</sup>; to estimate the consequence severity, one may often use such variables as <sup>b</sup>negligible<sup>Q</sup>, <sup>b</sup>slight<sup>Q</sup>, <sup>b</sup>minor<sup>Q</sup>, <sup>b</sup>moderate<sup>Q</sup>, <sup>b</sup>critical<sup>Q</sup>, <sup>b</sup>serious<sup>Q</sup>, <sup>b</sup>catastrophic<sup>Q</sup>; and to estimate risk control degree, one may often use such variables as <sup>b</sup>almost complete<sup>Q</sup>, <sup>b</sup>very high <sup>Q</sup>, <sup>b</sup>high<sup>Q</sup>, <sup>b</sup>medium<sup>Q</sup>, <sup>b</sup>low<sup>Q</sup>, <sup>b</sup> very low<sup>Q</sup> and <sup>b</sup>almost never<sup>Q</sup>; these subjective linguistic variables can be further defined in terms of membership functions. A membership function is a curve that defines how each point in the input space is mapped to a membership value between 0 and 1. Of these membership functions, the simplest are the triangular fuzzy number and trapezoidal fuzzy number. They are used to represent above linguistic variables, as shown in Table 3 and Fig. 5. Membership degrees of the three parameters associated with an elementary risk factor can be assigned by experts, with reference to Table 3.

4.3. Transform the fuzzy ratings of all parameters into belief structures with the same set of evaluation grades and calculate the risk level of each elementary risk factor

After the fuzzy ratings of all parameters associated with each elementary risk factor are $A _ { i j k }$ gained, the risk level of $A _ { i j k }$ may be calculated by using the information of these parameters. Generally, the evaluation of the risk level of the elementary factor may be described by the following linguistic variables or evaluation grades:

$$
\begin{array}{l} H = \left\{H _ {1}, H _ {2}, H _ {3}, H _ {4}, \right\} \\ \quad = \{\text {" very small", " relatively small)}, \\ \quad \text {" relativelybig", " very big"} \}. \end{array}
$$

These linguistic variables can be further defined in terms of trapezoidal fuzzy numbers. They are shown in Table 4 and Fig. 6.

In order to evaluate $A _ { i j k }$ in terms of these linguistic variables, it is necessary to project the fuzzy ratings $\mu _ { P _ { i j k } } , \mu _ { R _ { i j k } } , \mu _ { C _ { i j k } }$ onto the risk evaluation grades. In this section, a new approach is proposed to complete this transformation. Suppose the trapezoidal fuzzy numbers of risk evaluation grades $H _ { 1 } , H _ { 2 } ; \cdots ,$ $H _ { 4 }$ are $R _ { 1 } , R _ { 2 } ; \cdot \cdot , R _ { 4 } ,$ respectively. We define the similarity degree between $\mu _ { P _ { i j k } }$ and $R _ { n } ( n { = } 1 , 2 ; \cdots , 4 )$ as follows:

$$
S \left(P _ {i j k}, R _ {n}\right) = \frac {\int_ {- \infty} ^ {+ \infty} \left(\min \left\{\mu_ {P _ {i j k}} (x) , R _ {n} \right\}\right) \mathrm{d} x}{\int_ {= \infty} ^ {+ \infty} \mu_ {P _ {i j k}} (x) \mathrm{d} x}
$$

For example, the similarity degree between $\mu _ { P _ { i j k } }$ and $R _ { n } ( n { = } 1 , \ 2 ; \ \cdot \ , \ 4 )$ in Fig. 7 is calculated as follows:

$$
S \left(P _ {i j k}, R _ {1}\right) = \frac {0}{\text { area } 1 + \text { area } 2 + \text { area } 3} = 0
$$

$$
S (P _ {i j k}, R _ {2}) = \frac {\text { area } 1}{\text { area } 1 + \text { area } 2 + \text { area } 3} = 0. 4
$$

$$
S \left(P _ {i j k}, R _ {3}\right) = \frac {\text { area } 2}{\text { area } 1 + \text { area } 2 + \text { area } 3} = 0. 2 7
$$

$$
S \left(P _ {i j k}, R _ {4}\right) = \frac {0}{\text {area} 1 + \text {area} 2 + \text {area} 3} = 0
$$

If two membership functions are the same, that is $\mu _ { P _ { i j k } } { = } R _ { n }$ , we get $S ( P _ { i j k } , R _ { n } ) { = } 1$ . If two membership functions are completely different, the similarity degree is zero. The higher the percentage of overlap, the higher the similarity degree is.

After all the similarity degrees between $\mu _ { P _ { i j k } }$ and $R _ { n } , n { = } 1 , 2 , \cdot \cdot , 4$ are computed, a similarity vector $\mathrm { S V } _ { P _ { i j k } }$ can be constructed as follows:

$$
\mathrm{SV} _ {P _ {i j k}} = \left(S \left(P _ {i j k}, R _ {1}\right), S \left(P _ {i j k}, R _ {2}\right), S \left(P _ {i j k}, R _ {3}\right), S \left(P _ {i j k}, R _ {4}\right)\right)
$$

Furthermore, $S ( P _ { i j k } , R _ { n } )$ can be normalized by:

$$
\beta_ {P _ {i j k}} ^ {n} = \frac {S (P _ {i j k} , R _ {n})}{\sum_ {n = 1} ^ {4} S (P _ {i j k} , R _ {n})} \quad n = 1, 2, \dots , 4.
$$

It should be noted that the closer $\mu _ { P _ { i j k } }$ is to $R _ { n }$ , the bigger is $\beta _ { P _ { i j k } } ^ { n }$ Specifically, if $P _ { i j k }$ only overlaps with the nth expression $H _ { n } ,$ then $\bar { \beta } _ { P _ { i j k } } ^ { n } { = } 1$ and others are equal to 0. The sum of these indices $\beta _ { P _ { i j k } } ^ { n } ( n { = } 1 , 2 ; \cdots ,$ $^ { 4 ) }$ is equal to 1. Thus, $\beta _ { P _ { i j } } ^ { n }$ may be viewed as a degree of confidence that $P _ { i j k }$ belongs to evaluation grade $H _ { n }$ . In this way, the fuzzy rating $\mu _ { P _ { i j k } }$ can be transformed into the belief structure $S ( \ ' P _ { i j k } )$ with evaluation grades $H _ { 1 } , H _ { 2 } ; \cdot \cdot , H _ { 4 } . S ( P _ { i j k } )$ is expressed in the following form:

$$
S (P _ {i j k}) = \left\{\left(H _ {1}, \beta_ {P _ {i j k}} ^ {1}\right), \left(H _ {2}, \beta_ {P _ {i j k}} ^ {2}\right), \left(H _ {3}, \beta_ {P _ {i j k}} ^ {3}\right), \left(H _ {4}, \beta_ {P _ {i j k}} ^ {4}\right) \right\}.
$$

Using the same approach, the fuzzy ratings $\mu _ { R _ { i j k } }$ and $u _ { C _ { i i k } }$ can be also transformed into the belief structures $S ( R _ { i j k } )$ and $S ( C _ { i j k } )$ with evaluation grades, respectively.

The risk level $S ( A _ { i j k } )$ of elementary factor $A _ { i j k }$ is determined by three parameters: $P _ { i j k } , R _ { i j k } , C _ { i j k }$ Generally, the three parameters may play different roles in risk measure of $A _ { i j k }$ . In order to represent the relative importance of these parameters, we suppose that the weights of the parameters $P _ { i j k } , R _ { i j k } , C _ { i j k }$ are given by $\lambda _ { P _ { i j k } } , \lambda _ { R _ { i j k } } , \lambda _ { C _ { i j k } } .$ , respectively, and they are normalized so that

$$

$$

In this paper, we regard whether the risk $S ( A _ { i j k } )$ evaluation of elementary factor $A _ { i j k }$ belongs to $H _ { 1 } ,$ $H _ { 2 } , \ H _ { 3 }$ or $H _ { 4 }$ as a hypothesis. 4.4. Determine the weight of each risk factor in the hierarchical structure and perform the risk synthesis using the evidential reasoning approach

In the risk evaluation process of dynamic alliance, different risk factors may play different role. We use weights to represent the relative importance of each risk factor. AHP can be used to obtain the relative weight of each attribute based on a pair-wise comparison matrix. After the risk evaluation associated with each elementary factor is calculated, the evidential reasoning algorithm is used to carry out the synthesis at different levels until the total risk evaluation S(A) of alliance is gained.

## 5. An illustrative example

In this section, an example will be given to illustrate the risk evaluation process on dynamic alliance. Suppose a dynamic alliance has been formed. The alliance managers, who coordinate the activities of the independent partners, invite five experts to evaluate the risk of this alliance. In the past, the risk evaluation of alliance was all based on the experiences of managers. However, managers find that it is difficult to make evaluation since there are multiple risk factors involved and the measure of some factors are vague due to the highly subjective nature and lack of past experience. Therefore, it is required to have a decision–aid tool to assist managers to perform risk analyses.

## 6. Conclusions

During whole life cycle of a dynamic alliance, the risk management plays a major role in ensuring the effective operation of alliance, whereas risk evaluation is the basis and premise of risk management implementation. In the process of risk evaluation, it is inevitable that the risk measure of some elementary factors will be vague due to the highly subjective nature and lack of past experience. Therefore, it is difficult to apply tradition risk assessment techniques. A very generic formal assessment framework should be proposed to deal with such complicated problems.

In this presentation, the subjective risk evaluation of alliance is regarded as a multiattribute decisionmaking problem under uncertainty. An analysis process based on evidential reasoning framework and fuzzy set theory is proposed. Firstly, risk management process is described, risk factors are identified and their effects are measured by three parameters such as risk occurrence likelihood, loss severity and risk control degree. The ratings of parameters associated with the elementary risk factors are expressed in trapezoidal fuzzy numbers and obtained by utilizing fuzzy Delphi method. Then, we propose a new approach to transform the fuzzy ratings of all parameters into belief structures with the same set of evaluation grades. Finally, the ER approach is applied to perform the risk synthesis at different level. In sum, the decision support framework proposed in this paper provides alliance managers with a new and feasible tool to analyze the risk of an alliance.
