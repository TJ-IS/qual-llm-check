# Exploring Knowledge Filtering Processes in Electronic Networks of Practice

KELLY J. FADEL, THOMAS O. MESERVY, AND MATTHEW L. JENSEN

ABSTRACT: Electronic networks of practice (ENPs) have become an important mechanism for knowledge exchange among loosely connected individuals who share common knowledge interests. While prior research has explored factors that influence knowledge contribution in such networks, less is understood about the process by which individuals evaluate and ultimately adopt knowledge from ENPs. This study examines the process of knowledge filtering in online ENP forums. Drawing from dual process and information-evaluation theories, we hypothesize that performance on a knowledge-filtering task will be influenced by the constancy and directionality of search patterns employed by knowledge seekers. Hypotheses are tested in an experiment that utilized an eye tracker to record gaze data from professional software developers using an experimental ENP forum. By combining information-evaluation and dual process theory perspectives, our results deepen the insights offered in extant information-processing literature by showing that higher filtering accuracy is associated with (a) constant evaluation of some types of information attributes (solution content) but not others (peripheral cues), and (b) increasing attribute-based processing over time.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: electronic networks of practice, filtering, knowledge evaluation, constancy, directionality, field experiment, eye tracking.

With modern search engines providing access to unprecedented amounts of information, the Internet has become a central venue for knowledge exchange among individuals of all types. Such exchange is particularly common among electronic networks of practice (ENPs), collections of geographically separated individuals who share common interests but are loosely affiliated and communicate via technology-mediated channels [39, 40]. One of the most common tools used by ENPs is the online knowledge forum, a virtual bulletin board where ENP members can post and retrieve knowledge about various problems relevant to network members. ENP forums have been created to cover a wide range of topics, including open source software, mathematics, photography, home improvement, biology, programming languages, travel, specific hobbies, parenting, and natural languages, among others (http://stackexchange.com/sites). Experts and novices alike turn to these forums to pose questions and receive answers from others within the network. For example, a researcher investigating a new statistical technique might use an online forum such as www.talkstats.com to post a question to other members in the ENP about the proper application of the technique. The same researcher might also supply answers to questions posted by other ENP members. Trade studies indicate that even compared to other types of contemporary social media platforms, ENP forums remain a popular mechanism for online knowledge exchange [31].

As illustrated by the above example, knowledge exchange through an ENP forum entails both knowledge contribution and knowledge seeking by ENP participants. To date, research has begun to investigate what motivates individuals to contribute knowledge to an ENP [39, 40]; however, less is understood about the process by which individuals evaluate and ultimately adopt knowledge from ENPs. Due to the open nature of these networks [16], the quality of knowledge contributions to an ENP forum is rarely regulated or readily apparent to the knowledge seeker. Most searches in ENP forums produce several, potentially competing candidate solutions that can vary in completeness, accuracy, and utility for solving a given problem. Thus, knowledge seekers bear the responsibility of evaluating candidate solutions, filtering out those that do not appear promising, and ultimately deciding which (if any) will suit their needs. Since adopting flawed or inaccurate knowledge can be very costly, there is significant incentive to better understand the process by which knowledge seekers evaluate and filter knowledge contained in online ENP forums.

We have initiated a series of research studies aimed at achieving better theoretical understanding of how individuals evaluate and adopt knowledge encountered on ENP forums. These efforts include examination of both what knowledge seekers evaluate on such forums and how they go about doing so. We report the results of an experiment relating to the former element in another paper [22]. In this study, we focus on the latter component: the evaluation process in which knowledge seekers engage as they filter and evaluate knowledge encountered on an ENP forum. Specifically, our goal is to understand differences in knowledge-evaluation processes employed by knowledge seekers and whether these differences ultimately lead to more accurate filtering outcomes (i.e., properly retaining high-quality knowledge and filtering out low-quality knowledge). The overarching objective of this research stream is to contribute to a more complete theoretical picture of knowledge exchange within ENPs.

## Theoretical Background

## Knowledge, Search, Filtering, and Adoption

Decision theory suggests that when people face a problem that requires further information, they engage in a knowledge-seeking process involving three basic stages: search, filtering, and, ultimately, adoption of some knowledge element that is believed to solve the problem [34]. These stages are depicted in Figure 1 [adapted from 22]. During the search stage,<sup>1</sup> the individual collects information that she believes is relevant to the problem at hand. In the context of the example cited earlier, the researcher’s search may consist of navigating to an online ENP forum and entering search terms to find posts from other network participants that may be relevant to her questions about the new research method. Once the individual has found potentially relevant information, she engages in filtering, wherein she examines each knowledge element and determines whether it should be rejected or retained for further consideration. For example, the researcher might examine a number of forum posts relevant to her question and decide which, if any, are worth considering further. The process of filtering ultimately leads to a final adoption decision, wherein the person selects one of the available knowledge elements to be applied to solving the problem. Continuing our example, the researcher would decide on a particular post that she believes accurately addresses her question and would apply the new technique according to the recommendations in the adopted post.

The general process of knowledge seeking and adoption for decision making has an old and rich tradition in cognitive psychology [5, 26, 27]. However, it has also received growing interest among information systems (IS) researchers, due, in part, to the increasing role that technology plays in mediating the exchange of such information [37]. A number of IS studies have begun to explore how technology-mediated information is searched and adopted by knowledge seekers. For example, with respect to search, researchers have developed taxonomies of Internet search types [6], explored impacts that the type of task has on the search process [41], and examined how and when people stop searching for information online [7]. Another body of literature has explored the adoption of knowledge via technology-mediated channels, including e-mail [35], knowledge repositories [15], and closer-knit communities of practice [42]. Much of this work has relied on dual process models of human cognition, such as the elaboration-likelihood model (ELM) [29, 30] or the heuristic systematic model (HSM) [8, 13] of information processing, which posit that knowledge seekers attend to various types of central and peripheral cues as they determine whether to adopt technology-mediated knowledge.

While the above-cited literature constitutes an important contribution to our understanding of the knowledge-seeking and knowledge-adoption process, to date, the IS literature has devoted less attention to the filtering stage of this process. Studies exploring the knowledge adoption phase [15, 35, 42] have focused on the final adoption decision of only one or two knowledge elements that were preselected by the researcher or solicited by the knowledge seeker from a known source. While valuable, this approach assumes that the filtering process has already taken place or is superfluous. However, in the context of an ENP forum, the filtering phase is particularly important for several reasons [39, 40]. First, participation in ENPs is self-organizing and voluntary, meaning that “knowledge seekers have no control over who voluntarily responds to their questions, or the helpfulness or relevance of the responses to the current problem at hand” [40, p. 257]. Second, there are few if any constraints on participation in the network, which means that posts to a forum may be made by anyone from experts to novices and even to saboteurs [11, 16]. Finally, the asynchronous and geographically distributed nature of the network means that communication between its participants is void of innate social or other nonverbal cues that might ease the filtering process. The combination of these unique ENP characteristics increases the burden on the knowledge seeker, who must filter and evaluate multiple solutions from unknown sources in a contextually sparse environment.

As noted earlier, our focus in this paper is to understand the process by which knowledge encountered in an ENP forum is filtered and evaluated. However, doing so requires a theoretical framework for understanding what knowledge elements might be encountered on an ENP forum and how they might factor into filtering decisions. Following prior IS literature, we examine this topic through the lens of a dual process theory of information processing.

## Dual Process Filtering

Theory suggests that knowledge seekers have two competing goals when filtering knowledge en route to a final adoption decision [10, 37]. The first objective is speed: the filtering process should proceed quickly and require as little cognitive effort as possible. The second objective is accuracy: the filtering process should discard lowquality knowledge candidates and retain high-quality candidates for further consideration. A natural trade-off exists between these two objectives: quick, low-effort filtering is likely to compromise accuracy whereas more deliberate filtering has greater time and effort requirements [28]. This trade-off coincides with the core tenet of a dual process theory of information processing [13, 29]. Dual process theory suggests that information processing occurs along two primary routes: central and peripheral. Central-route processing involves deliberate evaluation of the knowledge content itself. Because central processing requires assessment of the knowledge on its own merits, it typically requires more time and effort. In contrast, peripheral processing relies on the interpretation of various peripheral cues surrounding the knowledge. These cues may or may not accurately reflect the quality of the knowledge in question, but they are readily available for quick evaluation. According to dual process theory, the more dominant route in a given situation will depend on the expertise and motivation of the knowledge seeker. If motivation and expertise are high, the person will rely more on central-route processing because accuracy is paramount and the person is capable of evaluating the information on its own merits. If motivation and expertise are low, the person is more likely to rely on peripheralroute processing, which requires less time and fewer cognitive resources.

Both central and peripheral processing can occur in the context of an ENP forum. Central processing, by definition, would require that the knowledge seeker analyze the content of a given forum post and decide whether it should receive further consideration. Peripheral processing, on the other hand, would rely on surrounding peripheral cues. In ENP forums, such cues commonly include indicators of contributor expertise [e.g., 35, 42] or external validation [e.g., 12, 15] of a post by one or more other ENP participants. These cues may or may not accurately reflect the quality of a given post, but can be quickly evaluated as surrogates for knowledge quality. Research indicates that knowledge seekers may be more likely to rely on such cues in cases where many alternatives are present [19, 33], a circumstance fostered by the openly participatory nature of ENP forums [39, 40]. In short, evaluating the content and peripheral cues of multiple forum posts constitutes a multi-alternative, multi-attribute filtering task [21] that must be performed by the ENP knowledge seeker.

Theory suggests that both central and peripheral processing are involved in most information-evaluation activities and that individuals can switch between them in a given evaluation task [9]. In an ENP forum, this would entail attentional shifts between the content of the retrieved solutions and any available peripheral cues surrounding them. However, exactly how different attentional switching patterns affect filtering accuracy remains unclear. It seems likely, for example, that an ENP knowledge seeker who examines the content of each available forum post on a particular topic before considering the peripheral cues surrounding each post might filter the information differently from someone who considers all aspects of one post (content and peripheral cues) before moving to the next. While such a proposition is not incompatible with extant theories of information processing—indeed, several studies of information gathering for preferential decision making have observed differences in filtering patterns and decision outcomes [e.g., 1, 20, 26, 37]—to our knowledge no study has yet examined how filtering patterns relate to performance in a normative decision-making task in the more contemporary context of an online ENP forum. Thus, our objective in this study is to explore how attentional switching patterns affect filtering accuracy when an individual encounters several qualitatively distinct solution alternatives in such a forum. In the following section, we draw from literature on information search for decision making and human–computer interaction to develop hypotheses regarding how variations in filtering processes affect the accuracy of filtering outcomes.

## Hypothesis Development

Literature on information search for decision making suggests two primary dimensions that characterize information-evaluation patterns in multi-alternative, multiattribute filtering tasks [26, 27]. The first dimension concerns the constancy with which the knowledge seeker evaluates the attributes of each solution alternative. To be considered constant, the person should examine the same attributes of each alternative in the filtering process. For example, a person presented with multiple possible solutions on an ENP forum might examine two of the three available attributes (e.g., the solution content and the source expertise) for each solution alternative. Importantly, constancy does not necessarily imply completeness in evaluating all attributes; that is, a person need not examine all of the available attributes, only the same attributes across each alternative to be considered constant [26]. Because a constant evaluation pattern implies that the evaluator is systematically considering all relevant aspects of each solution alternative, it is suggestive of a compensatory evaluation strategy wherein the attribute values of a given solution alternative are thoroughly considered, aggregated, and weighed against the comparative score of another solution alternative [27, 28]. Compensatory evaluation strategies typically require more time and cognitive resources because they require the individual to carefully balance the combination of attributes of one alternative against that of the other alternatives [5]. Consequently, compensatory strategies are often employed when accuracy is important and the individual has the capacity to process the all the relevant information. In contrast, a variable (inconstant) evaluation pattern is characterized by inconsistent examination of the attributes of each alternative. This is usually taken to denote a noncompensatory evaluation strategy in which the value for all solution alternatives on a single attribute (or set of attributes) may qualify or disqualify them for further consideration [27]. Congruent with the dual process concept of peripheral route processing, noncompensatory evaluation strategies are typically employed when quick evaluation is paramount and/or when the person lacks the inclination or capability to thoroughly process all the information at hand. For example, in the present context, a knowledge seeker might use a noncompensatory strategy by screening posted alternatives based on whether or not they have been validated by a third party, a common peripheral cue in ENP forums. While such a strategy is beneficial for speedy filtering, it is not likely to be as accurate as careful consideration and comparison of all solution attributes. This leads to the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1: In an ENP forum, a predominantly constant (compensatory) evaluation pattern will be associated with greater filtering accuracy than a predominantly variable (noncompensatory) evaluation pattern.

The second dimension of information evaluation patterns concerns the directionality of the search, or the relative frequency of attentional switches between the same attribute of different solution alternatives (attribute-based processing) versus attentional switches between different attributes within the same solution (alternativebased processing) [26, 37]. Directionality is a commonly measured characteristic in the information-based decision-making literature [5]; however, because most studies have focused on preferential decision tasks, there has been less theorization about how directionality alone influences filtering accuracy in a normative task.

In the context of an ENP forum, we hypothesize that attribute-based processing (characterized by focusing on the same attribute across different solutions) will lead to more accurate filtering decisions, particularly when such switching focuses on the content of the solution alternative being considered. The logic behind this hypothesis derives from dual process theory, which holds that systematic (central) processing of the information content itself, though more resource-intensive, usually produces more accurate assessment of objective information quality than does peripheral processing [8, 29]. Peripheral cues, although convenient for quick processing, are often superficial and may be misleading, particularly in the open context of an ENP forum, as discussed earlier. Thus, if knowledge seekers interrupt their analysis of solution content by alternating their attention to its peripheral cues (alternative-based processing), their judgments of its objective quality are more likely to be swayed by the potentially unreliable influence of these cues. Moreover, frequent switching among potentially contrasting cues of the same solution (e.g., high source expertise but lack of validation by others) can increase cognitive burden [32] and may obfuscate the evaluation process by interfering with the ability to isolate qualitative differences between the solutions themselves. In contrast, the individual who systematically compares the content of each solution (central-route, attribute-based processing) should be more likely to properly discern high- and low-quality solutions. Thus, we hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 2: In an ENP forum, an evaluation pattern characterized by higher levels of attribute-based processing focused on the solution content will be associated with greater filtering accuracy than a pattern characterized by higher levels of alternative-based processing.

Our final hypotheses concern how people’s filtering patterns change as they proceed through an information evaluation task. Prior literature indicates that filtering may be decomposed into two phases: acquisition and evaluation [14]. In the acquisition phase, the individual first scans through the available information and stores it in memory for future use.<sup>2</sup> She then proceeds to the evaluation phase, wherein the acquired information is combined to arrive at a choice. Theory suggests that patterns of attentional switching may differ for each of these phases due to the different cognitive demands placed on the individual. For example, Jarvenpaa [20] found that during acquisition, individuals’ predominant attentional switches (i.e., alternative vs. attribute) were largely a function of the format in which the information was presented. However, during evaluation, attentional-switching patterns changed based on the presentation format as well as the demands of the task (in Jarvenpaa [20], choosing between a set of six restaurants, each of which were described by seven attributes). As described by Jarvenpaa [20, p. 299], “participants first employed a processing direction that was least demanding and least prone to errors given the graphical format. Then, as the demands of the task became apparent in evaluation, participants changed to some extent to the processing direction that more effectively minimized errors and effort related to task demands.” Based on these results, we anticipate that individuals’ patterns of information filtering may shift over time as they proceed from gathering all of the available information to combining it to arrive at a filtering decision. During the initial acquisition phase, individuals should be more likely to move through the information according to the constraints of the layout. For an ENP forum, this would likely entail a sequential process wherein the individual considers each holistic solution block (i.e., content as well as peripheral cues) as presented on the screen. This is consistent with HCI research that has shown consistent layout-driven gaze patterns when an individual is first presented with a web page [e.g., 17, 24, 25]. However, as the individual moves from acquisition to evaluation, she is required to draw comparisons among different solutions in order to make accurate filtering decisions. This process is likely to require repeated attentional shifts among the alternative solutions being compared. Accordingly, we anticipate that the degree of attribute-based processing will increase later in the filtering task for all participants.

Hypothesis 3: When an individual filters knowledge encountered in an ENP forum, her filtering patterns will evolve to exhibit increased attribute-based processing later in the filtering task.

In addition, we anticipate that greater increases in attribute-based processing over time will be associated with greater filtering accuracy. In other words, as individuals transition from acquisition to evaluation later in the filtering task, those who exhibit more attribute-based processing will also exhibit greater filtering accuracy. Attributebased processing enables more direct comparisons between competing candidate solutions, allowing knowledge seekers to more accurately discern the differences between solutions. Moreover, past research comparing alternative-based and attribute-based processing in preferential tasks [e.g., 32] has suggested that evaluation using attribute-based processing can produce efficient and accurate filtering decisions. We expect similar results in a normative filtering task.

Hypothesis 4: Higher levels of attribute-based processing later in the filtering task will be associated with greater filtering accuracy.

In summary, the two dimensions of filtering patterns discussed (constancy and directionality) can be placed on a quadrant that characterizes an individual’s overall evaluation pattern. This quadrant is shown in Table 1. Taken together, the combinations of constancy and directionality characterize four prototypical information evaluation models identified in the decision-making literature [1, 5, 26]: additive, additive difference, conjunctive, and elimination-by-aspects models. In the additive model (constant, alternative-based processing), scores are assigned to each attribute within an alternative and added together. Each alternative is then evaluated holistically in relation to other alternatives. The additive difference model (constant, attribute-based processing) works in a similar fashion, except that single attributes are compared across alternatives rather than different attributes being compared within the same alternative. In the conjunctive model (variable, alternative-based processing), a threshold value is assigned to all attributes and each alternative is evaluated holistically until one that satisfies each threshold is found. Finally, in the elimination-by-aspects model (variable, attribute-based processing), attributes are examined consecutively across alternatives, and any alternative not meeting a minimum threshold on each attribute is eliminated from further consideration.

## Method

To test the hypotheses developed in this paper, we analyzed data from a field experiment reported in an earlier study [22], which focused on the moderating role of elaboration and the relative impact of peripheral cues in guiding participants filtering. The analysis presented here delves more deeply into the filtering stage of knowledge evaluation by examining the actual process by which participants compared candidate solutions.

In the field experiment, professional software developers were asked to evaluate eight candidate solutions to an array-sorting problem in an experimental ENP forum. Four of the candidate solutions presented would actually solve the array-sorting problem if implemented, but the other four would not. Participants ranked the candidate solutions in the order that they would use them to solve the array-sorting problem. The array-sorting task was selected because it was relevant and understandable to participants, there were multiple ways to solve the problem, and because it was sufficiently difficult to motivate an online search for a plausible candidate solution. Pilot testing with six professional developers confirmed that they would likely search for code online if they were asked to implement an array sorting algorithm [22].<sup>3</sup> Data were collected by three primary means: (1) an instrument that utilized an eye tracker to capture participants’ gaze during filtering, (2) participants’ ranking of each candidate solution, and (3) an online survey administered after participants completed the filtering task.

## Participants

The data set contained responses from 62 professional software developers from 4 organizations: (1) a large manufacturer of electronic, mechanical, and utility products, (2) a premier financial services company, (3) a municipal government, and (4) the information technology (IT) department of a research university. All participants had at least one year of software development experience and held job titles of developer, senior developer/project lead, tester, or manager. The participants’ mean age was 43.8, and 36 percent were female. Overall, participants reported a mean of 16.3 years of programming experience. In addition they indicated that they were knowledgeable about sorting algorithms and that they were highly motivated to complete the experimental task [22]. Participants completed the experiment at their place of employment and were entered into a drawing to receive one of ten \$25 gift cards.

## Stimulus Materials

We created an experimental instrument to simulate an ENP forum. The interface was designed to include the same cues that exist in popular ENP forums. Each of the eight candidate solutions had a unique combination of objective content quality (high versus low), an indication of the contributor’s expertise level (high versus low), and an indication of whether the solution had been validated (accepted versus not validated). The four high-quality-candidate solutions were objectively better than the four low-quality-candidate solutions in that they would correctly sort a given array, whereas low-quality-candidate solutions had serious omissions, syntax errors, and logic errors, and therefore would not correctly sort the array. The content of the candidate solutions for the array sorting task was taken from actual ENP forums and then manipulated to fit the appropriate condition. Pilot testing with professional developers prior to the data collection confirmed that the difference between highand low-quality candidate solutions was perceivable [22].

Participants viewed a total of eight candidate solutions that were randomly assembled (without replacement) from the three solution components so that each candidate solution had a unique combination of content quality, contributor expertise, and validation. All candidate solutions were displayed on a single screen and were positioned in two rows of four candidate solutions. Finally, the position of each candidate solution on the screen was also randomly determined to control for ordering effects [36]. A sample solution with high content quality, high expertise, and no validation is presented in Figure 2. Figure 3 shows the same solution with sample gaze data from a single participant in the form of a heat map.

## Procedure

Participants first provided consent and then were seated at the eye-tracker station where the eye tracker was initialized. Participants then received instructions that they were to review candidate solutions to two common programming problems and rank the candidate solutions in the order that they would actually try them. The first problem required participants to calculate a factorial value of a number and was intended to orient participants to the type of task, the eye-tracking instrument, and the environment to reduce novelty effects. The participants then moved to the arraysorting task described previously. Finally, the participants ranked the candidate solutions and completed the online survey.

## Measurement

Capture of participants’ gaze was accomplished via a custom application that, similar to prior IS research [e.g., 2], utilized a Tobii T60 eye tracker and Tobii Studio software. Our procedure was consistent with recommended research guidelines for physiological IS research [38]. The custom application used the raw coordinates of the participants’ gaze to determine the candidate solution and the solution component (solution content, contributor expertise, validation) that each participant was observing throughout the experiment. Thus we were able to track what participants were viewing, how long they spent viewing each component, and the order in which they viewed solution components.

The constancy of attributes consulted during filtering was captured by calculating the standard deviation of the normalized gaze duration for each of the solution components. To accomplish this, each individual’s gaze duration was first normalized for each of the three component types (solution content, contributor expertise, and validation) across all candidate solutions. Normalization of gaze duration was performed to enable comparison of gaze data across participants. After the duration was normalized across all solutions for a given attribute and participant, the standard deviation was calculated for each attribute. Thus, each participant had a constancy score calculated for all three attribute types. Low standard deviations reflected a more constant search strategy in consulting the respective content, expertise, and validation of candidate solutions, while high standard deviations indicated that a participant demonstrated high variability. Z-scores were then calculated for each constancy score in order to determine the relative positioning of each participant compared to the whole. For ease of interpretation, the score was negated (multiplied by –1) so that a larger score would indicate greater constancy (less variability).

The gaze data from each participant was then summarized on the basis of intraattribute and intra-alternative switches. An intra-attribute switch occurs when the same attribute is compared consecutively across alternative solutions. An intraalternative switch occurs when a different attribute is compared within the same alternative. These switches are indicative of the type of processing that occurred. A participant engaged in alternative-based processing if she shifted her attention from one attribute to another attribute within the same solution (intra-alternative switching). A participant engaged in attribute-based processing if she shifted her attention from an attribute in one solution to the same attribute in another solution (intraattribute switching).

Consistent with prior research [e.g., 1, 26], we calculated a directionality ratio to indicate which type of processing was employed by each participant. The directionality ratio is calculated based on the relative number of intra-attribute switches compared to intra-alternative switches (see Equation 1). A value of 1 for this ratio indicates a complete attribute-based processing strategy, whereas a value of –1 indicates that only alternative-based processing occurred.

$$
\# \text {   of   intra   -   attribute   switches   } - \# \text {   of   intra   -   alternative   switches   }
$$

$$
\# \text {   of   intra   -   attribute   switches   } + \# \text {   of   intra   -   alternative   switches   }\tag{1}
$$

Differences between acquisition and evaluation phases of filtering should be evident in the number of intra-attribute and intra-alternative switches over time. To examine potential changes in attribute-based processing, the total time each participant took to evaluate the candidate solutions was divided into quartiles. The number of intra-attribute and intra-alternative switches was then summed for each quartile.

Finally, a performance index measuring filtering accuracy was created by evaluating the relative ranking of the high- and low-quality solutions. The index was calculated by first multiplying the inverse rank of a candidate solution by 1 if the solution content was high quality or by 0 if the solution content was low quality. This was done for each ranked solution. For example, if the second ranked solution was high quality, that component of the performance index would be given a value of $^ \textrm { \scriptsize 7 } \left( \mathrm { i . e . , ~ } ( 9 \textrm { - } 2 ) \mathrel { \mathrm { ~ ^ * ~ } } 1 \right)$ ). These values were then summed to provide an overall performance index for a particular participant. Thus, participants received the highest performance scores if all four high-quality solutions were ranked in the top four positions. Participants were then categorized as high, medium, or low performers based on quartiles of the performance index, with high performers in the first quartile (17 participants), medium performers in the second and third quartiles (22 participants), and low performers in the fourth quartile (23 participants).

The performance index differs from the method of measuring performance in our related study [22]. This change in performance measurement was necessary because this work focuses on understanding the process by which participants evaluated both high- and low-quality-candidate solutions. The performance index incorporates the evaluation of both high- and low-quality-candidate solutions, whereas the related study focused solely on top candidate solutions. Furthermore, the number of years of professional experience for each participant, included as a covariate in the related study, was also initially included in the analyses reported below but was later removed as it was not found to have a significant effect.

## Discussion

The objective of this study was to examine knowledge-filtering patterns in ENPs, specifically to understand how differences in these patterns related to filtering accuracy in a multi-alternative, multi-attribute normative decision problem. Drawing from literature on information search for decision making [1, 5, 26], we classified filtering patterns by constancy (constant vs. variable) and by directionality (attribute-based processing vs. alternative-based processing). Based on reasoning rooted in this literature and in dual process theories, we hypothesized that accuracy in the filtering task would be higher when search patterns employed more constant evaluation of attributes across alternatives and more attribute-based vs. alternativebased processing. We also hypothesized an overall increase in attribute-based processing in later filtering phases, particularly for high performers.

## Theoretical Contributions

The results of our analysis provide general support for our hypotheses and offer several implications for both research and practice. First, with respect to H1, we found that constancy in evaluating different solution attributes was related to performance, but its effect on performance was not the same for all attributes. Specifically, high performers engaged in much more constant analysis of the content across all solutions, but they did not give equal attention to peripheral cues across all solutions. In contrast, low performers were more constant in their analysis of peripheral cues and less constant with respect to solution content. This result points to an important theoretical implication that extends prior literature on information search for decision making [e.g., 4, 14, 26]. Past studies on information-processing patterns have identified constancy as an important dimension of information evaluation but have not generally distinguished the effects of constancy across different types of attributes. By incorporating the lens of dual process theory to distinguish between central and peripheral cues, our study extends this literature by showing that the influence of constancy on filtering outcomes is—ironically—not constant, but instead depends on the type of attribute (i.e., central or peripheral) being considered. The pattern exhibited by high performers indicates that peripheral cues received attention for some solutions but not for others, indicating that these participants likely recognized the potential unreliability of these cues and abandoned them in favor of a more thorough (constant) evaluation of the content of each solution. Stated differently, high performers seemed to rely more steadily on central-route processing (analyzing the content of the solution itself) and only transitorily on peripheral route processing, and were thus better able to accurately distinguish the actual quality of each solution. The implication of this result for IS research is that constant (or compensatory) evaluation of attributes alone does not necessarily lead to better filtering outcomes in contexts such as ENP forums where some attributes are peripheral and are of questionable diagnosticity; rather, filtering accuracy seems to depend on whether constancy coincides with central processing of the actual content of a posted solution.

With regard to H2, our results support the hypothesis that increased attribute-based processing of the solution content (as opposed to peripheral cues) would be associated with higher filtering accuracy. This means that high performers tended to move from the content of one solution to the content of another solution, rather than switching between peripheral cues and content within a solution. Again, this pattern suggests that high performers may have recognized the fallibility of the peripheral cues and committed their attention to direct comparisons of the content of each solution. Low performers, by contrast, seemed to engage in more alternative-based processing, which may have hindered their ability to compare the content quality of each solution. As with H1, this result contributes to extant theory by integrating dual process concepts with the dimensions of filtering patterns studied herein and in previous information-based decision-making literature [e.g., 1, 26]. In particular, our results imply that the effect of directionality on performance may be contingent on the type of attributes (central vs. peripheral) upon which this processing occurs. Thus, future studies of information processing in contexts such as ENP forums should account for this qualitative difference in cues when examining filtering patterns and performance.

The results observed for H3 and H4 also have implications for ongoing theoretical development. Results for H3 showed that, in the present context, attribute-based processing increased over time as participants increasingly drew comparisons between the same attributes of different solutions. Interestingly, this outcome corroborates the findings of some prior information-processing studies [e.g., 3] but contrasts with the findings of others [e.g., 4]. For example, in their study of a multi-attribute, multi-alternative preference problem involving the choice between microwave oven brands, Bettman and Park [4] hypothesized an increase in attributebased processing in later phases (consistent with some prior studies and the hypothesis herein), but results showed just the opposite: later phases were dominated by alternative-based processing. According to the authors, one possible explanation for this discrepancy is the way in which the alternatives and attributes were presented to the user. Studies that arranged the information by alternative (e.g., brand) found that evaluation evolved from alternative-based to attribute-based (as observed herein), but the inverse was true for studies that arranged the information by attribute. Taken together, the theoretical implication of these findings is that the arrangement of cues on an ENP forum may have bearing on the way this information is filtered [20]. In our study, each solution was closely grouped with its peripheral cues, facilitating alternative-based (i.e., within-solution) processing. However, because participants had to eventually rank order all solutions, they were likely compelled to shift their processing patterns to compare attributes across solutions later in the filtering task. Thus, future theory development surrounding information filtering in ENP forums should explore how various layout options might alter the pattern of results observed here.

In addition, the increase in attribute-based processing is consistent with prior research, which suggests that two phases are involved in filtering: acquisition and evaluation [14]. As predicted by H4, our results suggest that the difference between these two phases was most prominent for high performers, who demonstrated a substantial increase in attribute-based processing later in the filtering task. Past research [e.g., 32] has suggested that people often prefer attribute-based processing because they expend less effort during evaluation and still achieve reasonable filtering accuracy. All performance groups, including medium and low performers, increased attribute-based processing over time. Our results indicate that the preference for attribute-based processing is not what separates the high performers from other groups; rather it is the volume of attribute-based processing (i.e., comparisons across attributes) that distinguishes higher performers from others in later filtering phases. This accords with research suggesting that the quantity of information processed is an important determinant of the accuracy of filtering outcomes [28].

Finally, from a broad perspective, this study makes important contextual and methodological extensions to prior knowledge-evaluation research. First, we extend previous theorizing by examining a normative-evaluation task rather than the preferential-evaluation task that has been done in most prior literature [e.g., 1, 26, 37]. When individuals filter candidate solutions from an ENP, they are looking for solutions that will solve a specific problem rather than simply satisfy a preference, which is likely to produce different filtering behaviors. Second, the use of the eye tracker to capture gaze data offers increased objectivity and precision over more subjective methods (e.g., verbal protocol, self-reports) for understanding filtering behavior.

## Implications for Practice

This research also carries implications for ENP participants who use forums sponsored by these networks as a knowledge source. First, the results of this study suggest that higher performance is associated with specific filtering patterns. Knowledge seekers should be aware that some patterns might lead to filtering decisions that are less accurate than others. To the extent that knowledge seekers can consciously alter their filtering behaviors, we suggest that they comprehensively and systematically compare all solution alternatives on a given attribute rather than short-circuiting the filtering process or interrupting the pattern by focusing intermittently on different attributes. Shifting attention between different types of attributes can be distracting and may impair the ability of a knowledge seeker to accurately judge a candidate solution’s quality. Our results suggest that knowledge seekers may be better off evaluating the same attribute across solutions rather than evaluating all attributes for a given solution. The acquisition of information from peripheral cues tends to be relatively quick; however, knowledge seekers should avoid becoming distracted by potentially unreliable peripheral cues, as this tends to decrease performance.

Second, the data from this study illustrate that individuals across performance levels increase their amount of attribute-based processing over time. This increase may be suggestive of an initial acquisition phase and a subsequent evaluation phase [14, 20]. Our analysis shows that high performers engaged in significantly more attribute-based processing at the end of the filtering process, indicating that better filtering results occur when knowledge seekers systematically evaluate and compare alternatives with increasing frequency.

Finally, this study also has implications for ENP forum designers. Designers can contribute to accurate knowledge filtering by presenting the information in ways that facilitate the comparison of attribute-alternative combinations. Research shows that filtering patterns are influenced by whether the information is grouped by alternative, by attribute, or by both (i.e., a matrix format). Thus, designers should carefully consider how the chosen layout might affect filtering outcomes.

## Limitations and Future Research

This study has limitations that should be considered. First, as with any experimental research, the external validity of our results may be tempered by the experimental context or artifacts used in our study. Although we took explicit steps to maximize the internal and external validity by using participants who were actual programmers, fashioning stimulus materials after actual ENP forums, measuring filtering behaviors using an eye-tracking device, and completely randomizing the presentation and combinations of all treatment levels to avoid ordering effects, it is possible that the pattern of results we observed here may differ from ENP forum knowledge filtering in the field. Therefore, additional research is needed to corroborate these findings. Second, to preserve internal validity, the peripheral cues were randomly assigned, resulting in some peripheral cues that accurately identified high- and low-quality solutions and others that did not. The diagnosticity of peripheral cues in actual ENPs may vary; however, it is likely that these cues contain at least some information that is useful for knowledge seekers. The diagnosticity of peripheral cues and its effect on filtering processes was unexplored in this work and represents an important area of future research. Finally, different results might be observed in other scenarios with more or less alternative/attribute combinations and decision problems that involve higher stakes or concern different topics. We have focused on the cognitive aspects of knowledge evaluation. A productive extension of this work may be understanding how affect can interact with cognitive process to influence knowledge filtering [18]. We encourage future researchers to explore these issues and further test the boundary conditions of the results observed herein.

## Conclusion

With modern technology facilitating unprecedented knowledge exchange through ENPs, understanding the processes by which knowledge is retrieved, evaluated, and adopted becomes increasingly imperative. This study extends previous knowledge-processing research by examining characteristics of knowledgefiltering patterns and showing how these patterns relate to performance in a normative-filtering task. The results observed indicate that the filtering patterns employed by knowledge seekers do indeed influence filtering accuracy in a normative task, and that highest filtering accuracy arises from constant, attribute-based filtering strategies that focus on the central-route processing of the solution content. By combining the insights of information evaluation and dualprocess theories, this work represents an important step toward understanding effective filtering in ENP forums.
