# Mobile technology at the frontlines of patient care: Understanding <sup>fi</sup>t and human drives in utilization decisions and performance

Iris Junglas <sup>a,</sup>⁎, Chon Abraham <sup>b</sup>, Blake Ives <sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Decision and Information Sciences Department, C.T. Bauer College of Business, University of Houston, United States

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Available online 20 November 2008

Keywords: Mobile healthcare information technology MICT Task-technology <sup>fi</sup>t Human drives

## a b s t r a c t

Mobile information communication technologies (MICTs) have considerable promise in patient care settings. But that promise can only be realized if the MICT applications are used by the medical staff. This paper reports on a study examining nurses' decisions to utilize MICTs. A mixed-methods approach is used, consisting of both qualitative and quantitative elements, that reveals and empirically tests the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of novel constellations of <sup>fi</sup>t (i.e., identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, information, patient interaction, physical, time criticality, user comfort, and work<sup>fl</sup>ow <sup>fi</sup>t) and individual characteristics, presented as basic human drives (i.e., drive to acquire, bond, defend, and learn). Findings indicate that <sup>fi</sup>t is a multi-faceted construct and that archetypical human drives have an in<sup>fl</sup>uence on these various notions, which in turn, impact technology adoption in the healthcare context.

## 1. Introduction

Mobile computing offers the potential to improve the quality and safety of patient care by decreasing the errors and inef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies currently caused by inadequate, and sometimes inaccessible, patient documentation at the point and time of care. To reach that potential, however, the intended users of these technologies must believe applications to be an acceptable match with the task at hand. We learn from the <sup>fi</sup>t literature that users are motivated to use a technology when they perceive it to be a better <sup>fi</sup>t than alternative methods [18,31,33,63,68,76,77]. Goodhue and Thompson's Technology-to-Performance Model is the most widely cited model for assessing the interplay between task, technology, and individual in determining technology utilization and the resulting increase or decrease in a user's work performance. The model states that only if the technology provided matches the characteristics of the user as well as that of the task at hand, will a performance increase result. However, the same study also showed that task-technology <sup>fi</sup>t alone, and ignoring any individual characteristics, does not predict utilization. Surprisingly, and counter to what was hypothesized, the decision to use the system was not based on how well the technology <sup>fi</sup>t with the task. The authors thus encouraged future research to determine if there are other factors, such as individual characteristics that would make this relationship signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and, by so doing, better explain what contributes or impairs technology usage [33].

With the emergence of mobile computing, individuals are again confronted with technology acceptance issues [62]. Compared to prior technologies, such as stationary computers, mobile information communication technologies (MICTs) are portable, and thus particularly helpful in situations where immediate access to information is vital [43,51,72]. In healthcare settings, these situations are the norm. MICTs are intended to be used by healthcare professionals, particularly nurses, at the point of care with the patient, for information processing and computational needs [4]. They are being employed both to help constrain the cost of healthcare and to improve safety. The U.S. Institute of Medicine has reported that nearly 98,000 people die each year due to medical errors, such as incorrect medication dosages delivered because of poor legibility in manual records, or due to delays in consolidating needed information to determine the proper intervention [42]. Nurses perform the bulk of documentation tasks during the patient care process [10,35,71]; their acceptance is essential for ensuring the success of new MICT applications. Thus, this study focuses on them.

Unfortunately, many nurses are reluctant to use MICTs. Their reasons include fear of being alienated from the patient, loss of patient eye-contact, being perceived as unprofessional by the patient [1,37], or because they deem the technology as a foreign tool that slows down their work [22]. Thus, the application of MICT seems to highlight the need not only for the task and technology to mesh, but also to consider some individualistic in<sup>fl</sup>uences. Therefore, our study seeks to discern “What are the various notions of <sup>fi</sup>t that determine MICT utilization and performance in a healthcare setting?” And “How do individual characteristics or basic human drives map into these types of <sup>fi</sup>t?”

These questions are particularly important as we look towards future innovations in mobile healthcare. For hospitals, intelligent locating systems are on the horizon that can track physicians, nurses, and specialists throughout a building so they can be found at a moment's notice, or automatically re-route phone calls if the recipient cannot be reached directly. Other systems provide comprehensive patient monitoring capabilities that capture a patient's medical condition and geographic whereabouts in real-time. The bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of these and similar IT-driven innovations in hospital care will only be fully realized if the design and implementation of these applications adequately re<sup>fl</sup>ect the factors that contribute to the utilization of current MICTs.

In this study we uncover the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of some underlying, basic human drives (i.e., drives to acquire, bond, defend, and learn [48]) that make novel types of <sup>fi</sup>t salient when researched in conjunction with task and technology characteristics in hospital settings. These novel types of <sup>fi</sup>t pertain to identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, information, patient interaction, physical, time criticality, user comfort, and work<sup>fl</sup>ow <sup>fi</sup>t. They do not necessarily represent novel types of <sup>fi</sup>t, but illustrate that various notions of <sup>fi</sup>t must be differentiated as they represent dimensions that are important or obvious to nurses.

In seeking a comprehensive understanding, we have chosen a mixed method approach, consisting of both qualitative and quantitative elements. For the qualitative element, we collected interview data from 50 nurses who were using MICTs across four different medical units (i.e., acute care in-hospital <sup>fl</sup>oors, ambulatory care, emergency department, and post-anaesthesia care) in three different Southeastern U.S. hospitals. This qualitative data revealed the saliency of human drives that in<sup>fl</sup>uenced nurses' perceptions of MICT <sup>fi</sup>t. These drives, or underlying motivators of behaviors, surfaced as fundamental characteristics of individual nurses; they were also found to have a bearing on how nurses dimensionalized <sup>fi</sup>t, which we present as the novel types of <sup>fi</sup>t. In the quantitative element of the study, we collected survey data from another 107 nurses to further validate our <sup>fi</sup>ndings concerning the relationship between human drives and <sup>fi</sup>t types in order to determine their impact on performance and utilization decisions for MICTs.

The paper is structured as follows. We <sup>fi</sup>rst describe a typical MICT used for patient care and illustrate the nature of healthcare tasks. We then dissect the theoretical underpinnings of existing <sup>fi</sup>t research followed by a description of the qualitative aspects of our study. The various <sup>fi</sup>t notions and the set of underlying human drives that emerge provide the basis for our quantitative study. We conclude our quantitative study by illustrating how nurses' individual characteristics in<sup>fl</sup>uence various <sup>fi</sup>t dimensions, which in turn interfere or enhance their utilization and work performance.

## 2. The MICT artifact in healthcare

Fig. 1 depicts a typical MICT used in a U.S. hospital setting. The system is mobile (in the sense of movable) and it provides ubiquitous (in the sense of omnipresence) and wireless (in the sense of being untethered) access to an information system within the boundaries of a hospital unit. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, a typical system comprises a mobile workstation with an encased lightweight computer, supplemented by wireless local area networks operated on the 802.11b standard. The size of the workstations (i.e., approximately the same as a laptop) is deemed appropriate by users—unlike Personal Digital Assistants [46] for which the screens are considered too small for clinical usage tasks such as vital statistics charting. Carts are typically equipped with a full keyboard.

Despite its importance, acceptance of an MICT in the healthcare context is still a reasonably untapped research area. The <sup>fi</sup>eld of nursing informatics, however, does seek to apply concepts concerning some socio-technical aspects to explain, predict, and alter the human– computer interaction behavior of nursing personnel. Many efforts to harness technology in healthcare environments seek physician buy-in, but rarely seek buy-in from nurses—though nurses, as front-line care givers, are a primary user group [61]. However, the tide is changing and there is increasing visibility of nurses as information gatherers and processors in the patient care process [59]. Yet, attaining acceptance by the nurses is an arduous endeavor [1,22,37]. In light of a severe U.S. nurse shortage (e.g., an estimated 400,000 shortage by 2020 [7]), healthcare institutions are employing tactics to improve nurses' working conditions while also increasing patient safety. Among these tactics are mobile technologies that afford access to information needed during patient care and electronic documentation that is less laborious and error prone than manual methods [66].

Tasks in healthcare are peculiar in the sense that they are typically highly interdependent [67] and require rich information at the right point in time. They are also, if done incorrectly, life threatening. For example, the activity that a nurse performs during a medical service encounter with a patient determines the characteristics of subsequent care giving activities [61]. Nurses require timely access to documentation in order to assess, diagnose and treat patients ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently, effectively, and safely [61]. Having no access to the right information at the right time can have detrimental consequences to the extent that it can impair a patient's life and a nurse's career. As all tasks in a healthcare environment necessitate actions to be taken at particular locations, times, and by and for speci<sup>fi</sup>c individuals, understanding the nature of the task as well as the nature of the individual nurse that uses the technology is essential [6].

## 3. Fit models

The Technology-to-Performance Model [33] combines theories of cognitive <sup>fi</sup>t with theories of attitudes and behaviors [2,24]; it has been suggested that the degree to which the functionality of a technology matches the task as well as the abilities of the individual who performs the task will determine the level of technology utilization [33]. Therefore, achieving <sup>fi</sup>t is of utmost importance as it signi<sup>fi</sup>es an imperative precursor to the utilization of technology and consequent performance increase. For time critical environments, such as in healthcare where inadequate task performance can be lifethreatening, the <sup>fi</sup>t between task and technology is of utmost importance due to the gravity of error or ignorance.

The original Technology-to-Performance Model identi<sup>fi</sup>ed task, technology, and individual characteristics as three essential components [17,33]. In this context, a technology is considered to be a tool, such as hardware, software, and data, in addition to user support services, such as training and assistance, which are employed by individuals in carrying out the aforesaid tasks [19]. Task characteristics, on the other hand, are considered to be the actions carried out by individuals in turning inputs into outputs and may provoke the user to depend more heavily on the information systems capacity to process information [33]. Tasks have been analyzed by various means that consider dimensions of their complexity (simple vs. complex) [52], such as repetitiveness, complexity of cognitive processes [55] and ambiguity [26]. Individuals, the third leg of the full model, are those entities that may decide or not decide to utilize technologies to assist them in performing work tasks.

Subsequent studies to the Technology-to-Performance Model have mainly focused on the sole relationship between task and technology and its impact on performance [33]. This reduced model, also referred to as the task-technology <sup>fi</sup>t model (TTF), has been applied across differing contexts, such as in the area of group support systems [76,77], software maintenance [20,21], and technical support groups [32,34], but only sparsely in healthcare information systems research [13,53,54].

By focusing almost exclusively on task-technology <sup>fi</sup>t [5], the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld has mostly ignored the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of individual characteristics— even though proposed early on by researchers such as the originators of the TTF model themselves [33]. Suggested characteristics included dimensions, such as past computer experiences, intrinsic and extrinsic motivations, or the level of received computer training, which were said to impact if, how, and to what extent a technology would be utilized [31,33]. Nowadays, with the increasing diffusion of mobile technology, it is expected that individual characteristics will play an even more vital role in the formation of <sup>fi</sup>t constellations. Cellular phones, for example, accompany an individual 24/7 and can be easily customized to personal preference; indeed with each new release, these devices are slowly metamorphing into personal assistants. With little notice, mobile technology has already penetrated our private and professional lives and has caused the fundamentals of social life and structure to shift [29]. Achieving <sup>fi</sup>t in a mobile context requires that we continue to consider the relationship between technology and task, but also to incorporate individual preferences and needs.

Other, non-IS related research streams have—less explicitly— examined the concept of <sup>fi</sup>t under a slightly different nomenclature. For example, researchers have tackled <sup>fi</sup>t from a business alignment perspective, such as studies about IT and strategic alignment [38], that try to link IT innovation and corporate vision and objectives into a synergistic blend. Other streams, mainly in psychology, approach <sup>fi</sup>t from a dissonance perspective, such as studies about cognitive or identity dissonance (e.g., [11,23]). Cognitive dissonance represents the discrepancy between what an individual currently knows or believes and some new information or impressions that counter this current cognition. Facing two cognitions that are psychologically inconsistent, a person will, according to the theory, always strive to minimize this “mis-<sup>fi</sup>t” or dissonance. The same strategy applies when an individual tries to reconcile a new identity role with his currently existing identity, thus reducing identity dissonance.

Ecological psychology, a <sup>fi</sup>eld that argues that human behavior is always situated or highly dependent upon the environment, has approached the concept of <sup>fi</sup>t from a task–person–environment perspective. For example, goal/task <sup>fi</sup>t is considered to bring personal objectives in congruence with what a task or work requirement imposes on an individual [30]. Individual knowledge/abilities <sup>fi</sup>t, on the other hand, refers to the gap between insights that are bene<sup>fi</sup>cial in problem solving or decision making and the intellectual capacity an individual possesses to process this information [30]; and <sup>fi</sup>nally, environmental <sup>fi</sup>t refers to matching behavior as a response to particular stimuli in the environment that the person feels is most appropriate for addressing a particular issue or decision [30].

In the Marketing literature the notion of <sup>fi</sup>t can be found in the concept of image congruence (e.g., [40]). Image congruence, also referred to as self-congruence, describes the match between a consumer's perceived self-concept and the image of a given product, brand, or store. Studies have shown that image congruence has a direct effect on consumer behavior (i.e., the more an individual identi<sup>fi</sup>es herself with a product, for example, the more likely she is to purchase it).

Three basic inferences can be drawn by looking across the rich but multi-faceted <sup>fi</sup>t literatures. First, each <sup>fi</sup>t lens describes some sort of interplay between a set of entities that, in an ideal world, should be brought to congruence. This process is often referred to as “gap closing.” Failure to close this gap, or not bringing entities in congruence, will likely lead to a bad <sup>fi</sup>t and, in turn, to unwelcome consequences. This implies that <sup>fi</sup>t is contingent (e.g., [5,18]). This means that differing performance impacts may result from and depend on the constellation of the set of entities. Second, it is a user or potential user who assesses this interplay between the demands of a task and the provided technological capabilities; each lens stresses the importance of that individual. However, in the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld, this component has not yet achieved enough attention despite, or even though, we witness technologies, and in this case, mobile technologies, becoming an integral part of our professional work lives. Third, there is not one overall <sup>fi</sup>t notion in the adoption of technology; rather, there are multiple notions and they depend on situational factors. With technologies changing, so does the individual that is engaged in a continuous process of adaptation and modi<sup>fi</sup>cation so as to achieve and maintain the best possible <sup>fi</sup>t. As pointed out in prior literature, “proposed <sup>fi</sup>t pro<sup>fi</sup>les suggest[ing] that one-size <sup>fi</sup>ts all approaches do not work” [5]. Instead, organizations have to take a segmented approach in order to achieve desired, or even acceptable, levels of utilization and consequent performance impacts.

As MICTs constitute a rather new technology in the healthcare context, we decided to apply a comprehensive repertoire of methodological tools in order to gain a deep and rich understanding of the MICT adoption phenomenon. We used a multi-method approach, consisting of qualitative and quantitative elements that allowed us to tackle the phenomenon from varying perspectives. Qualitative methods can provide an in-depth understanding of the phenomenon but are said to lack generalizability; quantitative methods can produce more generalizable results but suffer from limited explanatory power. In combination, the application of both methods potentially achieves what can be termed a “complementary research method circle.” Not only are qualitative <sup>fi</sup>ndings supported by quantitative <sup>fi</sup>ndings, but the opposite applies as well—qualitative <sup>fi</sup>ndings are further validated through the subsistence of quantitative <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Research in IS typically uses one or the other, but seldom both methodological approaches to study a phenomenon.

## 4. Study 1: A qualitative study

## 4.1. Research method

To study mobile technology utilization in a healthcare context, the <sup>fi</sup>rst part of our study employed a qualitative technique that comprised interviews with <sup>fi</sup>fty nurses across four hospital sites using MICTs. Four sites were chosen so as to be able to observe nurses working in different medical units and attending to different patient care tasks. The four sites afforded enough variation to explain acceptance of MICTs for critical tasks and increased the chances of discerning the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of different environments. The substantive criteria that we employed required that each site (1) employed similar technological solutions (i.e., MICTs) in use among the target study group (i.e., nurses) in performing work tasks (i.e., patient care) and (2) that the primary work task of the unit varied enough from the other three so that we could discern how different work environments and tasks in<sup>fl</sup>uenced user behavior.

Site 1 was an emergency department within a 528-bed, not-forpro<sup>fi</sup>t hospital in the US. Its IS department implemented MICTs and highly encouraged usage for the task of patient triage (i.e., initial assessment) because the IS and managerial staff of the emergency department wanted to standardize the process for triage while providing attending nurses with needed mobility as well as accessibility to historical medical information. The intended bene<sup>fi</sup>ts for the implementation were to decrease waiting times for patients needing medical care, to improve the quality of the triage assessment, and to promote patient safety. Site 2 was a Post Anaesthesia Care Unit in a 124-bed, not-for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t community hospital. Its IS department implemented MICTs and highly encouraged usage for the task of electronic charting of patients following general surgery. Anesthetized patients upon transfer from the operating room are monitored until they regain consciousness. The primary intended bene<sup>fi</sup>t of the implementation was a decrease in laborious manual documentation, a decrease in documentation errors, and improved patient safety. The same anticipated bene<sup>fi</sup>ts led to the implementation of an MICT at Site 3, an Ambulatory Care Unit in the same hospital. Here the IS department highly encouraged usage with the objective of enabling electronic charting for nurses preparing conscious patients for day surgery. Site 4 was a Regular Floor in a 478-bed hospital that has 13 regular <sup>fl</sup>oor units for inpatients. It was a federally funded veterans' medical center providing inpatient care as well as outpatient care programs, including for those with medical, surgical, and psychiatric needs. The main objective for MICT implementation was to decrease documentation errors associated with validating prescriptions as well as recording medical interventions.

## 4.1.1. Data collection

Techniques for data collection at all four sites included interviews, direct observations, and review of documents. Interviews were semistructured around questions designed to elicit respondents' personal experiences with MICTs in patient care. Overall, 50 interviews were conducted, averaging 45 minutes each. All interviews were audiorecorded and transcribed. Direct observation was employed to assess actual usage behavior so as to minimize self-report bias. In total, 57 instances of direct observations were conducted across sites. These instances include observations of nurses and registration personnel. Observations were interspersed between interviews in order to provide a holistic picture of the task environment and background for proposing new questions, revising existing questions, and clarifying informant comments. We also solicited and reviewed systems documentation prior to interviews at each site to give us insights into the intended usage and technical characteristics. We also reviewed system documents that interviewees referenced in conversations or used during task performance. In all, we reviewed 250+ pages of written materials inclusive of project proposals description, system requirements documents, end-user manuals, and training manuals.

Triangulating the interview, observation, and document analysis was suf<sup>fi</sup>cient for attaining theoretical saturation [65]. A detailed description of each site including a detailed listing of the data collection material can be found in Appendix A.

## 4.1.2. Data analysis

Data collection, coding, and data analysis are tightly interwoven in inductive research [9]. Accordingly, the processes for collecting, coding, and analyzing of data were iterative rather than sequential. Through a constant comparative technique, we evaluated and contrasted similar and dissimilar data units, which provided a richer understanding of the research phenomenon [65]. As a result, when data were coded and analyzed, interviews and observations became more focused. We used Atlas.ti, a visual qualitative data analysis tool, that provided a means to load and categorize transcripts, count text segments, customize labeling in the coding, automatically assess relationships between text segments based on these customized labels or codes, memo or journal, and query the text segments based on a number of criteria (i.e., associated code, similar memo, etc).

We elected to use the Straussian coding paradigm [64]. Accordingly, the analysis of data included three major types of coding—open, axial, and selective—with each type being at a higher, more abstract level of data analysis than the preceding one. In open coding (i.e., the process of breaking down, comparing, conceptualizing, and categorizing data), we created 61 codes related to 553 textual segments attained from the 50 interviews and 57 observations. Axial coding, which necessitates that the data be put back together in new ways by making connections between codes to form categories, resulted in the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of twelve categories. It led us to uncover the saliency of eight novel <sup>fi</sup>ts and four human drives. In selective coding, a conceptualization of the main phenomenon was developed. We used the Four-Drive Model ([48], demonstrated in the following) as a means to structure our <sup>fi</sup>ndings and as a basis for linking human drives with the novel constellations of <sup>fi</sup>t embedded in what we currently know about human MICT acceptance behavior.

## 4.2. Results of the qualitative study

## 4.2.1. Novel notions of fit in healthcare

In gathering our qualitative data, we were particularly interested in ascertaining when, how, and in which ways nurses would utilize an MICT as part of their care-giving work. We found nurses exhibiting different forms of utilization, some unanticipated by the designers or management. For instance, despite managements entreaties to do otherwise, some nurses used the system on another nurse's behalf. Also, some of the nurses reported that they would not use the MICT at the point-of-care, but in some other location, such as a hallway. Findings of the qualitative study showed that a nurse's decision to use the MICT was in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by overcoming various “mis-<sup>fi</sup>ts” on multiple fronts. As typical of their job description, nurses were faced with a plethora of demands and needs on a daily basis. A nurse's problem solving behavior therefore became more of a continuous “gap closing” procedure between speci<sup>fi</sup>c needs posed by her job and the technology that was intended to support her job. In the present study, we were able to identify eight notions of <sup>fi</sup>t.

Identification fit, for example, describes a nurse's need to identify a patient correctly, to identify the correct associated medical intervention, and to identify herself as the correct care-giver. The degree to which the provided MICT matched one or more of these needs determined her decision to utilize the MICT

Similarly, we also found that nurses continuously strove to match their need to have the most current information available at the right time with what was provided by the MICT, which we refer to as information communication fit. Having access to the most current and updated patients' information is vital, particularly in situations where the patient is unconscious.

Another <sup>fi</sup>t, location fit, was based on the need to have information access at the right location. This location is most likely at the point of care in which the likelihood of capturing correct data without reliance on either memory or alternative methods of documenting that take the nurse away from the patient's bedside. In essence, being able to document a medical intervention wherever needed was deemed to be most ef<sup>fi</sup>cient (in terms of computing time savings) and effective in that it was perceived to be less prone to medical mis-speci<sup>fi</sup>cations.

Patient interaction fit was driven by a nurse's need to bond with patients. Interacting with patients was important to nurses and served multiple purposes. It allowed the nurse to attain data about the patient's physiological condition and psychological state; also, it helped to establish a relationship between the nurses and patient that aided in the patient being agreeable to having the nurse perform care. In some cases it also decreased anxieties associated with providing and receiving care. The MICT supported this need in that the technology was used as a tool to engage patients in their own healthcare awareness even while the nurse was providing care. For example, a nurse displaying medical information, such as about the effectiveness of a particular medication regimen, helped to keep the patient compliant.

Physical fit was based on a nurse's need for maneuverability with the MICT to get to the patient's location, especially in physically constrained patient rooms. Characteristics of the technology in terms of how the hardware was designed to afford mobility determined the physical <sup>fi</sup>t capabilities of the MICT.

Time criticality fit was based on a nurse's need to be informed about urgent medical interventions, such as medication administration. Nurses were typically inundated with activities that became overengaging and hindered their ability to remember or refer to manual or electronic documents reminding them of interventions. Automatics alerts, as part of the MICT, allayed such problems.

User comfort fit was based on a nurse's need to be and feel comfortable with the MICT in front of a patient. This <sup>fi</sup>t was most analogous to computer self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy [15]. If the MICT was not designed ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently, or the nurse was not comfortable manipulating the system then the MICT was not considered be<sup>fi</sup>tting. As nursing is an aging profession [41], those that were trained <sup>fi</sup>fteen or more years ago did not necessarily have suf<sup>fi</sup>cient typing skills. We also observed that only few had received formal training in using computers for data access or processing.

Work flow fit was based on a nurse's need to streamline work processes and to most ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently perform work related tasks in a manner that did not impede the way one is accustomed to performing work. Job Fit [68] is most analogous re<sup>fl</sup>ecting how the technology characteristics needs to improve work or meet a speci<sup>fi</sup>ed goal.

Overall, good <sup>fi</sup>t was achieved if any of these needs was matched through the support of the MICT; bad <sup>fi</sup>t, in turn, resulted if the MICT met those needs only poorly. ## 4.2.2. Human drives as individual characteristics

Besides suggesting a richer conceptualization of <sup>fi</sup>t, we also found evidence in our data that suggested a set of psychological factors at play that could more fully explain individual characteristics and how these impact utilization decisions. Traditional <sup>fi</sup>t literature, as pointed out before, does not adequately address this individualistic nature of a user. However, our data led us to see the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of a set of basic human drives or motivations that guided nurses to de<sup>fi</sup>ne their task, determine their urgency, and then to ascertain not only if the technology <sup>fi</sup>ts the task, but also how or on what dimensional levels the technology most appropriately <sup>fi</sup>ts. One model that incorporates these basic human drives and applies them to explain decision making of workers in organizations is the Four-Drive model [48]. The four drives, conceptualized as a common set of characteristics that individuals embody, comprise: the drive to acquire material and status, the drive to bond with others to establish mutual relationships, the drive to defend oneself and others from threat, and the drive to learn to satisfy a curiosity.

The drive to acquire is the need to seek status, take control, retain objects and personal experiences that humans value [48]. Humankind has been (and still is) driven to acquire goods that are either material, such as food, clothing, and shelter, or positional, such as social acknowledgement and recognition [16,58]. The likelihood of survival was greater for those who were more apt at acquiring material goods as it elevated their social status and deemed them more capable of caring and providing for others, and thus increased their chances of reproductive success. In turn, their social status and power were based on the continued well-being of their acquired dependents and goods [75]. In the healthcare context, the drive to acquire is re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the nurses' need to be perceived as legitimate by the patient to gain status as a competent care giver. If not perceived that way, credibility is at stake and best and safest intervention cannot be guaranteed.

The drive to bond is the need to form social relationships and develop mutual caring commitments with other humans [48]. Our ancestors engaged in bonding activities in order to strengthen group cohesion on the inside and to form coalitions against the outside. The premise is that those who bond well had a relative advantage over those who did not. After all, establishing and maintaining groups of individuals that were bonded by a mutual caring relationship improved the odds of surviving environmental threats [16]. Bonding and its associated aspects, such as trust, empathy, compassion, loyalty, respect, partnership, and alliance, also manifests itself in form of behavioral outcomes altruism and the establishment of moral codes regarding social relationships [60,69,70,74]. Interestingly, many of the strongest reactions, both positive and negative, are linked to belongingness and engagement in a mutually caring relationship [8]. In the healthcare context, the drive to bond is re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the nurses' need for mutual assurance. They want to ensure that each participating entity (i.e., caregiver and patient) will act in the best interest of one another.

The drive to defend is the need to defend ourselves and our valued accomplishments whenever we perceive them to be endangered. At the individual level, the drive to defend is activated by perceived threats to one's person, valued objects, status, or beliefs [39]. At the collective or organizational level, the drive to defend is triggered when individuals perceive a threat either to the bonds with others in their group, or to the collectively shared resources, or as part of a deviation from socially accepted norms deemed as disloyal [39]. The human mind is preconditioned to enact to a variety of threats and the reaction will escalate as the severity of the threat heightens [12]. In the healthcare context, the drive to defend is re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the nurses' need to protect oneself, their reputation, and those in their supervision from threats that might be posed from others due to negligent accountability.

Lastly, the drive to learn is the need that pushes humans to collect information, to assess the needs of a situation, to examine their environment, and to make observations about explanatory ideas and theories in hopes of appeasing curiosity and making sound judgments [48]. This mechanism encourages individuals to seek out information to resolve problems associated with ful<sup>fi</sup>lling fundamental needs [45]. Individuals seek to learn in order to decrease their uncertainty, to bring about closure to a problem that challenges well-being, to appease curiosity that enhances well-being, or to make situations more consistent with what is perceived as a “normal” behavior [36,45,47]. In the healthcare context, the drive to learn is re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the nurses' need to collect information, examine the environment, and make observations about explanatory ideas and theories concerning the patient to assess their condition that aids in decision-making concerning medical interventions.

First, users will decide to utilize technologies only if it aids in appeasing their basic human drives, and second, the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of drives spur a richer and broader conceptualization of <sup>fi</sup>t that goes beyond scrutinizing just the relationship between task and technology.

## 5. Study 2: A quantitative study

Based on the selective coding results and our literature review of technology <sup>fi</sup>t models, we propose that individual characteristics in the form of four human drives contribute to the saliency of the discrete dimensions of <sup>fi</sup>t perceptions in the context of mobile patient care (Hypothesis 1). Like forecasted in prior literature, the deployment of MICTs to the context of healthcare is apparently set out to <sup>fi</sup>t the task at hand in a much better way than was traditionally possible [33]. Besides the fact that the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld has exclusively scrutinized the <sup>fi</sup>tting relationship between task and technology only and has mostly ignored the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of individual characteristics, it also has predominantly focused on the examination of technology designed to complete a particular organizational task in a structured, non-volatile business environment. This con<sup>fi</sup>ned perspective seems no longer appropriate. As computing evolves from traditional business (or tethered) computing, to mobile, on to pervasive and ubiquitous computing [49], technology will become an integral part of an individual's life [29].

We also conclude that the plethora of <sup>fi</sup>ts found in a healthcare setting between task, technology, and the individual in<sup>fl</sup>uence utilization decisions regarding the MICT (Hypothesis 2a) as well as a nurse's work performance (Hypothesis 2b). MICTs have the capability to improve quality and safety of patient care by decreasing errors that result from the lack of faster, more comprehensive, and more accessible patient documentation at the point and time of care [42]. Nurses perform the bulk of the documentation-oriented tasks in patient care. And nursing documentation is a critical part of clinical documentation, which is a precondition for good patient care and proper communication within the healthcare team [4]. Yet, documentation is considered a necessary evil in itself, and nurses apply varying levels of utilization and acceptance of computer-based nursing documentation [3,57]. However, nursing can bene<sup>fi</sup>t from access to medical data for medication and patient history to effectively perform work at the point of care (i.e., at the patient's location) [1]. For intended utilization of technology by a target user group, the technology must be deemed a proper <sup>fi</sup>t for the task on multiple dimensions, which we assert should also account for the inherent characteristics that individuals possess. ## 5.1. Research method

For the second portion of this study we sought to quantify the various notions of <sup>fi</sup>t and the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the human drives identi<sup>fi</sup>ed earlier. Thus, we developed a survey instrument, which we used exclusively to validate our qualitatively derived <sup>fi</sup>ndings. As we could not rely on the existence of prior measures, it was essential to carefully deploy a consolidated approach to establish valid measures for both: the notions of <sup>fi</sup>t and the human drives. This process was patterned after the one proposed by Moore and Benbasat [56]. A pilot survey was developed that based its measurement items on prior literature and the input received from the nurses across four different hospital sites that participated in the preceding qualitative phase of the study. Those four sites were also used to test our survey instrument across 31 voluntary nurses. This process helped us to develop an instrument that displayed solid psychometric characteristics with regards to internal consistency, convergent and discriminant validity. The resulting survey instrument used for Study 2 is listed in Appendix B.

In order to obtain study participants for this succeeding quantitative study, an e-mail invitation was sent to a select group of members of the American Nursing Informatics Association (ANIA) who were not participants in the previous qualitative study. These members were known to perform clinical care with MICTs and information systems management of nursing clinical systems in their respective hospitals. The survey was administered online, containing approximately 50 questions; questions did not seek out any personal information. Respondents were mandatory users of an MICT, utilizing the device for any of the following tasks: electronic charting on conscious patients in Ambulatory Care Units (ACU) (37%) and on unconscious patients in Post Anaesthesia Care Units (PACU) (21%), medication administration (24%), patient triage (9%), and patient registration (8%). Overall, 107 nurses participated in the survey, out of which were 96 percent female (and 4 percent male). On average, nurses were 45 years old and had on average 20 years experience in the <sup>fi</sup>eld.

Data were analyzed using structural equation modeling, more speci<sup>fi</sup>cally Partial Least Square (PLS), version 3 [14,28]. PLS, as every structural equation modeling technique, differentiates between a measurement and a structural model. Whereas the measurement model analyzes the relationship between the latent constructs and their associated items by scrutinizing their internal, convergent and discriminant validity, the structural model estimates the strengths of the relationship between latent constructs by providing estimates for path coef<sup>fi</sup>cients and variance explained.

## 5.1.1. Measurement model

Items for the human drives, the eight notions of <sup>fi</sup>t, and both dependent variables performance and utilization were measured using a 7-point Likert scale, ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (7). In order to establish internal validity for each construct, we examined item loadings and their respective composite reliabilities. As Table 3 demonstrates, each item loads above 0.75 on its respective construct. Further, all constructs display suf<sup>fi</sup>cient composite reliabilities. The composite reliability calculated in PLS is equivalent to Cronbach's Alpha, however, does not assume tau equivalency among the measures [73]. Convergent validity is established if the Average Variance Extracted (AVE) is above a suggested level of 0.5 [25]; all constructs surpass this criterion (see Table 7 in Appendix C). In order to establish discriminant validity, the Average Variance Extracted (AVE) also has to be greater than the squared correlations (i.e., shared variance) for each construct [14]. The calculated loadings and cross-loadings (Table 8 in Appendix C) also demonstrate that items loaded higher on their respective constructs than on any other construct. As with the human drives, the measurement model of the various notions of <sup>fi</sup>t displayed suf<sup>fi</sup>cient internal validity as well as adequate convergent and discriminant validity. However, an initial analysis of the data showed that information communication <sup>fi</sup>t and location <sup>fi</sup>t psychometrically overlapped, indicating insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient discriminant validity. It became apparent that nurses could not differentiate between having “the most current information at the time of care-giving” and “information access at the point-of care location.” This <sup>fi</sup>nding makes sense as mobile technology is exactly about that—it bridges time and space. For subsequent statistical analysis, both, information and communication <sup>fi</sup>t, were therefore collapsed and analyzed as one construct labeled “Information Fit” that comprises the notion of “having the most current information available at the time and location of caregiving.”

## 6. Discussion

The evolution of computing towards a ubiquitous nature that organizes and mediates social interaction when and where situations occur [49] has created its own set of organizational issues. Even though these new computing environments bring formidable advancements, they also pose tremendous challenges to user acceptance and design issues that may not be suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently addressed by traditional IS and behavioral theories [50]. In 2005, nearly 70% of healthcare providers (i.e., physicians, specialists, residents, and medical students) used mobile devices, such as Personal Digital Assistants (PDA), to access catalogued medical information to diagnose patients [27]. However, there are no documented acceptance rates for nurses who are the main users for documenting the medical intervention and tracking progression of the patient through the care system. This is not only indicative of the fact that nurses are an under-explored user group of information systems, but also that in light of these technical advancements, traditional IS theories need to be re-examined as their predictive validity is in question [50]. In this study we follow this call by studying technology acceptance behavior in these new computing environments and identify various types of human drives that determine an individual's perceptions of technology <sup>fi</sup>t.

A series of <sup>fi</sup>ndings can be inferred from the study. Besides varying notions of <sup>fi</sup>t perceptions, the study indicates that some nuances play a more signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in determining utilization decisions and individual performance than others. In the healthcare context, it seems that identi<sup>fi</sup>cation <sup>fi</sup>t and work<sup>fl</sup>ow <sup>fi</sup>t are the most prevalent determinants in MICT utilization considerations. Supporting the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of the patient, the type of medical intervention, and the nurse as an appropriate caregiver is a necessary pre-requisite that needs to be supported by the MICT in order for nurses to use it. In the same vein, an MICT must also be an integral part of the nurse's work<sup>fl</sup>ow. With a proper work<sup>fl</sup>ow in place, medical errors are expected to decrease. This <sup>fi</sup>nding is in accordance with nurses' comments in the qualitative study, stating that they are much more error prone and forgetful of pertinent information when they can not document their work at the time they perform the medical intervention.

Work<sup>fl</sup>ow <sup>fi</sup>t played also a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in determining the effectiveness and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of a nurse's work performance. In addition, user comfort <sup>fi</sup>t and time criticality played a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role as well. Using an MICT in the presence of a patient introduced a new dimension to the patient care process; no longer are there temporal boundaries between a nurse processing information and her physically interacting with the patient. Both activities are intertwined now and require a nurse to display a high level of con<sup>fi</sup>dence in doing both. The fact that most nurses have only limited typing skills [2] combined with the degree to which the MICT is easy enough to operate, in<sup>fl</sup>uences a nurse's user comfort level. In the qualitative data nurses noted that fumbling with the computer in front of a patient increased their anxiety, which, in turn, was perceived as something that patients would undoubtedly view as a lack of professionalism on their part. Nurses did not want to appear incompetent in any manner as any perceived lack of skill potentially can have physiological implications for the patient (e.g., increased high blood pressure, anxiousness, etc.). In addition, nurses were confronted with an overwhelming set of tasks that were time critical and highly interdependent. If an MICT, as part of their routine, afforded them with information when and where needed, and they were not afraid of using the MICT, then their overall performance improved.

Another major <sup>fi</sup>nding of our study is the viability of underlying human drives that impact perceptions of <sup>fi</sup>t and, in consequence, individual performance. A nurse's drive to learn (i.e., the drive to collect information, examine the environment, and make observations about explanatory ideas and theories) seems to be the motivating force behind identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and information <sup>fi</sup>t. The nurse's desire to insidiously identify what needs to be done, by whom and to whom and at what point in time is a direct result of one's desire to learn and aids in her decision-making process concerning medical interventions. Similarly, her quest to have the most current information available at her <sup>fi</sup>ngertips is also a direct consequence of this basic human drive.

The drive to bond exhibits signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uences on time criticality and physical <sup>fi</sup>t. Because of the volume of patients and limited resources, medical interventions are typically abbreviated and tasks have to be completed in a timely fashion as to not impede throughput. Bonding allows for patients to be much more amenable and tolerant of procedures that the nurse must accomplish at designated times. The qualitative data substantiates this in that nurses were more productive and successful in delivering the intended care when they were able to establish a mutually trusting relationship with the patient. Physical <sup>fi</sup>t, or the ability to maneuver an MICT in spatially constrained spaces, such as patient rooms, also appeared to be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by a nurse's bonding desire. In accordance with the qualitative study conducted, nurses felt that an ill-designed MICT distracted from their bonding experience and impeded a nurse's ability to prepare the patient for effective care. They noted, for example, that eye contact is a vital ingredient in establishing a trusting nurse–patient relationship and that an MICT, even though it might be considered useful, must not interfere with that. If it does interfere, nurses would decide to use the MICTaway from the point of care (e.g., in hallways), which, from an IS research perspective, signi<sup>fi</sup>es deviant utilization behavior.

The drive to acquire, or a nurse's strive toward gaining status as a competent caregiver, contributed to the perception of identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and patient interaction <sup>fi</sup>t. Achieving legitimacy and professional reputation is only possible if a nurse is able to correctly identify a patient, thus safeguarding that the correct medication or regimen is provided. The better equipped the technology is in ensuring this identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, the more likely a nurse will perceive it as a suitable <sup>fi</sup>t. As for patient interaction <sup>fi</sup>t, it is gravely important for nurses to interact with their patients in order to gain or provide pertinent information. For example, by interacting with a patient, a nurse might not only gather information about the effectiveness of medications or regimens, but she might also use this opportunity to provide educational information to the patient about his compliance with a prescribed regimen.

The drive to defend, or the drive of humans to defend themselves and their valued accomplishments wherever and whenever they perceive them to be endangered, exhibits in<sup>fl</sup>uences on information <sup>fi</sup>t, patient interaction <sup>fi</sup>t, and user comfort <sup>fi</sup>t. Nurses, or caregivers in general, are typically characterized by their imminent drive to preserve patient safety, which apparently also directs the need to ensure a healthy nurse–patient interaction. Preserving patient safety also manifests itself in professional creeds, mission and vision statements, as well as legislation. With the best possible information at hand at the right point in time, nurses try to follow the prescribed medical regimen that causes the least patient distress during the care-giving experience while decreasing the likelihood of medical error and potential legal consequences. However, this is only possible, if nurses feel at ease and are able to display con<sup>fi</sup>dence in their abilities with the technology in front of the patient.

Even though some of the predicted paths that we hypothesized did not turn out to be signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, we nevertheless think that deciphering the facets of <sup>fi</sup>t in a healthcare context as well as deciphering individualistic factors that contribute (or prevent) reaching any <sup>fi</sup>t facet is an important contribution as it will help IS designers to develop suitable guidelines for mobile technologies. We do expect that with the level of technology pervasiveness increasing, future studies will <sup>fi</sup>nd an even more salient connection between all human drives and all <sup>fi</sup>t notions. At present, however, this paper should be viewed as one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst that sheds light—in an exploratory and explanatory fashion—on various notions of <sup>fi</sup>t and the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of archetypical individual characteristics in the adoption endeavor of mobile technologies in healthcare.

## 6.1. Implications for research and practice

This study provides a preliminary test of the viability of human drives and their in<sup>fl</sup>uence on various notions of <sup>fi</sup>t to determine individual performance in a healthcare setting when using MICTs. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings provide insights into the notion of <sup>fi</sup>t by unfolding a series of nuances in <sup>fi</sup>t perceptions while pointing out the importance of individual characteristics in these <sup>fi</sup>t formations that have become salient in light of MICTs.

The basic premise of <sup>fi</sup>t models is that understanding the motivations of how people determine the extent to which a technology <sup>fi</sup>ts the task provides a more in-depth understanding of the cognitive assessments that take place regarding decision making about technology utilization. As technologies become more pervasive, this understanding about human drives can aid in effectively designing information systems and their supporting mobile technologies to better facilitate an individual's needs and promote intended utilization. For example, in order to address the drive to bond in the healthcare context, an information system should be designed in a way that it is unobtrusive and does not interfere with the eye-to-eye contact between nurse and patient. Future MICTs, for example, might consider the use of virtual displays instead of physical screens. In the case of the drive to learn, an information system should not only support a high quality level of information (i.e., it should support information that is most current, cross-linked across different platforms, and available in real-time at the point of care), but could potentially also provide additional information, such as phrophylactic measures that can be taken by the patient as part of his medical recuperation and education in the future. The latter feature would also enhance a nurse's drive to acquire as it would elevate her status in front of the patient as a competent care-giver. To appease the drive to bond, current information systems, for example, already proactively warn about con<sup>fl</sup>icting medications; future systems, however, could possibly raise the level of incidence detection by using, for example, intelligent emergency management systems, or comprehensive patient monitoring systems [72].

Scrutinizing the characteristics of an individual has received little research attention in the past. This needs to change as future systems become ever more pervasive, mobile, and personalizable, while having a higher degree of ubiquity. Our study has shown that more fundamental characteristics than the ones identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the traditional psychology literature are at play in determining technology acceptance. In fact, the four human drives that became salient in the study are rather biological in nature and can even be considered archetypical to any human being.

Besides the fact that designers have to increasingly incorporate individual characteristics, the current study has also demonstrated that technology <sup>fi</sup>t in healthcare contexts using MICTs cannot be considered homogeneous but is multi-faceted. In an era of mobile computing—an era in which information systems are portable and not tied to the network outlet of an organization anymore—the perceived <sup>fi</sup>t seems to be highly contextual driven. Abstracting from spatial and temporal boundaries [44], mobile devices have to follow design rules that, among other things, make them <sup>fi</sup>t their physical context. For IS designers, this means that information systems have to match the spatial aspects of the context—as re<sup>fl</sup>ected, for example, in the location and physical <sup>fi</sup>t notions. Designs of information systems also have to match temporal aspects—as re<sup>fl</sup>ected, for example, in the time criticality and information communication <sup>fi</sup>t notions. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings, even though limited to the healthcare context at present, should aid in designing well-suited MICTs for promoting ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency improvements and reducing laborious manual-laden and error prone processes while accelerating the delivery of information to the frontlines of care or work.

## 6.2. Limitations and future research

Even though seven notions of <sup>fi</sup>t and four human drives crystallized in the healthcare environment, this does not necessarily imply they will also do so in other contexts or that other notions of <sup>fi</sup>t might emerge. Findings are therefore not generalizable and future research therefore needs to explore other environments and roles. One possibility could be looking at training environments to determine the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of these drivers and the <sup>fi</sup>t of the technology for educational purposes. Also, the exclusive use of online surveys (instead of paper-based surveys) might have introduced a method bias. However, we believe that our multi-method approach paired with the careful attention paid to the development of the survey instrument and its simplistic administration assures a high level of con<sup>fi</sup>dence in the results.

## 7. Conclusion

The Healthcare Industry has been characterized as a technological laggard, plagued with antiquated methods of information management and delivery [42]. Meanwhile, health care costs are rapidly increasing, even as the public becomes more concerned with the increasingly visible safety breakdowns in the healthcare system. Technology, and particularly mobile technologies, can be major factors in improving decision making amongst healthcare personnel, cost cutting, and improved safety, but only if these applications are well designed and properly used. This study demonstrates the potential bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from the use of MICTs in healthcare settings while providing a template for factors to consider during systems design and implementation.
