<sup>1</sup>Vienna University of Economics and Business, Austria, peter.virag@wu.ac.at <sup>2</sup>Vienna University of Economics and Business, Austria, edward.bernroider@wu.ac.at <sup>3</sup>University of Innsbruck, Austria, ulrich.remus@uibk.ac.at

## Abstract

Agile information systems development (ISD) projects face the dilemma of control versus autonomy. Although autonomy benefits agile ISD, many projects in practice nevertheless feature project managers who exert control for more formal structure and guidance. To address this autonomycontrol dilemma, prior research on traditional ISD highlights the need for control ambidexterity, which is the simultaneous execution of contrasting control activities. However, little is known about achieving control ambidexterity in agile ISD projects, and, in particular, how agile project managers dynamically adapt controls to changing contextual requirements. Our in-depth case study in the IT department of a multinational container shipping company that runs several Scrum ISD projects identifies four agile project management styles (Landscaper, Buddy, Detective, and Commander) and associated balanced practices for control ambidexterity. We also show how agile project managers blend or shift their styles in response to conflicts, revealing different levels of adherence to agile development principles. We contribute to the ISD control literature by reflecting on different forms of contextual and temporal control ambidexterity and theorizing how the interplay between control conflicts and underlying factors leads to varied ambidexterity forms. Furthermore, our insights suggest signaling theory should complement agency and stewardship theories to better understand agile ISD control.

Keywords: Project Control, Ambidexterity, Agile, Project Management Styles, Case Study, Information Systems Development

## 1 Introduction

According to recent studies, the main challenge for information systems development (ISD) project managers is creating an environment where individual learning, experimentation, and creativity are in sync with efficiency, stability, and timely feedback (Lifshitz-Assaf et al., 2018; Moe et al., 2021). Addressing tensions between autonomy and control is particularly important in agile ISD project settings, especially in “pure” agile projects, where a specific project manager role is missing and structure and guidance are assigned to specific agile roles instead. Within Scrum, which is arguably the most popular agile ISD method (Digital.ai, 2021), these responsibilities can be assigned to the Scrum master (Shastri et al., 2021). Such projects are characterized by trust in individuals, leadership collaboration, and opportunities for self-organization (Highsmith & Cockburn, 2001), creating an environment for fostering the creativity and flexibility of developers (Conboy et al., 2009; Conboy & Fitzgerald, 2010).

Hence, it is not surprising that when adhering to “pure” agile principles (Beck et al., 2001), project management in ISD is advised to use informal controls enacted in an enabling control style rather than relying on formal authoritative command-control approaches (Maruping et al., 2009) to improve task performance and provide additional (formal) structure and guidance (Dreesen et al., 2020).

To manage these often-conflicting requirements in agile ISD, projects in practice seem to regularly employ dedicated project managers (Digital.ai, 2020) despite the fact that agile ISD theory does not include the role of a project manager (Schwaber & Sutherland, 2020; Shastri et al., 2021). Prior research has shown that agile project managers pursue agile-specific management styles for a number of reasons. For example, following a “soft” style, agile project managers may serve as coaches whose goal is to make the team self-sufficient and who try to remain uninvolved in everyday tasks. In the “moderate” style, they can take a facilitator-coordinator role across stakeholders and issues, while in the “hard” style, assertive agile project managers seek to draw their teams’ attention to persistent obstacles in the development process (Shastri et al., 2021). The Project Management Institute (2017) suggests a “servant” style, which is focused on addressing the needs and development of team members through mentoring, education, and removing impediments. With all these styles, however, agile project managers need to accept a dual role by simultaneously performing the activities of traditional project managers (e.g., performance reviews) as well as the activities required by agile frameworks (e.g., Scrum guides) with some variations across activities. As a result, agile project managers need to constantly balance autonomy, flexibility, and responsiveness to change while retaining the benefits of a tighter control regime (Dreesen & Schmid, 2018). This situation becomes even more difficult for agile project managers if senior management offers unclear guidance (Shastri et al., 2021). As they are typically responsible for avoiding and resolving resulting conflicts (Siddique & Hussein, 2016), an important question is how agile project managers should behave and think in order to address control requirements (Wiener et al. 2016), i.e., ensure appropriate structure, efficiency, and productivity while still allowing for team autonomy, which is often hampered by the former (Moe et al., 2021).

Prior research has suggested that control in ISD can be based on agency, stewardship (Wiener et al., 2019), and signaling theories (Virag et al., 2021, 2022). It has also drawn attention to different forms of control balancing (Chua et al., 2012; Gregory et al., 2013a; Gregory & Keil, 2014; Kreutzer et al., 2016; Ramesh et al., 2012; Syed et al., 2021; Tiwana, 2010). Taking this further, Gregory and Keil (2014) use the term control ambidexterity to emphasize the blending of two contrasting control approaches, which they call management styles, to meet such conflicting demands. They draw on a control-oriented conceptual framework from Lewis et al. (2002), which contrasts the “emergent” and “planned” management styles according to monitoring, evaluation, and control, typical key elements of control (Kirsch, 2004). Thus, Gregory and Keil define management style as a certain behavioral approach and mode of thinking to manage a project, motivate staff members, and ensure that they are acting in accordance with the objectives of the project (Gregory & Keil, 2014, p. 345). Their bureaucratic management style draws on formal controls and their collaborative management style employs informal controls. Based on their finding that blending these styles creates three kinds of tensions that are difficult for a single project manager to resolve, they suggest employing two project managers in tandem. This perspective has been extended by Syed et al. (2021), who found that blending different control styles alone and in combination with formal and informal control modes improves project performance.

While these studies have certainly advanced our understanding of control balancing in ISD, there is still a significant gap in the literature regarding how to achieve control ambidexterity in agile ISD projects. Agile project managers are confronted with very different (control) situations, and organizations may customize agile techniques and combine them with other development approaches (Cram & Newell, 2016; Digital.ai, 2021). Also, the level of adherence to agile values shows great variety (Zaitsev et al., 2018). With stricter adherence to agile methods, control configurations that are better aligned with selforganizing teams and autonomous, intrinsically motivated team members may be preferable (De O. Melo et al., 2012). Moreover, there is a gap in our knowledge about how and when agile project managers adapt these to achieve control ambidexterity.

Therefore, our research questions are: (1) Which main project management styles can be identified to control agile ISD under which circumstances? (2) How do these agile project management styles support control ambidexterity to resolve conflicts in agile ISD? To answer these questions, we conducted an in-depth case study focusing on the IT department of a multinational container shipping company that runs several Scrum ISD projects (Schwaber & Sutherland, 2020). The next section extends the theoretical background of this study. This is followed by methodology, findings, and discussion sections, as well as a final section on limitations and future research avenues.

## 2 Theoretical Background

While agile ISD emphasizes autonomy (Highsmith, 2002), it also requires structure and guidance (Harris et al., 2009; Maruping et al., 2009). Whereas autonomy is provided by following agile guidelines, structure is provided by control, and trying to do both usually results in conflicts (Ramesh et al., 2012). In this section, we develop our theoretical position, which builds on the view that agile ISD requires both autonomy and control and that conflicts can be managed by control ambidexterity. As essential theoretical background, we first introduce established IS control theory and then describe which conflicts may arise in the agile ISD. As a solution, we position prior research on control and control ambidexterity, which we consider embedded in management styles of agile IS project management.

## 2.1 Control Theory in IS Research

Control can be generally defined as any attempt to align individual behaviors with organizational objectives (Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003; Kirsch, 1996), and as a dyadic concept, it involves a controller as the source and a controlee as the target of control (Wiener et al. 2016). Table 1 provides an overview of established concepts in IS control theory. Whereas formal control modes (behavior and outcome controls) strictly describe the expectations of controllers, informal control modes (clan and self-control) should allow controlees to reach objectives more independently (Henderson & Lee, 1992; Jaworski, 1988; Ouchi, 1979) by creating shared norms and values (Wiener et al., 2016). Control styles describe the manner in which the interaction between the controller and the controlee takes place (Wiener et al., 2016). An authoritative control style enforces obedient controlee behavior, while an enabling control style involves constant collaboration (Adler & Borys, 1996; Heumann et al., 2015; Remus et al., 2020). The enabling control style offers a higher level of transparency that grants controlees a better understanding of the rationale of the enacted controls. It also provides repair, promoting responses and feedback on the enacted controls (Wiener et al., 2016).

In terms of control purposes, controls can be enacted to prevent value appropriation. This theoretically addresses the agency problem, which stems from information asymmetries between controllers (principals) and controlees (agents). According to agency theory, information asymmetry, such as hidden information on controlees’ abilities and performed work, allows controlees to follow self-interests, such as effort minimization, moral hazards, and adverse selection (Bosse & Phillips, 2016; Jensen & Meckling, 1976). In contrast, controls may seek to integrate the diverse skills and knowledge of stakeholders involved in IS projects by enhancing coordination and collaboration (Wiener et al., 2019) with and among controlees. Theoretically, this intention can be explained using stewardship theory (Davis et al., 1997). It moves away from the economic “model of man” of agency theory and claims that agents are, in fact, stewards whose interests are aligned with the principal (Hernandez, 2012). Both control intentions can be implemented through preventive control practices, when controllers seek to avoid issues from occurring, or corrective control practices, when they try to detect issues and then remedy them (Cram & Brohman, 2013).

In addition to agency and stewardship theories, signaling theory has also been used as a potential underlying theory of control activities (Virag et al., 2021, 2022), which provides more neutral views on actors while still providing guidance on how to handle information asymmetry (Spence, 1973). This view assumes that controlees do not exclusively behave opportunistically or in a collective-serving manner; rather, a mix of these characterizes their behavior. Moreover, the theory suggests that both sides are interested in reducing information asymmetries. As such, signaling theory can be used to provide additional explanations for both value-appropriation and valuecreation control purposes (Virag et al., 2021, 2022).

Explained using signaling theory, agile control mechanisms, such as agile events (e.g. daily standup) and tools (e.g., burndown charts), can also serve as opportunities for controlees to signal high quality, i.e. a high degree of competency and compliance with expectations (Gacasan & Wiggins, 2017). This is favorable for signalers who may get rewarded for their signals (e.g. bonuses or promotions) and for signal receivers who are interested in identifying signalers with a high degree of competency and compliance with expectations (Connelly et al., 2011).

## 2.2 Control in Agile ISD

While traditional ISD emphasizes extensive planning to produce solutions efficiently in a predictable way, agile ISD promotes lightweight approaches based on creativity rather than rigid plans (Highsmith & Cockburn, 2001). All the values in the “Agile Manifesto” (Beck et al., 2001) serve as guiding principles yet essentially indicate tradeoffs in terms of autonomy and control (Boehm, 2002). For example, while working software is deemed more important than comprehensive documentation, illustrating code still needs to be embraced (Highsmith, 2002). While there are several agile ISD methods to guide agile ISD in practice, such as Scrum, eXtreme Programming, and Kanban (Pikkarainen et al., 2008; Schwaber & Sutherland, 2020; VersionOne, 2019), these methods are often either only partially implemented or combined with other guidelines (Scrum Alliance, 2018; Zaitsev et al., 2018).

As the agile approach to ISD places an emphasis on autonomous, self-organizing teams (Highsmith, 2002), controlling such projects may seem contradictory to the aims of the method. Still, control exists in agile ISD as well (Harris et al., 2009; Persson et al., 2012). Its objectives are mainly process-related (e.g. speed, budget, risk reduction), and it is often used to detect issues rather than prevent them (Cram & Brohman, 2013). Other than that, we know little about how to control agile ISD (Cram, Brohman, Chan, et al., 2016; Dikert et al., 2016). For instance, the optimal trade-off between control and autonomy is unknown (Cram, Brohman, & Gallupe, 2016; Dreesen et al., 2020).

Although control theory does not discuss specific ISD methods (Cram & Brohman, 2010), it has been discussed how the characteristics of control modes and styles affect team autonomy. There is an agreement that informal controls provide developers with more space in determining ways to achieve goals (Henderson & Lee, 1992; Kirsch et al., 2002; Maruping et al., 2009) and some say that agile ISD, in general, relies more on informal than formal control (Cram et al., 2016). For example, clan control enables the team to identify key project goals while also providing autonomy to developers to reach them (Maruping et al., 2009). Others have found that formal controls are also needed in order to counterbalance team autonomy (Dreesen & Schmid, 2018). As an illustration, Maruping et al. (2009) highlight that outcome control allows for team autonomy while providing structure and clear goals, although Harris et al. (2009) note that it may hardly handle changes in goals. Another study mentions that control styles affect team autonomy more than control modes and that it is the enabling control style that allows for more team autonomy (Dreesen et al., 2020).

In agile ISD, control mechanisms are mostly exercised through agile practices (Harris et al., 2006; Mahadevan et al., 2015). However, the categorization of agile practices into control modes seems to be ambiguous. In their literature review, Dreesen and Schmid (2018) mapped 29 agile practices to their control modes and found that most agile practices were classified into more than one control mode—and sometimes both as formal and informal control. For instance, burndown charts were classified as outcome control (Gregory et al., 2013b; Harris et al., 2009) and as clan control (Gregory et al., 2013b). Iteration planning was categorized as behavior control (Harris et al., 2009; Mahadevan et al., 2015) while Mahadevan et al. (2015) also viewed this as clan control. Regarding retrospectives, Gregory et al. (2013b) argued that they formalize outcomes while acknowledging that they encompass elements of clan control through team socialization. In sum, it seems that many kinds of agile practices simultaneously serve as social (clan control) or people (self-control) tactics exemplifying informal controls and as means to provide guidance and structure embodying formal controls. This double role of controls in agile ISD is also visible in the concept of emergent outcome control (Harris et al., 2009). Emergent outcome control is different from regular outcome control as it combines structure (scope boundaries) with ongoing feedback processes so that it can adapt to changing requirements common in the agile context.

Finally, it is important to note that the project manager role, which traditionally enacts control (Wiener et al., 2016), is not mentioned in agile guidelines, e.g., in those of Scrum or eXtreme Programming (Schwaber & Sutherland, 2020; Shastri et al., 2021). Industry surveys, however, indicate that the majority of agile ISD projects still include this job title (Digital.ai, 2020; Shastri et al., 2016).

## 2.3 Control Ambidexterity in Agile ISD

To achieve the right balance between autonomy and control in agile ISD (Boehm, 2002), prior research in IS has suggested an ambidextrous approach to meeting conflicting demands (e.g., Binci et al., 2023; Ramesh et al., 2012; Vinekar et al., 2006). The ambidexterity idea has been known in management research for decades (Duncan, 1976). It essentially requires the “ability to pursue two disparate things at the same time” (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004, p. 210). Lewis et al. (2002) noted that polarized approaches (e.g. emergent/determined, freedom/responsibility, organic/ mechanical) are no longer viable in the modern world and suggested a “both/and” perspective to managers. Ambidexterity is categorized in different ways in prior work. The simultaneous use of contrasting control/management activities in the same project or organizational unit is called contextual ambidexterity, whereas in different units or projects, it is called structural ambidexterity (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004). The use of contrasting control/management activities that fluctuate over time is associated with temporal ambidexterity (Lewis et al., 2002).

Ambidexterity has been studied under various control theory perspectives in IS research, albeit mainly in terms of concurrently blending control modes (Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003; Chua et al., 2012; Kirsch, 1997; Kreutzer et al., 2016; Tiwana, 2010) and only recently in terms of blending control styles (Syed et al., 2021). There is a consensus that blending formal and informal control modes (e.g., Kreutzer et al., 2016) as well as enabling and authoritative control styles (Syed et al., 2021) generates complementary benefits at different levels. For instance, Syed et al. (2021) found that the simultaneous use of contrasting control styles increases project performance and enhances the performance-elevating effects of both formal and informal control.

Gregory et al. (2013a) moved beyond considering single dimensions of control by highlighting the benefits of multidimensional control balancing (involving control modes, control styles, control degrees) to execute authoritative and trust-based controls simultaneously. A noteworthy follow-up of this line of work investigates the use of contrasting management styles in general (not necessarily agile) IS project management linked with different types of control to achieve control ambidexterity (Gregory & Keil, 2014). The important distinction of this study is that it investigated control ambidexterity in relation to different behavioral approaches manifested in management styles to ensure that project goals are achieved. This study introduced two contrasting management styles—bureaucratic and collaborative— and highlighted that tensions can arise if a single project manager relies on both.

This extension to management styles in control ambidexterity research is particularly relevant to agile ISD projects because they require an “agile mindset” to allow for the high degree of flexibility in thinking and acting needed to adapt to quickly changing contexts and situations (Denning, 2018). As a result, agile project managers may need to draw upon different management styles and exercise control ambidexterity in order to manage the conflicting demands of control vs. autonomy in an environment emphasizing trust in the capabilities of responsible team members and the creation of an enabling environment for learning and continuous improvement (Mordi & Schoop, 2020). These situations are known to create conflicts (Ramesh et al., 2012) and are generally challenging due to dynamically changing environments and temporary project structures prompting agile project managers to periodically adapt their management styles beyond the use of only one particular angle of control (e.g., control modes or control styles).

## 3 Methods

Since the nature of this study is exploratory and involves investigating a contextually rich phenomenon, we chose to apply an inductive case study approach (Benbasat et al., 1987; Gioia et al., 2013; Yin, 2017). Following Yin’s suggestion (2017), we examined one “revelatory” in-depth case with the intention to develop novel insights into understudied phenomena on the basis of an interpretive research philosophy. Such a single-case study design is often applied in related studies (e.g. Nag et al., 2007; Nag & Gioia, 2012). Consistent with interpretive research, we not only considered how informants make sense of their experiences but also interpreted and structured informants’ interpretations in order to generate novel concepts (Strauss & Corbin, 1998). To achieve a more holistic view and mitigate information hiding—in particular, information hiding due to social desirability—we also approached agile project managers by using controlees as proxy subjects (Nederhof, 1985).

## 3.1 Case Study Background

Our case centers on the IT department of a multinational container shipping company with about 200 employees working on 30 agile ISD projects running simultaneously. Digitalization is becoming the norm in the shipping industry, given its focus on operational efficiency. The most important ISD projects target ship automation (e.g. navigation, collision avoidance), instant booking confirmation, remote container management, real-time cargo and container tracking, and network and fuel consumption optimization (Fruth & Teuteberg, 2017).

All agile ISD projects applied Scrum after an incremental transition from traditional ISD between 2016 and 2018. At the heart of Scrum is a development iteration called a “sprint,” which typically aims at a product increment and lasts for 3-4 weeks (Schwaber & Sutherland, 2020). Scrum events comprise daily Scrum meetings for coordination and communication, sprint planning meetings at the beginning of each sprint (Schwaber, 2004), and sprint reviews (Schwaber & Beedle, 2002; Schwaber & Sutherland, 2020). Across all ISD projects, the IT department organized these Scrum events according to Scrum prescriptions. Scrum artifacts are further important building blocks of Scrum projects and are designed to maximize the transparency of information (Schwaber, 2004). Such artifacts are the sprint and product backlogs that are ordered and emergent lists that describe what is needed to further and more generally develop the product in the given sprint (Schwaber & Sutherland, 2020). The case company not only used these Scrum artifacts but also additional artifacts that were borrowed from other agile methods (e.g. Kanban board).

This resulted in a relatively homogeneous sample that increased the internal validity of our findings. An IT project manager, a role not outlined in agile guidelines, typically oversaw one Scrum team. Senior IT project managers sometimes had two or three Scrum teams (see Figure 1). Each Scrum team included a Scrum master, who organized Scrum events, and the product owner, who served as the business voice inside a Scrum team and provided user stories—very much as prescribed by Scrum (Schwaber & Sutherland, 2020). However, the product owners were called business analysts, as the company had not yet harmonized job titles with agile terms. Scrum teams further included developers and testers, who were mostly employed by consulting firms. Finally, Scrum teams shared some resources between projects, e.g., enterprise architects responsible for the organizational IT architecture.

## 3.2 Data Collection

For data collection, we applied a mix of purposeful and theoretical sampling methods (Matavire & Brown, 2013) to select interviewees. We predetermined their job roles, while decisions about the size of the sample were not made until we reached adequate theoretical saturation (Bowen, 2008). Data collection occurred from October 2018 to June 2020. We conducted interviews with 12 controllers—IT project managers and senior IT project managers—and nine controlees (business analysts and senior business analysts; both groups referred to themselves as “product owners”) that served as proxy subjects (Nederhof, 1985) to limit social desirability (Fisher, 1993). We used an intensive interviewing technique featuring a flexible and open-ended approach (Charmaz, 2006). We conducted one interview with each interviewee (see Table 2), and interviews lasted 48 minutes, on average. The interviews began with questions about the informant and the goals of the agile ISD project(s). We then asked questions about the daily life of Scrum teams, project management techniques, and the intentions and activities related to control. Lastly, we asked about the type of control and its purposes in different situations.

For triangulation purposes, we also gained access to written communications (emails) between the controllers and controlees. These emails were extracts from the socalled Steering Committee meetings written by IT project managers to their team members. At these meetings, IT project managers informed senior IT management about their project performance and timelines for future project deliverables. Senior IT management provided updates on the department-level IT strategy and business strategy affecting the department and expressed their expected project deadlines. The emails were written between September 2017 and June 2019, and these provided the authors with further opportunities to examine the project environment, especially regarding senior IT management’s expectations.

In particular, this methodology offers a well-defined process for qualitative data analysis on the basis of firstorder accounts from informants and a more abstract researcher-centric second-order data analysis (Cornelissen, 2017). As prior research has noted the lack of unified opinions on the nature of control in agile ISD (Dreesen et al., 2020), we wanted to maximize the relevance and richness of our findings.

In accordance with the data analysis process suggested by Gioia et al. (2013), we conducted the interviews and created word-for-word transcripts and thematic analysis concurrently. We began the coding process by developing in vivo codes (Saldaña, 2013) in Nvivo 12 that closely matched the words used by our informants. These were then summarized into first-order categories (Gioia et al., 2013) through regular bounces from one informant to another, using additional email documentation as described in the previous subsection. This process resulted in 112 first-order categories. We then looked for linkages between the first-order categories by focusing on their similarities and differences. This step is similar to axial coding (Strauss & Corbin, 1998) and resulted in 22 second-order themes (Gioia et al., 2013). These themes were more abstract, included researchers’ own interpretations, and were the result of shifting back and forth between the first-order categories and the related literature. Appendix A provides empirical evidence of what comprises a first-order category. Once the authors developed a better understanding of the internal organizational context, we further grouped the secondorder themes into eight aggregate dimensions (Table 3). As Gioia et al. (2013) note, this step is rather abductive than inductive because data and extant theories are “considered in a tandem” (p. 21).

To create second-order themes and aggregate dimensions, we relied on the dominant ISD project control classifications of Wiener et al. (2016, 2019), as well as concepts of information asymmetry (Bergh et al., 2019), control ambidexterity (Gregory & Keil, 2014), agile and Scrum ISD (Highsmith, 2002; Schwaber & Sutherland, 2020), signaling (e.g. Connelly et al., 2011), agency (Bosse & Phillips, 2016), and stewardship (e.g. Davis et al., 1997). Finally, we asked two of our informants for “member checks” (Nag et al., 2007) of first-order categories and second-order themes to ensure that our findings had attained the required level of trustworthiness. Figure 2 depicts the resulting data structure (Corley & Gioia, 2004).

Next, we adapted the theoretical perspective and terminology offered by Gregory and Keil (2014) to examine control ambidexterity, which we conceptually embedded in agile project management (aPM) styles— defined in accordance with Gregory and Keil, based on

Lewis et al. (2002), as “repertoire of actions entailing a specific mode of thinking and behaving that project managers draw upon differently, depending on contextual and situational requirements” (p. 345) to ensure that the agile team creates maximum value for the organization. The investigated repertoires of actions within aPM styles also allowed for a multidimensional consideration of established control dimensions in IS theory (Wiener et al., 2016, 2019). Six of our aggregate dimensions comprised the core category aPM styles. Different control modes (1) and control styles (2) may be chosen to address characteristics of the agile mindset, such as the need for collaboration, flexibility, and agility (Harris et al., 2009; Mahadevan et al., 2015; Maruping et al., 2009; Taylor, 2016). Control purposes (3) explain the underlying thinking and intentions behind these choices, including perspectives on controlees. In line with Wiener et al. (2016, 2019), we allowed for the assumption that any combination of control modes, styles, and purposes can be applicable and that one particular aPM style may include multiple combinations, which we termed “control approaches.” The dimension adoption of agile practices and tools (4) captures the extent to which agile project managers initiate agile events, such as daily standups, and adopt agile tools, such as burndown charts and user storyboards. Adherence to agile values (5) refers to the extent to which agile project managers conform to assuming the values the method associates to stakeholders (e.g. trustworthiness) and the tasks these imply (e.g., supporting developers). Finally, the IS knowledge dimension (6) describes the level of technical and business domain knowledge possessed by agile project managers.

As seen in Figure 2, two more aggregate dimensions were created, which we called the external and internal project context. The external project context dimension captures the pressure from senior IT management to track and report project outcomes against project objectives. The internal project context dimension captures the levels of behavior observability and outcome measurability. The first stage was finalized by selecting a theoretical lens for interpreting agile project managers’ thinking and behavior in accordance with the observed control purposes and assigning an aPM style metaphor to them. Examples of analytical codes that informed our decision to select this theoretical lens are: only consider their immediate tasks, performance issue, disappear without working full hours, and don’t reach out to help for agency theory, which were categorized by the second-order theme: prevention of value appropriation with controls. Sample analytical codes for stewardship theory are: acts as a team player, treats colleagues with respect, share knowledge, flag when they are low on tasks, which were captured by the second-order theme: value-creation intention behind controls. Finally, sample analytical codes for signaling theory are: investigate activities, evenhanded mindset, attention to details, and search for justice, which we grouped both under the second-order themes: prevention of value appropriation with controls and value-creation intention behind controls. Based on this logic, the dominant theoretical lens we used to understand intentions behind control activities was stewardship theory for Landscapers and Buddies; stewardship, agency, and signaling theories for Detectives; and agency theory for Commanders.

The second stage extended the Gioia method (like e.g., Möhlmann et al., 2021; Zaitsev et al., 2020) with an integrated inductive-deductive approach (Cruzes & Dybå, 2011). The inductive part came partly from “left-over” in vivo codes created in the first stage; we used these to create new first-level categories. We also reused first-order categories established in the first stage as we developed a new case narrative in which we looked for conflicting demands faced by agile project managers and their ways of resolving these. For example, if we found in vivo codes in the same interview from the first-order categories telling team members how to solve a task and welcoming suggestions on ways of working that we identified in the first stage, we categorized these conflicting demands as team direction vs team autonomy conflicts, which are common in agile ISD (Binci et al., 2023; Ramesh et al., 2012; Vinekar et al., 2006). We further grouped our conflict categories into three aggregate dimensions (see Table 4) based on Gregory and Keil (2014).

For conflict resolutions, we again first turned to our data and used the existing in vivo codes to form first-order categories about how control approaches and/or aPM styles changed because of the conflicts. We then grouped these into second-order themes, assigned researchercentric labels to them (e.g. monitoring with belief), and again asked two of our interviewees for “member checks” (Nag et al., 2007) to ensure that our interpretations matched their lived experiences. Finally, we further categorized the second-order themes to aggregate dimensions that were taken from extant control ambidexterity research (e.g., contextual and temporal ambidexterity in Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004, and Lewis et al., 2002). Figure 3 shows the main categories and their relationships, for which we relied on the data structure diagram common in studies using the Gioia method (Gioia et al., 2013). Appendix A provides empirical evidence of what comprises a first-order category in this data structure.

## 4 Findings

Two key patterns emerged from our data. First, agile project managers drew upon four different aPM styles to control agile ISD (see Table 5). We associated each with a metaphor. Metaphors are useful means for exploration, theory development, and empirical analysis (Inns & Jones, 1996). While metaphors can never be fully accurate and cannot be empirically tested, they are beneficial especially at the beginning of scientific inquiries about under-researched phenomena (Pinder & Bourgeois, 1982). These benefits not only include allowing for interpretative variability (Van Maanen, 1995) but are also powerful tools for understanding social reality (Alvesson & Spicer, 2010). The metaphors Landscaper, Buddy and Commander were borrowed from leadership literature (Alvesson & Spicer, 2010) while the Detective metaphor was developed by the authors (though we admit that it could have been used elsewhere).

Second, we found evidence for control ambidexterity resulting from the need to handle conflicting demands either embedded in aPM styles through balanced practices or working across aPM styles through blends and shifts. Balanced practices refer to control mechanisms that are implemented as a blend of formal and informal control modes to achieve a balance between formal processes that emphasize stability, rigor, and discipline, and flexible processes that emphasize flexibility and coordination. Each agile project manager had a primary aPM style, and most of them applied balanced practices, blended their primary aPM style with a secondary aPM style, or shifted to a secondary aPM style. In this section, we first describe the four identified aPM styles and then highlight ways of handling conflicting demands in agile ISD.

## 4.1 The Landscaper

The Landscaper emphasizes value creation by providing maximal autonomy and support for project members to achieve project objectives (see Table 5). Landscapers act as growth facilitators who are primarily focused not on maximizing the amount that can be “harvested,” but rather on creating an aesthetically pleasing environment by fully aligning with agile theory and ethos. The Landscapers in our study closely followed textbook agile prescriptions, which they tried to implement as responsible stewards. They had a limited technical understanding of the tasks assigned to controlees and followed a “hands-off” approach, e.g., by organizing but hardly ever participating in agile events and avoiding interaction with agile tools, such as storyboards and burndown charts. For them, individual work expectations and outcomes were hard to define and measure, as reflected by this interviewee:

#2: A business analyst probably knows better than me what an okay requirement is. They get trainings on it. They have their own community. It’s not okay to say, oh, by the way, this or that is not good.

Landscapers’ primary purpose of control was to facilitate independent value creation, assuming that controlees act in a pro-organizational manner and are trustworthy collectivists. Through frequent interactions with controlees, Landscapers paid particular attention to controlees’ well-being, and when problems emerged, they tried to find remedies in a collaborative manner. They sought to intrinsically motivate and professionally grow development team members and avoided extrinsic motivators, as exemplified by the following statements:

#11: What I try to do is to work on intrinsic motivators of team members. Autonomy, mastery, and purpose, which I think good motivators. Extrinsic factors are also important, sure, but there are way more limitations.

#2: I’m there to support rather than boss them around. I do feel that people can contribute a lot. They have open hands if you don’t try to manage them.

In addition, Landscapers expressed the need to set and communicate explicit goals to the team while granting team members considerable autonomy to reach these goals. They believe in the need to create and maintain an environment for everyday work that reflected a “playing field” with rules and procedures that conformed to descriptions from agile textbooks. Maintaining the playing field was noted in the case of autonomy violations that team members imposed on each other. In these cases, Landscapers respectfully stepped in to resolve the conflict, a form of corrective behavior control in enabling style, and acted as referees. The following statement illustrates this approach:

#11: I believe that it is easier and more effective if you create an environment where creativity has a role. But there are rules and processes, let’s call it the playing field, that I have the responsibility to manage and change if needed, to get feedback to keep it effective, and I also have responsibility to communicate the goals to the team.

Landscapers refused to say that they controlled individual controlees; for them, the word “control” sounds negative; thus, they mostly relied on selfcontrol. This type of control has a preventive focus, as developers were asked to follow some pre-established rules, as the above quotations illustrate. We consider this type of control to be rather authoritative because Landscapers requested independent work without explaining their reasoning or discussing how it was perceived by controlees. Apart from self-control, Landscapers did not act as participants in control dyads. Instead, they applied some level of control at the project level: they inquired from time to time how the project performed in terms of speed and budget. We classified this as outcome control enacted in an enabling control style. This outcome control operated on trust because while agile project managers put efforts into comparing work outcomes with a priori specifications, they could not verify the accuracy of the information they received due to low IS knowledge, behavior observability, and outcome measurability.

## 4.2 The Buddy

The Buddy prioritizes value creation through socialization and shared norms to achieve project objectives (see Table 5). This metaphor refers to agile project managers who seek to convey a “one-of-us”

image and prioritize equality with team members. Unlike Landscapers, the Buddies in our study wanted to “belong” to the team, emotionally bond with team members, and create a strong sense of “we.” Just like Landscapers, Buddies are avid agile practitioners, who convey agile values and believe that their main task is to provide controlees with a friendly companion, as the next statement illustrates:

#1: Scrum is a touch point for us to communicate; they can highlight blockers. Tell me if you are blocked. This is your opportunity to call for help.

Like Landscapers, the Buddies acted as responsible stewards and saw controlees as collaborative and trustworthy collectivists. But in contrast to Landscapers, they aimed at clan-building through intensive socialization with team members to prevent issues from occurring, e.g. promoting shared offices and informal team events. Buddies also encouraged team members to participate in other clans, such as the business analyst community. Buddies mostly chose the enabling control style that was aligned with their intended “friend” image.

Their main intention was to ensure value creation with control, especially to enhance collaboration and communication with team members. They participated in every agile event, helped if needed, and improved social ties—a distinct difference between Landscapers and Buddies. They paid continuous attention to the product and sprint backlogs; they reviewed them and asked questions if an item had been pending for a long time. With a strong sense of collectivistic behavior and pro-organizational thinking, Buddies assumed that controlees would openly share information. We interpreted this as outcome control: while Buddies wanted to check work outcomes and remain informed about the project, their IS knowledge was low, making verification difficult. Hence, this control also relied on trust, just as it did for Landscapers. The below statements by an agile project manager illustrate the underlying thinking of Buddies:

#1: Imagine that I have a developer whose work is giving problems. Bugs coming in. First, let’s talk to this guy: “How can I help you? Need some knowledge? Some info?” Get it addressed, if this is not helping, I have to talk to his manager, what can be done? Is there anything I do not know? Needs training?

#4: For example, if I hire a developer, I want to make sure I have somebody senior in the role next to me. That I can trust. And this person does the technical part of the interview.

Another reason for Buddies to become as informed as possible about project updates was to serve senior IT management’s expectations. They believe that the more they knew about their project, the better they will look in the eyes of senior IT management. Pressure from above was also visible when they mentioned the need to follow undesirable procedures prescribed by their superiors, as reflected by this statement:

#1: Oh, I would say this was also, you know, kind of for my… my own sake so… so my management team is not coming to me. You are not performing, you know, as usual. That’s so…, so that’s kind of, you know, controlling others was kind of saving my ass.

## 4.3 The Detective

The Detective focuses on both value creation and value appropriation through scanning for and evaluating behavioral cues to achieve project objectives (see Table 5). This metaphor refers to agile project managers who collect and analyze “evidence,” such as general behavior in meetings, and “interview” stakeholders, such as business representatives, to make judgments on controlees’ competencies and compliance with expectations. The Detectives in our study engaged in control activities either to ensure value creation or because they believed that controlees would appropriate value from the project by focusing more on their own interests than on those of the project. Detectives loosely adhered to agile values and questioned developers’ level of commitment to the project. They had a rather neutral view of controlees: their general attitude was neither self-sacrificial nor self-serving, but somewhere in-between. They still opted to use the agile method in terms of practices and tools, which were mainly used to control project progress.

Detectives’ limited IS knowledge was coupled with a fair amount of trust in controlees. This changed according to how they interpreted behavioral cues, which were important to Detectives because they believed controlees had hidden characteristics, such as task competencies. Behavioral cues were collected from several sources. First, they generated reports from project tracking applications and examined them to reveal potential issues with competencies and compliance. We categorized this as outcome control operating on trust as their low IS knowledge made verifying the accuracy of information difficult. As a second source of behavioral cues, they interacted with agile tools, often checking statuses in sprint and product backlogs. They focused on the status of tasks without understanding the efforts needed to produce results, as illustrated by this statement:

#8: She [controlee’s agile project manager] broadly understood the activities that you were doing, but she didn’t need to know the specifics of requirements, instead, its status was important for her.

As another source of behavioral cues, Detectives participated in daily Scrums and other agile ceremonies and often arranged one-on-one meetings with controlees through which they attempted to challenge the developed solutions. Controlee responses to these queries were also seen as behavioral cues that formed the basis of subsequent performance evaluations. Additionally, Detectives often had meetings with business stakeholders to learn how they found working with different team members and how satisfied they were with the project’s progress. We classified these meetings as outcome controls. Detectives enacted controls in an enabling control style, which they found suitable for receiving behavioral cues. The following quote exemplifies the logic of Detectives:

#20: I can determine their attitude to the project at the meetings. Is the person giving updates which are relevant for business? Is he or she proactive at every meeting? In thinking about solutions and recommendations. Do they have suggestions for improvement and do they give out a level of passion and engagement towards the product? No passion is shown by the tone of voice when they speak about the product. Or if the person is late from meetings, or if somebody doesn’t give relevant updates, or there is no progress for 2-3 days.

## 4.4 The Commander

Finally, the Commander emphasizes preventing value appropriation through strictly instructing controlees how to achieve the desired project objectives (see Table 5). This metaphor refers to agile project managers who like to “break the rules” of textbook agile development and implement their own rules. We view these agile project managers as action-oriented managers who love responsibility, seek to convey an “alpha” image, and show little self-reflexivity. The Commanders in our study assumed that high levels of asymmetric information would be detrimental to project success. Accordingly, they tried to reduce it with active monitoring and directing, coupled with rewarding or punishing. Unlike Detectives, Commanders expected controlees to resist control and attempt to hide their private information.

Commanders asked controlees to provide accounts through instructions to minimize presumed opportunistic behavior. Commanders’ intentions behind control activities were mostly driven by concerns about value appropriation by controlees; in this regard, they are similar to Detectives. Commanders adhered to agile values less than the other types of managers and consequently offered the least amount of autonomy and trust to controlees, as illustrated in the following statement from a controlee:

#9: We [agile team members] are KPI [Key Performance Indicator] driven. An analyst’s KPI is to get sign-off in time, it happened that the sign-off was received, but the requirement was wrong. Some part missing, or there are issues that would be difficult to test. … I need to make sure that every party understands where we are going and make sure that everyone is on board.

Like Detectives, Commanders relied on formal controls, but behavior control was more common in their portfolio. They not only set goals for the team and for individual team members, which we classified as outcome controls, they also attempted to prescribe how these goals should be achieved. They exhibited a topdown, authoritative approach to control, with little room for explanations and feedback. Commanders also adopted agile processes and tools and not only participated in agile meetings but often had a leading role in them. They not only read but also edited sprint and product backlogs.

Commanders performed control under difficult conditions. They faced pressure from senior IT management, complained about their lack of formal authority, and reported human resource allocation conflicts, as highlighted by the following quote:

#9: With the new organization I lose the sense of pride and patience towards “how are you doing?” Too much focus on how we are supposed to deliver from the Leadership. And we are losing a lot of control inside the team.

Similar to Buddies, Commanders wanted to be informed about everything. They believed that the more informed they were, the better senior IT management would judge them to be. Nevertheless, they were mistrustful of the controls enforced from above (e.g. performance indicators for controlees) or from peers (sharing resources with other Scrum projects). They also assumed that reporting individuallevel performance would cause controlees to care more about their own performance than about the project. Trust in their controlees was low both in terms of their goodwill and their competencies. They believed they had the technical expertise to observe, evaluate, and then enforce compliant behavior.

## 4.5 Control Ambidexterity

In the case organization, agile project managers faced five main types of conflicts as different manifestations of the control vs. autonomy spectrum and resolved these by different approaches to control ambidexterity (Table 6). We classified the conflicts into three categories (as per Table 4) reflecting control-trust, efficiencycommitment, and stability-flexibility tensions consistent with prior work (Gregory & Keil, 2014). Resolutions manifested through control ambidexterity within and across aPM styles in three key ways: (a) balanced practices, (b) blending aPM styles, and (c) shifting aPM styles (temporarily or permanently). First, balanced practices (Ramesh et al., 2012) refer to control mechanisms that provide structure and clear guidance through explicit prescriptions yet firmly building on trust and team autonomy. We found three examples of balanced practices: monitoring with belief, top-down but temporary coordination, and control delegation.

Second, blending aPM styles refers to applying different aPM styles to different controlees based on certain conditions. Third, we observed that the primary aPM style could be replaced for the whole team and project, which could be temporary or permanent.

## 4.5.1 Control-Trust Conflicts

Outcome tracking vs. team development: The first type of control-trust conflict occurred because of the simultaneous need for tracking project progress, and developing, facilitating, and educating the team in agile practices. These conflicts were experienced by Landscapers, Buddies, and to a lesser extent by Detectives. The conflict originated from these aPM styles being characterized by low IS knowledge, and that Landscapers and Buddies preferring informal control modes. As one Landscaper said, he was “trying to build an environment where self-control can mature” (#11). However, due to pressure from senior IT management, tracking project progress was also necessary to “reach my objectives, reach company objectives, and stay true to company culture” (#11). To do so, checking work outcomes with outcome control was necessary and required higher IS knowledge than what they possessed to understand and verify the accuracy of the information they were receiving. To resolve this type of conflict, balanced practices (a) were used, reflecting monitoring with beliefs. Specifically, the idea was to ask for updates on progress in an agile project management tracking software despite the lack of technical skills and visibility to verify any given inputs. As agile project managers were aware that this control approach could be misperceived as surveillance, they introduced a counterbalance by emphasizing that its aim was to protect the team from being overloaded, as illustrated below:

#20: The primary purpose [of using a project management software] in, in my view, is to knowing the capacity of the team, make sure that what you take during the sprint is not more than what can be developed. So making sure that the team is not burdened. Second, to be able to track progress better. And to flag any challenges along the way easier.

This practice is balancing in that it is a formal control (outcome control) that is also trust-based and enacted in an enabling control style for value-creation purposes, resulting in a mix of preventive and corrective elements.

Reporting vs. team development: The second type of control-trust conflict was experienced by Buddies and Detectives and occurred as a tension between reporting requirements and the above-mentioned goal of team development. Reporting requirements by senior IT management were incompatible with their low IS knowledge, enabling control style, and, in the case of

Buddies, with dominant clan control. In case of delays and budget overruns, senior IT management put pressure on these agile project managers by frequently asking for project status reports and explanations for these problems. As one Buddy put it, “controlling others was kind of saving my ass” (#1). As another Buddy expressed:

## #10: I am accountable for their expectations. Towards my boss. And so I need to make sure that … say, we follow some processes.

The issue is that their low IS knowledge made it difficult to fulfill reporting requirements, as they could not fully understand and verify the accuracy of the information that senior IT management was asking for. For Buddies, the difficulties also stemmed from their desire for clan control, which was enacted to develop shared norms in the team rather than to check and share work outcomes. Moreover, the preferred enabling control style of Buddies and Detectives was less suited to enforcing the compliant controlee behavior that these difficult situations required.

Buddies resolved this conflict by blending (b) the primary aPM styles with a secondary style (Commander) for certain controlees in less technical roles, such as Scrum master and business analyst, and only when the work experience of the agile project manager and controlee were at least equally matched. In these cases, Buddies balanced their usual people strategies with strict guidance on how to perform tasks much like Commanders. As a business analyst for a Buddy who had previously worked in the same role highlighted:

#14: He [his agile project manager] seems to be such a friendly guy! Smiling and caring. … With everyone he has weekly one-on-one meetings; I guess also to show care and develop personal connection. But the funny thing is that at these one-on-ones, at least with me, actually … he tells me I should do this and that in this and that way. And then, at the performance evaluation, he thinks negative if I didn’t do what he suggested.

When these two rather contrasting aPM styles were blended, (1) clan control coupled with outcome control was blended with behavior and outcome control, (2) the enabling control style was blended with an authoritative control style, and (3) the value-creation control purpose was blended with the valueappropriation control purpose.

Detectives’ solution to slow deliveries and the resulting increase in reporting requirements from senior IT management was to temporarily shift (c) to the Commander aPM style and use this style for a short time. As this quote from a business analyst illustrates:

#13: We had a situation when [agile project manager’s name] said the Steering Committee would like to understand why the development takes so much time. He knew already why we were slow, and he understood and accepted the reasons; still, it felt like something broke in him, from this moment, he became a micromanager. … From this moment, he wanted to tell me every aspect of how to work. It was for a week or two; then, he became normal again.

This shift involved a reconfiguration of controls to primarily enact behavior control, reduce outcome control, and switch from an enabling to an authoritative control style while emphasizing preventive control practices.

Team direction vs. team autonomy: The third type of control-trust conflict occurred due to tensions between providing team direction and allowing for team autonomy. This conflict applied to Detectives. Providing team direction involves goal-setting and guiding behavior coupled with sanctions and rewards, Team autonomy refers to an empowered, self-managing team that has significant autonomy in setting and reaching goals without a need for rewards or sanctions. As expressed by a Detective:

## #2: Their personal kind of performance is done through the appraisal system. OK, so of course if somebody doesn’t perform and they do a lot of mistakes, it will reflect in how they go ahead in the company.

The source of the conflict was that Detectives’ primary purpose of control was to prevent value appropriation because they believed that team members might put their own interests before project interests. This is well aligned with the strategy of inducing desired behavior using carrots and sticks. However, satisfactory project performance transformed the image of team members, who were increasingly seen as trustworthy and selfsacrificial rather than self-interested. Thus, the individual reward system, uncommon in agile development, was gradually abandoned. Consequently, team members were granted more autonomy in decision-making, and performance evaluations were elevated to the team level. These initiatives constitute a permanent shift (c) to the Landscaper aPM style. The key mechanism was an increase in competence-based trust that convinced Detectives that they should be be less involved in the everyday life of the team. This shift was embodied by a more significant reliance on selfcontrol enacted in an authoritative control style and a change from the value-appropriation control purpose to the value-creation control purpose. This quote from an agile project manager illustrates this shift:

#21: When I joined the team, … I was suspicious about everything and everybody so that we develop something that meets expectations. When results came, I realized it is because of my amazing team, … so by now, we reached the point when I think the best I can do is to let them work and give them as much freedom as possible.

## 4.5.2 Efficiency-Commitment Conflicts

Top-down coordination vs. team self-organization: The fourth type of conflict relates to tensions between providing top-down team coordination and ensuring the team’s self-organization. Top-down coordination refers to distributing work and managing team members’ responsibilities and timelines. Team selforganization refers to allowing the team to distribute work and manage responsibilities and timelines. The conflict originated from the fact that given the strict adherence to agile values, Landscapers did not treat top-down coordination as their responsibility. In contrast to the other aPM styles, Landscapers did not coordinate project resources or the release process; they sought to minimize the use of their formal authority and empower team members instead. Nevertheless, the stakeholder and project context sometimes required more involvement in coordination than what came naturally to them. Two such situations occurred frequently.

First, rivalry and conflicts of interest were common among developers. For example, developers wanted to work on tasks leading to professional growth but not all tasks were suitable for this. Landscapers questioned whether these developers were acting as collectivistic stewards, as they believed that such behavior is primarily motivated by self-interest, even though individual professional growth is, of course, also important for Landscapers. These situations might have best been handled through behavior control enacted in an authoritative style; however, this would have been in conflict with Landscapers’ strict adherence to agile values. To make sure that “things [were] on track on the development side” (#6.) they relied on a balanced practice (a) to resolve such conflicts. They applied the balanced practice of topdown but temporary coordination, emphasizing that their involvement in team coordination was exceptional, for the purpose of restoring agility only. The balancing aspect was emphasized in their use of formal behavior control in an enabling style in a corrective fashion for a very limited, value-creating purpose—namely, restoring the autonomy of a certain developer in alignment with the agile philosophy. This is illustrated below:

#7: I want to give room and way, empowerment [to team members] to choose how to deliver, I see myself as a referee; if there is a conflict, let’s try to understand where the autonomy of a team member is going into the autonomy of the other person. It’s a tricky balance.

Second, it was difficult for Landscapers to keep track of responsibility areas among developers because they wanted to give the team autonomy to decide how to share responsibilities and because their low IS knowledge made it difficult for them to help coordinate work. However, they wanted to make sure that the development took place as per guidelines calling for formal controls that strictly describe expectations. Another balanced practice (a) was used to resolve this conflict, which we call control delegation—namely, appointing a lead developer as shown below:

#6. So this person is the single role to make sure that all the development is being done in accordance to … as per the guidelines, and things are on track on the development side. So in the same sense as you have a responsible on business analysis, responsible on architecture and one responsible on testing, you would have a single responsible on development.

By handing over control to a key member of the development team, Landscapers minimized team coordination and communication efforts and avoided top-down controls and unilateral decision-making. At the same time, tracking development brought discipline and made measuring progress easier.

## 4.5.3 Stability-Flexibility Conflicts

Team colocation vs. team distribution: The fifth type of conflict occurred due to tensions between team colocation and team distribution. Team colocation refers to each team member, including the agile project manager, working from the same geographical location. Team distribution refers to contexts where one or more of the team members, including the agile project manager, work from different geographical locations. Independent of the chosen aPM style, agile project managers preferred the colocation of team members because “They start developing their own way of thinking. If they are not sitting together they won’t know exactly what purpose they are working on” (#12). The source of the conflict was that the clan control preferred by Buddies and the behavior control favored by Commanders were less effective in project contexts characterized by low behavior observability due to geographically remote team members. Partly or fully virtual teams were common for cost savings or personal reasons.

Buddies applied blending (b) with a secondary aPM style (Detective) when the required socialization could not take place. In such situations, socialization was limited to calls and online meetings and agile project managers could not share the common vision, norms, and values that colocation facilitates. Buddies compensated for the inefficient clan-building opportunities by becoming Detectives in online meetings and phone calls with remote controlees, during which they looked for behavioral cues and made judgments. In terms of control approaches, with remote controlees, clan control was less articulated and was mostly replaced with outcome control. Their dominant preventive control practice was blended with corrective elements. There was no change in their enabling control style. The following quote illustrates how the two aPM styles worked together:

#3: I’ve found that I tend to reduce the amount of meetings with the apps that I sit next to the people working on them. In particular, one of them, I'm thinking of the two developers that I work with, they are based in Bangalore. So I try to have a catch up with them every day. Usually every day at least four times a week for kind of daily stand ups. I found the timeframe comes down a lot if you're in co-located teams. Because everyone knows the issues that have gone on ‘cause you sit next to each other. With remote developers I rely much more on statuses in Jira, little signs in meetings if everything goes fine.

Commanders blended (b) their primary aPM style with that of Detectives because of remote controlees. In these situations, behavior observability was lower than it was with physically present controlees, and their preferred control mode, behavior control, became less effective. With remote controlees, outcome control became more important and was enacted in an enabling control style. In addition, their preventive control practices were blended with corrective ones. The quote below is from an internal team member who worked in the same office as his agile project manager regarding a project with developers who are outsourced from a vendor and work remotely at the vendor’s site. As the quote shows, the agile project manager applied command-and-control style guidance with internal employees but not with the vendor’s employees, where outcome control is more pronounced.

#17: I tell him [his agile project manager] some problems, he replies, “I think you should solve it by so and so”. And I’m like, “No, it makes no sense”. But if he still says so, I would do it so and so. People’s way of approaching a problem is different, and he feels his way is better, just like I do. I mean he has the knowledge; he was a business analyst earlier too. With developers from the vendor, it’s different. So, if a developer tells something took two weeks to develop, he just has to accept it and cannot challenge that, so, for developers, he monitors the status of tasks instead.

## 5 Discussion and Contributions

Since the publication of the “Agile Manifesto” (Beck et al., 2001), significant advances have been made in the management of conflicting control and autonomy demands in agile ISD (e.g. Binci et al., 2023; Horlach & Drechsler, 2020; Iivari, 2021). The manifesto introduced key values and principles, including an “either/or” approach to agile ISD (Beck et al., 2001). As the authors noted: “While there is value in the items on the right, we value the items on the left more.” Instead, paradoxical perspectives in agile ISD have been established in the above studies due to a “both/and” approach (Lewis et al., 2002) to values and principles, which, however, can lead to tensions (Gregory & Keil, 2014). This section develops propositions to discuss the main factors involved in the complex interplay between control conflicts, their underlying forces, and the way that agile project managers respond through control ambidexterity. We conclude the section by deriving implications for theory and practice.

## 5.1 Agile Project Managers’ Control Ambidexterity in Agile ISD

Three different types of control conflicts stand out. We use these as the focal points to theorize on how Commanders, Detectives, Buddies, and Landscapers used control ambidexterity to resolve these agile ISD conflicts (see Figure 4).

First, certain conflicts occured due to hierarchical pressures external to the project interacting with the low IS knowledge of managers, which applied to all aPM styles with the exception of Commanders. Second, interpersonal conflicts occurred in settings where managers needed to account for increased trustworthiness and the work coordination issues of team members. Third, structural conflicts arose from the nature of geographically distributed work and its low behavior observability. Next, we discuss conflicts within each of these categories and develop research propositions capturing the main factors of interest.

## 5.1.1 Hierarchical Forces

Pressure from senior IT management: One of the main issues originated from senior IT management setting objectives for agile project managers that necessitated project outcome tracking and demanded frequent reporting, especially for underperforming projects. This interference created pressure and required continuous follow-up and communication about task statuses. The respective conflicts (1,2) involved agile project managers with diverse expertise areas, including Landscapers, Buddies, and Detectives, who lacked technical or business domain backgrounds. While diverse expertise areas among project managers are common in agile ISD (Highsmith, 2002), senior management pressure further reinforced these conflicts in many cases. These conflicts highlight the challenges of perceiving agile methodology as a philosophy (Zaitsev et al., 2018) of “being agile” (Denning, 2018) in contexts with rigid boundaries and strict objectives imposed by senior management where control-trust conflicts may arise (Gregory & Keil, 2014). Prior research has identified similar challenges during the adoption of agile methods involving customer collaboration (Ciriello et al., 2022). This study highlights that ISD teams and their customers have mutual influence on each other’s agile adoption. In other words, the agility of ISD teams depends on the agility of their customers, i.e. their level of trust in the ISD team, and on the extent to which they also engage in flexible collaborative routines.

The first conflict was successfully managed through a balanced practice (monitoring with belief), allowing Landscapers and Buddies to maintain their agile mindset, foster participation, and adhere to agile values and guidelines consistent with the collaborative management style reported in related work (Gregory & Keil, 2014). When projects were underperforming, the pressure from senior IT management further increased, which required different practices. In response, Buddies blended their aPM styles with the more authoritative Commander aPM style, while Detectives temporarily shifted to the Commander aPM style. Interestingly, even with these style changes, agile processes and tools were still heavily adopted. In theoretical terms, both conflicts demonstrate that control within agile ISD can be impacted by forces outside the ISD project and across hierarchical levels (i.e., from senior managers to agile project managers). Whereas prior research has reported on control emulation, reflecting the extent to which subordinates reuse the portfolio of control modes employed by superiors (Heumann et al., 2015), we observed style changes. By setting targets and requiring performance tracking (thereby enacting outcome control), senior management prompted different forms of control ambidexterity at the project level, which, in cases of underperforming projects, resulted in more dominant behavioral control (exercised through Commanders). Although extant research (Gregory & Keil, 2014; Syed et al., 2021) has examined control ambidexterity at the project level, a comprehensive understanding of control ambidexterity requires the consideration of the entire project hierarchy. Therefore, we offer:

Proposition 1: Pressure from senior IT management to not uphold agile values combined with the low IS knowledge of project managers prompts a balanced control approach among Landscapers, Buddies, and Detectives, emphasizing trust over behavioral control. In cases of underperforming projects, this hierarchical pressure further expands behavior control, leading Buddies to continuously blend with the Commander style and Detectives to temporarily shift to the Commander style.

## 5.1.2 Interpersonal Forces

Trustworthiness improvements of team member(s): Detectives experienced situations where they, as principals, favored an agency relationship, whereas agents leaned toward a stewardship relationship (third conflict), which corresponds to scenarios described by Davis et al. (1997). These situations are disadvantageous for both parties: They may lead to unnecessary costs in terms of monitoring, sanctioning, and rewarding for controllers, while leading controlees to experience diminished feelings of self-worth, self-responsibility, and self-control. However, as trust increased due to shared successes, Detectives began assuming more of a stewardship role rather than functioning as agents. This transformation led to a shift toward adopting the Landscaper aPM style, aligning control with agile values and integrating trust-based controls. It is noteworthy that this specific social conflict related to control vs. trust was only observed in instances of high-performing projects and lacked notable pressure from higher management to set targets or enforce performance tracking. Fulfilled expectations triggering the use of trust-based control were also noted in the context of ISD offshoring projects by Gregory et al. (2013a), who also reported multiple iterations between controls aimed at minimizing risks and trust-based controls. Although we did not observe a reversion to the Detective style, i.e. an agency relationship, in our specific case, it is likely that the response by the agile project manager might also involve iterative back-and-forth dynamics. Therefore, we suggest:

Proposition 2: As trust develops through shared successes in the absence of hierarchical pressure from senior IT management, Detectives begin to behave more like Landscapers, thereby acting as stewards instead of principals, which allows them to adhere more strictly to agile principles and extend trust-based controls, relying, in particular, on self-control.

Work coordination issues between team members: Another conflict arose from the dilemma between team self-organization and team coordination. Assertive developers prioritizing individual tasks over collective goals caused interpersonal conflicts (Barki & Hartwick, 2001). These disagreements clashed with managers’ desire to uphold the agile value of team selforganization. To address this conflict, Landscapers employed behavior control through a balanced practice referred to as top-down but temporary coordination, which involved sporadic behavior control in an enabling style for value-creation purposes. While Ramesh et al. (2012) observed similar balanced practices, in their study it, was enacted by a client manager to control agile ISD on the vendor side. Another resolution tactic was “control delegation,” where someone else on the team adopted the coordination role, mitigating challenges stemming from limited IS knowledge and allowing managers to uphold agile values without direct involvement. These situations underscore the importance of work coordination and decision-making in agile ISD. This aspect is often overlooked in agile literature due to the absence of a project manager role in agile ISD frameworks (Shastri et al., 2021) and the assumption of skilled and self-organizing team members (Hoda & Murugesan, 2016), which would suggest that team members themselves are handling these activities. However, if an agile team includes a project manager aligned with the agile ethos, such as a Landscaper, tensions may arise regarding responsibility for these activities. Therefore, we offer:

Proposition 3: When agile project managers try to adhere to agile values, interpersonal conflicts prompt Landscapers, who are characterized by their preference for team self-organization and limited IS knowledge, to employ balanced practices, which incorporate sporadic behavioral control and control delegation and enhance team coordination.

## 5.1.3 Structural Forces

Limited closeness of remote team members: Lastly, conflicts arose due to the preference of agile project managers for working in colocated teams. However, many teams included remote developers, creating challenges in communication and control ambidexterity. In the fifth conflict, the limited closeness of remote team members diminished the observability of behavior, rendering neither the clanbuilding approach favored by Buddies nor the authoritative behavior control implemented by Commanders suitable. As a solution, a hybrid approach was adopted, combining the primary style with elements of the Detective aPM style when collaborating with remote team members. The need for ambidextrous controls in distributed settings was already noted (Persson et al., 2012). Our results extend this finding by highlighting the most important characteristics of agile project managers that allow for the attainment of control ambidexterity in these contexts. Thus, we offer:

Proposition 4: In order to compensate for the limited capacity to observe the behavior of remote team members, Buddies’ clan control and Commanders authoritative behavior blend with the Detective style to improve outcome control.

## 5.2 Theoretical Contributions and Implications

This study offers three main theoretical contributions that address our two research questions concerning how control ambidexterity can be achieved in agile ISD project settings and how agile project managers dynamically adapt controls to changing contextual requirements.

The first contribution extends the knowledge of control ambidexterity by explaining the control responses that reflect different forms of contextual and temporal control ambidexterity. We offer propositions that highlight the complex interplay between situational forces, aPM styles, and their ambidextrous responses. Contextual control ambidexterity (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004) is reflected by balanced practices and the blending of aPM styles, showcasing the ability of agile project managers to address conflicting demands simultaneously. Prior research has noted that balanced practices can be explained by the distributed nature of the projects (Ramesh et al., 2012). In our study, however, these practices extend further and serve as strategies to safeguard control intentions without the need to shift or blend with another aPM style. The existence of balanced practices in our study implies that “both/and” control logics can be enacted through distinct control mechanisms and do not necessarily require two agile project managers operating under different aPM styles. Blending a primary with a secondary aPM style also supports contextual control-mode ambidexterity (e.g. Kirsch, 1996) and control-style ambidexterity (Syed et al., 2021) while explaining the conditions under which control purposes coexist (Wiener et al., 2019).

Additionally, the two shifts in aPM styles exemplify temporal control-mode ambidexterity, identified in both internal ISD (Kirsch, 2004) and outsourced ISD (Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003) contexts and temporal control-style ambidexterity, which have thus far only been documented in outsourced settings (Gregory et al., 2013a). Finally, we found no evidence of structural ambidexterity (Ramesh et al., 2012). While this finding might simply be a consequence of our sampling (see limitations), contextual ambidexterity may be more efficient than structural ambidexterity, which is associated with higher coordination costs (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004). There is also evidence that senior management should encourage agile project managers to adopt balanced practices that can be used in the same project rather than structurally separating these practices (Ramesh et al., 2012).

The second important theoretical contribution aims to advance our understanding of management styles applied in agile ISD projects (Gregory & Keil, 2014; Syed et al., 2021; Shastri et al., 2021, etc.). Such projects require agile project managers who can carefully navigate the two conflicting demands of control and autonomy, i.e., to establish an appropriate structure, ensure efficiency, and promote productivity while concurrently allowing for team autonomy (Moe et al., 2021). By identifying four agile-specific project management styles in the form of metaphors (Alvesson & Spicer, 2010) we provide a rich account of the mode of thinking and behavior of these styles (Shastri et al., 2016, 2021) as well as their approach to controlling ambidexterity (Gregory & Keil, 2014; Syed et al., 2021). For example, Landscapers used balanced practices to navigate conflicts and adhered to agile values and guidelines without resorting to alternative aPM styles—even if external pressure from senior IT management and internal context factors (coordination requirements) suggested switching to another aPM style. Buddies encountered conflicts arising from clashes between their commitment to agile values and the strong external pressure for control imposed by senior IT management. They adopted a hybrid approach combining their primary style with elements of the Detective style (outcome control). In contrast, Detectives dealt with conflicts between their flexible stance on agile principles and contextual changes (external, social) by applying balanced practices with an openness toward temporarily (Commander) or permanently (Landscaper) shifting to another aPM style. Finally, Commanders only engaged in blending their aPM style with the Detective style when their preferred mode of control (behavior control) did not work; otherwise, they orchestrated control using a topdown, authoritative approach and often deviated from standard agile practices by imposing their own rules.

Although the idea of certain aPM styles being constrained in their exercise of control could be further explored (e.g., this could be due to the adaptability of such aPM styles, with Commanders being the least adaptable), we leave this important question for future research, which should also control for psychosocial variables related to these styles. Another interesting research avenue would be to explore the limitations of aPM styles in their control activities by collecting and analyzing data on controlees’ and controllers’ emotions and applying the so-called emotion-centered model of IS project control dynamics (Murungi et al., 2019). According to this model, controllers’ activities trigger socioemotional responses from controlees that influence their behavior. In turn, this produces emotions on the controller side that impact their subsequent control activities.

An important theoretical implication of this analysis relates to the question of control emulation (Ouchi, 1978), which describes the degree to which team leaders reuse the control portfolio of their superiors (Heumann et al., 2015, p. 347). For example, Commanders—unlike Landscapers—faced pressure from senior IT management to control and experienced conflict in sharing human resources (e.g. shared enterprise architects) with other agile project managers. As a result, their decreasing autonomy led to the implementation of a “tighter” and more topdown control regime that decreased the autonomy of their teams. Similarly, Landscapers who were granted autonomy by their superiors (senior managers) also granted autonomy to their respective teams. We can see similar emulation processes happening in agile settings, with its strong focus on informal controls and team autonomy rather than (formal) management control (Highsmith & Cockburn, 2001). In other words, in order to fully understand control choices within the project, we need to extend the narrow perspective on internal control to external control existing outside the project (Wiener et al., 2017).

Finally, adding to Wiener et al. (2019), with our third contribution, we offer a nuanced understanding of contextual factors that clarify when agile project managers act as principals (Bosse & Phillips, 2016), stewards (Davis et al., 1997) or signal receivers (Virag et al., 2021, 2022). While prior research on organizational control has so far been dominated by agency theory (Cardinal et al., 2017; Wiener et al., 2019), the agency perspective seems less fitting in agile ISD—only Commanders seem to follow a clear agency logic. Instead, we found that a broader range of control activities are supported by stewardship and signaling theory. For example, in our study, agile project managers often assumed that highly committed, intrinsically motivated stewards possessed motives that were aligned with project interests (Wiener et al., 2019). Based on the Buddy or Landscaper styles, trust was developed in the control dyads, a known characteristic of stewardship relationships (Sundaramurthy & Lewis, 2003). The Detective control mechanisms also served as means to scan for and receive behavioral cues from controlees, which is more aligned with signaling theory. In this case, Detectives speculated that signals could have only been produced by controlees with high levels of competency and compliance with expectations. Detectives also acted as signal receivers in agile events using agile tools and relied on signaling mechanisms, which caused them to assume that controlees with high levels of competency and compliance with expectations would voluntarily disclose private information about themselves and the work they perform. Therefore, a key theoretical implication is that to further develop the understanding of controlling agile IS development projects, researchers need to look beyond agency and stewardship theories. Signaling theory may provide a useful lens.

## 5.3 Practical Implications

The findings of this study also have practical implications for agile project managers seeking to finetune their controls. First, we highlight that it is possible for a single manager to balance each aspect of control, thereby confirming the case of Gregory et al. (2013a), in which authoritative control was followed by trustbased control, which differed in all examined control characteristics. In our case, such a radical shift was exemplified by the Detective-to-Landscaper shift, as it constituted shifts in control modes, control styles, and control purposes. Moreover, we demonstrated that agile project managers also opted for partial control ambidexterity when certain control aspects remained unchanged. For instance, the Buddy-Detective blend of aPM styles was consistent in terms of exerting control in an enabling control style.

Considering Landscapers, our results also imply that when an agile project manager provides multiple control approaches, there is less of a need to blend or shift to other aPM styles. While Landscapers had a dominant approach to control, we also found traces of very different approaches (e.g. behavior control enacted in an enabling control style). This suggests that seeking control balance may not always involve a different aPM style. Nevertheless, it is also possible that Landscapers’ projects simply did not require a higher level of ambidexterity.

## 6 Limitations, Future Research and Conclusion

Four limitations and related future research paths are particularly worth mentioning. First, although we also included controlees as data sources, they were also used as proxy subjects (Nederhof, 1985). However, controlees may have different opinions on the aPM style and controls enacted by their superiors (Walser et al., 2021). Future research should therefore pay particular attention to the controlees’ perspective on aPM styles. Relatedly, although in the examined agile projects, it was clear who the controller was (the agile project manager), Landscapers were hesitant to say that they were in control because this sounded negative to them. In addition, some agile teams do not have a project manager (Digital.ai, 2020). In such projects, control is usually attributed to Scrum masters and product owners (Shastri et al., 2021); it would be useful to know which aPM styles these roles can take. Second, we admit that the lack of structural ambidexterity in our data might be a consequence of our sampling strategy. For example, while senior IT project managers had multiple Scrum teams allowing for the observation of structural ambidexterity, only a fraction of our data sources had this job position. Future research could address this limitation by using a more homogeneous sample in terms of job roles, paying special attention to source data from agile project managers who have more than one agile development team.

Third, we were unable to provide details on the extent to which signaling can replace agency-oriented monitoring. Future research could investigate the Detective aPM style while examining the conditions under which signaling can fully replace agencyoriented monitoring. This approach could thereby examine the extent to which signaling theory can explain control activities in ISD projects.

Fourth, we did not consider the costs that were accumulated and distributed using different aPM styles. Agile project managers might speculate about these issues before choosing a certain aPM style. Future research could pay particular attention to signaling because the costs of signaling are primarily borne by controlees (Connelly et al., 2011). In contrast, agency-oriented monitoring is also associated with reduced intrinsic motivations to perform well (Deci et al., 2017). Relatedly, while it was not the purpose of this study, an important future research avenue would be to examine how the identified aPM styles affect project outcomes.

To conclude, our ambidextrous perspective on aPM styles, control conflicts, and resolution strategies is sensitive to tensions that may manifest on the control vs. autonomy spectrum and need to be handled by agile project managers. In comparison to our main reference studies (Gregory & Keil, 2014; Syed et al., 2021), we offer a more fine-grained analysis of management styles (here called aPM styles), control ambidexterity in the service of conflict resolution, and antecedents of control ambidexterity. By highlighting specific factors and their interplay—such as IS knowledge, behavior observability, and control pressure from the top—we show how a single agile project manager can attain control ambidexterity. This is accomplished by effectively balancing control elements within an aPM style or by blending different aPM styles or switching to another style. Addressing tensions in agile ISD and utilizing their forces in a constructive way is important not only for agile ISD but also for scaling agility across an organization (Horlach & Drechsler, 2020; Limaj & Bernroider, 2022), which points toward other important avenues for future research.

## About the Authors

Peter Virag is an assistant professor of MIS at the Institute of Information Management and Control at WU Vienna in Austria. He received his PhD degree in economics and social sciences at the same university. In his PhD dissertation, he focused on the role of information asymmetry, signaling, and control ambidexterity in agile information systems development projects. He has published his research in the proceedings of leading IS conferences, such as ECIS and AMCIS. Due to his previous work in the IT departments of multinational enterprises, he also developed a strong interest in examining how employees interact in IT projects.

Edward W. N. Bernroider is a professor of MIS and Head of the Institute of Information Management and Control at WU Vienna in Austria. His research centers on current issues concerning IT governance, digital transformation, and information security management, and how these can impact individuals, organizations, and entire societies. He has engaged in a variety of educational programs, international consultancies, and advisory activities for commercial and nonprofit enterprises, and has co-organized and presented his work at numerous international conferences. Many of his publications have appeared in top rated IS and OR journals. He currently serves on the editorial board of two leading IS journals and is a regular member of academic and professional bodies.

Ulrich Remus is a professor and head of the Department of Information Systems, Production and Logistic Management at the University of Innsbruck (Austria). His research focuses on IS project management and control, algorithmic management, and negative consequences of IS, and has appeared in leading journals, such as MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, Information Systems Journal, Journal of Information Technology and European Journal of Information Systems. He regularly serves as a track chair and associate editor for major IS conferences and is on the editorial board of Information & Management.
