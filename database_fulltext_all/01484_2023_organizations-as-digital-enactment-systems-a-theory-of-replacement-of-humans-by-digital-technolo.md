# Organizations as Digital Enactment Systems: A Theory of Replacement of Humans by Digital Technologies in Organizational Scanning, Interpretation, and Learning

Ioanna Constantiou,<sup>1</sup> Mayur Joshi,<sup>2</sup> Marta Stelmaszak<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Copenhagen Business School, Denmark, ic.digi@cbs.dk <sup>2</sup>Telfer School of Management, University of Ottawa, Canada, mayur.joshi@telfer.uottawa.ca <sup>3</sup>The School of Business, Portland State University, USA, stmarta@pdx.edu

## Abstract

Digital transformation has become a dominant phenomenon of interest among information systems scholars. To account for this phenomenon, it is imperative to develop a theoretical understanding of its processes and objects. We adapt a seminal organizational theory that conceptualizes organizations as interpretation systems to a possible future of organizations. We theorize digital transformation as a progressive replacement of humans by digital technologies in performing an organization’s fundamental activities underpinning the processes of scanning, interpretation, and learning that encompass an organization’s interaction with its environment. As a result, organizations cease to be human interpretation systems and instead turn into digital enactment systems, where digita technologies, instead of humans, nearly autonomously create and act upon information. We illustrate this digital transformation theory using the example of high-frequency trading. This transformation redefines the relationship among organizations, information, and the environment, changing the role of humans and reshaping strategic decision-making. Thus conceived, digital transformation offers a concrete way of theorizing and accounts for deep implications on the nature of organizations and organizing in the digital age.

Keywords: Digital Transformation, Digital Enactment, Organizational Theory, Interpretation Systems

M. Lynne Markus and Frantz Rowe were the accepting senior editors. This research article was submitted on September 1, 2021 and underwent four revisions. This paper is part of the Special Issue on Envisioning Digital Transformation: Advancing Theoretical Diversity. All authors contributed equally and are listed alphabetically.

## 1 Introduction

Over the last few decades, organizations have been undergoing large-scale digital transformation (DT), which has attracted interest in research (Appio et al., 2021; Markus & Rowe, 2021; Wessel et al., 2021) and practice (as highlighted by Chanias et al., 2019; Hanelt et al., 2021; Hess et al., 2016). While there are divergent views on what DT actually entails, there is broad agreement that significant shifts are emanating from the spread and implementation of digital technologies (Appio et al., 2021; Hanelt et al., 2021; Vial, 2019), such as artificial intelligence (AI), machine learning (ML), and other data-intensive technologies (Gregory et al., 2022) that are often characterized as autonomous and intelligent tools (Berente et al., 2021; Kreps et al., 2020; Markus & Rowe, 2018). Most DT research focuses on tracing and understanding its impact on various aspects of organizations, from value propositions and business models (Baiyere et al., 2020) to processes and structures (Agarwal et al., 2010; Drechsler et al., 2020; Wimelius et al., 2021) and products and services (Vial, 2019).

Yet some information systems (IS) scholars have argued that DT may be fueling more fundamental transformations of organizations than the planned changes (Baskerville et al., 2020). While organizational transformations are, by definition, not captured in standalone projects literature, even planned implementations such as enterprise systems projects can trigger transformation (e.g., mergers and acquisitions—Cao et al., 2022). More importantly, DT represents a phenomenon of “deep-seated changes occurring at multiple levels” (Appio et al., 2021, p. 4) resulting in transformations of “almost all aspects of an organization’s inner and outer environment” (Chanias et al., 2019, p. 17). DT takes place in a dynamic context of uncertainty and constant change (Baiyere et al., 2020).

An illustrative example of DT is high-frequency trading (HFT), where organizations use specialized algorithms and high-speed, sophisticated computer systems to conduct trading (Borch, 2016; Marti et al. 2023). HFT organizations are a part of the market that has traditionally been at the forefront of technologyenabled transformation and were among the first adopters of innovations. As such, they provide for a robust historical context against which DT can be studied (see Appendix A for a detailed analysis). Unlike their predecessors, HFT organizations do not rely on humans directly observing, learning from, and acting upon information. Instead, these activities are largely performed by ensembles of complex digital technologies that replace humans.

With such organizations constantly progressing on the trajectory of DT, an ongoing challenge for IS scholars involves developing concrete theoretical accounts of these deep-seated changes (Markus & Rowe, 2023). One way to do this is to engage with seminal theories and “update them to inform our analysis of twentyfirst-century models of business organization and society, and … be open to developing entirely new theoretical perspectives to augment them” (Baum & Haveman, 2020, p. 269). We do so by drawing on a tradition of organizational theory that considers human information processing, sensemaking, decisionmaking, and learning as the core of organizations (Cyert & March, 1963; Daft & Weick, 1984; March & Simon, 1958; Weick, 2006; Weick et al., 2005). Specifically, we engage with Daft and Weick’s (1984, p. 285) conceptualization of organizations as interpretation systems, which are “highly specialized information receptors that interact with the environment” and obtain, filter, and process information to make choices in “a central nervous system” made up of humans. In such a vision of organizations, humans serve a fundamental role in organizing, as they collectively gather information about the organizational environment, give meaning to this information, and take action through the processes of scanning, interpretation, and learning, respectively. We adapt Daft and Weick’s model (1984) by revisiting some of its underlying assumptions in the wake of the wide-scale DT of organizational life (Baskerville et al., 2020; Markus & Rowe, 2018).

Our aim in this paper is to propose a theory of DT as a progressive replacement of humans by digital technologies in performing organizations’ fundamental activities that constitute the processes of scanning, interpretation, and learning. We explain how the three processes become integrated into one, resulting in organizations interacting with environments in a single mode: digital enactment. In this mode, information about the organization’s environment is not merely interpreted but instead brought into being by digital technologies. We propose a theory of DT that helps unpack the nature of the phenomenon and its profound impact on organizations while placing digital technologies at the center of DT. Overall, we contribute by responding to calls for a new understanding of the organization itself (Majchrzak et al., 2016). In line with this objective, we highlight the change in the role of humans and the locus of their expertise, the nature of strategic decision-making, and the relationship between organizations and information—in particular, when information surrounding firms could have some consequences on their environments.

## 2 Theoretical Background

## 2.1 Organizations as Interpretation Systems

The conceptualization of organizations as interpretation systems (Daft & Weick, 1984) focuses on organizations’ interactions with their environments. “Organizations must make interpretations” (Daft & Weick, 1984, p. 286) of information about both their internal and external environments (hereafter referred to as information). In this view, engaging with environments through scanning, interpreting, and learning from and acting on information are the foundations of organizing: organizations need to understand their environments and react accordingly. Daft and Weick (1984) proposed a model (henceforth the D&W model) of organizations as interpretation systems that theorizes the nature of—and relationships among—the key organizational processes of scanning, interpretation, and learning (Figure 1).

The D&W model is a seminal organizational theory<sup>1</sup> that offers a great starting point to systematically theorize about DT for several reasons. First, it brings into focus the relationship between organizations and (internal and external) information about their environments. While most IS scholars who have engaged with organizations as interpretation systems have drawn from this theory and used it as a lens (e.g., Chiasson & Davidson, 2005; Fayard et al., 2016; Park et al., 2020; Vandenbosch & Higgins, 1996), IS scholars are equally positioned to adapt and contribute to this theory by conceptualizing organizational interpretation in the digital age.

Second, the D&W model recognizes the role of information technology (IT) in the processes of scanning, interpretation, and learning. While humans used to play a key role in these processes and employed various IT in doing so, as acknowledged by the D&W model, these processes are now mediated by digital technologies in many ways to the point that “organizations can no longer be framed as separate from the technologies they deploy” (Alaimo & Kallinikos, 2021, p. 1385). Therefore, there is an opportunity to reexamine the role of technology in organizational interpretation. Third, the preponderance of information and its production makes the D&W model even more relevant: there is more information circulating now than in 1984; consequently, there is a greater need to interpret it. While humans are still involved in scanning, interpretation, and learning in organizations, digital technologies affect the interpretative capacity of organizations. In the following, we first discuss the conventional perspectives on scanning, interpretation, and learning (Section 2.1.1), and then explore the modes of interpretation and underlying assumptions (Section 2.1.2) inherent in the D&W model.

## 2.1.1 Organizational Scanning, Interpretation, and Learning

Scanning: Organizations are “consumers, managers, and purveyors of information. Rules of gathering, storing, communicating, and using information are essential elements of organizational operating procedures” (Feldman & March, 1981, p. 171). Scanning “is the means through which top managers perceive external events and trends” (Daft et al., 1988, p. 123). It is considered to be one of the key elements of organizational behavior (Pfeffer & Salancik, 1978) and is usually considered an antecedent to interpretation and action (Hambrick, 1982). Organizations obtain, filter, and process data and information about the external world to inform choices that are made by managers. Data can be acquired by employees in dedicated departments formally, or informally by senior managers and frontline employees of business units. Senior managers typically have access to more information than they can actually use (Mintzberg, 1973) and are hence selective in focusing on specific aspects of the environment (Ocasio, 1997) based on data collected by other employees. These employees may use technology for collecting data but retain real-time control of what is scanned and how.

Interpretation: Interpretation can be described as “the process of translating events and developing shared understanding and conceptual schemes among members of upper management” (Daft & Weick, 1984, p. 286). During the process of interpretation, data are given meaning by engaging human minds: “Perceptions are shared and cognitive maps are constructed,” and an “information coalition of sorts is formed” (Daft &

Weick, 1984, p. 286). In other words, individuals, including frontline employees and senior managers, play a key role in processing information and arriving at a convergent interpretation (Turner & Makhija, 2012). Interpretation is often considered to be an integral part of sensemaking (Weick, 1979); as such, it serves to reduce equivocality. Equivocality implies “multiplicity of meaning conveyed by information about organizational activities” (Daft & Macintosh, 1981, p. 211). The means of equivocality reduction are dependent on the nature of data sources and data acquisition mechanisms employed in the scanning process. For instance, data gathered through personal sources and informal mechanisms might need more equivocality reduction than data obtained through impersonal sources through formal mechanisms.

Learning: Organizations act upon the scanned and interpreted information through learning, thereby putting their cognitive theories into action (Argyris & Schon, 1978). Learning can be described as “the acquisition of new knowledge by actors who are able and willing to apply that knowledge in making decisions or influencing others in the organization” (Miller, 1996, p. 486). In other words, learning is, in part, a change in behavior in response to a stimulus (Cyert & March, 1963) as well as a conscious acquisition of knowledge (Huber, 1991) by organizational members. Learning also provides new information for scanning and interpretation, thereby forming a feedback loop (Daft & Weick, 1984). Organizational learning relies on “the ability of humans to develop diverse patterns of action by actively engaging their environments and applying substantive rationality” (Balasubramanian & Ye, 2021, p. 2). The way in which organizations learn is driven by the motivations, perceptions, and experiences of organizational members developed as they respond to information about environments (Balasubramanian & Ye, 2021; Mahoney, 1995; Simon, 1947).

It is worth emphasizing that employees engage in activities that support the processes of scanning, interpretation, and learning (as evident in the D&W model). At the same time, in line with Daft and Weick (1984), we place these organizational processes at the senior management level. Daft and Weick (1984, p. 285) argue that “many participants may play some part in scanning or data processing. The point at which information converges and is interpreted for organization level action is assumed to be at the top of manager level.” Thus, everyone in an organization partakes in some activities of data collection, giving meaning, or taking action; however, scanning, interpretation, and learning by senior managers are the most significant activities for organizations as interpretation systems. Therefore, we distinguish between the underlying activities supporting scanning, interpretation, and learning enacted by all (mostly frontline) employees, and the processes of scanning, interpretation, and learning embodied in senior managers’ cognition and action.

## 2.1.2 Modes of Interpretation and Underlying Assumptions

The D&W model posits that organizations adopt idiosyncratic configurations of scanning, interpretation, and learning depending on senior managers’ perceptions about the analyzability of the environment and the extent of their intrusiveness, forming a two-by-two matrix. Accordingly, organizations engage in one of the four interpretation modes based on the environment’s perceived analyzability and the organization’s intrusiveness: enacting (unanalyzable, active), discovering (analyzable, active), conditioned viewing (analyzable, passive), and undirected viewing (unanalyzable, passive). Each of the modes has consequences for organizational strategy and decision-making. For example, an organization adopting undirected viewing will adopt the reactor strategy, which is “not really a strategy at all,” as the organization passively accepts the environment (Daft & Weick, 1984, p. 292), while the defender strategy espoused by organizations in conditioned viewing relies on top management protecting the organization’s position. In the discovering mode, the analyzer’s strategy relies on maintaining core activities with peripheral innovations, whereas in the enacting mode, the prospector’s strategy relies on a high level of initiative from management toward the environment that is perceived as constantly changing and full of opportunities to develop new products and launch new initiatives (Daft & Weick, 1984).

At the core of these four modes of organizational interpretation are several assumptions that Daft and Weick (1984) made about organizations and their environments. First, organizations are conceived as open social systems that need to collect and process information about environments to reduce uncertainty and deal with equivocality. Organizations do so by developing specific information processing mechanisms enacted by humans with or without the use of technology. Second, organizations interpret by achieving convergence among the interpreting members. The members converge on approximate interpretations by sharing information, which is preserved by organizations in the form of knowledge, behaviors, mental maps, norms, and values. Third, strategic-level senior managers are the key members of organizations who formulate the interpretation. Many members may play a small role in specific activities, and senior managers play a key role in converging information and formulating the organizational interpretation that is required for strategic action. Fourth, organizational members’ perceptions about the analyzability of the environment and an organization’s intrusiveness are the most salient for dictating the mode of interpretation. All four modes assume that the environment is an independent, separate set of dimensions (which organizational members perceive). We will return to these assumptions and discuss the need to revisit them in the context of DT.

## 2.2 The D&W Model and HFT Organizations

The D&W model is widely applicable to organizations across industries. For instance, consider traditional and IT-enabled trading organizations in financial markets: human traders bid on a pit (activities constituting learning and action) and physically communicate with one another in a room or use telephones or computer systems (activities constituting interpretation) to obtain recent price updates (activities constituting scanning). As such, these organizations’ strategic decisions mainly focus on improving the organizational capability to make more profitable trades and outcompeting other organizations. Senior managers (trading firms’ directors) draw heavily on their interactions with frontline employees (traders in this case) and middle managers (trader managers) in the processes of scanning, interpretation, and learning, which has implications for their strategy and decisionmaking. Thus far, the underlying assumptions of the D&W model seem to hold.

However, the application of the model becomes less straightforward in digitally transformed organizations. HFT organizations offer a good illustration. They differ from their traditional counterparts in many ways, with a key distinction at the core: many activities and operational decisions that used to be performed by human traders are now performed by digital technologies, which, in turn, influence how senior managers interact with information in making strategic decisions about the organization’s competitive strategies. Complex systems of AI- and ML-based trading algorithms have largely assumed the activities of collecting data, as well as processing, analyzing, and interpreting them, in order to execute high volumes of transactions in short amounts of time (MacKenzie, 2019). Information about the market, such as prices and order volumes, is collected and processed by HFT algorithms that then decide on the next trades (Cooper et al., 2017). Human traders play no role in the activities of collecting or interpreting this information nor do they make operational decisions about trades (Hansen, 2020; Willems & Hafermalz, 2021). Instead, a limited number of humans focus on decisions regarding how to optimize the algorithms’ speed and their errorless functioning with the help of ML developers and data scientists (Borch et al., 2015; Hansen, 2020). However, strategic decisions in HFT organizations are still in human hands. Senior managers in charge of organizing, such as trading firm directors, perform the organizational processes of scanning, interpretation, and learning, and are consequently still responsible for strategic decisions, such as the design and choice of algorithmic strategies, which equipment to use, and the location of computing centers. These processes, however, have undergone a fundamental transformation. See Appendix A for a detailed account of this transformation.

The replacement of humans by digital technologies and the resulting transformation of the fundamental organizational processes call for revisiting the assumptions underlying the D&W model. At the core of all the model assumptions is the active role of humans in performing key activities underpinning the scanning, interpretation, and learning processes. For instance, the first assumption conceptualizes organizations as open social systems where humans collect and process information from the environment. However, given that algorithms, rather than humans, are performing these activities by collecting information from the environment and analyzing it based on predefined statistical models, this assumption needs to be updated.

The second assumption underlines the importance of achieving convergence among organizational members in the processes of interpretation. Given that algorithms, instead of humans, are responsible for the key activities underpinning the processes of interpretation, and interpretation and meaning tend to be precoded in algorithms, there is no scope for such convergence (MacKenzie, 2018a). In other words, convergence is predetermined ex ante, instead of in real time or ex post. Often, scanning and interpreting activities are performed by an integrated system of algorithms, thereby alleviating the diversity of human interpretations that calls for convergence in traditional, pre-DT settings.

The third assumption—that strategic-level organizational members play a key role in the interpretation system— may still hold. However, unlike in traditional settings, algorithms come with precoded interpretations, posing challenges for senior managers seeking to influence interpretation on a real-time basis. Any change in interpretation needs to be implemented in algorithms, necessitating that senior managers also understand precoded interpretations (Hansen, 2020). This poses new challenges, especially in the case of ML algorithms (Berente et al., 2021). Consequently, interpretations formed by senior managers are formulated differently.

Finally, the fourth assumption about the relationship between analyzability, intrusiveness, and the mode of interpretation may also need further reflection. Digitally transformed organizations may intrude into their environments not only because they perceive the environment as unanalyzable (as assumed in the D&W model) but also and crucially because digital technologies afford the possibility to intrude. For instance, HFT organizations actively shape their markets not because environments are unanalyzable, but because their algorithms are much better positioned to influence markets, e.g., increase their size by executing high-speed transactions with precision (MacKenzie, 2018b).

The other assertion of the D&W model is that senior managers interpret organizational environments based on limited sources of scarce information that have to be obtained—often painstakingly—by frontline employees. This reflected the observations of how organizations functioned at that time. However, as we illustrate with HFT organizations, the complexity of current organizations, the preponderance of information, and the new ability to process unprecedented amounts of information using digital technologies call for revisiting the conceptualization of organizations as interpretation systems. We do so by developing a theory of DT and offering a new conceptualization of organizations as digital enactment systems.

## 3 Theory of Digital Transformation: From Organizations as Human Interpretation Systems to Digital Enactment Systems

We conceive of DT as a progressive replacement of humans by digital technologies in performing an organization’s fundamental activities that constitute the processes of scanning, interpretation, and learning. During such transformation, the typical sociocognitive processes (Danneels, 2003; Schuetz & Venkatesh, 2020) involving the collective intelligence of organizational members in supporting organizational scanning, interpretation, and learning that shape the organization’s interactions with information about its environment are replaced with digital technologies. Digital technologies are here understood to be technologies capable of replicating these sociocognitive processes—that is, replacing humans in experiencing, learning, and decision-making (Baum & Haveman, 2020; Berente et al., 2021).<sup>2</sup> Such technologies—e.g., sensors, big data generating platforms and social media, analytics systems, or AI and ML algorithms (Appio et al., 2021; Baum & Haveman, 2020; Gregory et al., 2022)— are increasingly replacing human involvement in the fundamental organizing processes leading to digital scanning, digital interpretation, and digital learning.

## 3.1 Digital Transformation of Scanning, Interpretation, and Learning

Digital scanning: Digital scanning is the process of monitoring the environment with the help of sensors (Monteiro & Parmiggiani, 2019; Wessel et al., 2021) and related digital technologies that facilitate the collection of a variety of big data, including behavioral traces and social media interactions. Sensors generate digital representations of physical (or other digital) objects, properties, and processes and, as such, replace human sensory capacity (Grønsund & Aanestad, 2020; Monteiro & Parmiggiani, 2019). Scanning the environment becomes driven by models and data based on algorithms and visualization, leading to the emergence of synthetic knowledge (Monteiro & Parmiggiani, 2019). Digital technologies are also instrumental in the autonomous collection, aggregation, and computation of new types of data (Constantiou & Kallinikos, 2015; Alaimo & Kallinikos, 2019) from the environment, a significant departure from how organizations have traditionally scanned their environments, e.g., by employees who “may acquire data about the environment through personal contacts” (Daft & Weick, 1984, p. 286) or using IS to monitor customer behavior. For example, rather than conducting thorough, theory-driven market research using managers and professionals with expertise in the domain to identify target customers for advertising (which has been the case for many large organizations to date, e.g., incumbent banks), organizations are now gaining insights about their existing and prospective customers by collecting big data about them, e.g., behavioral traces collected from social media platforms (Alaimo & Kallinikos, 2018, 2022). In some cases, entirely artificial, synthetic data are generated by AI systems based on previously collected historical data (Floridi, 2019). Overall, in digital scanning, the core activities of collecting information from the environment are performed by digital technologies instead of frontline employees.

In this new form of digital scanning, there is a very limited scope for human attentiveness, ingenuity, or personal contact in collecting information. However, while frontline employees are being replaced by digital technologies, there are other employees who are still playing some role in the activities of scanning, albeit indirectly. These include, for example, developers and data scientists—while they contribute indirectly to the activities of digital scanning, they tend to be much smaller in number compared to the employees involved in traditional scanning activities. Other frontline employees may still be involved in digital scanning—for example, in some HFT organizations as part of event-driven trading, where traders detect corporate events that influence trading (Zaharudin et al., 2022). However, HFT organizations are increasingly using digital technologies to capture possible information about events from various sources, including the automated scanning of social media (e.g., Zhou et al., 2021), thus replacing human traders even in event-driven trading. These organizations may still host a limited number of human traders, but instead of engaging in the activities of scanning themselves, they serve as domain experts to help data scientists and developers build technologies. Senior managers are still responsible for the overall process of scanning, but their role becomes redefined toward envisioning all the possible scenarios for which they may want to scan the environment ahead of time before the scanning activities can be performed by digital technologies.

This transition from human scanning to digital scanning entails collecting information from the environment based on preprogrammed rules encoded in algorithms, which targets aspects of information that are codifiable and simultaneously moves attention away from those aspects of the environment that are not codifiable. On the one hand, this transformation increases the efficiency and precision with which information can be collected from predetermined sources, allowing the organization to consistently respond to repetitive environmental changes. On the other hand, it narrows data collection to information that can be captured via digital technologies, reducing the capacity to capture new, unexpected Information for interpretation. In digital scanning, senior managers’ key task becomes engaging in strategic foresight about which parts of the environment are important to target and thus codify. However, they need to develop specific expertise in identifying the aspects of the environment to systematically target, given that the parts of the environment that are not targeted do not get analyzed.

Digital interpretation: The process of interpretation i.e., “translating events and developing shared understanding and conceptual schemes among members of upper management” (Daft & Weick, 1984, p. 286), whereby data are given meaning, is also being transformed toward digital interpretation. Digital technologies give meaning to data by autonomously uncovering patterns, traits, and regularities. Once the data are collected through digital (or even traditional) scanning, they are increasingly interpreted by ML systems instead of being interpreted by employees in traditional interpretation, thus practically eliminating the human role in activities that contribute to the overall process of interpretation. In the underlying activities of digital interpretation, data are given meaning not by humans but by algorithms, removing the opportunity to communicate, create shared understandings, and develop “information coalitions,” as formulated by Daft and Weick (1984). For example, algorithms used for predictive purposes in advertising reveal statistical correlations between the user context—i.e., physical and/or online location and time of the day—and the user behavior, which the organization can use to strategically introduce programmatic advertising (Alaimo & Kallinikos, 2018) instead of advertising based on demographics. In this case, algorithms not only monitor “the reality” of user behavior to provide input to advertising tools but they also create a digital representation of that reality and heavily impact the tools of digital advertising. In these processes, there is little, if no room for human interpretation.

The interpretations from these tools come preprogrammed and with a limited scope. Like in scanning, here, a different set of humans, e.g., ML developers, also plays a role in precoding interpretations in the algorithms. Such transformation limits the scope for both making novel meanings out of scanned information and infusing human reasoning in interpretations. Senior managers receive pre-interpreted insights generated by algorithms (e.g., visualization tools) instead of the collective interpretations of employees. In turn, such visualization tools can help senior managers resolve uncertainties but may be less apt at navigating ambiguous situations. In fact, such ambiguous situations may not even form part of the visualizations that signify agreed-upon “universal truth,” offering little scope for alternative “truth,” a lens through which senior managers view the world in the case of digital interpretation. Senior managers need to think about what to include in such visual representations on an ongoing basis.

Such a transformation comes with benefits for organizations that can uncover previously unnoticed patterns and eliminate perceived human differences and inefficiencies in interpreting information. For example, HFT organizations use scalping as an approach to making very small profits from price differences occurring between two trades in extremely short time periods (Beverungen & Lange, 2018). HFT scalping relies on algorithms (instead of human traders in traditional settings) giving meaning to trading data by uncovering trade patterns and predicting whether the price will go up or down. Thus, such algorithms autonomously interpret whether a given trade can lead to a profit or a loss but senior managers decide on the actual placement or co-location of hardware and servers close to the exchange sites, which, in turn, influences the speed of information and thus the outcome of scalping strategies (Beverungen & Lange, 2018).

Digital learning: Digital technologies are also transforming the process of learning from interpretations and taking actions based on them. Digital learning executed by various autonomous, AI-based systems for problem-solving and decision-making marks a shift away from developing knowledge within organizations by “putting cognitive theories [of managers] into action” (Daft & Weick, 1984, p. 286) toward “data-powered algorithms” that allow “automation of cognitive, discretionary, and decision-making tasks that humans used to perform” (Grønsund & Aanestad, 2020, p. 1; see also Berente et al., 2021; Sturm et al., 2021; Willems & Hafermalz, 2021). These tools foster the transition from human intuition to reliance on computational, analytical, and so-called rational solutions partly based on the expertise (and hunches) of data scientists and developers of digital technologies, and partly based on what the algorithms themselves learn from the previous iterations of analysis. As a result, digital technologies “can reduce an organization’s demand for human explorative learning that is aimed at uncovering new ideas” (Sturm et al., 2021, p. 1581). Within these autonomous decision-making and acting systems, information about environments becomes a set of variables in models that can be changed or acted upon. In turn, algorithms learn from the interpretations of the outcomes of their earlier actions and accordingly take corrective actions. Such digital learning may seem to be a bias-free approach to learning, as human intuition no longer forms part of it. However, there is increasing evidence of algorithmic bias in various contexts (Kordzadeh & Ghasemaghaei, 2021; Marjanovic et al., 2021). These biases are conditional upon digital scanning and digital interpretation that form part of learning and emanate from the cognitive abilities of the developers of the algorithms that dictate problem formulations and create boundaries for algorithms to learn from.

Digital technologies limit the involvement of humans in acting upon information, and thus replace them in decision-making and taking action (Balasubramanian & Ye, 2021; Berente et al., 2021; Willems & Hafermalz, 2021). Digital learning, then, may bring organizations more efficiency and speed in decision-making, but removing employees from taking action increases the reliance of an organization on digital technologies in putting the learning into practice. Digital technologies that facilitate learning can be rule-based, where the rules are fully precoded in the algorithms by the developers, or they can be ML-based, where the developers define the boundaries but the exact learning and actions are dependent on feedback from previous actions. In either case, senior managers’ involvement in learning requires them to think ahead about the potential range of learning and action that can be programmed into digital technologies, driving them toward the need to imagine the consequences of digital learning with a limited ability to intervene on a real-time basis. In the HFT example, trading algorithms represent a source of competitive advantage but require senior managers to make certain trade-offs. Algorithms that need more computational cycles to execute an order are relatively slower than those that require fewer computation cycles and can be the first to execute an order. Shortening the algorithms’ cycles reduces the number of instructions, the information processing capacity, and thus the ability of the algorithm to learn and be better able to identify profitable opportunities and avoid losses (Ladley, 2020). Despite competition to be the first by being the fastest, HFT organizations use a variety of algorithms with different levels of sophistication: short cycle algorithms making large numbers of low-profit trades, and more complex algorithms identifying a smaller number of high-profit trades (Ladley, 2020); traditionally, such trading decisions would be made by traders. This approach to managing the trade-offs exemplifies the transformation inherent in digital learning and emphasizes the exclusion of frontline employees from the activities of learning while retaining the more strategic role of senior managers.

Taken together, the substitution of humans by digital technologies in activities that support the core organizational processes limits the organization’s ability to function as a system of interpretation. For example, while the transition from human scanning to digital scanning may increase the efficiency and precision of collecting information from the environment, digital scanning reduces the organization’s capacity to capture new, unexpected information feeding organizational interpretation. Similarly, while the transition from human interpretation to digital interpretation may contribute to uncovering new patterns and eliminating bias, digital interpretation constrains the range of possible interpretations of the environment to those preprogrammed in digital technologies, thereby making it less informed by shared understandings and perceptions. Finally, while replacing human learning with digital learning may improve the efficiency and speed of decision-making, digital learning reduces the organization’s ability to envision and take actions in an environment that is treated as a malleable and changeable variable by digital technologies. To summarize:

Proposition 1a: The digital transformation of the processes of scanning, interpretation, and learning through the replacement of humans by digital technologies in underlying activities may increase the efficiency and precision of these processes.

Conversely,

Proposition 1b: The digital transformation of the processes of scanning, interpretation, and learning through the replacement of humans by digital technologies in underlying activities may constrain the organization’s ability to function as a system of interpretation governed by humans.

Different organizations are likely to be on different trajectories of DT operating in their idiosyncratic environments. Thus, the exact implications of Propositions 1a and 1b for a specific organization would be conditional on other factors, e.g., environmental dynamism (Davis et al., 2009). Considering trading organizations, their environments can be characterized by high velocity and high unpredictability. Assuming that DT increases the extent of structure, HFT organizations may be able to leverage high velocity in acting faster, as more human involvement in these processes can actually slow down organizational action and reaction time, but they may not be well equipped to manage unpredictability due to the structure that digital technologies impose. On the other hand, traditional banking and trading organizations may be able to better manage unpredictability due to humans’ ability to adapt and flexibly engage in real-time sensemaking, though they may not be able to cope with high velocity.

## 3.2 Tight Coupling of Digital Scanning, Digital Interpretation, and Digital Learning

One could argue that digital scanning, digital interpretation, and digital learning build on one another and result in a comprehensive and progressive DT. In other words, replacing humans in some of the activities underpinning key organizational processes often entails the introduction of digital technologies in other activities and processes. Our theory of DT delves into one such possibility where an organization achieves full digital scanning, digital interpretation, and digital learning. In such a scenario, the replacement of humans in the activities contributing to the three processes results in their ultimate collapse into one. The underlying activities are often performed by an ensemble of digital technologies that cannot function without tight integration with each other, and they often function autonomously. When all key activities underpinning the processes of scanning, interpretation, and learning are replaced by an ensemble of digital technologies, such tight coupling at the activity level also reflects at the process level. Digital technologies at the core of DT become increasingly enmeshed with each other and collapse the previously separate processes of scanning, interpretation, and learning into one that cannot be easily distinguished, taken apart, or intervened in by senior managers.

In the thus-conceived DT, the three processes of scanning, interpreting, or learning no longer function in isolation. That is, even if digital scanning was in place but interpretation remained a human endeavor, the difficulty of analyzing big data by humans would render it futile due to the sheer amount of data generated; thus, digital scanning almost immediately calls for digital interpretation. Similarly, when digital technologies offer interpretations of information based on their computational reasoning, such interpretations may be too complex or not transparent or explainable enough for humans to be able to engage in learning and take action; in such cases, organizations swiftly implement digital learning where decisions are made by digital technologies. Conversely, most incumbent firms that experiment with ML algorithms start with implementing digital learning in making decisions and then require larger amounts of data that are usually obtained through digital scanning that needs to be implemented. This integration results from the fact that the various elements of digital technologies that support DT are designed and programmed to work together, and their inputs and outputs are configured to flow according to preestablished rules, entailing a progressive loss of sovereignty by humans as technologies gain autonomy (Kreps et al., 2020). In the HFT example, a small change in how a specific algorithm operates usually entails a reconfiguration of the whole system, as various algorithms feed their inputs and outputs following strict, preprogrammed operations (Hansen, 2020). Hence, HFT traders spend most of their time coding such small changes and ensuring that algorithms still function as required; their actions are bound by what such technologies dictate (Borch, 2016; Hansen, 2020; MacKenzie, 2019).

This integration heralds numerous benefits for organizations setting out on the course toward DT. For one, an ensemble of digital technologies performing all the activities underpinning the fundamental processes of organizing eliminates the need for employees to do so. Second, such a single activity is optimized and may seem more controllable, as the digital technologies involved are preprogrammed and predesigned to operate in specific ways. Third, some may argue that there might be less scope for errors or omissions. However, the emergence of such tightly coupled activities performed largely autonomously by digital technologies makes it difficult for senior managers to engage in organizational scanning, interpretation, and learning as distinguishable processes: data that are collected, given meaning, and acted upon are nearly impossible to tell apart, thus making it challenging to engage in these processes. A minor failure to spot one aspect in a process (e.g., identifying a specific dimension of the environment at the expense of another while scanning) can have far-reaching implications for strategic decision-making due to consequences to other processes (e.g., inability to interpret and learn anything about the specific dimension of the environment, which could be a small but important part of the environment). To summarize:

Proposition 2a: The digital transformation of the processes of scanning, interpretation, and learning leads to their collapse into a tightly integrated process performed by an ensemble of digital technologies, leading to efficiency and performance gains.

## Conversely,

Proposition 2b: The digital transformation of the processes of scanning, interpretation, and learning leads to their collapse into a tightly integrated process performed by an ensemble of digital technologies, making it challenging for senior managers to engage with each process separately and thereby changing their role in formulating organizational interpretations.

Returning to environmental dynamism in relation to Propositions 1a and 1b (Davis et al., 2009), banking and trading organizations can be considered to be operating in environments characterized by not only high velocity and high unpredictability but also high complexity. HFT organizations may be better equipped to handle complexities due to the information processing and computational capabilities afforded by digital technologies. However, since these environments are also unpredictable and volatile, the complexities can also change. Algorithms need to be constantly retuned to capture new complexities. Accordingly, senior managers in such cases are tasked with constantly engaging in strategic foresight in an attempt to predict the unpredictable and thereby prepare the digital infrastructure to capture the unforeseen. Alternatively, in the case of traditional trading organizations, senior managers endeavor to maintain loose coupling by relying on humans to accommodate the requisite variety and unpredictable demands. These organizations may take longer to respond and may thus not be able to leverage the high velocity of environments.

## 3.3 Digital Enactment

The tight integration of the underlying activities of the core organizational processes enabled by digital technologies may lead to digital enactment. In this mode, organizations no longer merely interpret but rather construct information about their environments. This construction is executed nearly autonomously by an ensemble of digital technologies engaged in a perpetual loop of tightly coupled digital scanning, digital interpreting, and digital learning. We depict the changes to scanning, interpreting, and learning constituting digital enactment in Figure 2.

We argue that a digitally transformed organization interacts with the environment in the digital enactment mode in the scenario of integrated processes. In the original formulation, the D&W model conceptualized four distinct modes of organizing based on organizations’ relationship with the environment across two dimensions: organizational members’ perceptions about the analyzability of the environment and the organization’s intrusiveness into the environment. As such, perceptions about the environment together with organizations’ intrusiveness dictated organizations modes of interpretation. Digital technologies changed these dynamics by introducing interdependence between the two dimensions. They uniquely enable intrusiveness because they not only give organizations the ability to conduct formal search in the environment but also offer organizations much more powerful tools to conduct systematic live market experiments. For instance, given the availability of large-scale data that can be collected and synthetically produced from so many new sources, a number of organizations are conducting large-scale experiments (e.g., A/B testing) to influence the behavior of their customers. This is prominent especially where products and services are first (and maybe exclusively) created digitally (Baskerville et al., 2020). As a consequence, organizations also perceive their environments as more analyzable with the help of digital technologies. In other words, unlike the original formulation of the D&W model where perceptions about the analyzability of the environment were causing the organizational mode of interpretation, in the case of digitally transformed organizations, perceptions about the analyzability of the environment become a consequence of digital enactment. This reversal is a fundamental shift in how organizations operate, pushing them into the realm of digital enactment.

Proposition 3: Digital transformation results in organizations ceasing to be human interpretation systems and becoming digital enactment systems, whereby they not only interpret information about their environments but also digitally enact the environments by creating information nearly autonomously.

In digitally transformed organizations, senior managers interaction with information changes. Since most equivocality is either resolved by digital technologies in their ongoing enactment of the environment or does not enter organizational boundaries due to digital technologies targeting specific (and analyzable) cues from the environment, senior managers in such organizations focus less on reducing the equivocality that enters the organization and more on residual equivocality, which is associated with the kind of information that does not systematically enter organizations. As a consequence, when senior managers in digitally transformed organizations sense that some very important information is not entering the organization and realize that the algorithms are behaving as usual despite the lacking information, they are tasked with taking the extreme step of pulling the plug (e.g., Omidvar et al., 2023).

## 4 Discussion and Implications

## 4.1 Theorizing Digital Transformation

In this paper, we propose a theory of DT, which posits a departure from previous conceptualizations of DT (see Appendix B, Table B1 for details) because it emphasizes the reconfiguring of organizations’ fundamental processes of scanning, interpretation, and learning performed by specific and defined types of digital technologies replacing humans. Most current approaches broadly conceptualize DT as a change triggered by an intentional action conceived by, implemented in, and bound to organizational members, but do not specify the nature of the transformation itself (Markus & Rowe, 2021, 2023). We build on a handful of recent studies (Hanelt et al., 2021; Lanamäki et al., 2020; Wessel et al., 2021; Holmström, 2022) that tease out the transformational aspect of DT, conceptualizing transformation as a process that results in “a qualitatively different organization” (Besson & Rowe, 2012, p. 103; see also Appio et al., 2021; Drechsler et al., 2020; Hanelt et al., 2021).

In our theorization, we focus on organizational scanning, interpretation, and learning as the objects of DT. Going back to the understanding of organizations as possessing a “central nervous system” (March & Simon, 1958, p. 9) tasked with obtaining, processing, and deciding upon information about both the external and internal environment, we propose a fundamental reconfiguration of this organizational central nervous system and place autonomous digital technologies squarely at the center. Our theory posits DT as a progressive replacement of humans in performing the activities that make an organization an information receptor and processor, with notable exceptions. Such replacement comes with the introduction of new roles, e.g., data scientists and ML developers, as well as the reconfiguration of existing roles, e.g., traders acting as subject matter experts in helping data scientists, and senior managers engaging in distinct strategies.

We recognize that there are perspectives other than the D&W model that may lead to a somewhat different theory of DT than ours. However, we argue that our theory is consistent with other prevalent views of organizations and organizing. For example, organizations can be viewed as systems (Demetis & Lee, 2016, 2018), and research in organization science, as well as IS, has explored them as complex or complex adaptive systems (Amaral & Uzzi, 2007; Eisenhardt & Piezunka, 2011; Marjanovic & Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2017), also in the financial market (Arena et al., 2020). A comprehensive discussion of systems theory and how it relates to our theory is beyond the scope of this paper, but we focus on one contribution where Demetis and Lee (2018, p. 930) employ systems theoretical principles to conceptualize systems of technology where humans become artifacts placed “out to the environment” where “the environment is what lies outside the boundary of a system.” The authors explain, also using the example of HFT and the flash crash, how the replacement of humans with digital technologies can be viewed from the systems perspective. In doing so, they cast humans out to the environment of a system of technology, which helps in conceptualizing the changing role of human agency in the overall system.

While this characterization by Demetis and Lee (2018) bears similarities<sup>3</sup> and is compatible with our theory of DT, our theory systematically demonstrates the role of information in organizing. Instead of focusing on technology as a system, we look at organizations to better account for how DT changes their nature. At the same time, with the D&W model as our starting point, we are not constrained by the systems theory requirement to separate the system from its environment (Demetis & Lee, 2016) and are thus equipped to discuss how organizations and their environments are intertwined through digital enactment. This is also the case with the complex adaptive systems view of organizations (Marjanovic & Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2017), which acknowledges that organizations are open systems that interact and exchange information with environments in a mutually shaping manner yet still draw boundaries between where organizations end and their external environments begin.

Alternatively, one could view organizations as collectives constituted by underlying sociotechnical practices in line with the practice lens (Orlikowski, 2000), as prevalent in IS, or strategy as practice (Jarzabkowski, 2004), as common in management. IS scholars have recently started conceptualizing the digital transformation of organizing practices in the form of digital practices (e.g., Baiyere et al., 2020; Baiyere and Fuerstenau, 2022). Our theory is compatible with this perspective, where one can view the processes of scanning, interpretation, and learning as constituted by human and digital practices. Our theory provides a higher-level view on such practices to discuss how their DT impacts entire organizations and extends this further by qualifying the specific practices that go into organizational interpretation and interaction with the environment through information. The focus on information and its scanning, interpretation, and learning is fundamental to the D&W model that we use as a starting point.

## 4.2 Relationship among Organizations, Information, and Environments in Digital Enactment

Our study paves the way for redefining the relationship among organizations, information, and environments. The conceptualization of digital enactment stands for organizations constructing their environments through information first, and this construction is executed by ensembles of digital technologies engaged in a perpetual loop of integrated digital scanning, interpreting, and learning performed nearly autonomously of human intervention (Kreps et al., 2020; Markus & Rowe, 2018).

Organizations engaging in digital enactment reverse the relationship between the organization’s actions and the analyzability of the environment. Instead of the perception of analyzability causing an organization’s actions, the organization’s actions enabled by digital technologies influence their perceptions about the analyzability of the environment. This entails a shift in what organizations are and what they do with information. Their raison d’être is no longer to collect, analyze, and act upon information, as these activities are taken care of by digital technologies with very limited if no involvement from humans at all (Markus & Rowe, 2018). Digitally transformed organizations can then become the engines of the ontological reversal (Baskerville et al., 2020) between the physical and the digital world. Such organizations, through digital enactment, create information that forms the basis of phenomena in the digital world and then, nearly autonomously, these digital representations impact, shape, and influence the phenomena in the real world.

The DT of organizations as we conceive it, then, has consequences that reach far beyond organizational boundaries and help to explain the impact of DT beyond organizations that undergo this process. In the example of HFT, financial organizations were probably aiming to adopt advanced digital technologies of collecting and analyzing information to increase their returns from trades but ended up impacting the entire financial market, e.g., through flash crashes that led to market indexes collapsing and rebounding within minutes (Currie & Seddon, 2017; Lindenbaum et al., 2020). In other words, by replacing traders with digital technologies that transform the processes of scanning, interpretation, and learning, trading organizations ceased being systems of interpreting the markets and instead became systems of digitally enacting their environments through the information they produced. Interestingly, when one digitally transformed organization digitally enacts information, that information then becomes the reality and constitutes the environment for other organizations in the market, even if those organizations are not digitally transformed. This helps us understand how the DT of a single organization can rapidly transform an entire market; further, if other organizations continue, at least in part, to try to interpret information about the environment that is actively being remade by one of the players, they may lose market power. The concept of digital enactment provides a detailed explanation of how “information makes reality”—that is, how, exactly, information exceeds virtuality and spills over to material and physical reality (Baskerville et al., 2020, p. 511). It also shows how DT can contribute to the ontological reversal between digital and physical reality and explains the mechanisms behind the “digital technologies shaping our world” (Baskerville et al., 2020, p. 518).

## 4.3 Digital Transformation and the Changing Role of Humans in Organizations

There is an increasing discourse around digital work (Baptista et al., 2020), and the changing nature of work (Barley et al., 2017) amid the large-scale adoption of intelligent technologies (Bailey & Barley, 2020). We offer specific arguments regarding the replacement of frontline employees, the emergence of new occupations and professions that engage in various forms of digital work, and the reconfiguration of work for senior managers.

First, our theory contends that in digitally transformed organizations, most of the activities underlying the processes of scanning, interpretation, and learning are conducted by digital technologies substituting for frontline employees. For instance, in the example of HFT, some organizations are heading toward the far end of DT, with trading algorithms fully replacing traders in performing the activities constituting the processes of scanning, interpretation, and learning (Min & Borch, 2021). It is increasingly common for such organizations to adopt a very flat organizational structure with thin payrolls. This is the case in born-digital firms (Huang et al., 2017) that tend to automate core activities and have a minimal physical presence in markets.

Second, in addition to the substitution and reconfiguration of work for existing frontline employees, DT also entails the emergence of a new breed of professionals playing an active role in organizations. These professionals are typically responsible for developing and supporting digital technologies in making sure that they perform the key activities constituting scanning, interpretation, and learning according to organizational expectations. IS scholars are increasingly examining the work of these professionals in the light of DT (Grønsund & Aanestad, 2020; Parmiggiani et al., 2022; van den Broek et al., 2021). For example, van den Broek and colleagues (2021) reported that, although founded with the objective of digitally transforming hiring decisions (digital learning as per our theory), their focal organization eventually ceased digitally transforming the processes of collecting and recording the data of existing employees (digital scanning) and evaluating the performance of employees (digital interpretation). Taken together, the replacement of humans in performing frontline work and the emergence of a new breed of digital professionals (ML developers in their study) signify the shifting locus of human expertise in digitally transformed organizations. Instead of engaging with the work hands-on, humans are increasingly performing tasks that facilitate and support the work of digital technologies: Human expertise is shifting from the front line to the backrooms (to borrow a phrase from Parmiggiani et al., 2022).

Finally, senior managers tend to retain their position as the leaders and key decision-makers in digitally transformed organizations; however, the nature of their work becomes reconfigured around digital technologies. That is to say, their work loses sovereignty in engaging in organizational interpretation, and the resulting strategic decision-making is dependent on and tightly coupled with autonomous digital technologies (Berente et al., 2021; Kreps et al., 2020; Markus & Rowe, 2018). While senior managers still make strategic decisions, they cannot do so without digital technologies. We discuss this in detail in the next section alongside the strategy and decisionmaking implications of DT.

## 4.4 Strategic Decision-Making in Digitally Transformed Organizations

Digital enactment has profound implications for the organization’s strategy. First, digital scanning enables the creation of representations of specific dimensions of the environment and empowers decision makers in efficiency-centered strategy formulations by increasing information richness and accuracy regarding specific dimensions of the environment, e.g., markets or industries (Iansiti & Lakhani, 2020). Yet digital representations of environments also have a significant influence on search strategy and the opportunities identified in the environment (Gavetti & Rivkin, 2007). The underlying information sensors bring efficiency but also narrow the scope since they build on a selective collection of information from the environment. This leads to a fundamental change in how organizations search the environment and consequently act upon their understanding of it (Gavetti & Rivkin, 2007; Ocasio, 1997). To add to this complexity, the actual effects of digital enactment on the environment cannot be known in advance, they can only be hypothesized. Hence, a digitally transformed organization’s strategy-making involves creativity when choosing the type of data to collect from the environment and designing algorithms for their aggregation and visualization, while envisioning an environment that the organization should digitally enact to meet its objectives.

Second, digital enactment requires senior managers to change the focus of their strategizing. As digitally transformed organizations create information that constitutes their environments, managers need to strategize the production of information, as it digitally enacts the organization’s environment and, consequently, the organization itself. Thus, managers are becoming increasingly concerned with the strategy of information. For example, senior managers at HFT organizations are becoming preoccupied with the location of their data centers to minimize the time it takes for information to travel between the HFT algorithmic computation sites, where trade decisions are made automatically, to the trading sites where these trades are executed (Currie & Seddon, 2017). This shift is a harbinger of changes to the strategic focus and a future need to develop tools for strategizing information.

In digitally transformed organizations, because the processes of digital scanning, digital interpretation, and digital learning have become inseparable and have collapsed into a tightly integrated process, there are limited opportunities for humans to intervene when digital technologies get things wrong at various stages. Instead, organizational members can only act through more dramatic moves to halt digital enactment from causing unwanted changes, highlighting the reduction in human sovereignty and the increase of technology autonomy (Kreps et al., 2020; Markus & Rowe, 2018; Demetis & Lee, 2018). This compresses the responsibility for strategy in ever-more-powerful top executives but, at the same time, it deprives them of the variety of insights about the environment that would have traditionally been obtained, interpreted, and acted upon by a wide range of employees from the front line to senior management. This may narrow the scope of the knowledge and capabilities that are drawn into strategy—in turn, making these executives more dependent on digital technologies.

We argue that strategies are becoming less about ways for organizations to adapt to their environments based on the information collected (Gavetti & Rivkin, 2007; Weick & Daft, 1983) and more about using digital technologies to shape environments, with implications for a variety of players (Cennamo & Santaló, 2019; Panico & Cennamo, 2022; Gavetti et al., 2017). DT at one organization often disrupts entire industries and, in consequence, forces other organizations to move toward digital enactment. This is clear in the accidental 2010 flash crash, where it is now widely accepted that none of the HFT organizations actually intended to bring the market down through digitally enacting it (Currie & Seddon, 2017; Lindenbaum et al., 2020). However, “a wrong trading decision may risk the survival of a financial firm in a split second” (von Krogh, 2018, p. 406). Strategy-making in this context may require developing competencies similar to high-reliability organizations (Weick & Sutcliffe, 2001), where the focus is on how to anticipate and avoid an accident with large spillovers impacting the rest of the environment. Organizations that rely on digital technologies in their core processes need to be able to anticipate accidents in the operation of these technologies that, without human intervention, could escalate into major disasters for the organization and its competitors (Min & Borch, 2021).

## 4.5 Future Research and Practical Implications

The theory we put forward opens avenues for further theoretical development and empirical work. For the definition of DT that we propose, it is important for future research to uncover how the replacement of humans by digital technologies in the activities of scanning, interpretation, and learning unfolds. As such, the process of DT should be traced with a focus on how organizations’ interactions with information from their environments change over time. Further research is also needed to understand the conditions under which organizations can benefit (or not) from this replacement. We summarize other areas that need to be addressed in Table 1 below.

We propose subjects for future theoretical developments, future empirical investigations, and specific research questions corresponding to our propositions (Table 1). More generally, our conceptualization of DT, entailing changes in the very nature of an organization, calls for a revision of current theories assuming that organizations are more like interpretation systems; at the same time, it requires new theorizing around organizations as systems of digital enactment. Future empirical investigations should focus on examining the theory in its ability to distinguish DT from other types of changes induced by digital technologies and evaluate its applicability in wideranging empirical contexts.

Our theory offers several implications for practice and can guide managers embarking on DT as well as organizations already operating in the digital enactment mode. First, managers not only need to focus on changes in operational aspects or business models but also on deeper changes that may result in a reconfiguration of the organization’s core—adding to the list of strategic considerations in formulating a digital strategy (Chanias et al., 2019; Hess et al., 2016). Second, managers must account for the changing relationship of the organization with information and, consequently, its environment. Organizations in digital enactment are no longer merely learning, interpreting, and deciding on information about environments but are actively producing them. Managers need to strategically direct their attention toward information that is created not only by their organization but also by competing organizations in the environment. Finally, digital scanning, interpreting, and learning are conducted by preprogrammed and predefined digital technologies that are far more fixed and integrated than human sociocognitive processes. Therefore, executives need to be actively involved in decisions concerning these digital technologies (Berente et al., 2021), as these are no longer solely operational decisions but can be consequential for the whole organization and its environment.

## 5 Concluding Remarks

We propose a theory of DT that highlights the possibility of a systematic replacement of humans by digital technologies in organizational scanning, interpretation, and learning. Such replacement puts digital technologies in charge of interpreting organizational environments, removing organizational members from this crucial task. DT conceived in this way is highly significant because it may limit or entirely prevent organizational members from recognizing automation biases, noticing environmental shifts, and taking appropriate action. Our theory proposes the possibility of scanning, interpretation, and learning collapsing into one process executed by a tightly coupled ensemble of digital technologies, which should be of prime concern to organizational members as well as scholars. Digitally transformed organizations can leverage the benefits of technological advancements but DT entails a significant change in the relationship between organizations, their environments, and the information that connects the two. Such organizations no longer function as human systems of interpretation but instead become systems of digital enactment that create those very environments, with bits of information serving as building blocks.

## Appendix A: Digital Transformation of Trading Organizations

To elucidate our theory of DT and to demonstrate how organizations as interpretation systems are transformed into digital enactment systems, we discuss in detail an illustrative example of HFT organizations and compare them against analog organizations based on human trading, and IT-enabled trading organizations that deploy IS in a nontransformative manner (see Table A1). HFT organizations are “associated with (1) a specialized form of algorithmic trading, (2) the use of high-speed, sophisticated computer programs and systems, (3) very high order-to-transaction ratios, (4) extremely short average holding periods, and (5) ending the trading day with flat positions” (Zaharudin et al., 2022, p. 101).

In this comparison, we discuss in more depth trading activities in three phases of a trade (Dabous & Rabhi, 2008). During the pre-trade phase, real-time information, such as trades and orders, is collected and correlated with other gathered company announcements, and relevant and historical information, corresponding to the activities supporting organizational scanning. In the trading phase, such information is interpreted to predict price movements up or down and indicate the most appropriate trades, corresponding to the activities of interpretation. The final step in the trading phase and the entire post-trade phase are concerned with executing the trade and learning from the outcomes of actions and decisions, for example, “analyzing past and current market behavior and trading patterns and predicting future behavior” (Dabous & Rabhi, 2008, p. 36), corresponding to the activities in support of organizational learning. We present an overview of changes to these phases against three historical periods that can be distinguished in trading organizations (Arena et al., 2020).

Analog trading organizations: In traditional, analog trading organizations, transactions were conducted bilaterally, typically between banks and other financial services firms via phone or telex (Period 1, Table A1). The transnational network of organizations did not share common information and had dispersed communications. Consequently, human traders had access to limited parts of the environment. In turn, traders were mainly tasked with finding out “where the market was.” Prices differed from place to place and had to be verified for every deal through long and effortful processes of phoning banks and waiting for lines when going through operators for overseas calls (Knorr-Cetina & Bruegger, 2002). Price discovery involved personal information sharing and information gathering from other institutions. Traders interpreted the dispersed information and conducted deals over the phone and telex. Coordination between traders was only feasible in the moments a particular connection via phone was taking place (Knorr-Cetina & Bruegger, 2002).

Stock exchanges were based on an open outcry call market; hosting accredited traders in a physical space offered equal and real-time access to the market’s data (Arena et al., 2020). Information to buy and sell assets came from the floor’s coordination. Traders’ references were mainly based on all the external economic factors able to influence stock values (Arena et al., 2020). Their interpretations were enabled by mathematical models as well as firsthand experience and embodied expertise in closely observing and trading in the market. Despite the prevalence of numerous financial models of optimal bidding, the high degree of equivocality in interpreting scarce market information challenged the traders’ decision-making.

In the overall processes of scanning, interpretation, and learning, senior managers aggregated information scanned by traders through scattered sources and collectively interpreted it based on inputs from traders as well as their own expertise in the domain. They decided on the details of the strategy to improve competitive positioning based on organizational interpretations they formed from direct interactions with human traders involved in the activities supporting organizational interpretation. The key task for human traders and senior managers was to reduce equivocality and information asymmetries. Strategic decisions involved competitive strategies for an information advantage that they built by interacting with traders and closely observing their performance. Hiring involved traders and experts who knew the markets and could bring crucial information to the organization was considered one of the key strategic investments (Knorr-Cetina & Bruegger, 2002). As per the original D&W model, these organizations operated through the mode of undirected viewing as the full environment was vastly unanalyzable and the data collection entailed several personal sources, thus limiting the ability of organizations’ intrusiveness in the environment.

IT-enabled trading organizations: In 1957, the introduction of information technology at stock exchanges enabled the computation of market indexes (e.g., S&P 500) at very short time intervals (Arena et al., 2020). It also allowed the matching of buyers and sellers without location and time constraints and offered the infrastructure for processing large amounts of data and storing historical data. Trading organizations introduced technical analysis enabling feedback trading (Arena et al., 2020). In the 1980s, automation of the stock exchange through electronic transaction processing systems (ETPS) enabled information processing through software programs and computers through electronic access to markets generated and executed large amounts of orders. Computers could be programmed to automatically send large orders when particular market trends prevailed (i.e., program trading) (Arena et al., 2020). Organizations introduced decision support systems for traders using OMS (order management systems), EMS (execution management systems), and transaction cost analysis (TCA).

As of the 1990s, trading organizations began investing in electronic broking services (EBS), which is an example of the deployment of technology that is neither digital nor transformative within our proposed theory (akin to human sovereignty where technology is just an instrument, Markus & Rowe, 2018). The introduction of EBS (Period 2, Table A1) made quotes of bids available at the same time to all traders. Each trader had a number of screens providing information about the market and could communicate with other brokers in the room. On separate screens, traders observed prices contributed by different banks worldwide as well as their current position in the market (e.g., being long or short on particular currencies or stocks), the history of deals made over recent periods, and their overall account balances (profits and losses over relevant periods). The system also provided headline news and economic commentaries (Knorr-Cetina & Bruegger, 2002). The wide range of information provided by the system influenced the human activities supporting scanning and interpreting. Information was systematically organized and sorted based on the price limits of traders on the screens, while the use of the EBS for the deals replaced real-life brokers. Decision-making for a deal involved a choice among predefined available options. Traders interpreted this information based on their experience and expertise while observing the bids they made and also “read” others’ bids, trying to derive knowledge from them. Hence, EBS supported traders’ learning, enabling access to more data and the systematic collection of market information, analysis, and presentation through visualizations that revealed causal relationships between specific bidding strategies and returns.

The rich information available to senior managers changed the focus of trading organizations’ strategies from information gathering to the speed of information processing as a competitive advantage, while strategic investments involved decisions about IT systems that would provide more information to traders (MacKenzie, 2018b). Senior managers decided on the overall strategy based on organizational interpretations they formed while interacting with information systems that stored and represented the outcomes of human involvement in the activities supporting organizational interpretation. For these IT-intensive organizations, scanning involved more accurate risk assessment and less information equivocality using DSS and introducing program trading (Arena et al., 2020). Accordingly, for these organizations, the markets started becoming more analyzable (at least in their perceptions), and they increasingly started collecting systematic information; however, interpretation remained within the boundaries of established data collection procedures, such as documents, reports, publications, and “information systems that have grown up through the years” (Daft & Weick, 1984, p. 298) and thus operated in the conditioned viewing mode (with some active organizations also engaging in discovering mode).

Digitally transformed HFT organizations: HFT organizations (Period 3, Table A1) introduced sophisticated algorithms to increase bidding efficiency. In the early days of HFT organizations, traders became less active in the trading process, sitting isolated from one another in a silent room observing their screens. Silence was only interrupted by a mechanical sound from software simulating the sound of a cashier each time a trader’s algorithm made a profit (Borch et al., 2015). There were two sets of screens. One set of screens presented the electronic limit order book, showing the bid and ask prices. Another set of screens displayed the operations of the trader’s algorithms, coding, and graphs, while some traders used privacy filters on their screens to hide source code (Borch et al., 2015). Eventually, HFT organizations started employing far more developers and data scientists (“quants”) who developed cutting-edge algorithms (Hansen, 2020) and continued automating the trading process further. They reduced their major activities to digital order books i.e., data files, on their trading servers collocated with other servers in the data centers (MacKenzie, 2018b), where transactions are agreed upon among algorithms and take place in nanoseconds (MacKenzie, 2019). HFT organizations use two types of trading algorithms (Min & Borch, 2021). The first type involves ML-based algorithms that autonomously devise their own trading strategies, implement them, and get feedback from the market. In this case, algorithms are given an objective function, representing the bidding strategy and a training dataset to autonomously learn patterns from the data (of pending orders to buy and sell financial products, from the electronic order book). The second type involves human-defined algorithms that are automated, but rule-based. They follow strategies designed by human traders or “quants” but are also automatically implemented in the market. In this case, each part of the trading strategy, i.e., the rules, is conceived of and coded by humans, while training data come from the electronic trading book as well. Trading algorithms monitor price movements as recorded in the electronic order book (the rule is applied to an ongoing flow of incoming data) and determine their action. The algorithms are designed to make decisions based on well-defined rules about the prices of financial products and the orders made and act swiftly to maximize profits (Cooper et al., 2017; MacKenzie, 2018b).

In both cases, trading in these HFT organizations is based on specific parameters of the market which are digitally scanned and interpreted to provide information. Human traders either do not exist or if they do, their role is limited to reviewing the aggregate results of algorithms’ decisions (Hansen, 2020) and focusing on how to optimize algorithm design to make the highest profit, instead of monitoring market trends, analyzing them, and making decisions (Ma & McGroarty, 2017). The smallest changes that need to be implemented in any part of the complex network of HFT algorithms may potentially affect the entire system, as various algorithms feed their inputs and outputs following strict, preprogrammed operations (Hansen, 2020). Traders, in turn, are removed from activities supporting scanning, interpreting, and learning; instead, they are tasked with ensuring that the HFT algorithms create market conditions aligned with the organization’s best interests.

Senior managers do not have a mechanism to consult with traders or observe algorithm performance on a real-time basis; hence their strategy-making is fundamentally different from traditional trading organizations. HFT organizations’ strategies focus on gaining an advantage through transaction efficiency in terms of discovery and speed of reaction to a new fund being available in the market compared to competitors (Hansen, 2020; MacKenzie, 2018b). HFT organizations use three key strategies of statistical arbitrage, directional trading, and market making—detecting and acting on opportunities of arbitrage, and market-making strategies while benefiting from very short-term (i.e., nanoseconds) price differences between financial products (Aldridge, 2013; Zaharudin et al., 2022). HFT organizations invest in developing models that can automate the identification of trade opportunities using algorithms (CTO Tyler Capital<sup>4</sup>). Strategic investments focus on the most sophisticated digital systems and the fastest servers (MacKenzie, 2018b) as well as experts in quantitative research to design unique algorithms. This ability to execute trades in fractions of seconds affords them the possibility to actively intrude in and shape the markets, signifying an enacting mode where the ensemble of algorithms seamlessly performs the activities of scanning, interpretation, and learning in no time, as explained in the case of digital enactment. The three activities of digital scanning, interpretation, and learning are so tightly coupled that they can neither be executed nor amended separately. Senior managers, instead, focus on making strategic decisions regarding the algorithms, such as the trade-off between speed and complexity and the choice of algorithms and digital infrastructure co-location.

Digitally transformed HFT organizations may not be equipped to scan, interpret, and respond to economic shocks, which are typically exogenous and unpredictable and may appear as irregularities to algorithms. Algorithms are not able to interpret the background information of a specific economic event (Borch, 2022). If there is no human intervention, e.g., to interpret an exogenous shock and stop trading, HFT algorithms may continue trading since they interpret the shock as an opportunity to make extra profits from trading (Hansen, 2020). To reduce errors, traders need to constantly monitor algorithms and proactively address external irregularities. However, it should be noted that while algorithms are running, humans refrain from tweaking them (Hansen, 2020). High-frequency traders and senior managers may suspend their algorithms around the announcement of key economic numbers to avoid producing unpredictable market activity, which is deemed too risky to program HFT algorithms to try to profit from it (Borch et al., 2015). The strategic decision in this case entails sanctioning the intervention by humans to interrupt digital enactment to stop algorithms from intensifying trading as a response to an economic announcement that could lead to a market collapse.

## About the Authors

Ioanna Constantiou is a full professor of information systems in the Department of Digitalization at the Copenhagen Business School. She served as the academic director of the CBS’s Business in Society initiative on Digital Transformations. From 2017 to 2020, Ioanna Constantiou was employed, as a professor of information systems in the Department of Applied IT at the University of Gothenburg in Sweden (part-time from July 2017). She received her PhD from the Department of Management Science and Technology, Athens University of Economics and Business in 2003. Her current research work focuses on the impact of digital transformation on traditional industries as well as the impact of digital technologies on everyday life. Her research has been published in academic outlets, including MIS Quarterly, Journal of Information Technology, Journal of Strategic Information Systems, European Journal of Information Systems, Decision Support Systems, and MIS Quarterly Executive. Ioanna Constantiou currently serves as a senior editor for the Journal of Strategic Information Systems and Electronic Markets.

Mayur Joshi is an assistant professor of information systems at the Telfer School of Management at the University of Ottawa. Previously, he was associated with Alliance Manchester Business School at the University of Manchester as an assistant professor of fintech and information systems. He earned his PhD in information systems from Ivey Business School at Western University. His research interests reside at the intersection of information systems and organization theory. An overarching research question he has been trying to unpack is how digital technologies shape and are shaped by the fundamental practices, processes, and strategies of organizing. Previously, his research has appeared in MIT Sloan Management Review and Cambridge Handbook of Qualitative Digital Research. Among other awards, he received an AIS Outreach Practice Publication Award, and his MIT Sloan Management Review article was recognized as one of the top 10 articles for the year. Prior to his career in academia, he worked as a banker for several years and served in various roles concerning technology implementation, process improvement, and branch banking operations.

Marta Stelmaszak is an assistant professor of information systems and the Daimler Truck Professor of Analytics at The School of Business, Portland State University. She holds a PhD from the London School of Economics and Political Science and postgraduate degrees in management, information systems and innovation (also from LSE), and data science (Birkbeck, University of London). Marta’s research interests concern data science and analytics and thei responsible, sustainable, and ethical development and management within organizations. In her work, she combines various conceptual and theoretical approaches to information systems research with quantitative, data-informed, and computational tools with the aim of developing methodological techniques that allow for new insights regarding data for management research and practice. Her research has appeared in Information Systems Research and Harvard Data Science Review, among other outlets. She serves as a reviewer for information systems journals and as an associate editor for leading conferences; she also serves as an early career board member at the Journal of Strategic Information Systems.
