## Abstract

The sharing economy is a defining feature of the millennial generation. We report on a study of 1047 millennials and find that trust has more of an impact on their willingness to engage in some sharing encounters than in others. In particular, trust matters more in high‐dimensionality sharing encounters—those encounters that have a longer duration, require a greater financial investment, involve more social interaction, etc. Further, it is important to note that all sharing encounters take place through two‐sided intermediary platforms, and we find that the role of trust among the different types of sharing partners is not symmetrical across the two sides. Trust in the intermediary platform matters more to customers than to service providers in their willingness to engage in service encounters. Overall, this research contributes to existing literature by developing the notion of a service encounter and associated dimensionality and by demonstrating that service encounter dimensionality affects the importance of trust in the sharing economy. Also, we show how trust matters differently for different sides of two‐sided sharing platforms. Finally, this research focuses squarely on understanding the behaviours of millennials— the generation important to the sharing economy.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Sharing economy business models have become increasingly popular as a promising alternative to traditional modes of service consumption (Hamari, Sjöklint, & Ukkonen, 2016; Hawlitschek, Teubner, & Gimpel, 2016). Particularly for the millennial generation (the generational demographic cohort born in the 1980s and 1990s), the sharing economy is a means to drive costs out of service provisioning, to decrease unsustainable resource consumption, and to improv resource allocation (Andersson, Hialmarsson, & Avital. 2013: Belk, 2014: Botsman & Rogers, 2010: Hamari et al. 2016). More and more organizations attempt to monetize peer‐to‐peer–based sharing on dedicated online platforms (Hamari et al., 2016)—to be like Airbnb or Uber in disintermediating their industries (Abramova, Shavanova, Fuhrer, Krasnova, & Buxmann, 2015; Andersson et al., 2013). For such sharing platforms to be successful, it is critical that consumers have adequate trust in both the platform and the sharing partner (Chen, Zhang, & Xu, 2009; Hawlitschek Teubner, & Weinhardt, 2016; Lu, Zhao, & Wang, 2010). Trust is central to interpersonal and commercial relationships and helps to overcome risk in service encounters (Halliday, 2004: McKnight & Chervany, 2001: Morgan & Hunt 1994; Spake, Beatty, Brockman, & Crutchfield, 2003). The need for trust to establish online relationships has bee extensively elaborated in related online industries, such as e‐commerce (Gefen, 2000; Gefen, Karahanna, & Straub 2003; Sun, 2010) and in the service industry in general (Coulter & Coulter, 2002; Gounaris & Venetis, 2002; Halliday, 2004). In the context of the sharing economy, trust that plays a crucial role is sometimes referred to as the currency of sharing (Botsman & Rogers, 2010; Hawlitschek, Teubner, & Weinhardt, 2016). In this research, we explore th impact of trust in sharing encounters among millennials

Sharing encounters are a special subclass of traditional service encounters in a variety of ways. First, sharing encounters take full advantage of contemporary online platforms and modern information technologies, such as smartphones, apps, and location‐based services to provide individuals with information to enable an optimal use of resources through the mutualization—a type of social encounter system in which the benefits that an individua receives from others do not depend exclusively on the resources provided by that individual (Arnould & Rose 2016: Hwang & Griffiths. 2017), Further, they are typically customer to customer (C2C), rather than business to con: sumer (B2C) (Hawlitschek, Teubner, & Weinhardt, 2016), and involve the temporary sharing of excess capacity (Mittendorf, 2017a; PricewaterhouseCoopers [PwC], 2015; Tussyadiah, 2015). Sharing encounters typically take place through interactions with strangers on short notice (Mittendorf, 2017b; Mittendorf & Ostermann, 2017). But how does trust matter to sharing encounters?

To address this question, we conducted a study of millennials who use sharing platforms as either service providers or customers. Young cost‐conscious millennials are an important demographic for the sharing economy (Hwang & Griffiths. 2017: Mittendorf & Ostermann. 2017: PwC, 2015). Specifically, we theorize about how the “dimensionality” of the sharing encounter will impact the role of trust in service encounters for millennials, By higher dimensionality, we refer to the aspect of sharing encounters that describes their relative scope and complexity— higher‐dimensional sharing encounters require more attention and greater commitment on the part of sharing partners. In this regard, we draw on established studies that assess service encounter dimensionality and extend their logic accordingly. Sharing encounters with higher dimensionality include more social interaction and more financia investment, involve higher control, are more personalized, and last for a greater duration.<sup>1</sup> Also, since sharing encounters occur through two‐sided platforms, we further theorize about how trust matters differently for different participants. Overall, we find that trust indeed matters to service encounters—consistent with previous work on e‐commerce and service provisioning (Gefen, 2000). But we extend this by explicitly theorizing about both the dimensionality of the sharing encounter and the two‐sidedness of the platform. We tested our hypotheses on a sample of millennials that use Airbnb or Uber. We find that trust in the sharing partner matters more for higherdimensionality sharing encounters and that trust in the platform matters more for customers than for service providers. Overall, this research contributes to our understanding of the sharing economy by (1) analysing both the customer and service provider perspectives across sharing economy platforms; by (2) demonstrating that dimension ality moderates the relationship between trust and the users' intention to engage in a sharing encounter; by (3) introducing the concept “sharing encounter” as an encounter between unfamiliar sharing partners initiated on sharing economy platforms; and by (4) providing empirical evidence that the effect of trust in the corresponding sharing part ner on the users' intention to engage in a sharing encounter rises for sharing encounters with higher dimensionality

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. First, we review the related literature on the sharing economy, including the relevant literature on service encounters, millennials, and trust. We then introduce the concept of sharing encounter dimensionality. Then we theorize on trust in sharing encounters and develop two hypotheses into the moderation of the effect of trust on intention to share specific to a sharing encounter context. We then present our research and findings. We conclude by discussing the implications of our findings, limitations, and directions for future research.

## 1.1 | The sharing economy

Sharing practices not only extend current market demand by addressing the needs of potential customers but also compete with existing businesses over available market share (Cusumano, 2014; Zervas, Proserpio, & Byers, 2017). Traditional businesses, which only offer goods and services over commercial channels, are challenged by contempo rary sharing platforms (Hamari et al., 2016). Practices associated with sharing platforms have been described in terms of “access‐based consumption” (Bardhi & Eckhardt, 2012), “collaborative consumption” (Botsman & Rogers, 2010), “commercial sharing systems” (Rose & Lamberton, 2012), “consumer participation” (Fitzsimmons, 1985), “cocreation” (Lanier & Jensen Schau, 2007; Prahalad & Ramaswamy, 2004), “co‐production” (Humphreys & Grayson, 2008), “the mesh” (Gansky, 2010), “online volunteering” (Postigo, 2003), “on‐demand services“(Andersson et al., 2013), “product service systems” (Mont, 2002), “prosumption” (Ritzer & Jurgenson, 2010), and “sharing economy” (Andersson et al., 2013; Hawlitschek, Teubner, & Weinhardt, 2016; Malhotra & Van Alstyne, 2014). The commonality among contem porary sharing practices is the realization of economic, cultural, and organizational benefits that could not be achieved with traditional ownership practices (Hamari et al., 2016). Accordingly, there is an ongoing discourse regard ing an accurate formulation for various types of sharing activities (Aloni, 2016; Arnould & Rose, 2016; Bardhi & Eckhardt, 2012; John, 2013). For the purpose of this paper, we adapt the term “sharing economy,” a two‐sided mar ket model where online intermediaries unlock the value of excess capacity by matching demand and supply in rea time on a global scale (Eisenmann, Parker, & Van Alstyne, 2006; Malhotra & Van Alstyne, 2014). While there is some disagreement about the definition of the sharing economy (eg, Arnould & Rose, 2016; Ertz, Durif, & Arcand, 2016b), we do not intend to get into this discussion but instead propose a general, neutral definition that captures the com mon elements of those definitions and the sharing economy. In this regard, we understand resources as persona assets that can be in the form of property and time investment. Resource in this sense is of monetary value for othe individuals. Thus, we define the sharing economy as a two‐sided market model that allows private individuals to share resources with potential customers through an online platform in the form of temporary access to goods and services with out transferring permanent ownership.

The sharing economy can be found in various industries incorporating a wide field of applications (Hamari et al., 2016). In particular, the transportation (Andersson et al., 2013; Cohen & Kietzmann, 2014) and the hospitality (Cohen & Kietzmann, 2014; Tussyadiah, 2015; Zervas et al., 2017) industries have been gaining a lot of traction as of late, as established sharing economy platforms, such as BlaBlaCar, Google Waze, Lyft, and Uber (contemporary online plat forms for transportation services) or 9flats, Airbnb, Housetrip, and Wimdu (contemporary online platforms for hospitality services), facilitate a new business model within the two industries. In the following, we take a closer look at millennials—a user group important to contemporary sharing economy services (Hwang & Griffiths, 2017)

## 1.2 | Millennials and the sharing economy

“Millennials” are the demographic cohort following Generation X (Gurău, 2012; Twenge, 2006) and are in many ways defining the sharing economy (Arthursson, 2016; Hwang & Griffiths, 2017; Mittendorf, 2017a; PwC, 2015; Rebell, 2015). Millennials are born between 1980 and the early 2000s (Gurău, 2012; Twenge, 2006).<sup>2</sup> According to the U.S. Census Bureau (2015a), millennials constitute the largest generation in many counties. For example, they make up 83.1 million of the U.S. population, making it the largest and also the most diverse population in U.S. history (Rebell, 2015; U.S. Census Bureau, 2015a). Millennials are characterized as digital natives (Howe & Strauss, 2009; Lenhart, Purcell, Smith, & Zickuhr, 2010; Twenge, 2006; Vodanovich, Sundaram, & Myers, 2010) They grew up in a connected world, allowing them to access resources via the Internet from anywhere at any time (Lenhart et al., 2010). Millennials are expected to have a disruptive effect on incumbent industries and organiza tions, favour social interactions, pursue open and frequent communication with friends and business partners, and are at ease with communication technologies (Hwang & Griffiths, 2017; Myers & Sadaghiani, 2010). In addition, researchers argue that an uneasy economy has made millennials cautious to spend money, particularly when it comes to accumulating debt (PwC, 2015; Rebell, 2015). Hence, millennials are continuously developing their spend ing and consumption habits and may not continue the ever‐increasing consumerism of previous generations in the developed world (Birdsall, 2014). Millennials have been recognized as an “important consumer group for sharing economy” services (Hwang & Griffiths, 2017). Millennials perceive sharing and being economical as smart and clever, whereas traditional ownership practices are sometimes considered an obstacle in care and maintenance (Bardhi & Eckhardt, 2012). Further, millennials have been found to value the social and environmental aspects that accompany the service encounter on sharing economy platforms (Bucher, Fieseler, & Lutz, 2016; Twenge, 2006). The millennial generation is an important constituent of the sharing economy.

Sharing economy platforms, such as Airbnb and Uber, offer convenience with no maintenance responsibility for consumers; hence, they often provide access to goods and services with a better service level than ownership can provide (Arthursson, 2016; Mittendorf, 2017a, 2017b). According to PwC (2015), 57% of young adults agree that access is the new ownership. The sharing economy, in terms of actively providing and receiving services by privat individuals, is about to become considerably important for millennials and future generations (Rebell, 2015).

## 1.3 | From service encounter to sharing encounter

The term “service encounter” is an established concept that has attained widespread use in information systems, management, and marketing literature (Bitner, Booms, & Tetreault, 1990; Bradley, Sparks, Zapf, Mccoll‐Kennedy, & Jimmieson, 2013; Lin & Lin, 2011; Lloyd & Luk, 2011; Scherer, Wünderlich, & Von Wangenheim, 2015). A “service encounter” is a temporary, interpersonal relationship involving the cocreation of value through social interaction (Czepiel, 1990). Thus, a service encounter necessarily involves dyadic interaction between two key roles—a custome and a service provider (Surprenant & Solomon, 1987). Service encounters are composed of various dimensions, suc as duration, investment, control, and personalization (Bolton, 1998; Chandon, Leo, & Philippe, 1997; Pleger Bebko 2000; Squire, Cousins, & Brown, 2009).

In many occasions, a service encounter between customers and service providers, such as on Airbnb and Uber, i introduced between two complete strangers on short notice (Belk, 2014; Hamari et al., 2016; Mittendorf, 2016) Hence, people that are unknown to each other perform arm's length transactions that are, according to previous research, influenced by a variety of factors, such as time, social and affective content, spatial proximity, and trust (Chandon et al., 1997; Czepiel, 1990; Price, Arnould, & Tierney, 1995). To emphasize encounters on sharing platforms as a special subclass of services, we coin the term “sharing encounter,” which ultimately represents a specia occurrence of a service encounter between two unfamiliar sharing partners initiated on a sharing economy platform. Sharing encounters involve two private individuals coming together on a platform, the use of excess private capacity, and involves strangers. Researchers have shown that trust is central to interpersonal and commercial relationships that are predominant on service encounters (Halliday, 2004; McKnight & Chervany, 2001; Morgan & Hunt, 1994; Spake et al., 2003). Especially for sharing encounters on sharing economy platforms, which are generally defined as one‐time interactions between two strangers, high levels of inherent risk are prevalent (Weber, 2014). As a result, inherent risk is an important barrier for sharing economy customers and service providers to engage in a sharing encounter, due to a potential chance of misconduct by the corresponding sharing partner (Spake et al., 2003).

## 1.4 | Trust in sharing encounters

Trust is one of the most complex and abstract concepts that has been studied broadly and deeply in information systems literature, rooted across numerous disciplinary fields, such as in psychology (Geyskens, Steenkamp, Scheer, & Kumar, 1996; McKnight & Chervany, 2001; Warren, 1999), sociology (Lewis, Weigert, Lewis, Weigert, & Dame, 1985; Rousseau, Sitkin, Burt, & Camerer, 1998), philosophy (Hosmer, 1995; Luhmann, 1979; Porter, 1996), and economics (Chiles & Mcmackin, 1996; Fehr, 2009). Regardless of the field, virtually, all trust definitions share the perception of relying on actions between two or more parties that take place in the future (Lewis et al., 1985; Luhmann, 1979; McKnight & Chervany, 1996; Morgan & Hunt, 1994; Xiu & Liu, 2005). Trust is a collective attribute that originates from interactions between different parties (Luhmann, 1979). Researchers find trust to be crucial whenever risk, uncertainty, or interdependencies exist and help to overcome, justify, and suppress thei consequences (Gefen, 2000; Hosmer, 1995; Rousseau et al., 1998; Schoorman, Mayer, & Davis, 2007)

The need for trust is particularly necessary in socially distant relationships, such as in computer‐mediated environments, due to the high transaction complexity and given uncertainties (Friedman, Kahn, & Howe, 2000; Jarvenpaa Tractinsky, & Saarinen, 1999; McKnight & Chervany, 2001; Ratnasingham, 1998). This is in particular true fo interactions in the online environment (Ang, Dubelaar, & Lee, 2001; Bart, Shankar, Sultan, & Urban, 2005; Kim, Ferrin & Rao, 2008; Morgan & Hunt, 1994; Pavlou, 2001). Trust is crucial for online interactions that are not fully insured or governed by rules and regulations—these interactions require an adequate trust basis to function successfully (Ang et al., 2001; Gefen, 2000; Li, Zhou, & Yang, 2011; McKnight, Choudhury, & Kacmar, 2002b; Wang & Emurian 2005). Trust is essential in a variety of computer‐mediated environments, such as in e‐commerce (Gefen, 2000 2002; McKnight, Choudhury, & Kacmar, 2002a; Pavlou & Gefen, 2004), crowdsourcing (Doan, Ramakrishnan, & Halevy, 2011; Mashhadi & Capra, 2011; Zheng, Li, & Hou, 2011), and virtual teams (Jarvenpaa et al., 1999; Jarvenpaa & Leidner, 1999). With the absence of trust in the online environment, individuals would need to consider and eval uate every possible eventuality of a counterpart's action to reduce uncertainty (Lewis et al., 1985). Thus, as complex ity rises, individuals would rather choose to refrain from an action than to deal with the overwhelming evaluation process (Gefen, 2000; Luhmann, 2000). In this regard, trust is among the most effective complexity‐reduction mech anisms, as it eliminates certain possible outcomes from a consideration set. In other words, applying trust as a complexity‐reduction mechanism enables individuals to deal with a manageable number of outcomes rather than uncountable possibilities (Luhmann, 1979).

This model has three key constructs.

1. Trust in intermediary: This construct refers to an intermediate entity that owns a digital exchange platform o marketplace. These third parties do not own the shared resources but provide an online channel that allows the respective sharing partners to engage in a sharing encounter (Belk, 2010). Accordingly, the resources exchanged via these platforms belong to individual service providers. Examples of intermediaries are Airbnb, Housetrip, Uber, Lyft, and BlaBlaCar. Sharing partners that have trust in the intermediary have the confidence that the online platform will support the sharing encounter in a favourable way and act in their interest

2. Trust in sharing partners: We define sharing partners as both customers and providers. These individuals exchange resources with one another through an intermediate entity. The resources exchanged are privately owned excess capacity. Sharing partners may be either customers or service providers of any given resource type (Ertz et al., 2016b). Trust in a sharing partner relates to the willingness to rely on favourable future actions of the corresponding sharing partner; eg, service providers trust customers not to harm their resources when providing access, and customers trust service providers to conduct the agreed service transaction in an expected manner.

3. Intention to engage in a sharing encounter: Intention to engage in a sharing encounter is defined as conducting the corresponding service transaction, eg, in case of a service provider—offering a sharing service or accepting a service request—and in case of a customer—requesting a sharing service. In general, the intention to participate in a service encounter is based on the assumption that users attempt to make rational decisions based on the information available to them (Ertz et al., 2016b; Kim et al., 2008). In case of missing information, drawing on the behavioural theory, many studies have shown that the users' intentions to engage in online transactions are significantly influenced by trust. Accordingly, high levels of trust lead to higher intentions to engage (Fang et al., 2014).

## 2.1 | Sharing encounters and trust

As we indicated above, a “service encounter” is a temporary, interpersonal relationship involving the cocreation of value through social interaction. Key elements of this interaction involve the degree of interactivity and the institu tionalization, or “rituality,” of the scripted behaviours that those interactions follow (Chandon et al., 1997; Winsted, 1997). Interactivity encompasses the service relations at work during the encounter, whereas rituality includes all the ceremonial and contextual aspects that shape the “climate” of an encounter (Chandon et al., 1997). Kim et al. Although the importance of encounters is widely recognized, service encounters are still often analysed globally without specifically digging into its dimensions (Chandon et al., 1997). Chandon et al. particularly developed a first scale for measuring encounter dimensions in a dyadic perspective. We adapted this dimensional approach, based on the foundation that customers and service providers use stable criteria when assessing potential interactions that involve trust and risk. Therefore, we follow the recommendation of Chandon et al. that a dimensionality approach is more appropriate to capture the interactive process of service encounters. ## 2.1.1 | Sharing encounter dimensionality

Like other service encounters, sharing encounters can vary with respect to their dimensionality (Chandon et al., 1997 Solomon et al., 1985). The notion of “dimensionality” of a sharing encounter is intended to capture the scope and complexity of the encounter. Some encounters are relatively simple, with limited scope and dimensionality, whereas others are rich in variety and require a stronger commitment and more attention because there are more aspects to the encounter—its dimensionality. This construct, because dimensionality generally involves greater uncertainty directly relates to issues of risk and trust (Coulter & Coulter, 2002)

Based on existing literature, we identified aspects or “dimensions” of sharing encounters that can be expected to affect trust. In other words, we found that duration, financial risk, personalization, control, and social interaction vary along different sharing encounters and are likely to moderate the impact of trust on the intention to engage from both the customer and provider perspectives. Next, we will define each of these dimensions and relate them to Ube and Airbnb—popular ride sharing and lodging platforms (respectively).

• Duration—We use the definitions of Chandon et al. (1997), and Bitner et al. (1990) of service encounter as a period of time during which customers interact with service providers. The duration of the service encounter is a major dimension that alters the perception of the service (Barger & Grandey, 2006; Bolton, 1998; Noone, Kimes, Mattila, & Wirtz, 2009). The dynamics of how customer and service provider relate to each other over time has been assessed by various researchers (Coulter & Coulter, 2002; Gounaris & Venetis, 2002). Servic encounter duration affects customer satisfaction (Noone et al., 2009), the relative level and importance of service provider performance (Price et al., 1995), and service quality, customer bonding, and the extent to which the client trusts the service provider (Gounaris & Venetis, 2002; Reinartz & Kumar, 2003; Squire et al., 2009). Whereas Uber specializes in short‐ride services that do generally not last longer than 1 hr, Airbnb focuses on overnight stays that typically last from 24 hr to several days. To conclude, the service duration on Airbnb is longer than on Uber.

• Financial risk—Financial risk reflects the value of the service encounter or money exchanged in a transaction that could be lost through the use of the sharing economy platform (Schoder & Yin, 2000). Financial risk is the users' belief about a potential negative outcome from engaging in a service encounter with an unknown counterpart (Kim et al., 2008; G. Wu, Hu, & Wu, 2010). Accordingly, researchers found that the role of potential loss influences risk‐taking behaviour (Arkes, Herren, & Isen, 1988); thus, financial risk is likely to moderate the importance of trust to compensate for the corresponding potential loss. Hence, financial risk is an important barrier for sharing economy users who are considering engaging in a service encounter (Schoder & Yin, 2000). Accommodation services on Airbnb are supposed to be a cheap option to hotel chain accommodation services. Uber, on the other hand, provides more transparent and often a cheaper transportation option compared with traditional taxi services. When comparing both prices from Airbnb and Uber on their respective websites, accommodatior services are generally more expensive than short‐distance ride services.

• Personalization—Personalization is another major dimension of service encounters that features the “recognition of uniqueness” (Surprenant & Solomon, 1987; Winsted, 1997). High personalization services are often associated with quality service and trust between customers and service providers (Surprenant & Solomon, 1987), an therefore, we would expect that personalization would amplify the importance of trust in an encounter. In con trast, a highly standardized or routine service encounter provides no differentiation from customer to customer, as the service is always provided in an identical manner (Jo, Booms, & Mohr, 1994). Hence, the importance o trust decreases when engaging in standardized services. In other words, trust matters less for standardize services (as the customers and providers exactly know what to expect from each other) compared with highly personalized and unique services (where individuals take time to asses a trustworthy corresponding service part ner; Solomon et al., 1985; Winsted, 1997). When examining accommodation experiences on Airbnb, we find that accommodations are generally unique as they mostly resemble private homes of other individuals rather than standardized, nonpersonalized hotel rooms. In comparison, we find that ride‐sharing services are more simila to both each other and respective taxi services. Hence, services are more standardized in ride sharing compared with lodging.

• Control—Services are primarily intangible; hence, they cannot be separated from their provider or stored in inventory (Walker, 2006). Sharing encounters, on the other hand, often consist of both a tangible element an an intangible element. Whereas some sharing encounters enable a joint usage of a particular good (Buche et al. 2016: Ertz Durif, & Arcand, 2016a), other sharing encounters aim at transferring the entire good betweer two sharing partners (Belk, 2014; Hamari et al., 2016). In other words, joint usage often allows the servic provider to stay in control, compared with ceding a particular good to a customer for a predefined time frame. Accordingly, the level of transferred control varies between certain types of sharing encounters. The customer, on the other hand, gains more responsibility when a good is allocated to him for a predefined time frame compared with a joint usage where the responsibility is shared (Kacperczyk, 2009), which in turn can moderate trust. In the example of Airbnb, customers usually get control over a room or the entire apartment for the duration o their stay, whereas in the case of Uber, customers usually stay on the passenger's seat while experiencing the transportation service. In other words, accommodation services generally imply a higher bar of responsibilit (such as locking doors and returning keys) compared with transportation services where the customer can stay completely passive for the entire duration of the service while the service provider retains complete control of the transportation element of the service.

• Social interaction—The service encounter literature indicates the aspect of social interactions between a customer and a service provider as a means of differentiation between services (Chandon et al., 1997; Czepiel 1990; Solomon et al., 1985). Kreijns, Kirschner, and Jochems (2003) found that individuals need to “trust each other, feel a sense of warmth and belonging, and feel close to each other before they will engage willfully in collaboration and recognize the collaboration as a valuable experience.” Furthermore, they argue that social relation ships foster the importance of both trust and belonging and the importance of building a sense of community between customers and providers (Kreijns et al., 2003). However, especially sharing economy platforms point out that social interactions in particular are a unique feature of the sharing economy and are especially important among young individuals (John, 2013; Molz, 2013; Ozdemir & Hewett, 2010; Teubner, Hawlitschek, & Henner 2016). Whereas social interactions are generally prevalent in a sharing encounter, their frequency and intensity varies between services that might ultimately moderate the relationship of trust (Bellotti et al., 2015; Lampinen & Cheshire, 2016). Social interactions for accommodation services, especially when the apartment is shared, are typically more intense than transportation services. First, social interactions among users before the sharing encounter are far more thorough in a lodging app, as potential consumers thoroughly communicate with each other in the form of reviews and often communicate with the lodging owner before the encounter. In ride sharing, the social interaction may occur during the ride, but often, there is little interaction. Similarly, in certain lodging instances, there is interaction; in others, there is not. But when there is interaction in the lodging service, it involves sharing a home and all of its facilities, which is a far more in‐depth interaction than merely sharing a ride.

Drawing on previous research into dimensionality, it is clear that dimensionality affects the role of trust in ser vice encounters. We also expect this to be the case for sharing encounters—a specific type of service encounters. As noted above, the context and dimensions of a sharing encounter vary across different services that ultimately affect the users' perception of richness and hence the implications of trust (Halliday, 2004; Scherer et al., 2015). Sharing economy platforms enable users to offer and request distinct sharing services that differ in their level o dimensionality—refer to Table 3 for a brief comparison of two popular sharing platforms, Uber and Airbnb Researchers go a step further and show that particular service dimensions influence the impact of trust between individuals participating in a service encounter (Chandon et al., 1997; Halliday, 2004). Although the weights of the individual dimensions might differ in the dimensionality construct, the notion of the respective five dimensions is uniform.

First, the influence of trust in customers and service providers is influenced by the duration of the service encoun ter (Coulter & Coulter, 2002; Gounaris & Venetis, 2002). In particular, researchers state that the importance of trus increases in long‐term service interactions (Coulter & Coulter, 2002; Gounaris & Venetis, 2002). Hence, the influence of trust on long‐duration sharing encounters is more profound (Price et al., 1995). For example, a sharing encounter in the hospitality industry, such as on Airbnb, usually lasts up to several days, whereas a sharing encounter in the trans portation industry, such as on Uber, rarely lasts over an hour.

Financial risk is an additional dimension that could alter the users' intention to engage in a service encounter (Murray & Schlacter, 1990). In this regard, individuals would consider any sharing transaction involving high value to be of higher risk. Further, risk is generally defined as the extent to which one believes uncertainty exists about whether desirable outcomes will occur (Nicolaou & McKnight, 2006). In general, a high financial investment with high potential loss leads to more sensitiveness in choosing the appropriate trustworthy service partner. Accordingly, the importance of trust increases with a higher financial investment with higher potential loss. For example, the tota financial investment with potential loss of a sharing encounter on Airbnb is usually higher (eg, higher investment for customers in the hospitality industry than in the transportation industry with higher potential damage of property on Airbnb than potential loss of a sharing encounter on Uber for providers)

In personalized service, unlike standardized service (Jo et al., 1994), customers and the service providers do not know in advance what to expect from each other, and therefore, trust matters more for nonstandardized services where the outcome is not always predictable compared with routine services. For example, the level of personaliza tion of sharing encounters on Airbnb is generally higher than on Uber (Edelman & Luca, 2014; Ert & Hebrew, 2015) Whereas every accommodation on Airbnb is unique and can be arranged to meet the needs of the customers, the transportation service of Uber is highly standardized. Thus, the importance of trust is usually greater when expectations are unclear including a lot of uncertainty (eg, on Airbnb) compared with standardized transportation service (eg, on Uber).

Sharing economy services are conceptualized to fall on a continuum ranging from goods to pure services. In the case of Uber, one can argue that the tangible element, which enables the transportation service, is not fully trans ferred to the customer, albeit the service provider stays in control during the time of sharing. In contradiction to Uber, in the case of Airbnb, the customer receives control over the shared good and gains responsibility. Hence, the service provider gives up control over the tangible element as it is transferred to the customer for a predefined time frame. In this regard, researchers argue that an adequate trust basis is usually required to compensate for the loss of control (Das & Teng, 1998) and to handle increased responsibility. Therefore, Hui and Bateson (1991) stat that perceived control is a crucial variable that constitutes the service encounter.

With regard to the sharing economy, the depth of social interactions varies between sharing economy services According to previous literature, we argue that the effect of trust in a sharing partner is greater for sharing econom services with a distinctive social element compared with sharing economy services that facilitate only minor socia interactions (Bucher et al., 2016). For example, in the case of Airbnb, customers and service providers often share the same space during a sharing encounter or at least introduce themselves to each other at the beginning of the encounter, whereas sharing encounters on Uber are handled more reservedly, often without any social element between customers and service providers (Mittendorf, 2017b).

In summary, by adopting service encounter literature, we conclude that different sharing encounters can have dif ferent levels of dimensionality, which in turn influence the importance of trust in the corresponding sharing partner. Therefore, we argue that dimensionality fosters the importance of trust (Shemwell& Cronin, 1994), which ultimatel influences whether customers and service providers engage in a sharing encounter, leading to our first hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1. The positive effect of trust in the sharing partner on the intention to engage in a sharing encounter is stronger for sharing encounters with higher dimensionality than for sharing encounters with lower dimensionality.

Dimensionality is not the only factor that influences the impact of trust on engagement with sharing encounters. Sharing platforms necessarily involve two sides—the customers and the service providers. Next, we address the implications of this two‐sidedness on the role of trust

## 2.1.2 | Two‐sidedness of sharing encounters

As Figure 2 indicates, the sharing economy is a two‐sided market moderated by online intermediaries that matc demand and supply of private excess capacity (Eisenmann et al., 2006; Malhotra & Van Alstyne, 2014). Ertz et al (2016) suggest the term “two‐sided consumer” as the concept of sharing goes beyond the notion of traditional buyers or consumers by inviting consumers as potential providers in the value creation process in order to reconcile their personal interests (Ertz et al., 2016b). The online intermediary supports the entire sharing process from the request to the execution of a booking, including price setting in some cases and payment procedures (Ikkala & Lampinen, 2014; Zervas et al., 2017). Following this logic, intermediaries fulfil different levels of intermediation derived from the terms and condition of distribution and consumption they stipulate (Ertz et al., 2016b).

Both customers and providers depend on the online intermediary that matches supply and demand of exces capacity and therefore enables sharing encounters. Therefore, as both parties depend on the online intermediary trust in the online intermediary facilitates the users' intentions to engage in a sharing encounter (Halliday, 2004) However, when comparing customer and service provider perspectives, the direction of the payment flow influence the importance of trust. In particular, individuals that execute a payment in the online environment require a suffi cient amount of trust to engage in a transaction (Fang et al., 2014). In case of the sharing economy, one can argue that customers need to make a payment to the intermediary in order to engage in a sharing encounter. The payment less service fee and taxes will then be transferred to the service provider after the encounter is successfully executed As a result, the payment flows from the customer over the intermediary to the service provider

Hence, the inherent financial risk for customers when booking a service is usually greater than the financial risk o service providers, who typically bear no financial risk for placing a service offer on the online platform. The amount o trust in order to cope with a financial payment is dependent on the amount of potential loss (Chellappa & Pavlou, 2002; Luo, Li, Zhang, & Shim, 2010). Following this logic, we assume that the inherent financial risk of customers is higher than the financial risk of providers when using the sharing economy platform in order to initiate a sharing encounter. As a result, we presume that customers' need of trust is greater than the providers' need of trust in the intermediary in order to engage in a sharing encounter. From this insight into the asymmetry of sharing encounters, we generate our second hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2. The positive effect of trust in the online intermediary on the intention to engage in the sharing encounter is stronger for customers than for providers in the sharing economy.

## 3.2 | Control variables

We included specific control variables in our model to ensure that the empirical results are not due to covariance wit other variables (see Table A4). Previous literature suggests that the users' gender, income, level of education, an expertise in online transactions may affect the intention to engage in transactions on the Internet (Fang et al., 2014)

Therefore, we identified the following control variables as relevant: First, we included age and frequency of online purchases on the grounds that platform usage might depend upon experience‐based characteristics (Abramova et al., 2015; Kim et al., 2008; Pavlou & Gefen, 2004). Second, personality‐orientated attributes, such as disposition to trust, education, and gender, were included to control for the possible effect of the extent of diversification (Gefen, 2000 Gefen & Straub, 2004; McKnight & Chervany, 2001; Rose & Lamberton, 2012). Third, in order to control for eco nomic and financial intentions of the participants, we included yearly income and the profession as control variable (Bucher et al., 2016; Hawlitschek, Teubner, & Gimpel, 2016). Finally, we included the marital status in order to cope with biases regarding social motives (Möhlmann, 2015; Rose & Lamberton, 2012).

## 4 | DATA ANALYSIS AND RESULTS

We used SPSS Statistics 22.0.0 for Windows and AMOS 19.0.1 to perform the statistical analysis on the collected data. ## 4.1 | Measurement model

To assess the measurement model, we examined the reliability and validity of the constructs. To check for internal consistency, we followed the established guidelines from Hair, Black, Babin, and Anderson (2014), Nunnally (1975), and Straub, Boudreau, and Gefen (2004). We could show that the composite reliability coefficients reach a satisfac tory threshold of 0.70 or higher for every construct (see Table 7), thereby suggesting good reliability (Bagozzi & Yi, 1988; Fornell & Larcker, 1981).

Construct validity was examined by assessing a confirmatory factorial analysis (CFA). We checked for convergent validity (O'Leary‐Kelly & Vokurka, 1998; Venkatraman & Ramanujam, 1987) and discriminant validity (Straub et al., 2004). In this regard, convergent validity defines the extent to which the measures for a variable act as if they are measuring the underlying theoretical construct because they share variance (McKnight et al., 2002a) and relates to the degree to which multiple methods of measuring a variable provide the same results (O'Leary‐Kelly & Vokurka, 1998). Convergent validity is considered acceptable when the average variance extracted (AVE) successfully exceeds the threshold of 0.50 for all constructs (Fornell & Larcker, 1981; Gounaris & Dimitriadis, 2003; D. Grewal, Monroe, & Krishnan, 1998). Moreover, convergent validity was established where the items for each construct load onto only one factor with an eigenvalue greater than 1.0 and the loading of each item is greater than 0.50 (Kim et al., 2008; Wixom & Watson, 2001).

Discriminant validity means the degree to which measures of two constructs are empirically distinct (Bagozzi & Phillips, 1991; Davis et al., 1989) and the degree to which measures of different latent variables are unique (O'Leary‐Kelly & Vokurka, 1998). We could show that the square roots of the AVE are higher than the correlations between the variables, hence meeting the Fornell and Larcker criterion for assessing discriminant validity (se Table 8). Overall, the results indicate that the study constructs possess convergent and discriminant validities.

## 5 | DISCUSSION

Our study focuses on the role of trust in sharing encounters—encounters between unfamiliar sharing partners initiated on sharing economy platforms. We hypothesize that the likelihood of users engaging in a sharing encounter rises with increased degrees of trust in the intermediary and trust in the corresponding sharing partner. Prior research has mainl examined the role of trust in the context of e-commerce intermediary frameworks (Fang et al. 2014: Gefen et al 2003; McKnight et al., 2002b; Pavlou & Gefen, 2004). The present study adds new understanding of sharing economy mechanisms by using the construct of dimensionality to assess the influence of intermediary trust and by differentiating between the perspective of customers and service providers when analysing the effect of trust on the intention to engag in a sharing encounter. Our examination of the sharing encounter, including the intermediary framework, type of sharing partner, dimensionality, trust, and the intention to engage, reveals several important research findings (see Table 15).

First and foremost, our study confirms that sharing encounter dimensionality moderates the relationship between trust in sharing partners and the intention to engage in a sharing encounter, such that the positive effect of trust in the intention to engage is stronger for high‐dimensional sharing encounters. By drawing on the theory of trust (Luhmann, 1979), our findings advance our understanding of moderators of trust by explicitly specifying sharing encounter dimensionality as an important boundary condition to address how trust influences sharing intention in the online environment. This result adds to the sharing economy literature by addressing the call for identifying important boundary conditions for the relationship between trust and sharing behaviour. Gefen, Benbasat, and Pavlou (2008) properly pointed out that the existing literature has largely ignored moderators of trust that distinctly influence behavioural intentions, thus oversimplifying the context in which trust operates. Several researchers already argue that trust is an important concept in the sharing economy across platforms (Bucher et al., 2016; Hawlitschek, Teubner, & Gimpel, 2016; Möhlmann, 2015), whereas we first empirically show that the sharing encounter dimensionality affects the influence of trust on the users' intention to engage in a sharing encounter. Our findings suggest that cementing trust in a sharing partner might not be as universally important as we once believed it to be if the sharing encounter dimensionality is comparably low.

In addition, our study finds that the relationship between trust in the intermediary and the intention to engage in a sharing encounter is stronger for customers compared with service providers. With this new insight, our study pro vides a plausible explanation to the finding that trust has a different effect, albeit still significant, depending on th perspective of the sharing partner (customer and service provider perspectives) (Hawlitschek, Teubner, & Weinhardt, 2016). In other words, trust in the intermediary seems explicitly influential regarding the intention to engage in a sharing encounter, whereas the influence is stronger for customers than for service providers.

These findings are an important contribution to our scholarly understanding of how trust is evaluated in existing sharing encounters. The literature has documented that, in general, trust has a positive effect on users' sharing inten tions (Bucher et al., 2016; Möhlmann, 2015; Weber, 2014) and the effect differs for customers and service providers (Hawlitschek, Teubner, & Weinhardt, 2016). However, to the best of our knowledge, existing literature has not considered potential contextual conditions in which trust is evaluated in the sharing economy, despite the call of Mayer et al. (1995) that a clear understanding of the context in which trust operates is necessary to make a prediction of its actual influence on behavioural intentions. Our study is among the first to address this theoretical gap in the sharing economy research by assessing service encounter aspects on the sharing economy and empirically verifying th important role of the sharing encounter dimensionality in affecting the general evaluation of trust on contemporary sharing economy platforms.

These complementary results help reveal the conditions under which trust in a sharing partner and intermediary can be qualified as a source of competitive advantage in the sharing economy context. Our study argues that trust in sharing partners is only important in the case of high sharing encounter dimensionality. This implies that the building of trust and trustworthiness in sharing partners, eg, with expensive trust‐building measures and corresponding quality, may only be necessary for sharing encounters with high dimensionality. Consequently, the strategic impact on differentiating trustworthy from untrustworthy sharing partners becomes less important. This view is largely con sistent with strategy theorists who suggest that trust can only build a competitive advantage when it is valuable, rare and necessary to facilitate user intentions (Barney & Hansen, 1994).

This study also makes an empirical contribution; Hawlitschek et al. (2016) argued that the effect of trust in the behav ioural intention is somewhat different between customers and suppliers on sharing economy platforms. This study is one of the first to examine this assertion across different sharing economy platforms, and it is interesting to overserve in our data that the direct relationship between trust and the intention to share is indeed significant for trust in the intermediar and dependent on sharing encounter dimensionality for the direct effect of trust in the sharing partner.

## 5.1 | Implications for practice

Our study has managerial implications for both sharing economy platforms and public policymakers. First, our study rec ommends the online platform to allocate their trust‐building resources according to the level of sharing encounter dimen sionality. Hence, sharing economy platforms that enable sharing encounters with lower dimensionality (such as Uber should spend most resources on building trust between the intermediary and the user, compared with sharing econom platforms with high dimensionality (such as Airbnb) that should focus on trust-building measures regarding the relation: ship between the user and the intermediary, as well as on the relationship between the user and the corresponding shar ing partner. Alternatively, online sharing economy platforms with lower‐dimensional sharing encounters may reduce trust maintenance costs by strategically targeting financial resources on solely building trust in the intermediary

Besides, sharing economy platforms should distinguish between trust‐building measures for customers and service providers. In this regard, we recommend committing more resources to foster trust‐building measures for cus tomers, as the effect of trust in the intermediary on the users' intentions is stronger for customers compared with the effect of trust in the intermediary on the users' intention for service providers. To the extent that dimensionalit and type of sharing partner are expected to vary across sharing economy platforms, this set of recommendations is particularly relevant to firms operating in the “two‐sided consumer market” (Ertz et al., 2016b).

Second, with our findings, Internet policymakers should be advised to commit themselves to building a risk mit igation mechanism in order to support the contemporary sharing economy infrastructure. A perceivable safer sharing economy environment could help make trust building a less difficult mandate for sharing economy platforms to embark upon, thereby contributing to the prosperity of the overall economy.

## 5.2 | Limitation and future research directions

Future research can take this study further by addressing several limitations of our study. First, our sample was drawn from an Internet population as a convenience sample. While the participants all belonged to the millennial generation and were experienced sharing economy users, future research can take this investigation further by drawing researc subjects from a more diverse population and comparing millennials with other generations. We think that this i highly valuable as millennials are not the key target of the sharing economy service providers, but rather one o the main target groups since they are digital natives. Nevertheless, other generations are also within the audienc scope of sharing economy service providers.

Second, as this study exclusively focuses on the general perception of trust in the intermediary and trust in th corresponding sharing partner, we theoretically sample the two sharing platforms in this study based on relativ dimensionality. Further research could check our findings for robustness by examining other sorts of sharing econ omy platforms that vary in dimensionality from these platforms to see if findings hold.

Third, we recommend performing research on the diagnostics of specific information types that are generall available on sharing economy platforms. Information types, such as profile photos, reviews, ratings, and historica information, might be highly influential towards the perception and necessity of trust in sharing encounters. In this regard, we recommend a study approach for analysing trust in the platform by providing or withholding certain infor mation in order to influence information transparency, as well as doing research on the specific information types on trust in the respective sharing partner(s).

Furthermore, the wording of our intention to engage items regarding the sharing encounter may have induced dif ferent interpretations by the respondents (customer vs service providers). Future research should consider improving the intention measures by developing additional items, such as intention to share and intention to create an account on the respective platform. Along these lines, it is important to note that intentionality does not necessarily lead to behav iour but the two generally correlate. Thus, the relationships we find between trust and intentionality remain relevant.

Moreover, the data gathered from users in our survey might induce a potential trust bias. Hence, future researc could consider replication of the study with alternative methods (eg, experiments). In addition, more objective measures, such as repeated sharing behaviours, may be used as an additional dependent variable to improve the pre dictability of our method. The current research model could also be extended by incorporating the well‐established risk‐reduction effect of trust for different sharing economy platforms. Finally, we suggest that future research may follow a similar approach to investigating the unexplored area of risk and attempt to quantify the practical impact of this risk effect in the sharing economy context
