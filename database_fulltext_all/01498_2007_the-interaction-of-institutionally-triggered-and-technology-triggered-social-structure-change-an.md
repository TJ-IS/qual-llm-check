## Abstract

Aligning social structures and technology capabilities is a significant challenge to information technology-related organizational change. It is particularly challenging in institutionalized settings such as hospitals. We report an interpretive field study of computerized physician order entry (CPOE) at an acute-care hospital, in which we investigated how institutionally triggered and technology-triggered change interacted in complementary processes to engender alignment. Social structure changes included increased interdependency among clinical departments, multidisciplinary cooperation across clinical disciplines, and standardization in clinical decision-making. Organization members also enacted institutionalized interaction patterns with physicians by deferring to their preferences for CPOE use. The cumulative influence of change triggers nonetheless facilitated the hospital's realization of clinical goals. We drew on Barley's (1990) role- and network-based model for technology and structure alignment. Nonetheless, we extended this micro-level analytic approach to account for the influence of change in the macro-institutional environment. Our analysis clarified the extent of structure change attributable to the CPOE technology and highlighted institutional forces that promoted yet inhibited change. The case also highlighted the importance of role networks on the trajectory and outcomes of organizational change processes.

Keywords: Social structure change, technology change, computerized physician order entry, health information technology, institutional theory

## Introduction and Motivation

Information technologies are viewed widely as enablers of organizational transformation. Nonetheless, organizations often fail to benefit significantly from IT investments (Markus 2004). Failure to realize expected outcomes can result from misalignment of IT capabilities and social structures within the organization (Orlikowski 1992, 2000). Researchers have investigated this misalignment from a variety of theoretic perspectives, often with inconsistent or contradictory results (Barley 1986; Robey and Boudrieu 1999). The accumulation of evidence has discredited technological determinism as an explanation for social structure change. Nonetheless, IT can play a role, albeit indeterminate, in triggering change in structures (Barley 1986; Orlikowski 1992, 2000). Moreover, mutual shaping of an IT artifact and social structures might be required to facilitate effective use (Orlikowski et al. 1995). Technology, though a “hard,” tangible artifact, often proves easier to change than “soft,” intangible social structures.

Examining technology and structure alignment at the microanalytic level of action and interaction can explicate social processes while avoiding deterministic assumptions (Barley and Tolbert 1997; Orlikowsk 1992, 2000). Nonetheless, micro-level analysis may overlook forces in the institutional environment that influence organizational processes and outcomes (Avgerou 2000; Chiasson and Davidson 2005; King et al. 1994). Macro-level change may challenge organizational practices that inhibit the alignment of IT and structure. Alternatively, social structures that reflect widely held beliefs and social rules present substantial barriers to organizational change (Barley and Tobert 1997; Scott 2001). Investigating how change arising from the macro-institutional environment interacts with micro-level technology-triggered change may provide a more complete understanding of technology and social structure alignment.

To examine the interaction of institutional and technology change triggers in alignment processes, we undertook an interpretative case study of the implementation of a computerized physician order entry system (CPOE) in an acute care hospital. Healthcare provides an ideal setting to examine these interactions. Healthcare organizations have experienced profound institutional and technical pressures for change. Nonetheless, many old forms and practices persist, reflecting “contradictory forces: rapid change in a sector noted for its highly institutionalized character” (Scott et al. 2000, p. 1). Healthcare information technologies (HIT) are promoted widely to address cost and quality issues, which are drivers of institutional change (Bodenheimer 1999; Institute of Medicine 1999). Effective use of HIT presents substantial challenges to organizational structures, institutional culture, professional practice, and leadership (Garabaldi 1998; LaPointe and Rivard 2005; Massaro 1993). That is, HIT is caught between opposing institutional forces that promote yet inhibit change (Robey and Boudrieu 1999).

Our first research objective was to investigate how institutionally triggered change in the organizational environment and technology-triggered change interacted to influence social structures associated with clinical order management practices at the hospital. We found that technology and institutional changes triggered complementary, overlapping processes that increased interdependency among clinical departments related to the CPOE system's operation, multi-disciplinary cooperation across clinical disciplines in disease management programs, and standardization in clinical decision-making. Enactment of established interaction patterns between physicians and nurses, pharmacists, and other hospital staff members moderated structural change. Nonetheless, the cumulative influence of institutionally triggered and technology-triggered change allowed the hospital to realize significant benefits from CPOE.

Our second objective was to consider how information systems researchers might account analytically for these complex interactions. We drew on Barley's (1990) role- and network-based analytic approach to assess technology-related change across levels of social analysis within the organization. We extended this approach, however, to account for the influence of macro-level institutional influences on micro-level actions, interactions, and social structures. Our analysis suggested that IT has a more-subtle role in the process of social structure change than Barley's (1990) approach implied. The IT artifact was a proximate trigger of role change, but change processes were triggered ultimately by the hospital's response to change in the institutional environment. Change in role networks preceded technology change, and the IT artifact per se was altered by network members. Drawing these fine-grained distinctions clarified the extent of social structure change attributable to the CPOE technology, highlighted institutional influences that promoted yet also inhibited change, and demonstrated the cumulative influence of technology- and institutional-change triggers on social structures at the research site.

The paper is organized as follows. We discuss the theoretic foundations that guided our research study and analysis. We then describe the research site and CPOE project and the research design and analytic methods. We present findings on the interaction of institutional and technological change triggers in this case and discuss the theoretic implications of these interactions and conclude with implications for research and practice.

## Theoretical Perspectives

Barley's (1990) model for the alignment of technology and social structure provided the starting point for our analysis.

Barley (1986, p. 79) defines social structure as “patterned action, interaction, behaviors, and cognition” that become taken-for-granted aspects of social life. Barley (1990, p. 70) considers how structure change can be explained without making inferential leaps across levels of analysis: “Using a role-based approach, a researcher would trace a technology’s influence, step by step, from what might properly be called an individual level of analysis to a dyadic level of analysis and from the dyadic level to the level of the organization or occupation.” Social change thus can be investigated systematically as “a series of reverberations that spread across levels of analysis much like ripples on the surface of a pond” (Barley 1990, p. 70).

Drawing on the distinction first discussed by Nadel (1957), Barley characterized roles as bundles of non-relational and relational elements. Non-relational elements include formal duties and behavioral regularities, obligations, expectations, skills, and tasks. Relational elements involve interactions, dependencies, and expectations between roles. Barley posits that changes in technology most likely affect non-relational elements directly, such as tasks and skills. Changes in non-relational elements may “spill over” into relational elements, however, such that “altered tasks may narrow or expand the range of one’s role set, shift the nature of one’s dependencies, or affect the frequency and content of typical interactions” (Barley 1990, p. 69). Changes at the dyadic level of analysis (that is, between two roles) might then precipitate changes in role networks. For example, organization members who previously had few interactions might have to work collaboratively to complete tasks, creating new dependencies between roles. Changes in hierarchical distinctions between roles and strengthening or weakening of intergroup boundaries are examples of social structure changes that might result from shifts in network configurations.

Drawing on Gidden's (1984) structuration theory, Barley and Tolbert (1997, p. 111) suggest that analyzing micro-level actions and interactions and “mapping” observed changes to changes in institutional forms provide “the sort of rich insights that are crucial to a full understanding of the nature of macro-micro linkages and the reciprocal interplay between actions and institutions.” Barley’s (1990) analytic approach thus begins with the introduction of a technology into an organization and traces its influences on technology users across levels of social analysis, in order to demonstrate how social structure change does or does not occur in organizations or professions.

Why a technology was implemented tends to fall outside this micro-to-macro analytic framework. Nonetheless, the rationale and impetus for implementing new technology influence the trajectory of organizational change processes and outcomes, even if planned changes are not realized. Of particular interest here, organizational leaders' decisions to adopt new technologies are often a response to institutional changes in markets or regulatory pressures in the organizational environment (Greenwood and Hinings 1996; Scott 1987, 2001). King et al. (1994) argue that institutional actors such as governments, professional and industry associations, or large corporations exert considerable influence on IT innovation through regulation, standards setting, subsidies, and so on. In healthcare, regulatory agencies, insurers, and large firms exert increasing pressure on hospitals to collect and report clinical data (Bodenheimer 1999).

Barley and Tolbert posit that institutional changes are enacted at the micro-analytic level. We concur that analytic attention on micro-level actions and interactions is important, but we suggest that investigating the influence of change triggers arising from the macro-institutional environment provides a more complete understanding of social outcomes at the micro-level of analysis. Theorists have viewed institutions primarily as stabilizing and inhibiting social structure change, but institutions do change. In doing so, they create forces for and against change (Scott 2001). Robey and Boudrieu (1999) suggest that accounting for institutional change and stability is one theoretic approach to investigate the outcomes of IT-related organizational change. For instance, hospitals operate in a highly technical and highly institutionalized environment—conditions that make any type of change difficult (Georgopoulos and Mann 1989; Scott 1987). Nonetheless, the U.S. healthcare industry experienced a period of “hyperturbulence” in the latter 20th century (Scott et al. 2000, p. 1). The result has been significant pressure to change institutionalized practices (Bodenheimer 1999; Iglehart 1999). Information technologies such as CPOE have been promoted as an important way to address quality, cost, and regulatory pressures (Institute of Medicine 1999; Kuperman and Gibson 2004; Metzger and Fortin 2003), yet hospitals continue to experience difficulty with CPOE acceptance, and adoption rates remain relatively low (Ash et al. 2004). To more fully understand these contradictory outcomes, we suggest that consideration of micro-level and macro-institutional influences should be incorporated in analysis.

To inform our analysis of a case study of CPOE, we thus drew on Barley's (1990) micro-level analytic approach and the macro-level perspective of institutional theory (Scott 2001). Our first research objective was to investigate how technology-triggered and institutionally triggered change processes interacted to influence social structures associated with clinical order management practices at the research site. Our second objective was to consider how IS researchers might account for the interaction of technology and institutional change processes and to discern the extent of social structure change attributable to CPOE use per se. Our analysis identifies two institutionally-triggered and one technology-triggered change processes and explicates how these processes, individually and cumulatively, engendered alignment between social structure and the CPOE technology at the hospital.

## Research Design and Methods

We conducted an in-depth, interpretive field study of one hospital's experiences with a CPOE system. Our choice of a research setting reflected theoretic choices and pragmatic opportunities. The institutionalized environment of hospitals provides a rich context to examine the interplay of technology and institutional change. Moreover, healthcare is a significant socio-economic sector, and IT use in healthcare is increasingly of interest to IS researchers (Chiasson and Davidson 2004; Kholi and Kettinger 2004; LaPointe and Rivard 2005). A single case study is appropriate for interpretive field studies. It allows researchers to develop a deep understanding of the IT artifact in its socially embedded context (Orlikowski and Iacono 2001) and of organization members' actions related to its use (Klein and Myers 1999). A case that is critical, unique, or revelatory can serve as the basis for generating theory (Yin 1994). The research site we studied implemented its CPOE system at a time when few U.S. hospitals had done so successfully (Ash, Gorman et al. 2004; Massero 1993; Metzger and Fortin 2003). Thus, we considered the research site's experience with CPOE to be a critical case. The second author's familiarity with the site and hospital management through professional education courses he conducted at the hospital facilitated our access to hospital staff and attending physicians.

## Research Site and CPOE Project

The research site, hereafter referred to as “the hospital,” is a nonprofit, urban, acute-care institution with 550 beds, over 3,500 employees, with 1,200 affiliated, attending physicians, and gross operating revenues exceeding \$300 million annually. The hospital provides a comprehensive range of primary and specialized care services. The hospital is a community-based institution, but it is affiliated with the state’s university medical school for residency training programs. Thus, it has some similarities with university hospitals that have adopted CPOE (Metzger and Fortin 2003).

In 1993, hospital leaders undertook a major initiative: the implementation of a CPOE system. They had two major objectives: improving patient care by streamlining clinical information flows and improving cost effectiveness though enhanced clinical decision-making. The first objective included reducing medication errors and increasing the speed of order fulfillment. The second objective entailed influencing physicians' clinical practices by providing them with cost information and standardized care guidelines as they made clinical decisions. The hospital was successful in meeting many of its clinical goals, as evidenced in self-assessment studies and external audits. Table 1 summarizes key clinical indicators and measures of goals, which the hospital evaluated after implementing the CPOE system. The hospital's success with CPOE and related quality programs was recognized with an industry quality achievement award.

The CPOE project took 3 years to complete and cost \$18 million. The system was brought on-line in 1995 and required ongoing commitment of organizational resources to support its operation, enhance the system, and integrate its use into clinical practices. Our field study was conducted in 1997. System usage and CPOE-related practices had stabilized, although minor adjustments and maintenance of the technical system and clinical practices were ongoing.

## The Computerized Physician Order Entry (CPOE) System

The hospital purchased a CPOE software package from a leading vendor but retained existing pharmacy, laboratory, and administrative systems. This approach reduced start-up costs and project scope, but it required the Information Systems department to construct an interface system (datagate) to pass information electronically between systems. During implementation and the two years of operations prior to our study, clinical specialists in the IS department custom-developed (using the package's coding language) over 20,000 computer screens. These screens incorporated standardized departmental order sets (predefined sets of laboratory test orders, medication orders, and so on) into the system's interface. The IS department operated and maintained the CPOE system.

Figure 1 depicts the hospital's CPOE system and flow of clinical-order information at the time of the study. Patient information was transferred from the hospital's admitting, transfer, and discharge (ATD) system into the CPOE system. Each nursing station housed three to four computers for system access and a printer. In the intensive care unit (ICU), physicians and nurses used a wireless laptop on a cart to transport the system to the patient. Physicians' orders were entered into the CPOE system, printed at the nursing station as a notification for the nurse, transferred electronically through the datagate to ancillary department systems (laboratory, pharmacy, diagnostic imaging), and printed there as a backup. Narrative lab and X-ray results were transferred from departmental systems to the CPOE system through the datagate. Nurses completed a limited amount of patient information, such as allergies and drug reactions, and documented information on patient condition (e.g., vital signs) and nursing actions (e.g., delivery of medications) in the system. Data from the CPOE were extracted and transferred to an enterprise data warehouse (EDR) daily for use by process improvement analysts. CPOE data stored in the EDR also were used in a disease management benchmarking system (CARE) to create cost profiles (“physician report cards”) for designated diseases, such as pneumonia and asthma.

## Data Collection and Analysis Methods

We used semi-structured interviews to collect data and purposeful sampling to select individuals to interview from a variety of clinical departments and specialties. Interviewees had worked at the hospital before implementation of the CPOE system. Some had experience with the implementation project and some had also participated in the system's ongoing support. To address the possibility of favorable bias toward the system, we included individuals who were non-users of the CPOE system and attending physicians who were independent practitioners as well as those holding administrative posts. The chief information officer provided a list of potential interviewees, who we contacted to scheduled interviews. Over a 3-month period in 1997, we interviewed 32 individuals: ten physicians from different specialties (cardiology, general surgery, internal medicine, orthopedics, oncology), five nurses and nurse administrators, one unit secretary, two pharmacists, two laboratory supervisors, one respiratory therapist, six health-care analysts (utilization specialists, nurse administrators) from a disease management task force, four IS staff members (including two nurses), and the CIO. We concluded data collection when we had an understanding of hospital-wide changes and variations in system use among clinical departments.

We used a semi-structured interview protocol to guide interviewees through topics such as their perception of how their tasks had changed due to CPOE use, whether (and how) their interactions with other clinicians or ancillary staff had changed, how (or if) they used the system, limitations they experienced with the system, and their assessment of the system's usefulness and success. We asked interviewees involved in the CPOE implementation project to describe this process. Interviews were audio-taped; we produced detailed interview notes from the tapes. Interviews provided two types of data: (1) the interviewee's perceptions of changes, and (2) the interviewee's examples of system use practices, which we analyzed for evidence of change.

We were interested in data on the hospital's clinical outcomes insofar as these changes indicated changes in social structure. The hospital had commissioned external audits and internal studies of clinical outcomes and of nursing productivity. The CIO and the director of Medical Informatics shared published reports with us and provided computer reports on CPOE usage statistics. We compared these with interview data to triangulate our analysis. For example, utilization reports indicated that CPOE usage had stabilized during the year preceding our study. This evidence substantiated nurses' observations about physicians' CPOE use leveling off.

Our goal was not to test theory but to use theory as a sensitizing device in our analysis (Klein and Myers 1999, p.75). To do so, we applied the principle of the hermeneutic circle. $^{2}$ We began by considering the “parts” of CPOE-related changes, using Barley’s (1990) analytic process to examine data for evidence of change or stability in non-relational and relational elements of roles, interactions of role dyads, role networks, and social structures. Examining the research site’s experiences with CPOE holistically, we identified three overlapping change processes. In iterations of the hermeneutic circle, we reexamined each change process and then reconsidered how the three processes interacted. We continued until we arrived at an integrated explanation of how technology and social structures became aligned and how institutional and technology change triggers interacted to engender this outcome.

## Case Findings and Analysis

We identified two institutionally triggered and one technology-triggered change processes related to CPOE at the hospital. Following the hermeneutic circle, we discuss each process and then examine their interactions and implications for social structure change.

## Institutional Change and the Initiation of the CPOE project

The first change process we identified at the research site (Figure 2) was triggered by hospital leaders' actions in response to changes in the institutional environment.

## [1] Institutional Change Trigger for the CPOE Project

Throughout the 1980s and early 1990s, steep increases in the cost of U.S. government-funded healthcare programs, GNP spending on healthcare, and the cost of insurance raised concerns among regulators, politicians, and business leaders (Bazzoli et al. 2004; Iglehart 1999; Scott et al. 2000). Government studies identified problems with the quality of healthcare delivery (Institute of Medicine 1999). Large U.S. firms, which finance health insurance for their employees, instituted a national program to assess quality of healthcare providers, and healthcare insurers and providers were then required to produce detailed information (Bodenheimer 1999). $^{3}$ Industry and government leaders promoted HIT to improve efficiency, monitor healthcare spending, and report data (Institute of Medicine 1999; Kuperman and Gibson 2004). Notable CPOE cases provided an impetus for hospitals to consider adopting CPOE (Metzger and Fortin 2003). Healthcare reform became a presidential campaign issue in 1992, and newly elected President Clinton commissioned a study in 1993 of how to reform the system. The hospital’s Medical Informatics director cited President Clinton’s initiative as a specific trigger that spurred hospital leaders to undertake the CPOE project in 1993, in response to impending regulatory change.

## [2] Change in Role Networks

Hospital leaders organized networks of clinicians to carry out their cost control and quality improvement agenda through the CPOE project. The CPOE project team included one physician working full time and six physicians working 10 hours per week on the project. These physicians participated in package evaluation and selection and were responsible for determining the implementation process, procedural issues related to physicians' practices, and system validation. The team reported to a medical informatics subcommittee of the medical executive committee and to the CPOE project steering committee, which was chaired by the medical director of clinical informatics. Nursing and ancillary departments, including lab, radiology, pharmacy, pathology, and EKG, formed user groups, with some members working full-time on the project to develop and test the system interface and to conduct training and support activities. Within and between groups, members worked together closely for three years to bring the project to fruition. The CPOE project leader explained this process:

In order to take advantage of the system, we had to put together department order sets. In order to do these, we had to have multidisciplinary teams look at the order sets. Doctors, nurses, and ancillary would look at how groups of patients were managed....It was the first time they had actually sat down and talked about it.

That is, prior to implementation of the CPOE system, multidisciplinary networks of clinical and ancillary roles formed to prepare the organization for CPOE.

## [3] Change in Technology

Although the hospital purchased a software package, the system interface required significant customization to accommodate department order sets for each clinical department. The CPOE teams designed the system's interface (called order pathways) to influence physicians' decision-making in ways that reflected the hospital's goals for cost control, quality improvement, and clinical standardization. For example, less-expensive generic drugs were substituted for brand-name drugs in an order set, and drug cost information was provided on screen. A physician administrator explained this goal:

The methods by which docs put in non-formulary drugs are rather cumbersome, and we made it cumbersome because we didn't want them to put in non-formulary drugs.

Nonetheless, the CPOE groups also deferred to physicians' preferences when implementing system features, even if the feature would aid other departments. A pharmacist noted:

One of the things we've requested is to force people to put in allergies before they enter medications, and the system could do that, but the physicians didn't want that [feature implemented] because it might slow them down.

A team composed of nurses, lab technicians, and pharmacists coded the order sets into the system interface. Thus, the CPOE technology per se was shaped through the actions of members of the networks of CPOE teams in order to promote changes in clinical practices.

## [4] Change in Roles

Physicians were the main targets of role change from CPOE system use. The director of medical informatics described the rationale:

We made the decision the system has to be physician-focused, because physicians make most of the decisions about what happens to the patient in the institution, what medications are given, what tests are ordered, and for that reason, the system should really be perceived as a decision support system.

The CPOE system influenced physicians' clinical decision-making by providing reminders, alerts, and cost information at the point of care and increasing their compliance with standardized order pathways through the interface design. A nurse commented:

It [the CPOE] doesn't lock the physician into doing anything he doesn't want to do, but it gives physicians choices, and helps them to make those choices based on the literature.

By 1997, physicians directly entered 62 percent of all orders (50 percent of attending physicians' orders; 80 percent of residents' orders). As Table 1 indicates, this level of hands-on CPOE use by attending physicians had a significant effect on physicians' actions and clinical measures at the hospital. The director of medical informatics commented:

The system has had a big effect, because you can change [physician] behavior in a month or two versus two to three years through simple education. When we built new screens [order sets], we saw a tenfold change in use [of certain drugs] immediately.

Physicians retained professional autonomy regarding clinical decision-making, and their autonomy extended to their use of the CPOE system. Attending physicians, who are not hospital employees, were not required to use the system directly. Residents (who are employees) were required to enter their own orders. Nonetheless, all physicians retained the right to issue verbal or telephone orders, which nurses then entered into the CPOE system.

## [2] Change in Roles

Implementation of the CPOE system did not significantly alter clinical roles per se. Physicians still diagnosed patients and prescribed treatments, nurses still carried out patient care, and pharmacists still filled medication orders. Indeed, the CPOE system was designed to mimic professional practices. Nonetheless, subtle changes in skills and tasks related to clinical order management (non-relational elements of roles) were evident (Table 2).

First, attending physicians and residents assumed some responsibility for entering clinical orders into the CPOE system. CPOE usage patterns varied by specialty and among physicians. For example, oncologists seldom used the system directly: orders typically included a number of pharmaceuticals and were complicated to enter into the system; and oncologists often issued orders in their office for delivery in the hospital. Attending physicians who seldom admitted patients to the hospital did not take the time to overcome the system's steep learning curve. On the other hand, some physicians used the system extensively. A physician commented on the advantages:

If your doses are way off the recommended doses that are already on the screen, it prevents you from making gross errors in dosing medications.

A few physicians had work stations installed in their office in the hospital's adjacent physician office building. They reported using remote access to patient data and test results to assess patients' conditions and to schedule in-hospital patient visits ("computer rounding").

Because nurses entered written, verbal, and telephone orders into the CPOE system, they needed to understand how the CPOE order pathway screens operated. In contrast to the paper-based system, a nurse commented that, “you have to talk the system language to put in an order.” The unit secretary’s role did not disappear, although the number of people in this role decreased and tasks changed considerably. Rather than transcribing orders, unit secretaries ensured the printer was working, distributed printed copies of orders to nurses, and managed the system’s paper reports, as a unit secretary described:

My responsibility is to ensure the [patient] chart doesn't overrun, get too large, so you can't even open it. Those have to be thinned.

A major task change for pharmacy and laboratory departments was the need to keep their departmental system synchronized with the CPOE system. CPOE orders were transferred electronically, but some orders failed to transfer or transferred incorrectly. Pharmacists and laboratory technologists diagnosed problems between the systems and entered problem orders into their own system. This placed an administrative burden on pharmacists:

We know that whatever comes across to our [pharmacy] system, our system has to match the CPOE system. The CPOE system is the chart system. We also have to look at ourselves, and prevent errors from occurring.

As a result of this dual system maintenance, pharmacists became knowledgeable about the CPOE system interface and its order pathways. Similarly, lab technologists had to understand where orders might be held up due to built-in timing delays or unexpected system downtime in the automated data exchange process. A laboratory supervisor explained:

The lab people need to do a lot more thinking about how the system itself works than before....There's so many things that could go wrong with an order, and so many different places where the order could be.

Lab supervisors believed that increased workload for electronic order handling had overwhelmed cost reductions from having orders transferred into the lab system electronically.

## [3] Change in Role Dyad Interactions

Changes in skills and tasks influenced the ways in which clinicians and ancillary staff interacted to manage clinical orders. We identified three patterns related to CPOE use: reductions in routine order information exchanges; deference to physicians' preferences for using the CPOE system; and heightened interdependency among nurses, pharmacists, and laboratory technologists to manage orders in the CPOE system.

First, with the CPOE system, physicians, nurses, and unit secretaries had less need to interact to exchange routine order information. Structuring physician orders in the CPOE system clarified some order-related communication. Nurses and unit secretaries did not need to interpret physicians' handwriting or intentions into an actionable order. A nurse commented that nurses and physicians were more self-sufficient in order handling tasks:

“Unit secretaries used to be the central focus that was direct to what was happening between her and the charge nurse, and now everyone is pretty much maintaining their own [patient information].

Many physicians retrieved test results and checked patient data rather than calling nurses or asking them for patient charts. A physician explained:

I can find out a good deal about a patient without calling the nurse, calling the lab, having to go physically over there and walk on the floor and pull the chart.

The nurses and physicians we interviewed perceived increased self-reliance as a modest change in their interactions. A physician commented that nurses have not “coddled” doctors for many years. A floor nurse similarly commented that while physicians could leave nurses “out of the loop” of patient care decisions by using the CPOE system, she had not observed this outcome.

Having access to the CPOE data allowed pharmacists to expand their consulting role and improve rapport with physicians for screening certain medication-related problems, “without leaving our seat or even without a phone call.” At the time of the study, the hospital had just completed a program to reduce overuse of a broad-spectrum antibiotic. Pharmacists used the CPOE system to identify physicians prescribing the drug and inform them of clinical guidelines.

On the other hand, shifting order-entry tasks to physicians created unanticipated ambiguities and procedural issues for hospital staff, as nurses, pharmacists, and laboratory technologists deferred to physicians' preferences for how to use the system. During the first few months of CPOE implementation, nurses tried to insist that physicians do their own order entry. They removed paper order forms from the nursing stations, as per the implementation team's suggestion. This tactic was only modestly successful, however. Trainers advised nurses not to "harass" doctors but to encourage them to use the system by offering help. A floor nurse commented:

The physicians don't have time to put it [an order] into the CPOE, so they give us a verbal order, and we're kind of doing their job. It's frustrating.

A unit secretary observed how each patient-care floor had settled into a routine that accommodated their physicians' preferences for system use:

We've gotten to know our docs real well. We don't want to cause stress. We know who writes, who clicks them in [the CPOE system], so people just adjust.

After 2 years, hands-on use by physicians had leveled off, leaving nurses to enter about a third of all orders.

Another example of role dyad interactions related to monitoring patient allergies. The pharmacy system provided allergy warnings, and the CPOE system also provided limited warnings. If the pharmacist received a warning about a possible allergic reaction from the pharmacy system, the pharmacist was uncertain whether the physician saw this alert in the CPOE system but considered it insignificant. Physicians were supposed to “status the allergy” in the CPOE system in this case. Sometimes, however, they walked away from the computer before the system flashed the warning. The pharmacist was left with the dilemma of calling the physician to clarify the order or assuming that he was aware of the allergy but did not “status the allergy” properly:

Before, we would still have to make the phone calls, but it was easier because we didn't have to assume whether the doctor saw it or not....Now with the system, we have more information but there's that uncertainty, did he see it? Actually it's the anxiety, did he see it and am I going to be bothering him?... They get upset if we bother them.

Before calling and possibly annoying the physician, pharmacists looked to their own system or the CPOE system for additional information, such as a previous drug reaction, or called the nurse to clarify the physician's intent, even though this created extra work.

Similar patterns of deferring to physicians regarding CPOE use were evident in interactions between physicians, nurses, and laboratory technologists. In the process of transcribing physicians' written orders in the paper-based system, nurses could detect and informally consolidate overlapping tests ordered by different physicians (for instance, a "chem 7" is a subset of a "chem 11"). The CPOE system made it difficult for physicians to check existing orders, and many physicians did not take the time to navigate through the order pathways to avoid requesting redundant tests. Nurses were reluctant to cancel a duplicate order in the CPOE system. They expected the lab technologists to manage duplicate requests. Lab technologists expected nurses to do so. One laboratory supervisor commented:

What we've found...is a lot of them [nurses] won't go in and cancel the duplicates they see, because they're overriding what a physician ordered....The nursing staff didn't feel they had the right to do any cancellations, it's not their place. But in the old days with the paper, they'd see it, they'd just merge it.

Lab technologists and nurses could not force physicians to alter their system-use behavior. Instead, the datagate system was adjusted to hold orders for a period of time in order to automatically consolidate overlapping orders. Interviewees indicated this solution had not proven to be as effective as the nurses' intervention in the paper-based system. Building in timing delays also ran counter to the clinical goal of expediting order completion.

The CPOE system was complex and operationally fragile, as was evident in the problems with automated order handling. Uncertainties about how physicians actually used the CPOE system (as described earlier) contributed to ambiguity and complexity. As a result, nurses, pharmacists, and lab technologists had to work closely together in day-to-day activities to ensure the CPOE system functioned properly. This increased interdependence among them for clinical order management. Nurses often called the lab to clarify and resolve the type of order processing glitches described above. The nurses and laboratory technologists we interviewed agreed that their interactions had become more interdependent and conflict-ridden. Pharmacists assumed a consulting role toward nurses. They sometimes advised nurses on how to enter difficult orders, particularly on night shifts when system support staff was not available, and they entered particularly complex orders for them.

## [4] Change in Role Networks

Clinicians who were knowledgeable about the hospital's procedures and its information systems were assigned or volunteered to join various permanent committees. Special interest groups (SIGs) for pharmacy and nursing, including staff members who helped customize the CPOE interface and train users, acted as problem solving groups to work on procedural problems and suggested improvements to the CPOE system. CPOE analysts, a group of two nurses, a pharmacist, and a laboratory technologist, left their clinical departments to become Information Systems department employees. They worked closely with the SIGs and the Medical Informatics Sub-Committee to evaluate system improvements, provide expert knowledge on clinical procedures related to the system, and code order sets changes into the interface. Members of these groups acted together and interacted with physicians, nurses, pharmacists, laboratory staff, and others across the hospital to resolve procedural problems and to plan future developments of the system.

## Institutional Change, Disease Management Programs, and CPOE Use

Although President Clinton's healthcare reform plan was never implemented, significant regulatory changes were enacted during his presidency (Bazzoli et al. 2004). Moreover, pressures to reduce costs and assess service quality continued to fuel turbulence in the U.S. healthcare sector (Bodenheimer 1999). Ongoing institutional change triggered a third change process (Figure 4) related to the CPOE system at the hospital.

## [1] Institutionally Triggered Change: Initiation of Disease Management Programs

To address cost containment, efficiency, and quality pressures, healthcare leaders increasingly looked to clinical standards. Furthermore, terms like evidence-based medicine appeared in the professional literature (Institute of Medicine 1999). Among hospitals, disease management programs developed as an organizational response to these pressures (Bodenheimer 1999). Briefly, disease management programs involve developing clinical pathways, or protocols for the diagnostic tests, medications, and clinical procedures, to cost-effectively manage patients with high-incidence and high-cost health problems (e.g., asthma, heart failure).

Following CPOE implementation, hospital administrators established disease management task forces to develop and implement clinical pathways. The goals of the disease management program were to influence the care of populations of patients with certain diseases, and to affect physicians' practice patterns treating these patient groups.

## [2] Change in Role Networks

Disease management task forces were composed of physicians, nurses, pharmacists, and healthcare analysts. At the time of our field study, task forces had developed clinical pathways for cardiac failure, pneumonia, and asthma and were addressing other high-cost, high-incidence diseases. These teams drew data collected through the CPOE system for evidence to support their clinical pathways. A task force member commented on how CPOE data was important to these groups' efforts:

It's a wealth of data, and it's going to come down to cost-effectiveness studies. That's what it's going to be used for...to see how the resources are being used and if those are the best ways to use the resources.

## [3] Change in Technology

The disease management task forces worked with SIG members and the CPOE analyst group to design the system interfaces that would implement the clinical pathways as order pathways. CPOE analysts then coded the interfaces into the system. CPOE system data were also extracted from the operational system and fed into an enterprise repository of clinical data (the ERD) and into a benchmarking system (CARE). Similar to the first change process, these role networks of clinical and ancillary staff members were actively involved in adjusting the CPOE system interface to incorporate clinical and administrative goals.

## [4] Change in Roles

Implementation of clinical pathways in the CPOE system further contributed to the standardization in clinical decision-making that was initially evident with the CPOE implementation. A specialist pointed out that clinical pathways reduced variations in cardiac care, particularly for general practitioners or residents:

Cardiologists know how to manage heart failure because we do it all the time. Primary care physicians are not that familiar with it....They miss things; they keep patients in the hospital longer, they forget key drugs; they don't get the right drugs up to the right dosages. So this tool is mainly for the physician who doesn't take care of a lot of patients with heart failure.

The effectiveness of clinical pathways depended in part on physicians' use of the CPOE system, because the system interface encouraged physicians to follow the prescribed set of orders. Nonetheless, all orders eventually were entered into the CPOE system, so that physicians' practice patterns were documented completely in the system. The CPOE system thus allowed disease management task forces and other established clinical review committees to tighten monitoring of attending physicians' clinical practices. A clinical data analyst commented:

That type of data [on compliance] is going to come back to haunt them because we're going to say, just for information purposes, did you know that you're hospitalizing your patients for that particular condition three times as long as everyone else? Maybe we can help you with that. Let's look at the way you're doing it.

At the time of our research study, the hospital was experimenting with a benchmarking system (CARE, see Figure 1), which used CPOE data to compare a physician's cost profile to other physicians at the hospital and to national benchmarks with so-called physician report cards. One physician envisioned his profession being transformed by such information:

What it will boil down to is economic credentialing where they actually credential us, and give us privileges, based on how much money we're spending at the hospital. Because these systems are able to compare us on cost efficiency and utilization of hospital resources.

The physicians we interviewed were aware of administrators' intent to influence their decisions and of cost pressures on the hospital. A physician commented:

The physicians hold the key to the hospital's future, what they order, the way they order. The more they know the alternatives, a better way to treat something, the better off the hospital will be.

Among the physicians we interviewed, their willingness to use the CPOE system and to comply with the hospital's goals reflected their awareness of institutional pressures on the hospital and on physicians, as this physician commented:

The medical game now is costs, it's all costs. We are trying to preserve quality of care while reducing costs. If you don't have cost information, you really can't do an effective job of reducing costs.

Instead, they interacted cumulatively to engender social structure change at the hospital. Figure 5 depicts this interaction, and Table 3 summarizes evidence of cumulative social structure outcomes.

This change was triggered institutionally in the first and third processes. The CPOE technology was neither the ultimate nor the proximate change trigger. Instead, the CPOE was an outcome of this social structure change. Multidisciplinary cooperation facilitated the hospital's use of CPOE to achieve clinical and administrative improvement goals.

## [B] Increased Standardization of Clinical Decision-Making

Physicians retained the authority to make clinical decisions, but they did so with growing adherence to clinical guidelines and standards. This social structure change was triggered by hospital leaders' actions to sanction the role networks discussed above. It was then propagated through the actions of these groups to develop standardized clinical protocols, embed them into the CPOE system, and use CPOE data to monitor physicians' practice patterns. Physicians enacted this structure change in their day-to-day use of the system in accordance with administrators' design, albeit with the limitations noted earlier. The CPOE system was a proximate trigger of physician role change. Increasing standardization in clinical decision-making ultimately was triggered, however, by hospital leaders' responses to institutional change triggers. The networks of clinical roles that emerged to support the CPOE and embed standards were critical to this outcome.

## [C] Deference to Physicians' Preferences for CPOE Use

Hospital leaders, CPOE support groups, and disease management task forces acknowledged the autonomy of attending physicians when they designed the CPOE system. They developed usage policies that allowed attending physicians choices on whether to use the system and how to use it. In day-to-day interactions, nurses, pharmacists, and laboratory technologists accommodated physicians' preferences for CPOE use, even when they had to undertake extra work. That is, organization members enacted institutionalized social structures of hierarchy, status, and autonomy for physicians. Enactment of these structures presented an oppositional force to optimal use of CPOE and might have overwhelmed any of the change processes. Here, it moderated, but did not prevent, the cumulative influence of change processes to increase standardization of clinical decision-making.

## [D] Increased Interdependence for CPOE Operation

Hospital staff became more dependent on each other to ensure the complex, operationally fragile CPOE system operated effectively. Use of the CPOE system modestly affected non-relational and relational elements of roles related to clinical order management, influenced interactions between role dyads (with both enactment and change in interaction patterns), and contributed to permanent changes in role networks at the hospital. This technology-triggered change (Figure 3) related to what might be characterized as the “care and feeding” of the CPOE system. Nurses, unit secretaries, pharmacists, laboratory technologists, and physicians spent significant time submitting data to the system and retrieving data from it. They monitored its operation to detect processing glitches. They managed orders through joint efforts in day-to-day interactions and technical and procedural interventions of support groups. Permanent groups evolved from role networks established in the first process (Figure 2) and provided support for disease management task forces in the third process (Figure 4). This social structure change facilitated operation of the CPOE system and its use to achieve the hospital’s clinical objectives.

## Discussion

A key implication of this study is that social structure change associated with CPOE at the hospital emerged from the cumulative influence and interaction of institutionally triggered and technology-triggered change processes. Hospital leaders' response to change in the institutional environment provided the impetus and fueled social change. Implementation of the technology triggered social change as well as enactment of institutionalized patterns, which nonetheless facilitated the system's integration into clinical order management processes and the hospital's realization of clinical objectives. By teasing apart these influences, we demonstrated how accounting for institutional change in the organizational environment extended Barley's (1990) technology-triggered, micro-level analytic approach to provide a balanced perspective on the extent of social-structure change directly attributed to the CPOE technology.

Our analysis suggests that information technologies have a more-subtle and varied influence in social processes that lead to structure change than Barley's analytic approach implies. Only in the second change process (Figure 3) did social change "ripple" as Barley outlined, from technology-triggered changes in roles at the micro-level to social structure change (increased interdependency for CPOE operation). Unlike Barley, we found we had to treat the CPOE technology analytically as an integral component in the change process rather than as a static, external change trigger. In the two institutionally triggered change processes (Figures 2 and 4), changes in role networks preceded technology change and influenced the design and specification of the IT artifact. These changes then contributed to role change (increased standardization in clinical decision-making). Hislop et al. (2000) illustrate the importance of social networks to mobilize knowledge and resources to implement organizational IT, and Orlikowski et al. (1995) demonstrate how user-initiated mediation of technology can influence social structures. Here, increased multidisciplinary cooperation developed as organizational networks formed to customize, implement, and apply the CPOE system and disease management programs, not as a result of micro-level interactions related to use of the CPOE.

Contrasting CPOE technology with the imaging technologies that Barley studied highlights ways in which information technologies may differ in terms of the implications for aligning technology and social structure. Unlike imaging technologies, a core production technology, CPOE is primarily a communication and coordination technology. In our case study, the CPOE did not radically alter non-relational roles of physicians, nurses, or ancillary staff and thus had less of a “ripple” effect than the imaging technologies Barley studied. Information technologies typically have a broad organizational scope, whereas imaging technologies are primarily used in one hospital department. The CPOE required substantial cooperative efforts across hospital departments to customize the system and support its operation. Multidisciplinary role networks, which preceded and directed technology-triggered change, were thus essential to effective use of the CPOE system.

Imaging technologies have interpretive flexibility in use, as do all technologies (Orlikowski 1992). However, information technologies are customizable in design and use, and their designs can be altered after implementation, as users discover new applications (Orlikowski et al. 1995). At the hospital, members of multidisciplinary role networks customized the CPOE software package to embed clinical guidelines and standards, which then contributed to increasing clinical standardization. That is, the system was both a trigger for and an outcome of social change processes. System configurability facilitated organization members' enactment of institutionalized patterns when they deferred to physicians' preferences in system design and use, presenting an oppositional force to change. Thus, the CPOE artifact presented forces both for and in opposition to social structure change. These technical features were not inherent in the CPOE system but developed through organization members' adaptations of the CPOE system in design and use.

Given this extensive interpretive flexibility, it is not surprising that researchers have reported contradictory organizational outcomes from IT use (Robey and Boudrieu 1999). One might question whether researchers could reliably project any patterns of IT and structural alignment across organizations. Nonetheless, institutional changes are likely to trigger similar responses among organizations within an institutional context (Scott 1987, 2001) and some degree of consistency in IT activities is possible (Avgerou 2000). For example, the hospital's agendas for influencing attending physicians' practice patterns through a CPOE system, clinical guidelines, and physician profiling are increasingly being adopted in U.S. hospitals (Bodenheim 1999; Kholi and Kettinger 2004). By integrating analysis of institutional change triggers in the macro-level of the organizational environment that promote the adoption of such approaches with micro-level analysis of action and interaction within an organization—not simply as background or context but as an integral component of the analysis—similarity of patterns across organizations may become evident. Moreover, theoretically informed explanations of IT and structural alignment within organizations will likely be more comprehensive.

Combining macro- and micro-level analysis may also generate insights on IT-related change dynamics within specific industries. Here, we speculate that the interaction of institutional and technology change triggers that we observed at the research site may also be occurring across the U.S. healthcare sector. Clinical managers we interviewed noted that regulatory agencies expect hospitals to have such systems. As more hospitals adopt HIT in response to institutional pressures, regulatory agencies and other institutional actors can require more detailed reporting. As a result, healthcare workers can be monitored more closely in their day-to-day practices, increasing institutional pressure to standardize healthcare delivery practices. Such standardization will occur, or not occur, at the micro-level of action and interaction (La Pointe and Rivard 2005). However, the result could be a dynamic process at the industry level, in which (1) macro-level institutional change spurs healthcare providers to expand their use of HIT; (2) technology-triggered changes in clinical data collection and monitoring allow institutional actors to demand healthcare organizations provide data for monitoring; and (2) macro-level regulatory pressures and monitoring within organizations facilitate social structure changes at the micro-level, such as increased standardization. Research on the mutual influence of the institutional environment and organizational decision-making related to IT innovation and organizational change, as suggested by Chiasson and Davidson (2005) and Crowston and Myers (2004), will be needed to investigate such possibilities.

## Implications for Practice

Organizations continue to experience major failures with technology-related organizational change (Markus 2004) and, of special note here, CPOE systems (LaPointe and Rivard 2005). Our analysis highlighted the institutional forces that promoted and inhibited social structure changes yet nonetheless facilitated the hospital's ability to achieve many of its clinical goals. Some reflections on the circumstances of this critical case of CPOE system use are thus warranted.

First, project design decisions influenced how change processes evolved at the hospital. Involving physicians in the implementation project made integration of standardized care protocols and the CPOE system more palatable to these professionals. Deferring to physicians' preferences constrained the system's clinical effectiveness to some degree but also encouraged attending physicians' to use the CPOE system rather than to resist its implementation or demand its withdrawal (see LaPointe and Rivard 2005). The decision to limit the scope of the CPOE project by retaining existing ancillary systems—a rational choice given the time and resource demands of the CPOE system—led to technical and procedural issues with synchronizing systems. Other procedural problems arose from subtle interface design choices and patterns of interaction among clinicians and other CPOE system users. Continued attention to improving human-computer interface design in CPOE systems clearly is needed. In addition, system designers must look beyond individual-level use to assess how interactions among CPOE system users prevent or create errors. Our findings are consistent with issues medical informatics researchers have raised about the efficacy of CPOE systems to reduce medical errors and the possibility these systems generate errors (Ash, Berg, and Corera 2004; Berger and Kichak 2004; Koppel et al. 2005).

This case highlighted the importance of role networks for mobilizing implementation and realizing benefits from HIT use. To maintain the CPOE system's viability, the hospital was required to invest in permanent network structures (the myriad committees involved in resolving procedural problems, updating the system, and implementing quality initiatives). Tightly linking disease-management teams with the CPOE support groups further enabled effective CPOE system use. The CPOE project transformed into an ongoing process of clinical and information technology adaptation and assimilation. Such investments in human resources to support CPOE system use and operation are often not recognized or budgeted in IT projects. These human resource investments, though costly, enabled the hospital to realize a level of clinical performance goals that many hospitals have failed to achieve with CPOE systems.

## Conclusions

Aligning social structures and technology capabilities continues to be a significant barrier to IT-related organizational change in the healthcare industry as elsewhere. Our analysis illustrated how institutional and technology-change triggers can interact to engender changes in social structure to facilitate effective organizational use of IT. By systematically clarifying the structure changes directly attributable to IT use, we highlighted the critical role that macro-level institutional change can play as an impetus for organizational change. Through hermeneutic analysis, we provided a plausible and theoretically informed explanation of change and interaction at the micro-level of action, which incorporated consideration of macro-institutional influences.

Our empirically grounded observations reflect the historical circumstances and actions of organization members at the research site. Following Lee and Baskerville's (2003) framework for generalization, we have not attempted to generalize these findings to other settings. Nonetheless, we believe our analytic approach of integrating macro-level and micro-level change triggers will be useful to investigate the process of technology and structure alignment in other settings. We suggest that researchers will find systematic analysis of change triggers arising in the macro-level institutional environment particularly helpful to investigate IT-related change in professionalized fields, such as public accounting, healthcare, law, and education. Moreover, balancing macro-level and micro-level analysis helps the researcher to avoid arguments based on social or technological determinism and “inferential leaps” across levels of social analysis (Barley 1990).

HIT research is growing in importance and interest as policy makers and healthcare organizations increasingly look to IT to address social and economic problems with healthcare (Chiasson and Davidson 2004). Drawing theoretical approaches from the organizational and IS literature, our study complements socially informed analysis of CPOE systems in the medical informatics literature (Aarts et al. 2004; Ash et al. 2003), evaluation studies of whether HIT technologies are clinically effective (Evans et al. 1998; Overage et al. 1997; Schriger et al. 1997; Tierney et al. 1993), and empirical assessments of CPOE system use (Ash, Gorman et al. 2004; Lee et al. 1996; Murff and Kannry 2001) with theoretically informed explanations of why CPOE system implementation and use may or may not be successful (Kaplan 2001).

## About the Authors

Elizabeth J. Davidson is the W. Ruel Johnson Distinguished Professor of Information Technology Management at the Shidler

College of Business, University of Hawaii at Manoa. In her research, she investigates how organization members identify opportunities for using information technologies and orchestrate IT initiatives, with emphasis on socio-cognitive processes, discourse analysis, and institutional theory. Recent projects include a study of collaborative applications of IT in scientific research activities and an action research project investigating barriers to the assimilation of health information technologies and chronic care management practices among small physician practices. She is an active member of the International Federation for Information Processing Working Group 8.2 on Information Systems and Organizations. Dr. Davidson serves as an associate editor for MIS Quarterly and European Journal of Information Systems and serves on the editorial board of Information and Organizations.

William G. Chismar is a professor of Information Technology Management and the Associate Dean for Academic Affairs at the Shidler College of Business, University of Hawaii at Manoa, where he is also an adjunct professor in the John A. Burns School of Medicine. Dr. Chismar's current research focuses on the economic evaluation and adoption of hospital information systems, the adoption of electronic medical record systems, and the use of the Internet in pediatrics. Dr. Chismar founded and serves as chair of the Information Technology in Healthcare Track of the Hawaii International Conference on System Sciences.
