# Enabling service co-production: a theory-building case study

Audrey Grace<sup>a</sup>, Rob Gleasure<sup>b</sup>, Patrick Finnegan<sup>c</sup> and Tom Butler <sup>a</sup>

<sup>a</sup>University College Cork, Cork, Ireland; <sup>b</sup>Copenhagen Business School, Copenhagen, Denmark; <sup>c</sup>University of New South Wales, Sydney, Australia

## ABSTRACT

Service co-production between professionals and customers is an increasingly popular alternative to traditional “o<sup>f</sup> the shelf” models of service delivery. There is, however, little empirical research describing how services are co-produced with individual customers, nor the most suitable roles for IS/IT in this co-production. This study unpacks how services are co-produced by developing a substantive Theory of Service Co-Production activities (and mediating contextual factors) in the <sup>fi</sup>nancial services sector. A two-phase theory-building approach is employed. First, a preliminary model is derived based on Activity Theory and extant research. Second, this preliminary model is re<sup>fi</sup>ned and elaborated upon through an extensive empirical analysis of the co-production of <sup>fi</sup>nancial services. The study further reveals four key contradictions driving service co-production. These contradictions highlight the incomplete alignment of motives and goals between service professionals and customers, as well as the trickle-down IS/IT impact of this misalignment. The study concludes by discussing the enabling and constraining in<sup>fl</sup>uence of IS/IT in service co-production.

## 1. Introduction

There is growing demand for the “co-production” of services, i.e., the constructive customer participation in the service design process (Auh, Bell, Mcleod, & Shih, 2007; Bettencourt, Ostrom, Brown, & Roundtree, 2002) by well-educated and informed customers (Jaakkola & Halinen, 2006; Laing, Hogg, & Winkelman, 2005; Prahalad & Ramaswamy, 2000). Such co-production of services appears the most reliable way to ensure those customers can create or co-create value when they begin using that service (Eichentopf, Kleinaltenkamp, & Stiphout, 2011; Lusch & Vargo, 2006; Vargo & Lusch, 2004, 2008). Yet co-production presents enormous challenges for organisations, primarily at the customer interface (Hogg, Laing, & Winkelman, 2003; Laing et al., 2005; Prahalad & Ramaswamy, 2002). This is because coproduction necessitates a collaborative and often information intensive dialogue between a service professional and a customer, in which both share and integrate each other’s knowledge and resources (Andreu, Sánchez, & Mele, 2010; Ballantyne and Varey, 2006; Etgar, 2008; Jaworski & Kohli, 2006; Payne, Storbacka, & Frow, 2008; Schultze, Salonen, Prandelli, & Van Alstyne, 2007). This culminates in joint agreement on a service: (i) that satis<sup>fi</sup>es the customer’s individual needs and expectations; (ii) that can be delivered by the organisation; and (iii) for which the customer is willing to pay (Ballantyne, Frow, Varey, & Payne, 2011).

It is unsurprising, therefore, that service coproduction has received considerable attention across many disciplines, including Service Science (cf. Jaakkola & Halinen, 2006; Moeller, 2008; Spohrer, Maglio, Bailey, & Gruhl, 2007; Vargo, 2008; Vargo, Maglio, & Akaka, 2008), marketing (cf. Aarikka-Stenroos & Jaakkola, 2011; Ballantyne & Varey, 2006; Etgar, 2008; Payne et al., 2008), and management (cf. Bettencourt et al., 2002; Möller, Rajala, & Westerlund, 2008; Ordanini & Pasini, 2008; Prahalad & Ramaswamy, 2004b), to name a few. Several landmark frameworks provide strong descriptive overviews of co-production (e.g., Gronroos, 2008; Payne et al., 2008; Prahalad & Ramaswamy, 2004b). However, they do little to explain the speci<sup>fi</sup>c tools and structures necessary to practically operationalise service co-production (Frow, Nenonen, Payne, & Storbacka, 2015; Gronroos & Ravald, 2011; Osborne & Strokosch, 2013; Perks, Gruber, & Edvardsson, 2012). Hence, there have been ongoing calls for empirically driven research into how these co-production activities are taking place (Ballantyne et al., 2011; Chesbrough & Spohrer, 2006; Greczy, Izumi, & Hasida, 2010; Gustafsson, Kristensson, & Witell, 2012; Payne et al., 2008; Roth & Menor, 2003; Vargo et al., 2008). More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, there is an increasing need for research on the e<sup>f</sup>ective tools, processes, and engagement platforms for service co-production (Barczak, 2012; Brandsen & Honingh, 2016; Frow et al., 2015).

Despite these calls for IT-focused research, most information systems (IS) research on service coproduction has focused on how IT can be used to gather information, customise interfaces, and automate processes. Notable areas of interest include: (i) customer relationship management (CRM) technology (Gonzalez, Giachetti, & Rameriz, 2004; Koole &

Mandelbaum, 2002; Zablah, Bellenger, & Wesley, 2004), (ii) self-service technology (Schultze et al., 2007; Shim, Shin, & Nottingham, 2002), and (iii) intelligent personalisation agents (Tam & Ho, 2006; Tien, 2006). These areas of interest, while valuable, neglect deeper issues of shared understanding and mutual persuasion that represent the practical negotiationbased bi-directionality of service co-production. The result is a lack of research that puts IT at the heart of the phenomenon as it appears in real-world contexts, meaning the implications of this body of research for design and practice remain vague (Benbasat & Zmud, 2003; Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001).

Therefore, the objective of this study is to build an empirically grounded model of IT-enabled service co-production by exploring the factors that mediate co-production activities between service professionals and customers. We <sup>fi</sup>rst derive a preliminary framework from extant research using Activity Theory (AT) to characterise social and tool-based mediators of service co-production activities. We then use a qualitative case study of <sup>fi</sup>nancial services coproduction in Ireland to re<sup>fi</sup>ne this model. We further identify contradictions arising from competing interests between Service Professionals (SPs) and customers, the nature of which signi<sup>fi</sup>- cantly impacts on the role of IS/IT. We conclude by discussing the implications of the study for practice and research.

## 2. Theoretical grounding

## 2.1. AT as a lens

AT is selected as a conceptual lens for theory development in this study for four reasons. The <sup>fi</sup>rst reason is the centrality of shared understanding in the service co-production process. AT has its origins in Vygotsky’s (1978) cultural-historical psychological theory of consciousness. Vygotsky (1978, 1979, 1997) argued an individual’s capacity for higher psychological functions was mediated by their ability to internalise sophisticated representations of the social world around them, which would then allow them to externalise increasingly nuanced desired outcomes in that social world. Thus, these internalising and externalising activities determine our internal mental representation of the world, the utility of which is continuously evaluated against the outcomes produced (Kaptelinin, 1996; Leont’ev, 1978). AT further argues that internalising activities cannot be understood if they are analysed in isolation from externalising activities because it is the constant transformation between “the external” and “the internal” that is the very basis of human cognition and activity (Bannon, 1997; Kaptelinin, Nardi, & Macaulay, 1999; Mwanza, 2002). Thus, the <sup>fi</sup>rst research question:

● How do specific internalising and externalising activities influence service co-production outcomes?

The second reason for selecting AT is this study’s focus on the technologies that support service coproduction. AT argues that a human being’s interactions with their environment are not direct; rather they are mediated by culturally embedded tools (Leont’ev, 1978; Vygotsky, 1978). These can be (i) primary tools, including material and digital artefacts (ii) secondary tools, including language, signs, or concepts, and (iii) tertiary tools, including cultural institutional systems (Hasan, 1998). Vygotsky (1978) argued that while physical instruments (primary tools) are relatively easy to create and adapt, psychological instruments (secondary and tertiary tools) were the result of long-term ongoing internalisation and externalisation sense-making processes. AT therefore brings tools and technologies to the centre of analysis for service co-production. Thus, the second research question:

## ● How do tool-based mediators influence specific service co-production activities?

The third reason to select AT is the social nature of service co-production. Modern AT extends the dyadic view of interactions with social mediators, speci-<sup>fi</sup>cally the nature of the community involved/a<sup>f</sup>ected, the explicit or implicit rules governing the activity, and the division of labour among di<sup>f</sup>erent actors (Engeström, 1987, 2000; Kaptelinin & Nardi, 2006; Kuutti, 1996). This perspective maps closely to the demands of this study, which positions co-production activities within an assemblage of nuanced and interdependent social and technical constraints. Further, the goal-oriented nature of service co-production resonates with AT, which assumes a work-based perspective in which activities are driven by tangible outcomes (Bannon, 1991; Bannon & Bødker, 1991; Bødker, 1991; Korpela et al., 2004; Kuutti, 1991). Thus, the third research question:

● How do social mediators influence specific service co-production activities?

The fourth reason for selecting AT, and perhaps the most important, is the potentially adversarial nature of service co-production. AT encourages the analysis of “contradictions” and “tensions” as a driving force of change (Engeström, Meittinen, & Punamäki, 1999). These contradictions can be (i) primary contradictions within an element of an activity (e.g., con<sup>fl</sup>icting rules), (ii) secondary contradictions between elements (e.g., rules that con<sup>fl</sup>ict with tools), (iii) tertiary contradictions between competing versions of the same activity (e.g., di<sup>f</sup>erent goals), and (iv) quaternary contradictions between di<sup>f</sup>erent activities in the same activity system (Engeström, 1987). The diversity of stakeholders means service coproduction must adapt to varying interests among SPs and customers. This analysis of contradictions o<sup>f</sup>ers a driving explanatory mechanism for this adaptation. Thus, the fourth research question:

● How do diferent tensions or contradictions drive the adaptation of services during service coproduction activities?

The <sup>fi</sup>rst three questions guide preliminary model development in the following sections. The fourth question is used during data gathering and analysis to explore broader contextual factors that may impact how this model should be operationalised and interpreted.

## 2.2. Types of service co-production activities

Analysis of extant literature identi<sup>fi</sup>es four types of co-production activities; one externalising construct and three internalising constructs (see Table 1). The <sup>fi</sup>rst (externalising) construct refers to Information Exchange and Dialogue between the SP and the customer, i.e., all external communication. Greater levels of Information Exchange and Dialogue are essential between parties endeavouring to solve equivocal or ambiguous problems (Daft, Lengel, & Trevino, 1987), particularly when participants’ task-speci<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge is high (Kahai & Cooper, 2003), as is generally the case in service co-production (Laing et al., 2005; Xue & Heim, 2005).

The second construct describes an internalising activity, Service Relationship Building, between the SP and the customer. This is argued to be very important for co-production (Gutek, Groth, & Cherry, 2002; Hsu et al., 2012), as the SP and the customer internalise their experiences of interacting together over time (Ballantyne & Varey, 2006). Each party thus forms opinions of the other’s capabilities and integrity (Gutek, Groth, & Cherry, 2002; Ballantyne and Varey 2006). This provides structural support for co-production (Ballantyne & Varey, 2006) and enables SPs and customers to increase their productivity within ongoing service interactions (Ballantyne & Varey, 2006; Mattsson, 1994; Mills, Chase, & Margulies, 1983).

The third construct describes a further internalising activity, namely Mutual Learning between the SP and the customer. The importance of higher levels of Mutual Learning for service co-production is well documented (Lundvall, 1993; Mattsson, 1994; Voss et al., 2004; Ballantyne & Varey, 2006). SPs can encourage this shared bi-directional evolution of understanding by asking customers to assist them in interpreting service-related information (Matthing et al., 2004). This enables a form of learning that ultimately lays the foundation for innovation in service co-production (Seely-Brown & Duguid, 1991; Lundvall, 1993). Thus, greater Mutual Learning “result[s] in new ways of acting according to the insights gained by the re<sup>fl</sup>ection” (Wikström, 1996, p. 11).

The fourth construct describes the third and <sup>fi</sup>nal internalising activity, Mutual Adjustment. It is through Mutual Adjustment that the SP and the customer alter their beliefs/expectations of the service and/or how they will interact together in response to each other’s expressed beliefs and/or behaviours (Prahalad & Ramaswamy, 2000; Price et al., 1995). Mutual Adjustment is posited as the primary means of organising and co-operating during co-production between an SP and a customer (Larsson & Bowen, 1989; Ramirez, 1999; Surprenant & Solomon, 1987). It is argued that the SP must lead the service interactions in co-producing the service (Larsson & Bowen, 1989), so playing a “diagnostic role” (Surprenant &

## 2.3. Tool-based mediators of service co-production

According to AT, a tool may be both enabling and limiting in that it provides a set of options from established patterns of achieving the object of the activity, yet it restricts the interaction around the capabilities of that particular tool (Kuutti, 1996; Wertsch, 1997). A detailed analysis of extant literature identi<sup>fi</sup>es two constructs concerning the tool-based mediators of service coproduction. These constructs are listed in Table 2, along with brief explanations and supporting references from the literature. The <sup>fi</sup>rst construct, the Collaboration-Oriented Servicescape describes the technica and psychological tools used during service interactions between the SP and the customer (Bitner, 1992; Bowen & Jones, 1986; Mills & Moberg, 1982; Wener, 1985). It has been argued that a more Collaboration-Oriented Servicescape can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly impact on service coproduction because “customers rely on this evidence for clues in forming their own personal mental reality of what the service is” (Bowen & Jones, 1986, p. 375). IS/ IT applications constitute part of the Collaborative-Oriented Servicescape and are particularly important, as they have the potential to in<sup>fl</sup>uence service coproduction outcomes (Meyronin, 2004).

The second construct, Information Accessibility, describes the accessibility of service-related information outside of interactions between the SP and the customer. The information that the SP or customer access independently empowers both parties for the service co-production activity (Hogg et al., 2003; Laing et al., 2005; Prahalad & Ramaswamy, 2004a; Xue & Heim, 2005). For example, Hogg et al. (2003) found that patients who extensively accesses health care information over the Internet prior to a health care service interaction were much more informed about the service, thus more likely to critically evaluate the suggestions of health care professionals.

## 2.4. Social mediators of service co-production

Analysis of extant literature identi<sup>fi</sup>es three constructs concerning the social mediators of service coproduction (see Table 3).

The <sup>fi</sup>rst construct describes the Customer-Oriented Service Climate, a construct characterised by a commitment by SPs to engage in collaborative processes with customers (Bowen & Ford, 2002; Wikström, 1996; Zablah et al., 2004). A more Customer-Oriented Service Climate has been established as an important factor for successful service co-production (Mattsson, 1994), however maintaining this climate means managing all of the service information visible to the customer (Bowen & Jones, 1986) as well as the values, meanings and assumptions that customers perceive in their formal and informal interactions with the SP (Schneider & Bowen, 2010).

The second construct to emerge from existing literature is Organisational Socialisation (Groth, 2005; Lengnick-Hall et al., 2000). Greater Organisational Socialisation o<sup>f</sup>ers more ad hoc involvement of the customer in speci<sup>fi</sup>c tasks during the service interaction (Schneider, 1986), leading to more favourable perceptions of the SP and more e<sup>fi</sup>cient co-production (Kelley et al., 1990). Socialisation of customers can be accomplished through training and incentives (Lengnick-Hall, 1996, Bowers, Martin, & Luker, 1990), realistic service previews (Rodie & Kleine, 2000), and exposure to organisational literature and other environmental cues (Kelley et al., 1990, Bowers et al., 1990).

The third construct is Service Professional Autonomy, which is needed to empower an SP with the freedom and <sup>fl</sup>exibility to respond to customers’ requests without unnecessary administrative interference (Bowen & Ford, 2002; Frenkel et al., 1998; Hartline et al., 2000). Jaakkola and Halinen (2006) found a degree of contradiction regarding the importance of Service Professional Autonomy, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally the value may di<sup>f</sup>er according to the nature of the problem, the service interaction, and consumers’ professions. Nevertheless, there is evidence that Service Professional Autonomy typically plays a vital role in successful service co-production (e.g., Bowen and Ford, 2002; Hartline et al., 2000; Lusch et al., 2007).

## 3. Research method

A summary of the constructs identi<sup>fi</sup>ed and the proposed relationships is presented in a preliminary model in Figure 1. This study performs an interpretive case study (Walsham, 1995) to inform and expand this preliminary model. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we seek to determine the presence of speci<sup>fi</sup>c constructs, the themes that characterise those constructs, and the relationships linking di<sup>f</sup>erent co-production activities and mediating in<sup>fl</sup>uences.

## 3.1. Data gathering

The case study presented in this paper is a single-case design focusing on the <sup>fi</sup>nancial services industry in Ireland. The <sup>fi</sup>nancial services domain is selected for three reasons: (i) it is mediated by long-established symbolic and material tools as well as emerging disruptive digital technologies, (ii) it is mediated by both longstanding social structures as well as emerging digital relationships, and (iii) the desired outcomes are comparatively easy to gauge. These three qualities a<sup>f</sup>ord a variety of rich but relatable co-production behaviours that are well suited to exploratory theorising. Ireland is selected as a geographical site because <sup>fi</sup>nancial services are viewed as a key component of the Irish economy, a view that has survived despite notable pressure on the sector following the global <sup>fi</sup>nancial crisis and changes to the EU (cf. Armour, 2017; Bergin, Garcia-Rodriguez, Mcinerney, Morgenroth, & Smith, 2016; Honohan, 2016). This has required signi<sup>fi</sup>cant adaptability in <sup>fi</sup>nancial o<sup>f</sup>erings as institutions seek to attract and sustain high value customers (Howarth, 2018). A singlecase design therefore allows this study to “stay close to the data” in this context and explore complex and subtle behaviours that could become obscured in the rush to abstract across comparative cases (Flyvbjerg, 2006; Stake, 1994).

The embedded units of analysis for the study were three business ecosystems (Gossain & Kandiah, 1998; Iansiti & Levien, 2004; Moore, 1996), each of which incorporated: (i) a lead organisation which provides individually tailored <sup>fi</sup>nancial services to customers; (ii) a number of producer organisations who produce and supply the lead organisation with <sup>fi</sup>nancial products/services (including banks and building societies); (iii) a number of partner organisations who also provide <sup>fi</sup>nancial products/services to customers (including stock broking organisations and <sup>fi</sup>nancial consultancy <sup>fi</sup>rms); (iv) IT providers who supply the lead organisation with IT-based <sup>fi</sup>nancial service solutions (including application software providers and strategic IT consultancy organisations); and (v) private customers of individually tailored <sup>fi</sup>nancial services. This allowed multiple opportunities to observe di<sup>f</sup>erent co-production behaviours and to pursue emerging insights. Table 4 presents an overview of study participants, including some overlapping across ecosystems.

Data were gathered over a 24-month period, and involved a total of 60 h of contact time with 10 SPs who participate in service co-production; 8 directors within these <sup>fi</sup>nancial service organisations (including 2 operations directors with responsibility for their organisations’ IT operations); 13 internal and external IT providers; and 3 industry experts. In addition, direct observations were used to gain a deeper understanding of how service co-production is achieved in practice. Additionally, ongoing service interactions between an SP and one customer in Ecosystem B were also observed to provide additional depth and witness activities at <sup>fi</sup>rst hand. This phase of the research occurred over 13 weeks from the initial discussion on the desired service to <sup>fi</sup>nal agreement on the service o<sup>f</sup>ering. It involved attending four SP/customer meetings and one meeting between the customer, their accountant, and the SP (total of 7 h). Post-observation interviews (5 h) were also conducted with both the SP and the customer for veri<sup>fi</sup>cation purposes. Semi-structured interview guides (Patton, 1980) were used to guide all interviews. Following interviews, telephone conversations, and emails with interviewees were used to clarify and re<sup>fi</sup>ne issues that emerged during transcription. In addition, data were triangulated using document analysis with approximately 200 pages of documentation gathered as part of the study.

## 3.2. Data analysis

Data were analysed using coding techniques proposed by Strauss and Corbin (1990). These techniques were originally developed to assist in grounded theory studies, whereby theory building avoids any commitment to preliminary theorising (cf. Charmaz, 2000; Glaser & Strauss, 1967; Urquhart, Lehmann, & Myers, 2010). However, grounded theory coding techniques are also commonly and legitimately applied in other forms of qualitative research to expand and re<sup>fi</sup>ne preliminary theorising (Strauss & Corbin, 1994; Mcghee, Marland, & Atkinson, 2007; Thornberg, 2012) – indeed such uses outnumber “classic” approaches that eschew a-priori theory (Matavire & Brown, 2013). This does not imply carte blanche for researchers to apply these techniques liberally across any context. Rather there is an obligation for researchers to ensure speci<sup>fi</sup>c techniques are applied in a manner that is philosophically consistent, does not compromise core assumptions, and that <sup>fi</sup>ts the needs of the study in question (cf. Birks, Fernandez, Levina, & Nasirin, 2013). For this study, we use constructs and relationships identi<sup>fi</sup>ed from previous literature as sensitising categories during Open and Axial Coding, without committing to these constructs and relationships. Used in this manner, these methods encourage researchers to be <sup>fl</sup>exible and creative (Sarker, Lau, & Sahay, 2000) while imposing systematic coding procedures to inform theory building (Strauss & Corbin, 1990). However, the use of data to build theory in this way also means the theory is not tested in the same way as hypothetical-deductive methods (Glaser & Strauss, 1967). Thus, the actual testing of these hypotheses falls to subsequent research.

The question of reliability (Golafshani, 2003; Patton, 1980) or “interpretive validity” (Maxwell, 1992) was addressed as follows. First, data analysis followed a rigorous coding and memoing processes, providing an audit trail of the process by which conclusions are reached. Second, the coding was undertaken by one of the researchers and veri<sup>fi</sup>ed by another, so ensuring conclusions were robust to diverging interpretations. Third, the validity of speci<sup>fi</sup>c themes was assessed using relational and variational sampling as recommended by Strauss and Corbin (1990). Finally, member checks (Creswell & Miller, 2000) or venting (Goetz & Lecompte, 1984) was used to validate researcher interpretations and observations – these were discussed in the aforementioned follow-up interviews with selected study participants. A more detailed account of coding is presented in Appendices 1 and 2.

## 4. Empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings

This section outlines the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the case study, summarised as the re<sup>fi</sup>ned model in Figure 2. The re<sup>fi</sup>ned model conceptualises the successful outcome of service co-production activities with high networth customers in <sup>fi</sup>nancial services as Agreeability of Service Solution (i.e., joint agreement on a suitable service for the customer). Three constructs (i.e., Service Relationship Building, Mutual Learning, and Mutual Adjustment) directly impact on the achievement of this outcome. These three constructs describe internalising activities that involve reconceptualisation and/or altering of knowledge and beliefs (Engeström, Brown, Christopher, & Gregory, 1997; Leont’ev, 1978; Vygotsky, 1979) of both the SP and the customer as they interact together over time. A further externalising activity, Information Exchange and Dialogue, impacts on co-production outcomes in secondary terms, as this externalising activity shapes how information is internalised.

Four other mediating constructs – two tool-based (Collaboration-Oriented Servicescape, Information Accessibility) and two social (Customer-Oriented Service Climate, and Process Formalisation) – were found to impact on these externalising and internalising activities. The latter of these, Process Formalisation, emerged directly from the data, while two other social mediators were abandoned that had been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the preliminary framework (i.e., Service Professional Autonomy and Organisational Socialisation).

## 4.1. Agreeability of service solution

Service co-production in the Irish <sup>fi</sup>nancial services sector is chie<sup>fl</sup>y component-based. Service components are used primarily because service o<sup>f</sup>erings must adhere to the compliance requirements of the Financial Services Industry Regulator. The SP interprets personal input from the customer about their individual context/preferences and then uses available service components to (iteratively) determine suitable service options for the customer from pre-existing service components (e.g., income protection policies, saving/investment plans, etc.). One SP described the co-production activity as a journey; “you bring them to the bottom of the hill and then say now which direction next? Then they’ll point in the direction and you bring them to the next point. . .but you do need both parties involved”.

Data suggested the Agreeability of Service Solution increases when both the SP and the customer agree on the service options to be delivered, the details of how they will be delivered, and the cost of such service options. The ultimate decision as to whether to deliver the service typically resides with the customer. However, this decision is made with the direction and consensus of the SP and the understanding that all parties must be satis<sup>fi</sup>ed to proceed. This mutual perceived <sup>fi</sup>t is key. One customer explained, “you [the customer] have the <sup>fi</sup>nal say, but you’d like to see that we’re all happy with the decision that you’re making”. Thus, higher Agreeability of Service Solution (see Table 5) occurs only when the SP and the customer both perceive bi-directional value in the proposed service solution.

## 4.3.2. The role of Process Formalisation

The construct of Process Formalisation (the extent to which formal procedures underlie the provision of the service to the customer) emerged as a key social mediator of service co-production activities. This construct was originally coded as part of the psychological tools in<sup>fl</sup>uencing service co-production (speci<sup>fi</sup>cally a tertiary tool (cf. Hasan, 1998)). However, data suggested Process Formalisation was less concerned with culture and more focused upon the mediating social elements Engeström (1987) describes as the “rules” and “division of labour” that coordinate the “community”. Nonetheless, data suggested IT applications play a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in encapsulating Process Formalisation. CRM systems and <sup>fi</sup>le templates come with pre-speci<sup>fi</sup>ed formats for particular documents and spreadsheets (e.g., the customer report) that have been mandated by the organisation and these templates guide what information and how information is gathered from or shared with the customer. One senior SP explained “I think the service being consistent is vital. . .you are dealing with a non-scienti<sup>fi</sup>c process [service co-production], so you’ve got to try to put as much science into it as possible. . .IT formalises that in a way”.

In relation to Information Exchange and Dialogue, Process Formalisation of <sup>fi</sup>nancial compliance regulations requires the SP gather extensive information (e.g., existing personal and <sup>fi</sup>nancial circumstances) from the client in a “fact-<sup>fi</sup>nd process” in advance of making any service recommendations. One SP explained that “compliance systems are put in place to make sure you know the customer inside out”. These regulations also require that the SP explains all available options to the customer, as well as their implications. Another SP explained “when we’re audited every year, we have to prove. . .that we have looked at [all possible service options] and prove that we have shown that to the client. . .and that the client has read and signed the recommendations”. Thus, the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 8: Process Formalisation has a positive impact on Information Exchange and Dialogue.

In relation to Mutual Adjustment, Process Formalisation is used to gather and synthesise independent <sup>fi</sup>nancial research and ensure adherence to <sup>fi</sup>nancial compliance regulations. This ensures rigour in how the SP adjusts/adapts service components to suit the individual customer. This was illustrated by one senior SP who explained that:

under [regulatory compliance] legislation. . . [the SP] must give [to the regulator] the reasons why they have recommended something to the client and have that all documented. . . to show that [the service option recommendation] suits the client based on risk versus expected return. . .and show that this was a good investment opportunity. . .and that must then match into the various other variables in relation to understanding the client.

## 4.3.3. The role of information accessibility

Information Accessibility was found to be important for Mutual Adjustment. Data suggested SPs rely on physical/psychological tools both to learn more about the customer in advance of interactions and to inform/update/prepare up-to-date service information, including relevant options and latest compliance requirements. As one SP explained, “our job is to know the industry and products. . .then to know the individual customer”. Similarly, data suggested customers also independently accesses external service-related information to satisfy themselves that an SP’s service recommendations are suitable for them. One senior SP described the usefulness of third party <sup>fi</sup>nancial information, remarking “[customers] use it to satisfy themselves that what you’re saying makes sense [and to] <sup>fi</sup>nd out a little more about it”.

IT plays a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in allowing both the SP and the customer to access information outside of service interactions. Many customers use Web-based <sup>fi</sup>nancial information sources to independently access external service information. All <sup>fi</sup>nancial services organisations involved in the study use a CRM system to enable the SP to access internal information relating to the customer. Many organisations also use computer telephony integration in conjunction with their CRM system to enable SPs to view and record customer information when speaking on the telephone with the customer. One senior SP emphasised “a good CRM system is important in terms of knowing your customer. . .knowing the contacts that you’ve already had, or somebody else in the organisation might have already had with them. . .it’s vital”. SPs also use email to track down internal brochures/service option information, and obtain customer information from external organisations (e.g., other <sup>fi</sup>nancial institutions). Thus, the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 10: Information Accessibility has a positive impact on Mutual Adjustment.

## 4.3.4. The role of Collaboration-Oriented Servicescape

Collaboration-Oriented Servicescape is the extent to which physical and psychological tools in the environment support service interactions between the SP and the customer. Interestingly, IT was generally viewed as a limiter of the Collaboration-Oriented Servicescape, as it often imposed too much structure and control when a more human and unstructured interaction was required. One senior SP explained that in face-to-face conversations:

every question can go o<sup>f</sup> on a tangent and you can <sup>fi</sup>nd out more about the customer and their plans. . .that’s what <sup>fi</sup>nancial planning is all about. . . it’s about the future, more than about hard facts, more about them personally and you can only do that by conversation.

Many of the SPs involved in the study argued that it is necessary to “get inside” the customer’s head, to “dig a bit deeper” into what they are saying and to “get behind” what the customer thinks he/she wants, in order to determine the appropriate service for that customer. Another SP argued “email is not good if there is any ambiguity”, as opposed to face-to-face conversation where “you know straight away if [the customer is] understanding you”.

Despite this, IT-enabled interactions were seen as valuable to initiate and later follow-up with customers. For example, the Director of one <sup>fi</sup>nancial services organisation described how he uses email to inform the customer about particular service options “where customers can take it or leave it”, adding that face-toface conversations “are great once interaction has been agreed [by the customer]”. Email was also seen to be used to arrange, prepare for, or to follow up, face-toface conversations, and to subtly ask the customer to carry out actions required for the service. One senior SP argued that email is a “cost e<sup>f</sup>ective and non-intrusive” way of “requesting information” and another senior SP pointed out that email is a “convenient” way to provide “timely responses to customer queries”, and to reiterate what was said or agreed in a face-to-face conversation. Thus, the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 11: Collaboration-Oriented Servicescape has a positive impact on Information Exchange and Dialogue.

## 4.3.5. The role of tensions and contradictions in service co-production

Four contradictions were observed that drive the adaptation of services during service co-production activities. These contradictions extend beyond the speci<sup>fi</sup>c constructs and relationships captured by the explanatory model; however, they are essential to contextualise service co-production and account for deeper causal mechanisms.

The <sup>fi</sup>rst contradiction is quaternary, i.e., it occurs between service co-production and parallel activities. This contradiction is perhaps the most fundamental, as it describes a mismatch in SPs’ and customers motives for engaging in the co-production activity. SPs typically wish to satis<sup>fi</sup>ce customisation to maximise existing structures and reusable knowledge. They understand the need to tailor a solution. However, departures from standard solution templates are treated as a source of uncertainty that creates escalating costs; costs that must be matched by return on investment for the customer. As one SP described, “if it was all bespoke. . .nobody could a<sup>f</sup>ord it”. Conversely, customers often wish to maximise customisation, perceiving the options to be relatively open-ended. A customer described:

it’s co-creating tailored towards your needs. . . you [the customer] could say no to the advice [from the SP] and then he [the SP] could suggest something else. . . he might bring a number of suggestions that he thinks would suit you and you can tell him which ones don’t suit you and then you’d agree on the way forward.

Both parties are ultimately trying to maximise the pro<sup>fi</sup>tability of service co-production. For SPs, pro<sup>fi</sup>tability is a function of cost and pricing. SPs are aware their scope to raise pricing is limited, hence their motives are tilted towards reducing costs. For customers, pro<sup>fi</sup>tability is a function of pricing and returns, hence their motives are driven by returns.

The second contradiction is tertiary, i.e., it occurs between competing versions of the service coproduction activity. The diverging focus on cost and returns between SPs and customers, respectively, means SPs are typically looking to “close the deal” during service co-production, whereas customers are trying to identify hidden risks or incompatibilities. According to one senior SP, their challenge is to “convince [the customer] that we’re up to what they’re actually looking for”. Once this “convincing” is done, they can begin matching the details of the service with the customer’s domain. This was supported by another SP who admitted “[customers] generally don’t disclose everything at the <sup>fi</sup>rst meeting. . .but by the second meeting. . .they’re starting to trust me and they’re starting to open up more”. Another SP described “I might have 4 or 5 meetings. . .before I start giving them advice”. At an action-level, the goals of SPs are therefore to satis<sup>fi</sup>ce critique of <sup>fi</sup>t for the proposed service and maximise information sharing, while the goals of customers are to satis<sup>fi</sup>ce information sharing and maximise critique of <sup>fi</sup>t.

The third contradiction is secondary, i.e., it occurs between elements of the service co-production activity; in this case, tools and the division of labour. Ultimately, the process of service co-production depends on the customer being able to specify what they want. Yet this is often di<sup>fi</sup>cult, as the customer has a limited or <sup>fl</sup>awed understanding of the possibilities. A senior SP explained “often a customer will say they want. . .but when you actually go into it. . . [what they need] could be something totally di<sup>f</sup>erent”. A Director expressed similar views, commenting “[the customer] gets what they need, not what they thought they wanted”. Customers tend to agree with this position, yet they are often directed to websites or other online sources to browse the available options. These tools lack the ability to contextualise general information for customers, prioritise items of interest, or frame services in their preferred language. This creates a frustration among customers, who feel SPs have wasted their time. One customer summarised “you’re paying your SP to get to know what you want and you [the customer] don’t necessarily know [what you want] . . . you can go onto a website when you know what you want”. Service <sup>fi</sup>rms often make an e<sup>f</sup>ort to create more nuanced online information, yet as one senior SP explained “there are only so many questions you can put on a [online] form”.

The fourth contradiction is primary, i.e., it occurs within an element of the service co-production activity; in this case, within the tools. Building on the <sup>fi</sup>rst three contradictions, a key concern for customers is that SPs are genuinely attentive to their unique needs. The use of IT often erodes this sense of personal connection. One example is the use of laptops to record information. One SP explained “if you’re typing when with a client, it’s as if you are not giving them your full attention, which is what they really want”. This was con<sup>fi</sup>rmed by customers, one of whom explained “[SPs] are not concentrating on you when they’re typing”, further arguing that if the SP “is <sup>fi</sup>lling out a form [on a computer] . . . then they’re putting a barrier [between the SP and the customer]”. This can create a sense of suspicion among customers, as “there’s nothing worse than not being able to see what [the SP] is typing”. Thus, at an operations-level, the conditions created by these tools act in direct opposition to overarching broader persuasive and relationship-building motives and goals.

## 5. Discussion

This study has presented an empirical study (Rowe, 2012) of service co-production, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally the coproduction of <sup>fi</sup>nancial services with customers. No service co-production context can be called truly “representative”, and the same is true for <sup>fi</sup>nancial services. Notably, unlike other service domains such as education or entertainment, the desired outcomes for <sup>fi</sup>nancial services are relatively clear from the outset. Thus, unintentional misalignments around desired outcomes may be less likely to occur in a <sup>fi</sup>nancial service context. Further, service domains such as cloud-hosting or social media have relatively little pre-digital history when compared to <sup>fi</sup>nancial services. This limits those domains’ need to balance physical tools and proximate groups with emerging digitally enabled tools and social structures. The extrapolation of <sup>fi</sup>ndings from this study to such domains requires careful consideration of such di<sup>f</sup>erences. The focus on <sup>fi</sup>nancial services has nonetheless allowed this study to dive deep into speci<sup>fi</sup>c service co-production behaviours and uncover multiple important insights. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings have important implications for research and practice.

## 5.1. Implications for research

This study makes six key contributions with implications for future research. First, this study contributes a lucid and testable Theory of Service Co-Production, which advances theorising in this area by moving from abstract loosely related concepts to more formal hypothesis-based theory (Sutton & Staw, 1995; Weick, 1995). The constructs, hypotheses, and empirical themes presented by the model provide researchers with a lucid IT-centric understanding of service co-production that does not shy away from the persuasive qualities of the activity.

Second, this study demonstrated the primacy of collaborative interactions between service professionals (SPs) and customers. This reinforces the human-centred nature of service co-production research, adding weight to existing calls for a crossdisciplinary focus in order to achieve the innovation needed in the services economy (Bohmann, Leimeister, & Möslein, 2014; Chesbrough & Spohrer, 2006; Spohrer & Maglio, 2008). Such future research must be mindful of the subtle but important in<sup>fl</sup>uence of social and tool-based mediators on the evolution of co-production activities. This was exempli<sup>fi</sup>ed in this study by the observation that speci<sup>fi</sup>c co-production activities were strongly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by social and tool-based mediators. For example, Service Relationship Building was strongly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by Customer-Oriented Service Climate and Mutual Adjustment was strongly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by Information Accessibility. Such elements of service co-production are recognised as key to sustainable service professional growth (Ballantyne & Varey, 2006; Gummesson, 1987, 2002), yet could easily be overlooked without su<sup>fi</sup>cient conceptual breadth.

Third, the primary contradiction observed when SPs used IT during service interactions suggests the IS discipline must fundamentally reconsider of the role of IS/IT in service co-production. This lends empirical support to calls (Brohman et al., 2009; Piccoli et al., 2004) to take IS beyond the basic automating and informating of service delivery (Patrício, Fisk, & Constantine, 2011; Zubo<sup>f</sup>, 1988; Zubo<sup>f</sup> & Maxmin, 2002) in order to facilitate collaborative service co-production with the customer. For example, Bohmann et al. (2014) call for research that seeks a deeper understanding of the underlying principles of service systems interaction and draws on reliable design knowledge on information-intensive interactions in order to design new and innovative forms of interaction.

Fourth, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from this study illustrate a need for research on designing non-invasive IS/IT infrastructures that can better support the range of internalising activities (i.e., Service Relationship Building, Mutual Learning and Mutual Adjustment) which enable Agreeability of Service Solution to be reached. These core elements of the service coproduction process are in danger of being oversimpli<sup>fi</sup>ed and automated among some emerging service production domains – for example, self-service technologies (Schultze et al., 2007; Shim et al., 2002) and intelligent production agents (Tam & Ho, 2006; Tien, 2006). Rather, we argue that service co-production activities are an example of the type of phenomena described by Ciborra (1987) in which information is not just an input into the organisation’s decisionmaking process, but is also used to educate and to persuade other decision-makers (i.e., the customer in this study’s research context).

Fifth, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study indicate that processes that underpin service provision need to be formalised in order to facilitate Information Exchange and Dialogue and Mutual Adjustment. In particular, the study concludes that scripts guiding the service interaction between the service professional and the customer need to be speci<sup>fi</sup>ed a priori. This resonates with <sup>fi</sup>ndings by Eichentopf et al. (2011, p. 660) regarding the importance of scripts for co-production in order to help customers to “organize and express their needs in interactive value creation”. Further research is needed to examine how this could be achieved, as well as the most appropriate tool-based mediators to support these processes.

Sixth, this study contributes to AT by providing support for three major assumptions of that theory:

(i) the assumption that contradictions drive evolutions in activity systems (Engeström et al., 1999). Contradictions were observed at all four levels of service co-production and these were key to understanding the deeper meaning of the activity;

(ii) the assumption that activity systems must be understood in terms of both internalising and externalising activities (Kaptelinin et al., 1999; Mwanza, 2002). This study found Service Relationship Building, Mutual Learning and Mutual Adjustment (internalising activities) could only be properly explained through their relationship with Information Exchange and Dialogue (the externalising activity);

(iii) the impact of social and tool-based mediators (Engeström, 1987; Vygotsky, 1978). This provided an essential sense-making distinction for extant service co-production literature, as well as a powerful analytical pillar during data analysis.

Thus, we endorse the growing number of researchers who argue in favour of using AT to research complex socio-technical phenomena (Barki, Titah, & Bo<sup>f</sup>o, 2007; Chen, Sharman, Chakravarti, Rao, & Upadhyaya, 2008; Hasan & Gould, 2001; Igira, 2008; Kietzmann, 2008; Korpela, Soriyan, & Olufokunbi, 2000; Wiredu & Sorensen, 2006).

## 5.2. Implications for practice

The foremost implication for practice is the recognition that service co-production should not be treated simply as a rational decision-making exercise. Rather, it is a negotiation in which SPs and customers must be creative, must make concessions, and ultimately, must take a leap of faith. The tools employed are therefore not simply a means of sharing information, they are a means of designing and operationalising a shared commitment. For SPs, understanding the incomplete alignment of interests is an important step to accommodating a more mutually agreeable solution. In practical terms, the Theory of Service Co-Production helps SPs to examine how they manage key co-production activities in this complex creative process. The model should guide them in identifying the most e<sup>f</sup>ective role of IS, for example in Process Formalisation, or as providing the infrastructure for Information Accessibility.

More broadly, SPs should appreciate that IS/IT should be employed with great care when co-producing services with customers. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study challenge the conventional wisdom in Service Science, and elsewhere, which postulate an increasingly central role for IS/IT in service innovation and delivery (e.g., Chesbrough & Spohrer, 2006). In particular, SPs must learn to use IT in a way that does not interfere with face-to-face human collaboration with the customer. Where such face-toface interaction is not possible and absolute reliance on IT-enabled interaction is unavoidable, SPs must learn to compensate for the associated shortcomings by making additional e<sup>f</sup>orts to inform, motivate, and reassure customers.

From an IT-design perspective, system designers may need to enhance existing means of IT-enabled communication to compensate for these limitations. For example, CRM systems could be improved to more e<sup>f</sup>ectively support the dynamic and iterative nature of exploratory face-to-face dialogue between SPs and individual customers. The study also reveals that much of the key information provided by the SP to the customer (for individual informationprocessing and re<sup>fl</sup>ective decision-making) is provided in paper form. We thus argue there is an opportunity to use IT to further guide and in<sup>fl</sup>uence the customer’s information processing and individual decision-making outside of face-to-face interactions.

## 6. Conclusions

This study has made four key contributions. First, this study has presented an empirically grounded model that suggests the Agreeability of Service Solution is underpinned by three internalising co-production activities (Service Relationship Building, Mutual Learning, and Mutual Adjustment) and one externalising activity (Information Exchange and Dialogue). Second, this study shows how internalising activities are shaped by Information Exchange and Dialogue, much of which takes place in face-to-face conversations and in the absence of IT. Third, this study shows how service coproduction activities are mediated by both tool-based mediators (the Collaboration-Oriented Servicescape and Information Accessibility) and social mediators (the Customer-Oriented Service Climate and Formal Processes) which incorporate IT to varying degrees. Fourth, this study observed key tensions and contradictions that drive service co-production, stemming from the imperfect alignment of interests between SPs and customers. Collectively, these four contributions should inform service co-production practice and encourage increasingly IT-focused research on service coproduction in the future.

## Appendix 1: Data Analysis and Theory Development

Formal data analysis and theory development was undertaken using the coding techniques proposed by Strauss and Corbin (1990), speci<sup>fi</sup>cally Open Coding, Axial Coding, and Selective Coding.

## Open Coding

Initial Open Coding was informed by the preliminary model derived from extant literature, though the researchers also actively looked for new constructs and relationships in the data. This approach requires the researchers to get close to the data (cf. Glaser & Strauss, 1967) and to also draw on extant theoretical knowledge (cf. Matavire & Brown, 2013; Mcghee et al., 2007; Thornberg, 2012). It therefore encourages the researchers to be open and adaptable to what the data reveal (cf. Sarker et al., 2000), while maintaining some systematic structure for coding (Strauss and Corbin (1990).

The researchers <sup>fi</sup>rst examined event-related data from each <sup>fi</sup>nancial service ecosystem. We analysed speci<sup>fi</sup>c events (captured in observation transcripts) and interpretations of these events (captured in interview transcripts). This examination took place “line by line” and consciously attempted to extract the perspectives and voices of the participants, as recommended by Strauss and Corbin (1990). We further analysed relevant documentation to triangulate <sup>fi</sup>ndings and extract additional event-related insights. Emerging codes were then grouped by themes to reveal categories and subcategories, as well as their associated properties/dimensions in the data. Following the recommendations of Strauss and Corbin (1990), incidents were carefully compared at property/dimension level and the researchers made a point not to “unquestionably” accept the beliefs or assumptions of respondents.

The constructs identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the preliminary model provided a basis for comparison for data at the property/dimension level. Again, guided by Strauss and Corbin (1990), care was taken when applying concepts from the framework. The researchers were precise with the meanings of these concepts and were vigilant in scrutinising if these concepts were embodied in the data. While some categories emerging from the data were found to be identical to constructs in the preliminary model, no evidence was found to support two of the constructs present in that model (i.e., Organisational Socialisation and Service Professional Autonomy). Furthermore, one new category became apparent (i.e., Process Formalisation).

The result of this coding step was the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of initial categories, subcategories, and properties/dimensions that enable the development of Agreeability of Service Solution. ## Appendix 2: Use of Qualitative Data Analysis Software

The researchers utilised QDA Software known as NVivo (developed by Qualitative Research Solutions International) in the analytical process of this research study. This software provided the researchers with tools that enabled the researchers to: (i) store and search data gathered in the case; (ii) mark-up/code data; (iii) link and reorganise data; and (iv) represent and store the researcher’s own re<sup>fl</sup>ections, ideas, and theorising.

While additional facilities and sophistication are provided, the researchers primarily utilised the QDA software for coding data and retrieving all instances in the data that share a code. Using such software to code is reported to be superior to manual coding (Richards and Richards 1994) because “computers are good at working with structure, not content. . .[and through coding] we express or de<sup>fi</sup>ne content by coding the text” (Richards and Richards 1994, p. 447). Furthermore QDA software, which also allows real-time representation of a researcher’s thinking, can be used in the discovery and management of unrecognised ideas and concepts and can be a substantial aid to theorising (cf. Richards and Richards 1994).

In this research study, the di<sup>f</sup>erent pieces of data (i.e., interview and observation transcripts/notes and other documentation) were initially organised and saved as “data sources” in an NVivo project. This gave the researchers an initial overall impression of the data. The interview transcripts/notes were classi<sup>fi</sup>ed according to the ecosystem of the interviewee and this was fundamental to later identifying whether the interpretations of service coproduction varied across ecosystems. These data sources were then available for coding using the NVivo coding function.

NVivo facilitated all three coding steps in the analytic process (i.e., Open Coding, Axial Coding, and Selective Coding). Categories and sub-categories were represented in NVivo using “tree nodes”. These tree nodes were used to code category/subcategory data, broken down to the dimension/property level. In this way, a hierarchical organisation of nodes signi<sup>fi</sup>ed either a category or a subcategory and all of its properties/dimensions. Coded data held in each tree node provided the researchers with good visibility of all property/dimension coded for each category/sub-category, while always being able to instantly access the overall context of this coded data in the origina data source.

Separate nodes called “relationship nodes” were used to code relationship data between tree nodes (i.e., categories/ sub-categories). In the majority of node linkages, multiple aspects of a relationship were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed and coded. Similar to tree nodes, each relationship node held coded data and allowed a click-through to the original data source of the code. All tree nodes and relationships nodes recorded a count of the number of data sources coded at that node and a count of the number of individual coded references across all data sources. This gave the researchers a good sense of categories/sub-categories that needed further development and provided an overview of the strength of relationships between categories/ sub-categories.

In all, a total of 1027 references were coded using NVivo software. This included 574 references coded for the properties/dimensions of the 9 tree nodes (i.e., categories and sub-categories that were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the data) and also included 453 references coded for the 14 relationships that were found to exist between these concepts.

The researchers also utilised some of the tools available within NVivo to facilitate the overall process of data collection, analysis, and theorising. This included non-hierarchical listing of categories (known as “free nodes”) which enabled the researchers to code data that seemed to be important, but did not “<sup>fi</sup>t” with any category/sub-category. It also included search tools and the ability to link nodes, memos, and documents which assisted the researchers in organising the data, as well as gaining an overview of the ideas, concepts, and relationships that were emerging from the data. For example, free nodes were found to be very useful to record initial ideas and emerging categories in early Open Coding. NVivo memos were used to record theoretically sampled data and were found to be a rich and invaluable source of both category and relationship codes.

Furthermore, the NVivo tools enabled the researchers to move iteratively between the Open Coding phase of analysis and deeper analysis. Major coding iterations were backed up and saved in digital format in order to provide an audit trail of the coding process. Consistency in the use of codes was achieved by recording and constantly referring to the online researcherde<sup>fi</sup>ned description of a node.

Having completed the coding of all concepts (including their properties/dimensions) and relationships between these concepts, the data were then re-examined in order to scrutinise the role of information systems in service coproduction. Additional tree nodes were used to code data relating to how information systems supported each speci-<sup>fi</sup>c category/sub-category. This process assisted the researchers in establishing which of the categories/subcategories encompassed signi<sup>fi</sup>cant IT elements and which incorporated minor IT elements.
