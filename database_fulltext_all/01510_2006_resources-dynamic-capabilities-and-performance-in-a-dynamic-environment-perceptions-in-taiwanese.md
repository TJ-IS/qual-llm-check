# Resources, dynamic capabilities and performance in a dynamic environment: Perceptions in Taiwanese IT enterprises

Lei-Yu Wu <sup>\*</sup>

Department of International Business, National Taipei College of Business, Taipei, Taiwan, ROC Received 21 February 2005; received in revised form 19 September 2005; accepted 5 November 2005 Available online 15 December 2005

## Abstract

This paper presents the results of an analysis of resources, dynamic capabilities and performance in a dynamic environment. The author used data from 244 Taiwanese information technology firms and found that, in an unstable environment, resources, whether from the firm itself or from associated support firms, did not directly influence performance. Instead, resources influenced performance through exercising dynamic capabilities. Furthermore, this effort showed that information technology firm performance can be directly assessed by examining the innovation speed, market response speed, production efficiency, and production flexibility of a firm.

## 1. Introduction

Unlike previous studies on the resource-based view (RBV), this study was conducted to evaluate an important topic in improving our understanding of a firm’s resources and performance in dynamic markets. Apparently, firms achieve and sustain competitive advantage by deploying valuable resources with inelastic supply [7,18,21,50]. However, well-known companies such as IBM, Texas Instruments and Philips, appear to have pursued a resource-based strategy of accumulating technology assets, using an aggressive intellectual property stance to protect their interests. Unfortunately, this approach is often insufficient to sustain competitive advantage, because alteration is nonlinear and unpredictable in dynamic markets [20].

In such settings, simply investigating relationships between firm resources and performance can produce erroneous conclusions when using an RBV. I therefore proposed an exploratory method of assessing resourcebased logic by using a class of mediating dynamic capabilities, as well as integrating, building, and reconfiguring internal and external resources and competences, as a way to address rapid change in business environments [55]. Consequently, this work largely focused on improving the comprehension of dynamic capabilities, and thus improving RBV.

Most prior research on firm performance adopted financial indicators, but these have limited applicability to IT industries. Particularly, due to rapid change in the IT industry, financial performance figures do not necessarily indicate the continued accumulation of strength [51]; e.g., the productivity study of Henderson and Cockburn [25] involving pharmaceutical firms indicated that these firms do not enjoy strong financial performance, although they may enjoy important competitive advantages in their research processes.

Accordingly, my investigation attempted to identify what factors should be used to evaluate the performance of IT enterprises.

The IT sector was chosen because short product life cycles and high demand for customization characterize products such as personal computing, network and communication devices [12,39,43]. These were identified by Teece et al. as those that stand to benefit from dynamic capabilities in a dynamic environment. Indeed, Taiwan has become the largest OEM producer of PC products. However, recent pressures from foreign OEM partners have driven Taiwanese firms to assume global logistics responsibilities. Furthermore, these partners, for example Dell, require ‘‘983’’, meaning that they should receive 98% of finished goods within 3 days of their ordering them. Thus, survey results conducted on Taiwanese technology firms provide a rich data set of information on dynamic capability behaviors in unstable environments; conclusions based on this data therefore have strong implications for all managers.

## 2. Theory and hypotheses

The origin of the resource-based view is attributable to Penrose [45], who proposed that sustained firm growth depended on internal firm characteristics, such as management capability and economy-of-scale in technological expertise. Following the implications of Porter’s [48] exploration of firm strategy from the viewpoint of industry analysis, the strategic studies field included numerous arguments focused on ‘‘acclimation’’. Only when Wernerfelt [57] proposed the resource position barrier concept did people and organizations egin to consider differentiated firm resources as sources of sustainable competitive advantage. The core competence perspective developed by Prahalad and Hamel [49], and the competence-based competitive strategy [24] derived from it, resembled the RBV conceptually.

Expressed simply, resource-based strategy formulation is an ‘inside out’ process, differing from the normal perspective of industry economics. RBV holds that a sustained construction of firm core resources represents a better strategy than continuously adjusting firm operating categories to match environmental changes. Moreover, firms with abundant resources can continue surviving and developing because of their strong competitive advantage, regardless of external environmental changes [58].

Firms that lack sufficient resources to thrive often use cooperative methods like strategic alliances to gain the requisite complementary resources and capabilities from support firms [6,32]. Laumann [34] contended that balanced reciprocity would entice cooperative firms to supply resources provided that it had considerable resources. Hite [27] discovered that cooperative partners assessed the likelihood of reciprocity by determining the abundance of the firm’s resources. Therefore, such firms evaluated the extent of a firm’s resources as well as considering the economic benefits that could be accrued through cooperation. Consequently I proposed:

Hypothesis 1. Firm resources positively affect the willingness for cooperation in associated support firms.

A firm’s resources include its core resources as well as complementary resources provided by cooperating entities. They are different from capabilities, which refer to the ability to deploy resources and utilize organizational procedures to integrate them and thus achieve the desired results [1,40]. Dynamic capability is the firm’s ability to integrate, learn and reconfigure resources; its ability to transform the existing resource base and to internalize external resources in order to be effective in a rapidly evolving environment. For example, in the wafer industry, Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company Limited and United Microelectronics Corp. enjoy a high global market share: 60%. Both integrate and absorb specialized know–how and technologies more effectively than do their rivals (e.g., Chartered Semiconductor Manufacturing Ltd.): they have better dynamic capabilities.

Many factors determine whether or not firms can integrate, learn and reconfigure resources. A precondition is that they have plenty of them. Only then is the subsequent effort meaningful. Therefore:

Hypothesis 2. A firm’s resources positively affect its dynamic capabilities.

However, if the resources are limited, the cooperation of associated support firms will generally be sought to gain complementary resources: greater external support increases the total disposable resources. One also would suspect that higher external support probably leads to higher dynamic capabilities. Thus:

Hypothesis 3. Associated support firms’ cooperation positively affects a firm’s dynamic capabilities.

Dynamic capabilities are the antecedent organizational and strategic routines by which managers transform their resource base to create new valuecreating strategies [22,47]. They also lie behind the creation, evolution and recombination of other resources into a competitive advantage. For example, Toyota has employed its superior product development skills to achieve competitive advantage in the automotive industry [13]. Iansiti and Clark [31] examined integration capability in the automobile and computer industries and discovered that knowledge integration capability in product development correlated positively with firm performance and performance improvements over time. Therefore:

Hypothesis 4. A firm’s dynamic capabilities positively affect its performance.

## 3. Methodology

## 3.1. Sampling and respondents

The primary research subjects were CEOs from Taiwanese IT firms, which were selected from Taiwan’s Hsinchu High Technology Industrial Park Council’s Science Industry Association Registry and the Taiwan Manufacturers Registry (published by the China Credit Information Service). Survey questions were pre-tested on a selected group of CEOs. Ambiguities in the wordings were noted and clarified.

Before sending out the questionnaires, researchers contacted the firms by phone to confirm that the targeted respondents were indeed the CEOs. After confirmation, subjects were asked to help by answering the questionnaires; the questionnaires were then mailed to the respondents, who were re-contacted to ascertain whether they had received the questionnaires and could return the completed questionnaires quickly [52].

The questionnaires were sent to 1045 firms and 15 were returned because of incorrect addresses. Of the 1030 firms receiving questionnaires, 257 responded. Of these, 13 questionnaires were deleted because the respondents gave ‘‘non-applicable’’ answers to the majority of the questions. The final number of responses was thus 244 firms, corresponding to a valid return rate of 23.7%. Financial and timing constraints prevented us from sending reminders to non-respondents.

The structure of the sample firms was sufficiently diverse and heterogeneous. In terms of IT sub-industry categories, 35 (14.3%) were firms belonging in the integrated circuits industry, 83 (34%) were computer and peripheries producers, 29 (11.9%) provided communications equipment, 31 (12.7%) dealt in optoelectronics, 19 (7.8%) in precision machinery, 6 (2.5%) in biotechnology, 30 (12.3%) in software and 11 (4.5%) were others.

To confirm that the response sample was representative of the population, several tests were performed to assess for non-response bias. ANOVAs were employed to check differences for two related variables between early and late respondents [4]. Responses returned within four weeks of the first mailing were classified as early (n = 131), while those after 4 weeks were considered late (n = 113) [42]. The ANOVAs were conducted against the null hypothesis that there are no mean differences in annual turnover sales and employee numbers across the early and late responding groups. The hypothesis was not rejected: no significant difference existed between these two groups (for annual turnover sales: $p = 0 . 8 2$ ; for number of employees: $p = 0 . 9 4 )$

## 3.2. Measure

Self-administrated questions were employed with measurements primarily using Likert and semanticdifferential scales. Five constructs were investigated: resources, willingness for associated support firms cooperation, environmental turbulence, dynamic capabilities and performance.

Resources are firm-specific assets; these are difficult or impossible to imitate. Measures were based on five variables: specialized know–how [36], financial capital [10,56], management capability [15,38], reputation effects and past alliance experiences [17,28].

Willingness for associated support firms’ cooperation was determined by requesting the respondent to assess, on a Likert scale (from strongly agree to strongly disagree), the willingness of associated firms to cooperate. They were first asked to identify one of the most important partners (in technological or managerial terms) and all questions then focused on that specified partner.

Environmental turbulence is dynamic, with rapidly changing consumer needs and an uncertain competitive and technological environment. The dynamism scale of Miller [41], measured by the amount and unpredictability of change in customer tastes, production or service technology, and modes of competition in key firm industries was used. The researchers asked respondents to assess, this on a Likert scale ranging from strongly agree to strongly disagree.

Dynamic capabilities represent the firm’s ability to integrate, learn, and reconfigure internal and external resources; it is essential in determining competitive advantage under environmental volatility. The method of measuring this conforms primarily to that of Teece et al. The respondents were asked to reply to four questions; questions based on semantic differential scales were also employed to provide additional assessment.

Due to rapid change in the IT industry, financial performance figures do not necessarily indicate continued accumulation of strength. Thus, the researchers adopted six measures to gauge firm performance:

 speed of innovation in creating new products and processes [9];

 speed in responding to the market by achieving rapid responses to customers so that it could outperform competitors in identifying and satisfying needs [26];

 production efficiency so that the inputs required to produce a given output decrease due to better production efficiency [46];

 product quality so that customers perceive greater value in a specific product than rival products [35];

 manufacturing flexibility of manufacturing technologies that reduce setup times for complex equipment, increase the use of machines through improved scheduling, and improve quality control during all stages of the manufacturing process;

 R&D capability that improves the design capability of products and production processes [19]; this was measured using semantic-differential scales.

## 3.3. Analytical methods

Following Anderson and Gerbing [2], the hypotheses were tested using a two-stage structural equation model [33]. First, a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) evaluated convergent and discriminant validity. Second, a path analysis was performed for testing the hypotheses. The path-analytic procedure has become popular for studies where a small sample size restricts the use of full structural equation models [11,37]. Furthermore, the validity of the model was tested using competing models [54].

## 4.3. Discussion

The validity of the model was tested using other competing models. First, an additional research hypothesis – firm resources will positively affect performance – was added to form competing model A (CompmodA). This formed a natural competitor when resources must be mediated by dynamic capabilities to affect performance. Comparing CompmodA with the original investigate model revealed a $\chi _ { ( 1 ) } ^ { 2 } = 0 . 0 0 0 1 1$ with a chi-square variation value of 0.46, which is insignificant (g = 0.05, t-value = 0.69). This implied that CompmodA was less desirable.

This investigation also examined a hypothesis that the willingness for associated support $f i r m s '$ cooperation will positively affect performance. This was also natural in the light of Hypothesis 2 and Hypothesis 3 and complied with ideas of Johnson and Sohi. Competing model B (CompmodB) was compared with the original model. Again, the comparison test found that the alternative was unattractive with a $\chi _ { ( 1 ) } ^ { 2 } = 0 . 4 5$ with a $\chi ^ { 2 }$ variation value of 0.1 $( \gamma = - 0 . 0 1$ 1, tvalue = -0.1).

The model comparison tests therefore showed that dynamic capabilities were significant, transforming resources in place into performance, and that the mediating variable between resources and performance had dynamic capability. Without dynamic capabilities to convert current resources into advantage, resources do not translate into performance [16,59,60].

The researcher proposed that there was an intermediate variable, dynamic capabilities, between a firm’s performance and its resources. Moreover, in an actual operation, the investigator posited that a firm with dynamic capabilities can manage resources and achieve better performance than its rivals.

## 5. Conclusions

The resource-based view of firms stresses idiosyncratic firm resources, particularly those that reside within it. RBV regards the firm as a bundle of resources and suggests that the resources significantly affect firm competitive advantage and thus performance. Recent studies have found that RBV has not reached dynamic markets, because alteration is nonlinear and unpredictable. Additionally, Argote [3] has argued that existing resources can even be a disadvantage if managers overgeneralize based on the past. The research discussed here demonstrated that, in an unstable environment, firm resources, internal or external, do not directly influence firm performance. Instead firm resources influence performance through the dynamic capabilities. Consequently, RBV remains useful in a rapidly changing environment.

Most previous empirical studies on RBV have examined developed countries. This investigation validated findings in a context-specific developing country (Taiwan) and also provided useful recommendations about assessing IT enterprise quality using dynamic capabilities.

This study proposed that IT firm performance should be assessed by measuring innovative speed, market response speed, production efficiency and production flexibility. The investigation also found that dynamic capability tended to enhance performance. Other ancillary results were that: (1) firm dynamic capabilities are related to firm resources; (2) willingness for associated support firms’ cooperation tends to increase with firm resources and (3) willingness for associated support firms’ cooperation tends to help in developing firm dynamic capabilities.

The findings clearly indicated that, for firms facing a rapidly changing environment, strengthening dynamic capabilities would be the key concern. Firms can obtain complementary or crucial resources via strategic alliances, thus improving their performance.

## 6. Limitations

One limitation of this study is that the analysis is based on perceptual data [44], which makes it difficult for management of a business to identify appropriate actions based on the results. Furthermore, the measurements are not of the firm but of the CEO’s perception. The relatively low response rate and the fact that the respondents come from one country also limits the generalizability of the results.
