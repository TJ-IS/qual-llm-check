# The business value of information technology as measured by technical ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency: Evidence from country-level data

Winston T. Lin ⁎

The State University of New York at Buffalo, School of Management, 325A Jacobs Management Center, Buffalo, NY 14260-4000, United State

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 5 October 2006 Received in revised form 5 October 2008 Accepted 18 November 2008 Available online 24 November 2008

Keywords: Information technology capital Productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency Productivity paradox Developed countries Emerging economies Stochastic production frontier

## a b s t r a c t

The value of information technology (IT) in a business production process has long been a debatable issue. Explanation of the so-called “productivity paradox” has been one of the central topics in this arena. Much work has focused on <sup>fi</sup>rm-level analyses. This paper investigates IT contribution at a country level, using production theory in microeconomics. It negates the relationship between IT value and productivity based on grouping of countries, when the individual analytical method is applied and technical ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency is used as the performance criterion. Findings obtained by applying stochastic production frontiers to a comprehensive country-level panel data set suggest that the IT contributions, as determined by the increase or decrease in the technical ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, to individual countries differ in magnitude from a production frontier to another, but are robust. It also shows that IT spending does not necessarily improve technical ef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies of individual countries, regardless of whether they are developed or developing countries, and that given technological changes, the so-called productivity paradox may exist in a country, no matter whether it is a developed or developing nation. Finally, the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant implications of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings are presented

## 1. Introduction

The contribution of information technology (IT) to organizational performance has been examined extensively by numerous researchers in production and information systems research during the past two decades. IT has signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly changed the ways <sup>fi</sup>rms conduct business and these changes are expected to continue with the advent of electronic commerce. Resources have been allocated to IT by management to procure state-of-the-art hardware equipments, to purchase and develop application systems for business reengineering, to install networks for communication between manufacturers, suppliers, and customers, and to support the education of the IS staff and employees to be capable of using IT [53]. Davenport [24] has pointed out that approximately three trillion dollars have been spent on IT investments in the U.S. over the last decade. At the country level, the same trend can also be clearly seen. It is expected that the trend of increased IT investments will continue to persist.

The increasing trend of investing in IT seems overwhelming. Faced with the extensive spending in IT, top management is required to justify IT payoffs. The motive of extensive investing is based on the anticipated bene<sup>fi</sup>ts promised by IT. However, these bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are usually intangible, such as enhanced capability, coordinated control, improved communication, and competitive advantages, and are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to quantify in order to justify the IT investment [53]. Therefore, it is critical to <sup>fi</sup>nd evidence to justify such extensive IT investment [31].

Researchers who have investigated the justi<sup>fi</sup>cation of IT investments have published their <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the impact of IT on organizational performance over the last two decades. The economic measures of IT bene<sup>fi</sup>ts in organizational performance frequently used include profitability, productivity, costs, quality, operative efficiency, consumer surplus, and Tobin's q (cf. [55])

Quite a few research results were able to con<sup>fi</sup>rm the contribution of IT in organizations. However, some of them derived only weak or even inconclusive results. For example, the so-called productivity paradox of IT [5,17,18,20,53–55,69] has confused both managers and researchers during the 1980s [40] and is claimed to have disappeared in early 1990s. The productivity paradox of IT suggests that the huge amount of investments in IT has been found uncorrelated with signi<sup>fi</sup>cant organizational performance improvement in aggregate output productivity. Typical explanations for the productivity paradox include the following: (i) massive investment in IT started only from recent years [65]; (ii) because of the time-lagged effects of IT, it takes time to realize the bene<sup>fi</sup>t of IT [25]; (iii) output mismeasurement [17,47,79]; (iv) input mismeasurement [27]; (v) overinvestment in IT [61]; and (vi) lack of organizational changes accompanying the IT investment [18].

A different performance measure called productive (technical) efficiency has been rarely used in the study of IT value in the past. It was introduced by Lin and Shao [53] to evaluate the business value of IT at the <sup>fi</sup>rm level in the MIS literature for the <sup>fi</sup>rst time and is becoming more frequently applied to the measurement of the impact of IT investments on the <sup>fi</sup>rm's performance in production processes. The use of productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency is motivated by the following reasons: (i) productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency exerts a positive effect on productivity growth [19,52,76]; (ii) productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency can be applied to all types of organization, unlike some <sup>fi</sup>nancial measurements that can only be applied to <sup>fi</sup>nancial organizations; (iii) productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency is closely related to productivity and effectiveness, but is not the same as productive effectiveness [53]; (iv) this performance measure is built upon the parametric stochastic frontier approach used [20,52– 55,57,75–78]; and (v) if it is combined with other measures, a more complete analysis of IT contribution can then be offered.

This research pursues a country-level study on IT value. There are compelling reasons why we undertake this country-level analysis. The prominent reasons include, but are not limited to the following: (i) since there has been too much research on large U.S. <sup>fi</sup>rms [20,30,80], research work through comparative studies at the country level is in need; (ii) lack of updated IT data beyond 1992 at the <sup>fi</sup>rm level [53–55] makes it impossible to conduct such research as this study intends to do; (iii) IT value research at the <sup>fi</sup>rm level is abundant but, by contrast, knowledge accumulation of IT value at the country level is poor [20,59], thereby this study serves to bridge the gap; (iv) most research in recent years has focused on IT value at the <sup>fi</sup>rm level but little work has been done to validate the <sup>fi</sup>rm-level results to see whether they are applicable across national boundaries [80]; and one way to validate the <sup>fi</sup>ndings at the <sup>fi</sup>rm level is to compare IT impacts across different countries, meaning that the external validity of <sup>fi</sup>rm-level conclusions on IT value is required; (v) it has been emphasized [70] that a key dimension of external validity is international generalization and that a country-level study on IT value is an international generalization; and (vi) methodologically, a careful review of the literatures on production research/ economics and information systems reveals that there are no countrylevel studies on IT value that have ever applied the time-varying stochastic frontiers of the Cobb–Douglas (CD) function, the Box–Cox (BC) and Box–Tidwell (BT) transformations, and the translog (TL) function, as used in the present study.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 conducts a literature review, while Section 3 presents the analytical framework. Section 4 discusses the data and variables used, and Section 5 reports and analyzes the results. Finally, Section 6 concludes the research with some remarks and discusses some limitations and future research.

## 2. A literature review

As mentioned earlier, there has been too much emphasis on U.S. <sup>fi</sup>rms and lack of cross-country studies [20,59] as far as the business value of IT is concerned; and, as a consequence, knowledge accumulation concerning macro-characteristics and IT value at the country level has been inhibited and poor. This suggests that research on IT business value at the macro-level is needed.

Indeed, research on IT value at the <sup>fi</sup>rm level appears to be abundant as re<sup>fl</sup>ected by a long list [1,3,7,9,10,11,15–18,28,32,38,49,54,55,58,60– 63,65,66,75–77,83,84, among many others]. On the contrary, the studies devoted to IT value at the country level are countable. The review of the literature on IT value at the country level is summarized in Table 1.

As one can observe from Table 1, the conclusions reached by different studies are mixed and contradictory [20,48,78,80]. In particular, some studies have concluded that the productivity paradox has essentially been dispelled in developed countries [29,30], while some others have argued that the paradox is absent from developed countries and/or developing economies as well [20,29,30,42,52]. It is noted that [20] has concentrated on the impacts of inputs substitution and complement and macro-characteristics upon the value of IT.

This research joins the short list of relatively few studies to address the important issue of how to assess the business value of IT at the country level. Since cross-country research is scarce, further research is called for with different designs (in samples of countries, time periods, statistical methods, economic theory, performance measures, etc.) to help clarify the issue or actually provide answers to the issue. In particular, stimulated by the lack of comparative analyses using productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency as a performance measure at the country level, this research aims at <sup>fi</sup>tting various stochastic production frontiers into a country-level panel data set of developed and developing countries. Thus, the objective of this paper is two-fold: to assess the impact of IT on the productive (technical) ef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies between developed and developing countries collectively and to compare the productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies with and without IT of individual countries across different stochastic production frontiers.

More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, this study is motivated by (i) the lack of research in this area; (ii) the need to use a novel approach which is considered superior to the regression analysis approach used in previous research at the country level; and (iii) its importance and relevance to such key constituents as <sup>fi</sup>rms and their managers who have the decisionmaking bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of this country-level work (e.g., applying the frontier approach and the performance metric to determine whether the <sup>fi</sup>rm's IT investments are justi<sup>fi</sup>ed, to better the <sup>fi</sup>rm's competitive position, and to improve the <sup>fi</sup>rm's productivity of its business processes), as well as international investors and countries and their governments (policy-makers) who can apply the approach to develop their IT investment strategies and to identify the countries that are productively (technically) ef<sup>fi</sup>cient for their foreign investments, so that blind investments (domestic and foreign) can be avoided.

Therefore, we intend to answer such open questions as: (i) Based on the theory of production and the stochastic frontier approach derived from the theory, is the classical regression analysis approach used in previous research adequate? (ii) Realizing the need to differentiate the collective analytical method from the individual analytical method, are the conclusions reached based only on the former method convincing or misleading? (iii) Is grouping of countries proper? (iv) Is it true that the productivity paradox is absent from the group of developed countries but remains in the group of developing countries?

## 3. Analytical framework

## 3.1. Production theory

Production theory centers on a transformation process in which a <sup>fi</sup>rm (a country) utilizes different resources (inputs such as capital, labor, etc.) and produces tangible goods or intangible services (outputs) to satisfy the needs of its customers. The transformation of inputs into outputs is called a production process. The production frontier, which de<sup>fi</sup>nes the relationship between inputs and outputs, speci<sup>fi</sup>es the maximum (ideal or desired) output level realizable from a given combination of inputs. The difference between the ideal and actual output levels is deemed productive inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. Hence, two possible scenarios could happen—a <sup>fi</sup>rm either operates on the frontier (if it is productively or technically ef<sup>fi</sup>cient) or below the frontier (if it is productively or technically inef<sup>fi</sup>cient). The simpli<sup>fi</sup>ed <sup>fi</sup>gure shown in [76] is useful to illustrate the concepts of a typical production frontier f with one input X and one output Y and its associated productive (in)ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency.

$$

$$

Hence, productive (in)ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency is one important factor determining the <sup>fi</sup>rm's (the country's) productivity. The (in)ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency change over time by IT may be caused by over- or under-investments in IT over time, (mis)management of IT investments over time, etc.

## 3.2. Research models

It is widely believed that, theoretically and empirically, the stochastic production frontier is better for measuring productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency than the deterministic approach [52,71]. Formally, a stochastic production frontier model is given by $Y _ { i t } = f ( X _ { i t } ; \beta ) + \nu _ { i t } - u _ { i t } , i = 1 , . . . ,$ n and $t { = } 1 , . . . , m ,$ where $Y _ { i t }$ is the observed output produced in country i at time $t , X _ { i t }$ is a set of inputs such as capital, labor, and IT-capital, β is a set of unknown coef<sup>fi</sup>cients, $f ( \cdot )$ represents the ideal (maximum) output, and $\nu _ { i t }$ is the traditional random error distributed according to $N ( 0 , \sigma _ { \nu } ^ { 2 } ) .$ . The actual (observed) output $Y _ { i t }$ by de<sup>fi</sup>nition, is less than or equal to the ideal output $f ( X _ { i t } ; \beta ) .$ . Thus, $u _ { i t }$ is the difference between the ideal and the observed output in country i at time t, represents the productive inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency in the production process, and has to be nonnegative, which means to require $u _ { i t }$ to be half-normal. In other words, $u _ { i t }$ represents the one-sided distributed stochastic inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency error distributed according to $\textstyle | N ( 0 , \ \sigma _ { u } ^ { 2 } ) |$ . The normal distribution of $\nu _ { i t }$ and the half normal distribution of $u _ { i t }$ will hereafter be referred to as the normal-half normal speci<sup>fi</sup>cation.

In order to transform productive inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency into productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne the productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency $( P E _ { i t } )$ for country i at time t as $e ^ { - u _ { i t } } [ 2 0 , 4 2 , 5 2 , 5 3 , 5 4 , 5 5 , 5 7 , 7 5 , 7 6 , 7 7 , 7 8 ]$ . The justi<sup>fi</sup>cation for the de<sup>fi</sup>nition is simply that, since $u _ { i t }$ lies between 0 and $\infty , e ^ { - u _ { i t } }$ will have a value ranging between one and $Z { \mathrm { e r } } 0 ,$ meaning that when $u _ { i t } { = } 0$ (no inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency), $e ^ { - u _ { i t } } = 1$ (the highest ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency); and when $u _ { i t }$ tends to in<sup>fi</sup>nite (the highest inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency), $e ^ { - u _ { i t } }$ tends to zero (no ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency). Just because of this one-to-one correspondence property and because of the fact that the higher the $u _ { i t } ,$ the lower the $e ^ { - u _ { i t } }$ will be, and vice versa, the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency (equal to e<sup>−uit</sup>) is generally and widely accepted [e.g., 19,20,37,52,53,54,55,57]. Thus, $P E _ { i t } { = } e ^ { - u _ { i t } }$ will be used to measure the productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency for country i at time t. Moreover, to rank the performance of IT among the countries considered, we will use the average productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of country i $\begin{array} { r } { \big ( \mathrm { A P E } _ { i } \big ) = \sum _ { t } P E _ { i t } / m ; } \end{array}$ and, consequently, the overall average productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency (APE) can be de<sup>fi</sup>ned as $( \Sigma _ { i t } P E _ { i t } ) / n m$ , where n is the number of countries and m is the number of time periods. The stochastic production frontier serves as the methodological foundation of assessing the value of IT investments as measured by the productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency which can be calculated from a stochastic production frontier model.

For the purpose of comparison, four speci<sup>fi</sup>cations of the stochastic production frontier are deployed in this research: namely, the generalized Cobb–Douglas (CD), Box–Cox (BC), Box– Tidwell (BT), and translog (TL) functional forms (see Appendix and [13,14,52,53,54,75,76] for details). Two scenarios are considered: (i) IT spending treated as a production factor, and, hence, we have three inputs (non-IT capital, labor, and IT capital) in these stochastic frontier models; and (ii) IT spending being absent from the production process, thereby only non-IT capital and labor appear as input factors.

Thus, we have eight stochastic frontier models to be estimated: the generalized CD frontier model, the BC transformation model, the BT transformation model, and the TL model, each model with and without IT capital.

Using the time-varying stochastic frontier approach, we conduct a study for <sup>fi</sup>fteen countries over the period from 1993 to 1999. Thus, $i \mathop { = } 1 , . . . , n ~ \left( \mathop { = } 1 5 \right)$ and $t = 1 , . . . , m ( = 7 ) ,$ leading to the total number of observations equal to $n m = 1 0 5$ . These <sup>fi</sup>fteen (15) countries are Canada (CN), France (FR), Germany (GM), Italy (IA), Japan (JP), the United Kingdom (UK), and the United States (US), Australia (AU), Hong Kong (HK), South Korea (SK), Taiwan (TW), Singapore (SG), Malaysia (MA), Thailand (TH), and China (CH).

According to the 2002 Digital Access Indices (DAI) released by International Telecommunication Union, Geneva, on November 19, 2003, and gross domestic product (GDP) per capital for the countries studied (http://www.itu.int./ITU-D/ict/dai), the <sup>fi</sup>rst twelve economies and the last three nations may be referred to as Group 1 – the developed countries group and Group 2 – the developing countries group, respectively. This classi<sup>fi</sup>cation seems to be consistent closely with [29,30]. However, Chen and Lin [20] have argued that the comparison of average ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency estimates obtained from two different subsamples (groups) need special care. The reason for this is as follows. Consider how a stochastic frontier production model is used to estimate productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency (PE). It, <sup>fi</sup>rst, estimates the production function which is the best performance from the subsample countries and, second, <sup>fi</sup>nds the difference between the actual output of each country and the frontier. As such, the PE measure of the same country could change upon the subsample countries selected. For example, suppose the United States (US) be the most ef<sup>fi</sup>cient country. In a subsample with US, the PE of Taiwan could be estimated to be low since its ef<sup>fi</sup>cient level is away from that of US. On the other hand, it could be estimated to be high if the subsample (group) does not include US and consists of similarly developing countries. Therefore, dividing the sample countries into Group 1 and Group 2 and comparing the individual countries' average ef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies estimated from Group 1 and Group 2 separately does not include valuable information [20,46]. The same argument applies when the regression analysis approach is used [28–30, etc.].

For these compelling reasons, we take the same strategy of estimation and analysis as [20], that is, we estimate the frontier models based on the whole sample, then we separate the sample countries into two groups as described above to compare the average ef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies of individual countries in each group. Doing this way would avoid controversies over how the countries are grouped and, more importantly, overcome the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty caused by wrong skewness (see Subsection 5.1 below) and inability to estimate Group 2 due to a too small subsample consisting of only 21 $( = 3 \times 7 )$ observations.

Consequently, in total, we have eight models to be estimated. These are CD with and without IT, BC with and without IT, BT with and without IT, and TL with and without IT.

## 4. Variables and data

## 4.1. Variables

The following variables are needed: (i) capital, (ii) labor, (iii) IT spending which includes computer hardware investment and spending (investment) in software, and (iv) IS spending which is the expenses for information system staf<sup>fi</sup>ng. Then, we can calculate the independent variables (input factors): (v) non-IT capital $\left( K _ { i t } \right)$ and (vi) non-IT labor $\left( L _ { i t } \right)$ by subtracting IT spending and IS spending from capital and labor, respectively, and (vii) IT capital $\left( I _ { i t } \right)$ which is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as IT stock = computer capital + 3× IS staf<sup>fi</sup>ng spending $[ 2 0 , 2 8 , 4 0 , 5 3 , 5 4 , 5 5 ]$ , where the multiplier of three in the calculation of IT stock represents the assumed service life of the asset created by IS labor and is commonly used in the literature.

The dependent variable refers to the value-added by the input factors. It is measured by GDP (Gross Domestic Product).

## 4.2. Data sources

The data on the dependent and independent variables were retrieved from these sources: (i) the “Yearbook” of a country, (ii) the United Nations Common Database, (iii) the Statistics Department of a country, (iv) the OECD Database, and (v) Digital Planet 2002-the Global Information Economy (published by the World Information Technology and Services Alliance). This is the only source of country-level IT data we have found so far over the period from 1993 to 1999.

The data obtained have to be transformed into a common basis to rule out the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of in<sup>fl</sup>ation, exchange rates, national accounting standards, and other relevant factors. Hence, the data were transformed into U.S. dollars and the transformed data were then de<sup>fl</sup>ated by the U.S. consumer price index (CPI). All the data used are transformed into 1995 U.S. dollars. Thus, it is our <sup>fi</sup>rm belief that the data used are of good quality as those used in other cross-country research.

## 5. Results and analysis

## 5.1. Estimation

The task of estimation was carried out by the LIMDEP statistical software package, which is capable of performing the nonlinear maximum-likelihood (NML) estimation and the Waldman test required in the two-stage estimation process. In estimating the stochastic production frontier models with a one-sided distributed inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency error $\left( u _ { i t } \right)$ as well as in measuring the productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, two stages are required as follows. In Stage 1: we obtain the OLS, SUR [50,86], 2SLS [36,43], EC [64], and EC-SUR [4,51,56] estimates. The estimates obtained from different methods then are used separately as the starting values for estimating $u _ { i t }$ and the unknown coef<sup>fi</sup>cients of the stochastic production frontiers in Stage 2 in which the NML estimation is undertaken. However, before the NML estimation starts, the skewness of the OLS, 2SLS, SUR, EC, and EC-SUR residuals is checked <sup>fi</sup>rst. The NML estimators for a stochastic frontier model are simply the OLS, 2SLS, SUR, EC, and EC-SUR estimators if their residuals are negatively skewed [82]. Hence, if the so-called exception condition (negative skewness), usually referred to as wrong skewness (w.s.), prevails, the estimation process is terminated immediately. Otherwise, the residual is determined by the formula E $[ u _ { i t } | ( \nu _ { i t } – u _ { i t } ) ]$ according to Jondrow et al. [42]. We have arrived at the same estimated parameters based on different sets of the initial values obtained from different methods. Consequently, we are able to conclude that the estimated results reported are certainly reliable.<sup>2</sup>

## 5.2. Estimation results

Tables 2–6 report estimation results of the models that are statistically acceptable. Included in Tables 2–5 are the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient estimates and their signi<sup>fi</sup>cance levels, APE, and $R ^ { 2 } .$ . In the cases of BC (Table 3) and BT (Table 4), λ⁎, θ⁎, and $L _ { m a x }$ are also included. Table 6 provides a comprehensive comparison across different models with IT versus without IT, based on the whole sample of <sup>fi</sup>fteen countries under consideration.

The CD production function has been widely used in the literatures (e.g., CD has been used in four of the country-level studies reviewed in Section 2). Thus, it is of considerable importance to discuss the results in Table 2 in terms of factor shares or, equivalently, elasticity measures [53]. We can observe from Table 2 that, since the sums of the coef<sup>fi</sup>cients are slightly greater than 1 (1.008 and 1.066 for CD with and without IT, respectively), the production process is homogeneous of degree greater than 1 (increasing returns to scale), and the source of a small extra portion (0.8% and 6.6%, respectively) of the product is still unexplainable. However, in either situation, the production process is very close to homogeneity of degree 1. At the <sup>fi</sup>rm level, [53] has claimed a production system of decreasing returns to scale.

The results in Table 2 also indicate that the ordinary capital factor gets the highest share (68.5%, and 69.3%) for the models considered, which conform to traditional economic <sup>fi</sup>ndings: the share of output paid to capital is much higher than that paid to labor. For the model in which IT is used as a production factor, the IT-capital factor gets the lowest share (9.9%). At the <sup>fi</sup>rm level, [53] and [55] have concluded that labor gets the highest share and the IT factor earns the lowest share.

Finally, the lowest factor share of IT capital implies that the elasticity of output with respect to IT capital is much lower than that with respect to labor and that with respect to capital, meaning that output is least elastic with respect to IT capital. That is, output is much less responsive to IT stock than to labor and capital. The empirical results raise an important managerial and policy question, namely, how to increase the elasticity of output with respect to IT stock? Nevertheless, the estimate of $\beta _ { 3 }$ does show statistical signi<sup>fi</sup>cance regardless of the magnitude of the estimated coef<sup>fi</sup>cient in terms of the factor share or the output elasticity.

## 5.3. Analyzing IT value collectively

The analyses of the estimated results are carried out in two different ways: one way analyzes the business value of IT in terms of productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency based on the sign and statistical signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of the IT capital (I) as commonly practiced, whereas the other way analyzes and ranks the contributions of IT to productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies individually, i.e., for individual countries. The latter way of analysis represents an obvious departure from the common practice. This subsection takes up the <sup>fi</sup>rst way.

Based on the CD stochastic production frontier models (Table 2), the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient estimates of $\beta _ { 1 } , \beta _ { 2 } ,$ and $\beta _ { 3 }$ related to K, L, and I (with and without IT) are signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly positive at the 1% level, except $\beta _ { 3 }$ (at the 5% level). R<sup>2</sup> increases with the introduction of IT as a production factor, indicating that the inclusion of IT investment along with the two traditional production factors (K and L) leads to higher explanatory powers $( R ^ { 2 } = . 9 8 4$ with IT in comparison with $R ^ { 2 } { = } . 9 7 9$ without IT).

The signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly positive estimate of $\beta _ { 3 }$ suggests that the IT investment has a positive impact on output. Furthermore, since the estimate of $\beta _ { 3 }$ is statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, the introduction of IT stock changes the factor shares of capital and labor as well as the output elasticities with respect to capital and labor. We can observe that the inclusion of IT stock as a third factor lowers the estimates of both $\beta _ { 1 }$ and $\beta _ { 2 } .$ As an example, looking at the results in Table 2, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that when the IT stock is included, the estimate of $\rho _ { 1 }$ decreases from .693 to .685 and the estimate of $\beta _ { 2 }$ declines from .373 to .224.

A careful review of the overall average productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency (APE) in Table 2 suggests that the APE of .809 in the presence of IT is higher than that of .788 in the absence of IT stock, meaning that the use of IT stock as a production factor in the production process contributes an increase of 2.7% to the APE. To state alternatively, we conclude that the spending on IT has a positive impact on the productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of the sample countries considered as a whole. In other words, the use of IT capital as an additional input indeed has led to a higher APE than when it is absent from the production process (i.e., .809 with IT vs .788 without IT). These <sup>fi</sup>ndings seem to agree to the conclusion of the <sup>fi</sup>rm-level analysis by [53] where it has concluded that the relative size of IT investments has positive effects on the organizational performance as measured by the productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, and that for those <sup>fi</sup>rms investing relatively more in IT, their production processes are, on average, technically more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient than those spend less on IT. Collectively, our results seem to somewhat support the observation of [29,30] that even though the so-called production paradox disappears in the developed countries, it still exists in the developing nations. This collectively analytical conclusion is, however, not supported by the tale of the individually analytical outcome (Subsection 5.4 below).

We now turn to discuss the estimates of the BC, BT, and TL stochastic frontier models, as presented in Tables 3–5. As shown in these tables, $R ^ { 2 }$ again increases with the addition of IT capital as a production factor. In the frontiers of BC and BT, the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient estimates are all signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly positive at the 1% or 5% level, implying that IT investments again have a positive impact on output. Unlike the CD frontier, the estimates based on the BC and BT frontiers indicate that the presence of IT capital as an additional input does not result in a higher APE than when it is absent from the production process (e.g., .624 with IT vs. .808 without IT from Table 3 and .645 in the presence of IT vs. .814 in the absence of IT). In contrast, like the CD frontier, the use of IT capital along with ordinary capital and ordinary labor in the TL frontier model also leads to APE gains (Table 5).

In other words, normally and desirably, the productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of a with-IT model is greater than that of a without-IT one. Though the empirical results from both the CD and TL frontier models have delivered such a desirable message, consistent with conventional wisdom, the results of the BC and BT transformation frontiers are at variance with conventional wisdom; instead, they show that the productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies from the with-IT models are less than those from the without-IT frontiers.

Consequently, the empirical results based on the BC, BT, and TL do not necessarily imply the presence (absence) of the productivity paradox in developing (developed) countries. The empirical evidence is consistent with [55] but obviously contradicts [53,54] that have used a <sup>fi</sup>rm-level data set and have concluded that there is a robust and consistent relationship between IT investment and productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, irrespective of the production frontier model employed.

To conclude, the numerous empirical results have an important methodological implication. The traditional regression procedure of collectively analyzing the sign and signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of the IT capital along with ordinary capital and ordinary labor based on a panel data set to challenge the productivity paradox for developed countries or to con<sup>fi</sup>rm it for developing countries may prove to be successful if the CD production function is used; but it does not necessarily succeed when more general production functions such as BC, BT, and TL are deployed. Moreover, the collective analytical method fails to evaluate the varied performance of individual countries as far as the value of IT is concerned. Thus, further effort is directed to analyzing the contributions of IT to productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies in the <sup>fi</sup>fteen countries individually.

First of all, we can observe from Table 6 that on the average, the developed countries (i) have spent relatively more on IT than the developing countries have (i.e., higher IT ratio of 9.01 compared to 3.10, where the IT ratio is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the proportion of the IT spending to the sum of the spendings on capital, labor, and IT), and (ii) are uniformly more technically (productively) ef<sup>fi</sup>cient than their counterparts in the developing countries across differing frontier models as a result of the engagement in IT spending (i.e., the averages of APE 's of Group 1 are .8178, .6240, .6391, and .8723 based on CD, BC, BT, and TL, respectively, compared favorably to those of Group 2: .7763, .6233, .5376, and .8353, respectively). This conclusion is robust and consistent with respect to the choice of stochastic production frontiers. Previous research has failed to compare the IT ratio with the contribution of IT.

Looking carefully at the results for individual countries tells a quite different story. The driving forces may be summarized from social, economic, and cultural perspectives. Politically, China is communistic under one party's monopoly rather than democratic, quite different from US, UK, TW, HK, and other developed countries; but socially, economically, and culturally, China is getting closer to these countries. (i) Socially, China tends to put more emphasis on law and order, (ii) economically, China pursues a market economy under the open economic policy in the spirit of capitalism, and (iii) culturally, the Chinese are engaged actively in making money as a lifetime goal and becoming rich as a hero; especially, under an authoritative power system, humiliation has now become a tradition: workers who have worked too slowly or made the most mistakes during the production/ assembly process are humiliated by their supervisors and/or, faced their co-workers, must endure a several-minute-long lecture.

Overall, the rankings of the productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of individual countries are fairly robust and consistent across different frontier models. TH (Thailand) seems to be an exception. It ranks 11th, 9th, 14th, and 11th by the frontiers of CD, BC, BT, and TL, respectively.

Finally, the individual analytical method allows us to ask an important question: Does IT investment increase (I) or decrease (D) $\mathrm { A P E _ { i } } ?$ Table 7 shows the picture which reveals numerous points of interest: (i) based on CD and TL, IT investments lead to an increase in the productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of a country over the period 1993–1999 for about two-third of the countries (e.g., IA, JP, UK, SK, TH, and TW); (ii) but based on BC and BT, IT investments result in a decline in the productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency over the same period for virtually all the 15 countries except for US based on BC; (iii) in the cases of CN and SG, the IT spending decreases their productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies regardless of the choice of the frontier model; and (iv) all the 15 countries considered, no matter whether they are developed or developing , suffer from the loss of productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies because of the addition of IT capital as a production factor in at least two or three out of four frontier models.

The empirical evidence has signi<sup>fi</sup>cant implications: (i) the IT contributions as measured by the productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency differ from one country to another; (ii) IT spendings do not necessarily improve the productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of a country no matter whether it is a developed or a developing nation; (iii) according to Eq. (1), given technological changes, the productivity paradox may exist in a country regardless of whether it is a developed or a developing country , contradicting previous research [e.g., [29,30], but lending a support to [20] at the country level; and (iv) using CD or TL alone may reach misleading conclusions as far as the contributions of IT investment to productivity or productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency is concerned; and CD or TL happens to be employed popularly and widely in applied research.

## 5.6. Identification assumptions and specification error tests

There are fundamental differences in identi<sup>fi</sup>cation assumptions and speci<sup>fi</sup>cation error test between the classical multiple regression model (CMRM) and the (time-varying) stochastic production frontier model (SPFM) considered in this study (cf. [1,2,6,8,12,21–23,26,33– 39,41–43,45,52,67,68,71–74,81,82,85]). The identi<sup>fi</sup>cation assumptions in this study are the nonlinearity underlying a production function and normal-half normal speci<sup>fi</sup>cation. (Goldstein [35] has noted that the assumptions for the SPFM are mostly ad hoc and hard to justify theoretically and empirically.) Here, the speci<sup>fi</sup>cation error tests are based on those found available for the SPFM in the literature.

First of all, one important speci<sup>fi</sup>cation error test is the testing for the existence (absence) of the SPFM (i.e., the existence of the inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency effect represented by $V _ { i t } ) ,$ , since the distinction between the CMRM and the SPFM (i.e., the normal-half normal frontier model) is the one-sided error [72]. Under the half normal assumption, the null hypothesis is $H _ { 0 } \colon \sigma _ { \mathrm { u } } ^ { 2 } = 0$ against the alternative hypothesis $H _ { 1 } \colon \sigma _ { \mathrm { u } } ^ { 2 } > 0$ [23,72]. The hypothesis is equivalent to testing $H _ { 0 } \colon \lambda = 0$ against $H _ { 1 } \colon \lambda >$ 0 [1], where $\lambda { = } { \sigma } _ { u } / { \sigma } _ { v }$ The test statistic is called the Wald statistic or $Z \mathbf { = } \hat { \boldsymbol { \Lambda } } / s ( \hat { \boldsymbol { \Lambda } } ) \sim \mathbf { N } ( 0 , 1 )$ , where λ<sup>ˆ</sup> is the NML estimate of λ and s(λ<sup>ˆ</sup>) is the estimated standard error of λ̂ [22,23]. Because the observed values of the test statistic (5.21, 7.35, 10.40 and 2.87 for CD, BC, BT, and TL, respectively) are all greater than the critical values (1.645 and 2.326) at the 5% and 1% levels, the null hypothesis of no inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency effects is rejected, implying that the presence of is justi<sup>fi</sup>ed statistically and con<sup>fi</sup>rmed by the data. Using the Lagrangian multiplier (LM) test proposed by [72] and the likelihood ratio (LR) test [22] have led to the same conclusion. The conclusion represents a witness to robustness regardless of the frontier (CD, BC, BT, or TL) employed. But, BT that includes CD and BC as special cases seems to be the best choice and TL is the least desired one.

Second, under the normal-half normal speci<sup>fi</sup>cation for $\nu _ { i t }$ and $u _ { i t } ,$ it is also of critical importance to pursue a test of the half normal distribution assumption imposed on the inef<sup>fi</sup>cient component $\left( u _ { i t } \right)$ To this end, Lee [45] has proposed a LM test on $H _ { 0 } ; u _ { i t }$ is half-normally distributed versus $H _ { 1 } \colon u _ { i t }$ is not. The LM test statistic is shown to be chi-square distributed with three degrees of freedom under $H _ { 0 } .$ The values of the test statistic (4.31, 3.03, 2.57, and 5.48 for CD, BC, BT, and TL, respectively) compared to the critical values (7.81 and 11.34 at the 5% and 1% levels) strongly suggest that the half-normal distribution is not misspeci<sup>fi</sup>ed. The LR test [22,23] has led to the same conclusion. The conclusion is another witness to robustness and, again, it indicates that BT is the most, and TL is the least, desired frontier.

Third, the JB test [41] was applied to con<sup>fi</sup>rm that the normal distribution of the traditional (uncontrollable) error component $\left( \nu _ { i t } \right)$ is a proper speci<sup>fi</sup>cation.

Fourth, although the Z (Wald), LM, LR, and JB tests suggest that the normal-half normal model is adequate, the question is whether the frontier is to be preferred to OLS. Here, we do not have simultaneous equations systems and, as such, we cannot compare OLS with simultaneous equations methods (e.g., 2SLS, 3SLS, etc.) According to [22,72], this question is essentially a test of whether the composite error ${ \varepsilon } _ { i t } = { \nu } _ { i t } - { u } _ { i t }$ is also normally distribution. In theory, $\varepsilon _ { i t }$ may not be normal with a nonzero mean [36]. The negative (wrong) skewness is likely to occur in $\varepsilon _ { i t } .$ As mentioned before, the wrong skewness (w.s.) condition has been incorporated into the two-step NML estimation process. That ${ \mathrm { i } } s ,$ if w.s. prevails, the estimation process halts automatically; otherwise, the process is successful, meaning that the normality of $\varepsilon _ { i t }$ is satis<sup>fi</sup>ed. The third moment test which is equivalent to a standard normal test proposed in [67] has supported this argument. As [22] has correctly pointed out, when the third moment is negative, negative (wrong) skewness will occur. The results reported in Table 2 show no w.s.

Fifth, as far as the issue of unequal variances is concerned, we feel that it is very relevant when two groups (developed and developing) of countries are used as did in [29,30]. But, we have disclaimed the appropriateness of comparing average ef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies from group data. More importantly, the tests [21,73,81] on structural changes in relation with (un)equal variances are invalid for SPFM, and we have found no formal tests to deal with this issue associated with the SPFM in the presence of a compound error.

Sixth, in this study, the interrelationships between the independent variables (inputs) are relative to the issues of nonlinearity (as speci<sup>fi</sup>ed in the frontiers CD, BC, BT, and TL used, which are all nonlinear) and multicollinearity. Nonlinearity has been transformed into linearity (Appendix). The absence of multicollinearity is con<sup>fi</sup>rmed by empirical evidence (signi<sup>fi</sup>cant estimates with expected signs and the Pearson's correlation matrices [8,36,43,68]).

Seventh and last, in the CMRM, the potential interrelationships between the variables and the error term (v ) relate to the exogeneity of the independent variables as well as ARCH [33], ARCH-M [34], and GARCH [12]. However, the SPFM (with a composite error) counterparts of the tests developed by [12,26,33,34,39,85, etc.] for the CMRM are not readily available.

## 5.7. Theoretical contributions and implications of the findings

Management research is often criticized as lack of economic theory, use of inappropriate methodology, and commitment of mismeasurement. Contrary to these criticisms, this research (i) is based on a powerful theory called the theory of production; (ii) is the <sup>fi</sup>rst country-level study of its kind in DSS/MIS that applies a novel methodology called the time-varying stochastic production frontier approach – a companion of the theory, using productive (technical) ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency derived from the frontier approach – a companion of the approach, as the performance measure, thereby unlike other studies, it is unnecessary to seek performance metrics from outside the research models; (iii) makes use of the quantitative data collected from reliable sources, rather than from surveys; and $( \mathrm { i v } )$ the research approach allows for both collective and individual analytical methods in discussing the results, where the latter is shown to be more useful than the former method.

The empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings have several signi<sup>fi</sup>cant implications. First, collectively IT has a positive impact on productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency but, individually, IT appears to have adverse effects on the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies for some individual countries, no matter whether they are developed or developing nations. This con<sup>fl</sup>icting evidence implies that, as stated above, both collective and individual analytical methods used to analyze empirical results are needed. This in turn implies that previous research [e.g., 28–30, and others] using only the collective analytical method is de<sup>fi</sup>cient and, accordingly, its conclusions may be misleading.

The second implication is relative to the <sup>fi</sup>rst one and is associated with our conclusion that IT investments do not necessarily improve the technical ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of a country, irrespective of whether it is a developed or a developing country. This <sup>fi</sup>nding certainly implies that the traditional regression analysis approach is not an adequate methodology on the grounds that it does not permit the use of the individual analytical method. The collective analytical method is deemed to fail to evaluate the varied contributions of IT investment to the productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies of individual nations. Using the individual analytical method, we have found that IT may not be a contributing source of productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies. Thus, our empirical evidence supports the productivity paradox and challenges the existing notion that the paradox does not exist in developed countries but remains as an unpleasant phenomenon in developing economies.

The third signi<sup>fi</sup>cant implication relates to our argument that the division of a sample of countries into two subsamples (a group of developed nations and a group of developing economies) is inappropriate [20,46]; in particular, the division is often arbitrary. Our <sup>fi</sup>nding obviously suggests that the productivity paradox exists in a country, regardless of whether it is developed or developing. This <sup>fi</sup>nding provides a good explanation of why the con<sup>fl</sup>ict exists between previous two-group studies and the present study. In essence, the con<sup>fl</sup>ict arises from the lack of theory and the different research approaches and analytical methods used.

## 6. Concluding remarks, limitations, and future research

Since researchers have con<sup>fi</sup>rmed the promising contribution of IT investment and the disappearance of the productivity paradox at the <sup>fi</sup>rm level in the past decades, country-level studies concerning the contribution of IT investment are needed more than ever. Governments start wondering whether the increase in IT investment would enable them to better their competitive position. The ever-growing IT investments need to be justi<sup>fi</sup>ed for national, managerial, and organizational reasons.

By applying the time-varying frontier approach and by inspecting productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies derived from the Cobb–Douglas (CD), Box–Cox (BC), Box–Tidwell (BT), and translog (TL) stochastic production frontier models, a topic never studied in the country-level literature on IT value, this study distinguishes itself from previous research. Thus, the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant contributions of this research rest with the theoretical, methodological, analytical, and empirical fronts, as mentioned before.

In closing, let us mention several areas of future research in relation to some limitations. The question of how to capture the time lag effect in the regression model is worth noting. The issue of time lag effects of IT investments on productivity gains has drawn some attention [18,40,44], but statistical solutions and empirical results in relation to this issue remain lacking. Formally, a distributed lag scheme involving lagged $I _ { i t }$ in a two-equation system [9,52,75] that controls for lagged ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency as an explanatory variable was used to analyze the effects of the time lag and the lagged ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. The coef<sup>fi</sup>cients of the lagged $I _ { i t }$ and lagged ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency were found insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant and, consequently, the lagged ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency variable has little impact on productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies. However, it is argued that this is an issue that is worthy being pursued as more time series data are available. The question that remains is how to determine the number of lags (g) [51]. If the gains in productivity or productive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency (used in this research) led by IT were not seen until 30 or 40 years [25], then under this situation, a panel data set covering a very long time period is required. This would be a good topic for future research.

In extending studies of IT value at the <sup>fi</sup>rm- to the country-level, it is important to consider whether IT exports constitute a key application of technology spending. Inter-country trade surplus or foreign direct investment was used as proxies in the two-equation system; but, in either case, wrong (negative) skweness occurs, thereby no estimates were obtainable. In spite of this outcome, we strongly feel that as a panel data set in a longer time period becomes available, this issue merits further pursuit in the face of increasing globalization and international investing activities.

The use of simultaneous equations modeling may be a potential extension for future research while dealing with a large set of endogenous (dependant) and predetermined (exogenous and lagged dependent) variables, in addition to the observed output and the production factors, in complex modeling circumstances in which the issue of simultaneity becomes a major concern. However, such a nonlinear stochastic simultaneous equation model may encounter a great deal of dif<sup>fi</sup>culty in estimation which may not be solvable (at the present time), when a set of panel data is used, since it involves nonlinear stochastic frontier functions and half-normally distributed random inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency variables besides the traditional random errors.
