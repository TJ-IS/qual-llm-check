Christian Peukert

Católica-Lisbon School of Business and Economics, Lisbon, Portuga

## ABSTRACT

We study how <sup>fi</sup>rms decide whether to continue an existing relationship or switch the vendor of outsourced services. Because of incomplete contracts and relationship-speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments, client organisations may face switching costs, which in some cases may be large enough to render vendor switching an unattractive option. Based on an extensive review of the literature, we develop hypotheses about relationship-, <sup>fi</sup>rm-, and market-speci<sup>fi</sup>c determinants of switching costs. We then develop an econometric model to identify those determinants and their relative importance and estimate the magnitude of switching costs. In the empirical study, we observe accounting data of 1318 US credit unions (CUs) and follow their IT outsourcing choices over 11 years. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that the largest portion of switching costs is due to relationship- and <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c factors, while market-speci<sup>fi</sup>c variables are much less important. Our estimates suggest that average switching costs account for one-third of the average annual expenditure for professional services. We further highlight that the average hides substantial heterogeneity. Switching costs of CUs that decide to switch vendors are about 50% lower than those of CUs that stay. Looking more closely at stayers, our most conservative estimate of the lock-in e<sup>f</sup>ect, i.e., the di<sup>f</sup>erence in switching costs of deliberate and nondeliberate stayers, is equivalent to about 70% of the average annual expenditure for professional services.

## 1. Introduction

Firms across a wide range of sectors increasingly rely on external supply of IT services to enhance productivity (Han, Kau<sup>f</sup>man, & Nault, 2011; Hu, Saunders, & Gebelt, 1997; Loh & Venkatraman, 1992b). The realisation of those productivity gains often hinges on complementary organisational innovation, making successful IT outsourcing highly <sup>fi</sup>rm and relationship speci<sup>fi</sup>c (Lee, Miranda, & Kim, 2004). As a consequence, IT services are usually not traded on a spot market but require negotiation of individual contracts involving few parties (Hecker & Kretschmer, 2010).

In this decision, <sup>fi</sup>rms trade o<sup>f</sup> di<sup>f</sup>erences in fees or quality against the costs incurred by switching. Case studies suggest that switching costs are “large” (Lacity, Willcocks, & Feeny, 1996, p. 17), “high” (Kern & Willcocks, 2002, p. 12), “extremely high” (Hirschheim & Lacity, 2006, p. 327), “massive” (Feeny, Willcocks, & Lacity, 2006, p. 544), or even “the single most threatening aspect of IT outsourcing” (Poppo & Lacity, 2006, p. 278), which makes switching “extremely costly and painful” (Weeks & hold up (Mani, Barua, & Whinston, 2012; Susarla, 2012; Susarla, Subramanyam, & Karhade, 2010). A speci<sup>fi</sup>c mechanism to mitigate contractual hazards is to reduce contract length (Crocker & Masten, 1988; Gurbaxani, 2007; Joskow, 1987) and evidence indeed suggests that the average duration of IT outsourcing contracts tends to be less than 3 years. For example, in Susarla et al.’s (2010) sample of large-scale outsourcing contracts, the median duration is 29 months and about 28% of the contracts are longer than 4 years. Fifty-eight per cent of the contracts studied in Susarla (2012) are shorter than 3 years. As a result however, <sup>fi</sup>rms are regularly confronted with the decision to renew an existing contract (Susarla, 2012) or switch vendors after evaluating the market (Whitten & Wake<sup>fi</sup>eld, 2006).

Feeny, 2008, p. 127). Still, switching costs have been characterised as hidden costs that “managers <sup>fi</sup>nd hard to quantify” (Barthélemy, 2001, p. 66).

This motivates the research questions we want to address in our paper. First, we are interested in testing hypotheses about the determinants of switching costs. We then go on to ask whether we can estimate the economic size of switching costs, and how switching costs vary across <sup>fi</sup>rms.

To do so, we develop a structural econometric model that allows to estimate the determinants and magnitude of switching cost from accounting data. The starting point is a stylised model where <sup>fi</sup>rms switch vendors if the new vendor’s fee is lower than the incumbent vendor’s fee minus the cost of switching (Elzinga & Mills, 1998). The econometric challenge is to account for the endogeneity of expenditure for outsourcing caused by renegotiation, or changes in service quality or supply-side competition that make it di<sup>fi</sup>cult to distinguish unobserved contract renewal (deliberate staying) from non-deliberate staying (because of lock-in e<sup>f</sup>ects). The identi<sup>fi</sup>cation strategy is to explicitly model those changes, assuming that a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s expenditure on outsourced IT services grows at a constant rate in the long run, but renegotiation, changes in quality and market characteristics, and vendor switching cause short-run <sup>fl</sup>uctuations.

Prior research has shown that the switching decision and managers’ perceptions of switching costs are determined by factors such as search and set-up costs, uncertainty about service quality, learning costs, and relationship quality (Ono & Stango, 2005; Whitten & Wake<sup>fi</sup>eld, 2006). Our model can be useful to understand the relative importance of <sup>fi</sup>rm-, relationship-, and market-speci<sup>fi</sup>c drivers of switching costs. Recognising that at least some of those factors can be strategic variables in negotiations, understanding their relative weight in the determination of switching costs can yield valuable insights for practice.

While estimating average switching costs in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c empirical setting is complementary to a largely case study-based and practitioner-oriented literature, exploring the heterogeneity can yield additional insights that expand beyond the speci<sup>fi</sup>c setting. IT outsourcing relationships typically result from a competitive vendor selection process (Gurbaxani, 2007; Michell & Fitzgerald, 1997). To win an account from a competitor, vendors undercut rivals’ fees (or alternatively o<sup>f</sup>er an increase in quality) by an amount at least equivalent to the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s switching costs (Chen, 1997; Taylor, 2003). Consequently, because the new vendor bares the cost of switching on behalf of the <sup>fi</sup>rm, switching costs can become negligible for the <sup>fi</sup>rm if competitive forces are su<sup>fi</sup>- ciently strong. Our econometric model allows to test this idea by estimating switching costs for <sup>fi</sup>rms that switch vendors and for those that non-deliberately do not. This can be useful to understand switching inertia and ultimately supply-side market structure (Li, Madhok, Plaschka, & Verma, 2006).

The empirical application is based on a rich set of panel data, which lets us track <sup>fi</sup>rms and vendors from 2002 to 2009. We observe <sup>fi</sup>rm-, market-, and relationship-speci<sup>fi</sup>c variables of 1318 US credit unions (CUs) (cooperative banks) and 21 respective vendors. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that average switching costs account for about 30% of annual expenditure for professional services. Firms that do not switch face substantially higher switching costs – in the order of 70–210% of annual expenditure for professional services.

Our paper complements a literature that has studied the returns to IT outsourcing (Han et al., 2011), in that we highlight an important cost factor that can (at least partially) be a<sup>f</sup>ected by <sup>fi</sup>rm strategy and market dynamics. We further add to a literature that has studied how contractual arrangements can mitigate opportunistic hazards in IT outsourcing (Anderson and Dekker, 2005; Chen & Bharadwaj, 2009; Fitoussi & Gurbaxani, 2012; Gopal, Sivaramakrishnan, Krishnan, & Mukhopadhyay, 2003; Susarla, 2012; Susarla et al., 2010), in that we study factors that foster or hamper switching between contracts and vendors. Our empirical insights may also be helpful to further develop theory on the optimal pricing structure for outsourcing contracts (e.g., Dey, Fan, & Zhang, 2010).

The remainder is structured as follows: <sup>fi</sup>rst, we review the literature to derive testable hypotheses. Thereafter, we develop an econometric model of vendor switching. A description of the data and operationalisation follows. Finally, we present and discuss the estimation results and conclude with a discussion of key contributions.

## 2. Hypotheses

For a number of reasons, <sup>fi</sup>rms regularly consider to switch the vendor of IT services. Sometimes organisational changes may require a di<sup>f</sup>erent type of service or contractual agreement (Sia, Lim, & Periasamy, 2010), sometimes the current vendor may deliver poor quality or act opportunistically (Whitten & Leidner, 2006). However, the most common reason is simply that the end of the current contract period is approaching (Barthélemy, 2001). The <sup>fi</sup>rst best option is usually to renegotiate contractual terms (Susarla, 2012) and compare the new o<sup>f</sup>er to o<sup>f</sup>ers of competing vendors. Long-term IT outsourcing contracts typically institutionalise this by including a provision for scheduled renegotiations (Gurbaxani, 2007, p. 108). Switching vendors can be costly when investments speci<sup>fi</sup>c to the current vendor have to be duplicated. Hence, when comparing fees and service quality across vendors, <sup>fi</sup>rms need to take switching costs into consideration. The size of these costs is determined by various factors (Whitten & Wake<sup>fi</sup>eld, 2006). In the following, we develop hypotheses about how market-speci<sup>fi</sup>c, relationshipspeci<sup>fi</sup>c, or <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c factors determine switching costs.

## 2.1. Market-speci<sup>fi</sup>c factors

Competition in outsourcing fees, competition in quality, and competition for attention can have direct e<sup>f</sup>ects on the fees that <sup>fi</sup>rms pay after renegotiating outsourcing contracts, perhaps reducing the fees to a level that is low enough to convince the <sup>fi</sup>rm to stay with the current vendor. However, market-speci<sup>fi</sup>c factors can also reduce the switching costs that <sup>fi</sup>rms incur, and therefore making a switch to an alternative vendor more likely. While the former is important for our econometric identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, only the latter is of direct economic interest in this paper. For this reason, we only develop hypotheses about the relationship between market-speci<sup>fi</sup>c factors and switching costs, not outsourcing fees.

## 2.1.1. Vendor market structure

The industrial organisation literature shows that switching costs can create entry barriers. Markets with switching costs tend to be less competitive, which results in ine<sup>fi</sup>ciency and high average prices (Farrell & Klemperer, 2007). Nevertheless, competitive forces may still play a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in such markets. In a typical IT outsourcing vendor selection process, <sup>fi</sup>rms solicit competing bids from di<sup>f</sup>erent vendors (Gurbaxani, 2007; Michell & Fitzgerald, 1997). In the process of renegotiations, the incumbent vendor takes advantage of the fact that the <sup>fi</sup>rm will incur costs when switching to a di<sup>f</sup>erent vendor. Correspondingly, under perfect information and symmetric bargaining power, the incumbent vendor’s o<sup>f</sup>er matches the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s switching (Klemperer, 1995). In turn, a rival vendor will undercut this o<sup>f</sup>er in expectation of higher pro<sup>fi</sup>ts in later periods (Chen, 1997) and switching costs become negligible in equilibrium. However, incomplete information and bounded rationality limits the ability to formulate complete contracts, which can lead to opportunistic behaviour of the contracting parties (Klein et al., 1978; Williamson, 1975). Once relationship-speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments have been made, ex-ante competitive markets transform to ex-post small numbers bargaining situations. Switching costs then shape up as a direct consequence of complex contracts and relationship-speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments of the <sup>fi</sup>rm.

Competition can a<sup>f</sup>ect the size of switching costs in two ways: statically and dynamically. When there is a large number of equally quali<sup>fi</sup>ed vendors to select from ex-ante, competitive forces may reduce ex-post vendor-opportunism, because the <sup>fi</sup>rm can more credibly threat to terminate (or not renew) the current contract. If the vendor market structure does not change over the course of the contract and the expost e<sup>f</sup>ects of competition can be foreseen, the initial negotiations should internalise the described e<sup>f</sup>ects. In that sense, competition induces vendors to commit to non-opportunistic behaviour and switching costs are lower when the vendor market is less concentrated at the time when the contract is signed. Turning to the dynamic perspective, accordingly, any measure of increased ex-post competition is likely to reduce the vendor’s ability to extract quasirents and in turn lower switching costs.

<sup>Hypothesis 1.</sup> Vendor market concentration is positively associated with switching costs.

## 2.1.2. Quality

The argument so far was based on the assumption that vendors o<sup>f</sup>er identical services. In that scenario, if they o<sup>f</sup>er di<sup>f</sup>erent fees, <sup>fi</sup>rms will opt for the vendor charging the lowest fees. Let’s now explicitly take into consideration that vendors are heterogeneous in their service o<sup>f</sup>erings. Assume that some vendors provide a higher service quality than others. Not working with a vendor of higher quality represents an opportunity cost of the current relationship, for example when contracting a high-quality vendor allows the strategic acquisition of speci<sup>fi</sup>c capabilities (Linder, 2004). When deciding whether to switch, the <sup>fi</sup>rm trades o<sup>f</sup> this opportunity cost and the cost of migrating (which jointly de<sup>fi</sup>ne switching costs) with the fees of the high-quality vendor. Note that the investments in quality of an alternative vendor may of course be re<sup>fl</sup>ected in higher fees compared to the current vendor. If the relative di<sup>f</sup>erence in quality is high enough (i.e., the opportunity cost is large enough), switching costs (cost of migrating and opportunity cost) become low enough to make switching an attractive option. Hence, we expect:

<sup>Hypothesis 2.</sup> Higher quality of a vendor is negatively associated with switching costs.

## 2.1.3. Visibility

When deciding about the relevant set of vendors to approach with a request for information or request for quotation, the <sup>fi</sup>rm may not be fully informed about all vendors in the market. To increase their visibility to potential clients, vendors participate in conferences and trade fairs and invest in public relations and advertising. As a result, <sup>fi</sup>rms are more likely to consider switching when alternative vendors are more visible (Oliver, 1999; Shum, 2004). The underlying mechanism is similar to that of relative quality. Higher visibility of an alternative vendor can represent an opportunity cost of the current relationship in the sense that it is less costly to collect and verify information about an alternative vendor (e.g., concerning product characteristics that may become relevant for the <sup>fi</sup>rm in the future). Then again, if the relative di<sup>f</sup>erence in visibility is high enough, switching costs (the sum of migration and opportunity cost) can become low enough to make switching an attractive option. As a result, we postulate:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>3.</sup> Higher visibility of a vendor is negatively associated with switching costs.

## 2.2. Relationship-speci<sup>fi</sup>c factors

Relationship-speci<sup>fi</sup>c factors can be costly to duplicate, which makes it expensive to switch vendors. In what follows, we develop hypotheses regarding the relation between switching costs and asset speci<sup>fi</sup>city, relationship duration and bargaining power.

## 2.2.1. Speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments

Williamson de<sup>fi</sup>nes asset speci<sup>fi</sup>city as “the degree to which an asset can be redeployed to alternative uses and by alternative users without sacri<sup>fi</sup>ce of productive value” (Williamson, 1991, p. 281), which is related to the concepts of standards and compatibility in the industrial organisation and IS literatures (Farrell & Klemperer, 2007; Liu, Gal-Or, Kemerer, & Smith, 2011). The more speci<sup>fi</sup>c (less standardised or less compatible) an asset, the harder it is to transfer it to a di<sup>f</sup>erent relationship, independent of its absolute value. In the case of IT outsourcing, what are the speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments that a <sup>fi</sup>rm makes? A vendor may o<sup>f</sup>er some software features as part of a bundle and others only on a customised basis. This can allow not only to charge higher fees but also to strategically raise the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s switching costs. While the vendor may face costs to customise software, these are likely small relative to the overall value of the contract, and with su<sup>fi</sup>cient bargaining power, it may be possible to pass these costs on to the client. With symmetric bargaining power, there is no incentive for vendors to treat these investments as “cost of doing business”, because, without a credible outside option, the <sup>fi</sup>rm will have to accept a higher fee. In addition to that, the <sup>fi</sup>rm needs to invest time and e<sup>f</sup>ort to develop capabilities required to optimally use the vendor’s service – perhaps more so if there are more customised features – which further increases switching costs (Brush, Dangol, & O’Brien, 2012). Accordingly, we postulate:

<sup>Hypothesis 4.</sup> Larger speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments of the <sup>fi</sup>rm are positively associated with switching costs.

## 2.2.2. Relationship duration

When speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments of the <sup>fi</sup>rm are able to increase switching costs, the <sup>fi</sup>rm may want to set incentives such that the vendor makes more investments, in order to reduce the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s switching costs. With longer contracts, the <sup>fi</sup>rm commits to purchase a larger volume of services, generating greater incentives for the vendor to make speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments (Gurbaxani, 2007). However, what is the optimal contract duration? A short contract may trigger too little investment when the vendor’s total bene<sup>fi</sup>ts over the contract term are too small to fully recoup the investment. Increasing the contract duration will induce more investment of the vendor and therefore decrease switching costs of the <sup>fi</sup>rm. This idea is in line with evidence that suggests that longer contracts tend to be more extensive and foreseeing more contingencies (Susarla et al., 2010). However, a too long contract duration can induce the vendor to invest too much up front. Too long contracts can limit the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s <sup>fl</sup>exibility, for example by adopting transaction-speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments (Crocker & Masten, 1988) and by investments in obsolete technology (Afuah, 2001). This will again increase switching costs. Balancing these forces, an intermediate contract duration can create the right amount of incentives to induce optimal investment (Guriev & Kvasov, 2005). Note that intermediate contract duration only induces optimal investment at the time when the contract is signed, becoming suboptimal later, because it becomes “too short” as time elapses. Guriev and Kvasov (2005) propose that a sequence of <sup>fi</sup>xed-term contracts that constantly replace each other provides incentives for e<sup>fi</sup>cient investment. The general notion that contracts can be either too short or too long, however, remains. We therefore conclude:

<sup>Hypothesis 5.</sup> The duration of the outsourcing relationship a<sup>f</sup>ects switching costs in an inverted u-shaped form.

## 2.2.3. Bargaining power

The assumption so far was that bargaining power is symmetric. Higher bargaining power of the <sup>fi</sup>rm, for example, because the <sup>fi</sup>rm accounts for a large share of the vendor’s revenues, will therefore put the <sup>fi</sup>rm into a situation where it can negotiate a bargain on outsourcing fees – conditional on the competitive environment in the vendor market. Further, a <sup>fi</sup>rm with bargaining power will be less likely to fall prey to strategic behaviour that intends to create switching costs. For example, it should be more di<sup>fi</sup>cult for the vendor to impose a dynamic pricing structure, where fees increase over time (“bargain-then-ripo<sup>f</sup>”, Farrell & Klemperer, 2007), on a <sup>fi</sup>rm that has more bargaining power. Further, a <sup>fi</sup>rm with higher bargaining power may, for example, be able to negotiate smaller fees for converting data from the current vendor’s system to the new vendor’s system. When relative bargaining power is constant over time, the e<sup>f</sup>ect will play out in the initial negotiations, leaving fees in ex-post negotiations una<sup>f</sup>ected. Hence, switching costs are lower when the <sup>fi</sup>rm has high bargaining power at the time when the contract is signed.

In some cases, however, the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s bargaining power may increase over time, e.g., because the <sup>fi</sup>rm has grown extensively, or because other important clients have left the vendor, etc. This may enable the <sup>fi</sup>rm to subsequently hold up the vendor in ex-post negotiations. The vendor’s o<sup>f</sup>er will at least need to match the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s switching costs. Resulting from a trade o<sup>f</sup> between the risk of termination/non-renewal and forgone pro<sup>fi</sup>t, the new o<sup>f</sup>er may even be below switching costs if the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s bargaining power is su<sup>fi</sup>ciently high. Renegotiated contractual terms are therefore likely to feature lower fees and a <sup>fi</sup>rm with high bargaining power will bene<sup>fi</sup>t from lower switching costs.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>6</sup>. The <sup>fi</sup>rm’s bargaining power is negatively associated with switching costs.

## 2.3. Firm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c factors

Finally, we discuss how the resources that the <sup>fi</sup>rm invests in the vendor selection process, its experience in switching vendors, and <sup>fi</sup>rm size determine switching costs.

## 2.3.1. Search e<sup>f</sup>ort

The selection of vendors for outsourced IT services typically follows a structured process (Gurbaxani, 2007; Michell & Fitzgerald, 1997). A <sup>fi</sup>rst phase involves drafting a short list of vendors, where <sup>fi</sup>rms either directly approach candidates or openly advertise for vendors. Both often include a request for proposals that outline the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s objectives and detail the functions it seeks to outsource. This process often involves visits and presentations as well as an exchange of documentation. In a second phase, the <sup>fi</sup>rm issues formal invitations to tender to the vendors on the short list. Based on a comparison of alternatives, the <sup>fi</sup>nal decision is taken. The overall process is both time-consuming and involves deployment of resources for documentation and negotiation. However, investing in search e<sup>f</sup>ort reduces uncertainty about vendor quality and fees and enables a more informed decision, which will ultimately lower switching costs (Barthélemy, 2001).

<sup>Hypothesis 7.</sup> Greater e<sup>f</sup>ort to search a suitable vendor is negatively associated with switching costs.

Note that the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s investments in search are similar to the vendor’s investments in visibility that we have discussed above.

## 2.3.2. Switching experience

Although we have concluded that substantial investments in search e<sup>f</sup>ort may be necessary to reduce uncertainty about fees and vendor quality, we have also made the argument that the selection process typically follows a predetermined structure. This implies that there might be bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from experiential learning when a selection process is carried out repeatedly (Vanneste & Puranam, 2010). However, this e<sup>f</sup>ect may decay over time because of bounded rationality and changes in the external environment such that returns from experience are highest right in the beginning of a new contract (Argote, Beckman, & Epple, 1990). That is, when the latest experience is still fresh. Figure 1 illustrates this as a positively sloping curve with decreasing returns. However, IT outsourcing contracts typically include clauses that de<sup>fi</sup>ne termination fees, which vendor use to hedge against the risk of opportunistic behaviour of the <sup>fi</sup>rm (Gurbaxani, 2007). Depending on the speci<sup>fi</sup>cs of the individual contract, fees can be <sup>fi</sup>xed over the entire length of the contract or may only apply in the beginning of the contract. Termination fees, of course, are a factor that directly increases switching costs. The combined e<sup>f</sup>ect of experience and termination fee depends on the functional form of the experience e<sup>f</sup>ect, and the level and type of termination fees. The example in Figure 1 illustrates a possible scenario. At the beginning of a contract, termination fees increase switching costs, up until a point where they are no longer important. Although the combined e<sup>f</sup>ect (marked by o in Figure 1) is discontinuous, it can be approximated polynomially as illustrated by the dashed lines in Figure 1. This leads to the following testable hypothesis:

<sup>Hypothesis 8.</sup> Switching experience is negatively associated with switching costs, and this association has decreasing returns.

## 2.3.3. Firm size

Larger <sup>fi</sup>rms are typically governed by a more complex organisation and corresponding management practices. More complex organisational structures require larger complementary investments to make new or improved IT systems productive (Bresnahan, Brynjolfsson, & Hitt, 2002; Brynjolfsson & Hitt, 2000). These investments then need to be duplicated when switching vendors. Whitten and Wake<sup>fi</sup>eld (2006) describe these “management system upgrade costs” as monetary cost of acquiring additional system assets or human resources, or as an intangible cost of time and e<sup>f</sup>ort to integrate assets. On top of that, complexity may be an obstacle in the search for quali<sup>fi</sup>ed vendors, causing more inertia of larger <sup>fi</sup>rms (Li et al., 2006; Oliver, 1999). However, because larger <sup>fi</sup>rms can bene<sup>fi</sup>t from economies of scale, such investments will also have increasing returns to scale (Hecker & Kretschmer, 2010). Such an argument would imply that switching costs decrease with <sup>fi</sup>rm size. Evidence seems to indirectly support this notion. Susarla et al. (2010) show that larger <sup>fi</sup>rms are less likely to renew IT outsourcing contracts with their current vendors, and Ang and Cummings (1997) show that larger <sup>fi</sup>rms are more likely to outsource in the <sup>fi</sup>rst place, both potentially because of lower switching costs. We therefore expect:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>9.</sup> Switching costs are smaller for larger <sup>fi</sup>rms.

## 3. Econometric model

In this section, we brie<sup>fl</sup>y describe the econometric model that will allow us to estimate switching costs. The interested reader is referred to Technical Appendix for a detailed description. In what follows, we use the term expenditure to refer to what the <sup>fi</sup>rm actually pays for using external IT services. Inter alia, changes in expenditure can occur because the <sup>fi</sup>rm switches vendors that charge di<sup>f</sup>erent outsourcing fees.

The basic idea of the model is simple. The switching decision is driven by a comparison between the outsourcing fees that the <sup>fi</sup>rm faces if it stays with the current vendor and those it would face if it switched vendors. In this decision, the <sup>fi</sup>rm also takes the associated switching costs into account. That is, the <sup>fi</sup>rm switches if the value of switching $\nu _ { i , t + 1 } ^ { * }$ is positive, i.e.,

$$
\nu_ {i, t + 1} ^ {*} = \Delta p _ {i, t} - s _ {i, j, t} ^ {*} > 0,\tag{1}
$$

where $\Delta p _ { i , t } = p _ { i , t } - p _ { i , t + 1 }$ is the change in expenditure of <sup>fi</sup>rm i from period t to period t 1, and $s _ { i , j , i } ^ { * }$ are the switching costs <sup>fi</sup>rm i incurs with vendor j.

Switching costs are latent in this model, as is the value of switching. Some of the factors that will lead to a reduction in outsourcing fees over time are caused by switching (i.e., lower fees charged by an alternative vendor), others are unrelated to switching. For example, the <sup>fi</sup>rm may successfully renegotiate contractual terms with its current vendor and because of that, outsourcing expenditure will decrease from one period to another. Such a case is what we refer to as deliberate staying. When we can only observe whether or not a <sup>fi</sup>rm has switched vendors, but not whether or not a <sup>fi</sup>rm has renegotiated the contractual terms with its current vendor, it becomes challenging to estimate switching costs. In such a scenario, we overestimate switching costs if everything we observe is a <sup>fi</sup>rm that does not switch vendors, despite relatively large savings. We therefore develop an econometric model that allows to distinguish between deliberate staying and non-deliberate staying and therefore estimate switching costs consistently.

The approach is based on the idea that outsourcing expenditure follows a stable trend in the long run, but there are short-run <sup>fl</sup>uctuations caused by renegotiation and vendor switching, among other factors. The left-hand panel of Figure 2, plotting aggregate producer price index data for “data processing, hosting and related services”, suggests that this assumption is not overly unrealistic (see also the discussion in Section 5.2). There is some short-run <sup>fl</sup>uctuation, but there is also a clearly visible long-run trend over the period 2000–2009.

Formulated as a di<sup>f</sup>erence equation, we can write $\Delta { p } _ { i , t }$ as a function of outsourcing expenditure in an arbitrary reference year τ and other observables <sup>z</sup> that can explain changes to the long-run trajectory (e.g., changes in bargaining power, competition, and quality):

$$
\Delta p _ {i, t} = \alpha p _ {i, \tau} + \mathbf {z} _ {i, j, t} ^ {\prime} \boldsymbol {\zeta} + u _ {i, j, t},\tag{2}
$$

where α and $\zeta$ are parameters and $u _ { i , j , t }$ is an error term (see Technical Appendix for details).

We model switching costs $s _ { i , j , t } ^ { * }$ as a function of observable characteristics $\mathbf { x } _ { i , j , t }$ and use Equation (2) to rewrite Equation (1) as follows:

$$
\nu_ {i, t + 1} ^ {*} = \alpha p _ {i, \tau} - \mathbf {x} _ {i, j, t} ^ {\prime} \boldsymbol {\beta} + \mathbf {z} _ {i, j, t} ^ {\prime} \boldsymbol {\zeta} + \varepsilon_ {i, j, t},\tag{3}
$$

where $\varepsilon _ { i , j , t } { \sim } N ( 0 , \sigma ^ { 2 } )$ is an error term. We can estimate the parameters of this equation in a standard probit model, in which we infer the latent value of switching $\nu _ { i , t + 1 } ^ { * }$ from the observed switching decision. However, probit estimates will only be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed up to a scale, i.e., instead of an estimate of $\mid \beta ,$ we will only be able to estimate $\beta / \sigma$ . We therefore propose an estimation procedure that <sup>fi</sup>rst estimates σ and then uses this to estimate $\beta .$ Now we can predict switching costs – remember that $\beta$ is the parameter vector that de<sup>fi</sup>nes the relative “importance” of observed determinants of switching costs $s _ { i , j , t } ^ { * } .$

The intuition for our estimator is similar to the well-known two-stage IV estimator. The idea is to obtain an estimate of α from a <sup>fi</sup>rst stage regression on a variable that is correlated to $\Delta p .$ . This will give us an estimate of $\alpha .$ Combined with the estimate of $\alpha / \sigma$ from the probit model, we can infer $\sigma .$ What is a variable that is correlated with IT outsourcing fees? We observe accounting data on expenditure for professional services $\left( c _ { i , t } \right)$ that is correlated with IT outsourcing fees by de<sup>fi</sup>nition, because it is the sum of IT outsourcing expenditure $\left( \boldsymbol { p } _ { i , t } \right)$ and expenditure for other professional services $( o _ { i , t } )$ . We then make the same assumption of a long-run trend and short-run <sup>fl</sup>uctuations in the expenditure for professional services that led to Equation (2). The right panel of Figure 2, plotting combined price index for “certi<sup>fi</sup>ed public accountants-tax preparation and planning”, “business and commercial legal services”, and “management and technical consulting services”, again suggests that this assumption is not overly unrealistic. Also here, we see some short-run <sup>fl</sup>uctuations, but there is also a clearly visible long-run trend over the period 2000–2009. The <sup>fi</sup>rst stage regression can then be written as a function of IT outsourcing expenditure in an arbitrary year $\displaystyle { \mathit { p } } _ { i , \tau } ,$ , expenditure for professional services in the same arbitrary year $o _ { i , \tau } ,$ , and other observables <sup>z</sup> that can explain short-run changes to the long-run trajectory:

$$
\Delta c _ {i, t} = (\alpha - \gamma) p _ {i, \tau} + \gamma c _ {i, \tau} + \mathbf {z} _ {i, j, t} ^ {\prime} \boldsymbol {\zeta} + \tilde {\varepsilon} _ {i, j, t}.\tag{4}
$$

Note that the dependent variable in this regression is the change in expenditure for professional services of <sup>fi</sup>rm i, from period t to period t 1. Together with the estimate from the probit model de<sup>fi</sup>ned in Equation (3), i.e., $\boldsymbol { \hat { \alpha } } = \boldsymbol { \alpha } / \sigma ,$ we can derive $\widehat { \sigma } = ( \widehat { \alpha - \gamma } + \widehat { \gamma } ) / \widehat { \alpha } _ { \nu }$ . In Technical Appendix, we discuss the estimation of the variance matrix for this system of equations. Finally, we can compute the size of switching costs directly as a prediction from the model, i.e., $\hat { { s } } _ { i , j , t } = \mathbf { x } _ { i , j , t } ^ { \prime } \hat { { \boldsymbol { \beta } } } .$

## 4. Data and empirical speci<sup>fi</sup>cation

## 4.1. Industry background

Our empirical setting is the <sup>fi</sup>nancial industry. We use a unique panel of micro-data of US CUs and their IT service vendors, 1999–2009. CUs are not-for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t member-owned <sup>fi</sup>nancial cooperatives. Membership is based on a community, organisational, religious, or employee a<sup>fi</sup>liation (Branch & Grace, 2008). In 2009, CUs had more than 91 million members which accounts for roughly 44% of the economically active US population. According to data from World Council of Credit Unions and Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation, approximately 8600 CUs held some \$760 billion in deposits accounting for about 15% of total deposits in the United States. IT has been playing an important role in the <sup>fi</sup>nancial sector since the 1950s with applications such as check handling, bookkeeping, credit analysis, ATM, and e-banking (Franke, 1987). The signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly higher IT investment compared to other industries (Dewan & Min, 1997) is accompanied by a high degree of IT outsourcing (Ang & Cummings, 1997). In order to capitalise on economies of scale and gain access to technology and expertise, organisations do not operate own data centres but choose to resort to external suppliers of IT services. CUs particularly rely on ITO for essential technology services like processing of deposit and loan data, customer information <sup>fi</sup>les, and general ledger processing (Robbins & Van Walleghem, 2004). Share and loan processing is the umbrella term for IT that allows the CU to manage the records of their share and loan transactions. This can be understood as the technical infrastructure needed to carry out book keeping, computing credit scores, and to enable communication between decentralised terminals and a central server infrastructure. CUs vary greatly in which speci<sup>fi</sup>c tools and services they purchase from the variety of solutions o<sup>f</sup>ered by vendors (Asterisk, 2016). Like most other commercial banks (Rowe, 1994), the business processes which are supported and enabled by this technical infrastructure, however, are not outsourced and remain within the organisation. This implies that outsourcing of share and loan processing, even though the name might suggest otherwise, is not a form of business process outsourcing – typically de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the external provision of entire processes along the value chain of an organisation (see, e.g., Duan, Grover, & Balakrishnan, 2009). On the contrary, it seems to squarely <sup>fi</sup>t Loh and Venkatraman (1992a: 9) de<sup>fi</sup>nition of IT outsourcing, which is “the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant contribution by external vendors in the physical and/or human resources associated with the entire or speci<sup>fi</sup>c components of the IT infrastructure in the user organization”.

## 4.2. Data sources

We collect data from multiple sources including a regulatory authority, an industry association and trade periodicals.

## 4.2.1. NCUA call report data

The main data set comes from the regulatory authority, National Credit Union Administration (NCUA). The NCUA commits CUs to <sup>fi</sup>le quarterly Call Reports spanning a wide range of variables. In part, these data are subsequently made available to the public. We use data from 1999 to 2009 and match additional variables from other sources. The survey’s focus is on <sup>fi</sup>nancial information, but we can also distinguish di<sup>f</sup>erent types of IT organisation. Systems can be either (1) manual (no automation), (2) vendor-supplied in-house (VIH), (3)

vendor online service bureau (VOL), (4) developed in-house, or (5) other. With more than 98%, CUs mainly adopt VIH and VOL during 1999–2009. In VIH arrangements, CUs purchase a software licence typically running on in-house hardware operated by own IT sta<sup>f</sup>. In this paper, we focus on CU that uses a VOL system, which amounts to more than a quarter of all CU. We exclude CU that switched the system during the observed period. In VOL systems, “hardware and software systems reside at the vendor’s location with the exception of those systems a CU requires in order to access and transmit data to the vendor. Most, if not all, data processing occurs at the vendor’s site. Service bureau vendors generally provide reports (paper, electronic, micro-<sup>fi</sup>che, or other) on a regular basis. Connection is made to a service bureau through dial-up connections, private lines, the Internet, frame relay, or other WAN services. The service provided may be real-time (transaction post upon data entry) or batch processing (data is accumulated throughout a predetermined time period and then transmitted to the vendor for processing at predetermined intervals)” (NCUA, 2004, p. 6a). We identify corresponding vendors by name.

## 4.2.2. Information systems guide

The Information Systems Guide is published as a yearly supplement to Credit Union Magazine starting in 2002. Credit Union Magazine is a tradepress journal issued monthly by Credit Union National Association (CUNA), a trade association. It has the largest circulation of any industry periodical. We obtain very detailed yearly information on the major IT vendors in the CU market by observing a total of 97 categories of technical features, such as ATM network integration, Internet banking, customer relationship management systems, or security. For every vendor and year, we know whether a particular feature is (0) not available, (1) standard, (2) optional, or (3) needs to be custom made. We match this information to the NCUA Call Report data by vendor name, which reduces the observed years to 2002–2009. In some years, the Information Systems Guide lists more than one software system of a particular vendor. As we do not observe such a distinction in the NCUA Call Report data, we selected the most advanced software system (as measured by the number of features that are indicated as standard).

## 4.2.3. Technology and spending report

Data on expenditure for IT outsourcing come from the Technology & Spending Report 2008 (CUNA, 2008). The document details results from a survey conducted in November 2007. The total sample in CUNA (2008) includes 581 CU respondents with \$1 million or more in assets (response rate 19%). We match aggregate information from this source to our NCUA call report data based on 10 size classes, measured in total assets. The size classes are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as (1) less than \$2M, (2) \$2–5M, (3) \$5–10M, (4) \$10–20M, (5) \$20–50M, (6) \$50–100M, (7) \$100–200M, (8) \$200–500M, (9) \$500– 1B, (10) \$1B or more.

In sum, our <sup>fi</sup>nal sample consists of 6879 yearly observations from 1318 CUs and 21 vendors covering the period 2002–2009. Note that it is not possible to update the data because publicly available <sup>fi</sup>les no longer include information of core processing vendors after 2009.

## 4.3. Operationalisation

In the following, we describe how we operationalise the theoretical constructs in the empirical analysis. Descriptive statistics and a correlation matrix can be found in Tables A1 and A2.

## 4.3.1. Vendors and vendor switching

We check and verify each vendor name in the data using SEC <sup>fi</sup>lings, tradepress publications, and corporate websites. During the observed period, the vendor market has been subject to MandA (see Table $\operatorname { A } 3 ;$ cf., Currie, 2000). Around 49% of CUs in our sample have been contracting a vendor that was involved in mergers and acquisitions (M&A) at least for 1 year. In most cases, CUs continued to report the acquired vendor for a period of 1 or 2 years after the acquisition. We correct this by treating a CU as a client of the acquirer with the year of acquisition. That is, we make sure not to count it as a switch if CUs report a new vendor name because of an acquisition. We further pool subsidiaries and treat the a<sup>fi</sup>liated group as one single vendor.

The switching variable is then constructed as the dummy $\nu _ { t + 1 }$ that equals 1 if the name of the vendor changed between period t and period t 1. We observe a total of 131 switches (1.90%). Correspondingly, 103 (7.81%) CUs have switched at least once in our sample period. The average CU switches 1.3 times, the maximum number of observed switches is 4.

A possible reason for the relatively low number of observed switches can be high switching costs. Anecdotal evidence reported in the industry trade press suggests that “most of the time, the best approach to vendor contracts is to renegotiate with the current vendor because of the cost of switching vendors and negotiating a new contract” (Gilpatrick, 2006). Reasons that lead CUs to switch despite the associated costs are reported to result (1) from a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant reduction in expenditure, (2) lost con<sup>fi</sup>- dence in the vendor because of a situation and/or lack of service, or (3) are a response to a change in business strategy and product innovation that the CU is seeking to implement. Further, it could be that the average contract length (which is unobserved) exceeds our observation period of 7 years.

## 4.3.2. Expenditure

Information from the Technology & Spending Report (CUNA, 2008) serves a measure of outsourcing expenditure $\bar { p } _ { \tau }$ . We observe average annual vendor expenses for technology functions in dollar units. For each CU size class, we use the sum of average annual vendor expenses for internal PC hardware, internal o<sup>fi</sup>ce software, operational applications, website maintenance, operational hardware, telephony/communications, internal network, internal e-mail, online security, security training, and vulnerability testing. We account for in<sup>fl</sup>ation by using PPI data for the sector Professional and Technical Services provided by the Bureau of Labor Statistics such that expenditure is given in prices of 2009. The mean value is \$135,615 in the combined dataset which matches Technology & Spending Report data and NCUA call report data. Note that in results not reported here but available on request, we show that using only average annual vendor expenses for operational applications and operational hardware yields very similar estimates of average switching costs.

To construct measures of $\Delta c$ and $c _ { i , \tau } ,$ we use information on yearly expenses for professional and outside services on the individual level from the NCUA call report data. This includes outside IT servicing beside legal fees, audit fees, accounting services, and consulting fees. To be consistent with the timing τ in $\bar { p } _ { \tau } ,$ we use values from 2007 (in prices of 2009) to de<sup>fi</sup>ne the variable $c _ { i , \tau } .$ The mean is \$328,177, which, together with the CUNA (2008) data, suggests that IT outsourcing accounts for some 41% of expenses for professional services. The di<sup>f</sup>erence between period t and period t 1 is used to construct $\Delta c _ { i , t } = c _ { i , t } - c _ { i , t + 1 }$ . A mean of −\$8508 implies an average yearly growth of 2.60%. For the subsample of switchers, the implied growth rate is much higher at 6.54%.

## 4.3.3. Bargaining power

Most of the IT tasks that CU typically outsource are directly related to consumer or corporate savings and loan accounts (Dernovsek, 2005; Robbins & Van Walleghem, 2004), i.e., the vendor’s data processing workload should be proportional to a CU’s amount of shares and deposits. Perhaps especially under a usagebased fee, CUs with higher transaction volumes are more valuable clients to the vendor. We use this as a basis for our measure of <sup>fi</sup>rm bargaining power by calculating the sum of shares and deposits of all clients of a vendor in a given year. Because the NCUA call report data include the entire population of CUs, we can observe the complete relevant market. An individual CU’s bargaining power is then measured as its share of this number. While our data are rich enough to allow to calculate the bargaining power relative to all other CU clients of a vendor, it may well be that CUs per se do not represent an important enough client base of a vendor to have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant bargaining power. This may be the case with very large vendors, such as Fiserv, that have many other clients outside the CU industry. We specify vendor <sup>fi</sup>xed e<sup>f</sup>ects in the econometric model to control for such time-invariant vendor-speci<sup>fi</sup>c factors.

To cope with a skewed distribution, we take the natural logarithm. Therefore, the variable takes on only negative values. As we would expect, the mean of switchers is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly larger than the mean of stayers. We use values from period t in the model.

## 4.3.4. Vendor market HHI

A speci<sup>fi</sup>c issue is to de<sup>fi</sup>ne the relevant market as IT outsourcing contracts are typically drafted to provide solutions to idiosyncratic needs. We incorporate the idea that <sup>fi</sup>rms select vendors in a multistage process. First, <sup>fi</sup>rms screen the overall market by issuing a request for information, where numerous vendors respond. Based on this, a request for proposals is sent to a much smaller set of vendors that match the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s requirement pro<sup>fi</sup>le.

Empirically, we implement this idea by “reverseengineering” the set of vendors that respond to requests for proposals by a given CU. We do this under the assumption that each vendor that serves a speci<sup>fi</sup>c type of CU has as least one client of that speci<sup>fi</sup>c type. How do we measure type? Using information on the number of electronic services a CU o<sup>f</sup>ers, we proxy for expected contract complexity (see Table A4 for an overview). We then categorise CU in <sup>fi</sup>ve complexity classes. In a next step, for each complexity class and year, we create a list of vendors that serve CU in that class. With 7 years and <sup>fi</sup>ve complexity classes, we can de<sup>fi</sup>ne a total of 35 markets. Put di<sup>f</sup>erently, a market is our empirical measure of set of vendors that a given CU can choose from in a given year. Finally, we calculate the HHI for each market. The empirical analysis includes this variable with a 1-year lag, i.e., period t.

## 4.3.5. Higher quality

Inter-period quality changes are measured by the number of standard features a vendor o<sup>f</sup>ers in period t 1 on top of the number of o<sup>f</sup>erings of the vendor in period t. Below, we refer to this measure as More Features. When the switching decision is taken in t 1, this may change the CU’s options to introduce new products and services in the future. We code negative values (meaning the vendor in t 1 o<sup>f</sup>ers less standard features than the vendor in t) equal to 0.

In this sense, we treat less scope for innovation the same as “not more” scope for innovation. De<sup>fi</sup>ned in this way, the variable also captures a change in the availability of software features of the current vendor in case of no switch. The vendor may either introduce such innovations due to contractual agreements or renegotiation of current terms. Descriptive statistics show that the mean value of this variable is signi<sup>fi</sup>- cantly larger when a switch took place in the same period. This may not be a perfect measure of service quality. We discuss the robustness of our results in this regard in Appendix B in Section B.2.

## 4.3.6. Higher visibility

Vendor visibility is measured by counting the number of times the name of the vendor appears in a given year in the three major trade publications Credit Union Magazine, CUES Credit Union Management Magazine, and Credit Union Times. We obtained this information from keywords searches in EBSCO Host’s Business Source Complete Database. Manual inspection of a random sample suggests that these appearances span a wide range of categories. For example, there are business cases that describe how a CU has implemented the system of speci<sup>fi</sup>c vendor, announcements of strategic alliances and mergers, reports of big CUs switching to speci<sup>fi</sup>c vendors, product announcements, and reports about conferences organised by vendors. Most strikingly, we did not <sup>fi</sup>nd a single article that portraits a vendor in a negative light. This leads us to conclude that appearance in the trade press can be interpreted as helping to increase the brand visibility of a vendor. We specify relative di<sup>f</sup>erence in visibility as a dummy that takes the value 1 if the vendor in period t 1 occurred more often in the trade press than the vendor in period t. The mean is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly higher when a switch took place in the same period. Note that this speci<sup>fi</sup>cation is also able to capture greater visibility of the current vendor relative to itself in the period before.

## 4.3.7. Asset speci<sup>fi</sup>city

The functionality o<sup>f</sup>ered by vendors varies substantially. Data from the Information Systems Guide suggest that the average standard package o<sup>f</sup>ered by vendors includes 54% of all features that are available from the vendor. Thirty-eight per cent of the features are optional and 8% are custom. To derive a measure of asset speci<sup>fi</sup>city, we match four distinctive electronic services o<sup>f</sup>erings of CU to the corresponding vendor’s software features. Software features can either be standard (1), optional (2), or customised (3). If, for example, a CU has indicated to provide Internet banking, and the corresponding vendor o<sup>f</sup>ers this feature only in a customised way, the speci<sup>fi</sup>city of Internet banking is 3. We construct this variable for ATM, audio response, Internet banking, and wireless access capabilities, all of which represent electronic modes of access. The corresponding software features are “ATM Card Management”, “Audio response: Loans by Phone”, “Internet Banking”, and “Wireless Capabilities”. We are limited to matching four pairs because these are the only software features that have unambiguous counterparts in the NCUA dataset and are reported in every year. The <sup>fi</sup>nal measure of asset speci<sup>fi</sup>city is the average speci<sup>fi</sup>city score out of the four service o<sup>f</sup>erings. The overall mean is about 2.36, the di<sup>f</sup>erence between switchers and stayers is small and insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant. The fact that the average CU uses features that go beyond the functionality of the standard package its vendor o<sup>f</sup>ers is likely to be accommodated by higher outsourcing fees compared to a o<sup>f</sup>- the-shelve solution. The provision of optional and custom features may require some upfront investment by the vendor, for example by auditing the speci<sup>fi</sup>c needs of the client and by (slightly) adjusting software modules to the client’s speci<sup>fi</sup>c needs. This would of course reduce switching costs for the client, because there is no need to replicate relationshipspeci<sup>fi</sup>c investments when switching. We were not able to <sup>fi</sup>nd evidence that suggests that vendors make substantial relationship-speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments in the empirical context of CUs.

We use values from period t in the model.

## 4.3.8. Relationship duration

Because we cannot observe the actual contract length, we have to rely on a measure of relationship duration. We count the number of consecutive years a CU reports the same vendor. By de<sup>fi</sup>nition, this measure is truncated. To minimise this issue, we use the full initial sample starting in 1999 to compute this variable. Therefore, the observed maximum is at 11 years.

## 4.3.9. Search e<sup>f</sup>ort

To proxy for search e<sup>f</sup>ort, we include the natural logarithm of travel and conference expense per fulltime equivalent employee, using values from period t 1. This captures authorised expenses incurred by o<sup>fi</sup>cers, directors, and employees for travel, attendance at conferences and other meetings. Industry associations and advisory <sup>fi</sup>rms regularly organise trade fairs, conferences, and workshops on technology topics, where CU learns about vendors and their products. In fact, the regulatory authority NCUA recommends that CUs carry out an extensive ex-ante evaluation of third parties to minimise risk. Travel expenditure may therefore also be related to the process of risk reviews and third party due diligence (see, e.g., NCUA, 2007). The descriptive statistics show that there is a signi<sup>fi</sup>- cant di<sup>f</sup>erence in our measure of search e<sup>f</sup>ort between switchers and stayers.

## 4.3.10. Switching experience

This variable captures the number of years since the last vendor switch. For interpretational convenience, the variable is coded reversely (see Figure 1). Consider an example where the total number of years after a switch (i.e., before the next switch or until the end of our observed period) is three and the switch occurred in 2006. The experience variable is then coded 0 in 2006, 3 in 2007, 2 in 2008, and 1 in 2009. In the model, this variable refers to period t.

## 4.3.11. Firm size

We measure <sup>fi</sup>rm size in period t by the natural logarithm of the number of employees in full-time equivalents. An alternative measure of <sup>fi</sup>rm size could be total assets, or the sum of shares and loans. One of the reasons why we measure <sup>fi</sup>rm size by the number of employees is to avoid multicollinearity issues with measures that directly incorporate these data (see above). Further note that CUs with larger stocks of shares and deposits do not necessarily have to be large in terms of employees. This will depend on their business model, their IT infrastructure (the share of members that use e-banking rather than using human-capital-intensive physical branches), which type of membership they have (corporate, community, etc.), and how geographically spread out their members are (more dispersion implies more demand for physical branches).

## 4.3.12. Control variables

We <sup>fi</sup>nally include <sup>fi</sup>xed e<sup>f</sup>ects for years and the vendor in periods t and t 1 to control for unobserved heterogeneity. We control for correlation within CU–vendor dyads by calculating multiway clustered standard errors (Cameron, Gelbach, & Miller, 2011, p. 242).

## 5. Results

## 5.1. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cation tests

We <sup>fi</sup>rst turn to the idea that supply-side competition and bargaining power may have led to an internalisation of switching costs already in the initial negotiation. Correspondingly, those variables only a<sup>f</sup>ect switching costs if they have changed since the last negotiation. We can test this by regressing a time variant measure of vendor market concentration/bargaining power on vendor market concentration/bargaining power at the time when contract was signed. If the time-invariant variable explains much of the variation in the time-variant variable – as measured in the $R ^ { 2 } \mathrm { ~ - ~ }$ this suggests that vendor market concentration (bargaining power) does not change over time, and we only need to include it as control variable in the vector $\mathbf { z } _ { i , j , t }$ . A low $R ^ { 2 }$ would suggest that the equilibrium is not stable over time, and if the contract has not been renegotiated, i.e., fees have not been adjusted, the <sup>fi</sup>rm can hold up the vendor. As we postulate in Hypotheses 1 and 6, this in turn would have a negative e<sup>f</sup>ect on switching costs. In this case, we would need to treat vendor market concentration/bargaining power as a determinant of switching costs and include it in $\mathbf { x } _ { i , j , t }$ . To control for the e<sup>f</sup>ect of initial vendor market concentration/bargaining power on equilibrium prices, we would then also need to include a measure of vendor market concentration/bargaining power at the time of contract signing in $\mathbf { z } _ { i , j , t }$

Results of the corresponding speci<sup>fi</sup>cation tests can be found in Table 1. Firm bargaining power at contract signing (proxied by the <sup>fi</sup>rst observed year of the client– vendor relationship) is highly correlated with the timevariant measure of bargaining power. This suggests that the variable should not be included as a driver of switching costs. As a result, we cannot directly test Hypothesis 6, since our measure of bargaining power does not su<sup>fi</sup>ciently vary over time. Vendor market concentration shows more dynamics. ## 5.2.7. Heterogeneity in switching costs

We now focus on heterogeneity in switching costs across <sup>fi</sup>rms. The upper panel of Table 5 shows that the median value is very similar to the average value. Interestingly, the minimum is negative, suggesting direct evidence for the notion that vendors pay <sup>fi</sup>rms to switch. The standard deviation is around 25% of the mean, with a similarly sized interquartile range. This suggests that the distribution of switching costs is fairly symmetric, but these statistics also indicate that there is a substantial amount of heterogeneity. We continue to explore this dimension in more detail.

We <sup>fi</sup>rst investigate how much switching costs of <sup>fi</sup>rms that switch vendors at some point in the sample di<sup>f</sup>erent di<sup>f</sup>er from those that do not switch. The mid and lower panel of Table 5 report estimated switching costs for stayers and switchers. While the mean for stayers is roughly equal to the overall mean, our model predicts that mean switching costs for switchers are much lower (about 50%). In the next step, we use the fact that our model allows to distinguish between deliberate and non-deliberate stayers to quantify the lock-in e<sup>f</sup>ect. Within stayers, we can contrast the size of switching costs of non-deliberate stayers (i.e., CUs that are locked-in with their current vendor) and deliberate stayers (i.e., CUs that have renegotiated contractual terms with their current vendor).

To categorise CUs in our sample as either deliberate or non-deliberate stayers, we need to de<sup>fi</sup>ne an empirical cut-o<sup>f</sup> to transform the underlying latent construct into a dichotomous variable that de<sup>fi</sup>nes the two groups of observations. Any cut-o<sup>f</sup> we pick is of course somewhat arbitrary, which is why we report estimates based on a range of values. As discussed in the section, the empirically relevant variables that determine renegotiation of outsourcing fees (fee shifters) are Bargaining Power and Vendor Market HHI at Signing. Non-deliberate stayers are those CUs for which there is relatively little change in the fee shifting variables. To say something about the percentage of non-deliberate staying, we need to de<sup>fi</sup>ne what relatively little change means. Changing from lowto-high levels of bargaining power in cases where the vendor market was highly concentrated at the beginning of the contract should help CU the most to reduce outsourcing fees in renegotiations. Analogously, we should expect that outsourcing fees do not decrease in the absence of a change in bargaining power, especially when the vendor market was highly concentrated at the beginning of the contract.

We apply this idea by de<sup>fi</sup>ning the group of nondeliberate stayers as those observations that are in 1st percentile of Bargaining Power and in the 99th percentile of Vendor Market HHI at Signing. In this de<sup>fi</sup>nition, 1% of all observations of stayers classify as observations of non-deliberate stayers. Table 6 shows how the frequency changes when we use different cut-o<sup>f</sup> values, i.e., the 5th and 95th percentile, 10th and 90th percentile, and the 25th and 75th percentile. As we de<sup>fi</sup>ne broader boundaries, naturally we increase the number of observations that qualify as non-deliberate stayers (decreasing the probability of false-negative classi<sup>fi</sup>cation errors), but the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation also gets more fuzzy (increasing the probability of false-positive classi<sup>fi</sup>cation errors).

We <sup>fi</sup>nally calculate the level of switching costs based on the estimates in Table 3, given these cuto<sup>f</sup> values and report the results in Table 7. First, it is reassuring that the average estimated switching costs for stayers are around 30–35% and therefore very similar to those reported in Table 5. Second, the estimated switching costs of non-deliberate stayers are between 8.7 and 3.8 times larger than those of deliberate stayers. This substantial di<sup>f</sup>erence explains why those CUs stay with their current vendor. The di<sup>f</sup>erence in switching costs between deliberate and non-deliberate stayers can be interpreted as a measure of the size of lock-in. Depending on the speci<sup>fi</sup>- cation, our results suggest that lock-in is equivalent to about 70–215% of average annual expenditure for professional and outside services. This wide range suggests that the point estimate of the lock-in e<sup>f</sup>ect strongly depends on the speci<sup>fi</sup>c choice of classi<sup>fi</sup>cation cut-o<sup>f</sup>s. ## 6. Discussion

## 6.1. Internal validity

We are able to analyse a rich dataset covering almost the entire population of an industry and their respective vendors over a long period of time. Nevertheless, the data carry some caveats. First, we lack detailed information on contracts and corresponding renewals. Our econometric set-up should capture this issue by explicitly accounting for endogenous changes in outsourcing fees that are unrelated to vendor switching. Second, the present dataset allows to measure IT expenditure on a group-level only. We therefore include a number of <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c variables and vendor <sup>fi</sup>xed e<sup>f</sup>ects to capture as much of the idiosyncratic unobserved variation as possible. In the worst case, our estimates should be biased towards the mean. Third, the speci<sup>fi</sup>c operationalisation is dictated by data availability. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, one might be concerned whether our measures of vendor quality and search e<sup>f</sup>ort are capturing the theoretical concepts in a satisfactory way. We discuss this at length in Appendix B in Section B.2 and carry out robustness checks that lead us to conclude that the estimation bias that would be introduced by measurement error of this type is likely to be negligible.

## 6.2. External validity

The <sup>fi</sup>nancial industry, and CU in speci<sup>fi</sup>c, may have some idiosyncratic features that threaten the external validity of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. First, with an average IT outsourcing expenditure of \$135,000, compared to an average of 20 employees, the CUs in our sample rely strongly on outsourced IT. With the exception of Anderson and Dekker (2005), who study outsourcing contracts of small and medium-sized <sup>fi</sup>rms (5–200 employees) with an average contract size of \$45,000, ranging from less than \$15,000 to more than \$120,000, most empirical work on IT outsourcing has focused on large <sup>fi</sup>rms. For example, the average number of employees is 8800 in Whitten and Wake<sup>fi</sup>eld (2006),

11,000 in Susarla et al. (2010), and 38,000 in Fitoussi and Gurbaxani (2012). Average contract values are \$10 million (Susarla et al., 2010), or range from \$1500 to \$89 million (Whitten & Wake<sup>fi</sup>eld, 2006) or from \$50,000 to more than \$1 billion (Chen & Bharadwaj, 2009). Our results suggest that switching costs decline with <sup>fi</sup>rm size. It is conceivable that we would get lower estimates in industries where the average <sup>fi</sup>rm size is larger. However, our results also suggest that <sup>fi</sup>rm size is certainly not the most important driver of switching costs, because other variables make up for almost 90% of switching costs. While the CU industry, and the <sup>fi</sup>nancial industry in general, is more IT intensive than other industries (Dewan & Min, 1997) and therefore perhaps more likely to outsource IT, this seems to change in recent years (Greenstein, Lerner, & Stern, 2010). Increasing levels of digitisation across sectors create more data – e.g., about transactions with business partners – that needs to be stored, processed, and analysed. Hence, the insights that our paper generates in the context of IT outsourcing in the <sup>fi</sup>nancial industry may become applicable more generally.

This is related to a second concern. The studied time period may have some idiosyncratic features that preclude generalisation of our results. The data are limited to the period 1999–2009, which predates recent technological advances in IT outsourcing, such as software-as-a-service, cloud computing, or decentralised database systems. However, the underlying task – storage, processing, and analysis of business data – seems largely independent of a speci<sup>fi</sup>c technology. More importantly, even if speci<sup>fi</sup>c data processing technologies might evolve or change over time, the market-, <sup>fi</sup>rm-, and relationship-speci<sup>fi</sup>c determinants of switching costs we discuss are to a large extent independent of the underlying technologies. Technological advances may allow additional and innovative tasks to be performed by the vendor. Perhaps IT outsourcing has or will become more strategic with more outsourced analytics and arti<sup>fi</sup>cial intelligence applications. If it is costly to duplicate analytics e<sup>f</sup>orts, in that sense, our estimates might provide a lower bound of switching costs.

Nevertheless, it is important to recognise the limitations of the dataset that we use in the empirical application of the econometric model. Future work could test whether the estimated magnitudes and relative importance of individual determinants of switching costs are robust in di<sup>f</sup>erent industries, different regulatory and country-speci<sup>fi</sup>c environments, and di<sup>f</sup>erent time periods.

## 7. Conclusion

Prior research argues that ex-post rent-seeking and hold-up causes substantial switching costs. In this paper, we propose an econometric methodology that allows to test theoretical predictions about switching costs using <sup>fi</sup>rm-level accounting data. A speci<sup>fi</sup>c problem is to distinguish between deliberate staying and non-deliberate staying. While the <sup>fi</sup>rst suggests that ex-post vendor opportunism is not a big threat to the <sup>fi</sup>rm, the latter suggests that <sup>fi</sup>rms are locked into suboptimal relationships. Our model can identify switching costs by controlling for endogenous changes in outsourcing fees induced by renegotiations.

We <sup>fi</sup>nd that switching costs account for a third of annual expenditure on professional services. This con<sup>fi</sup>rms prior narrative evidence in a large-scale empirical study. We then move beyond this average unconditional estimate of switching costs and investigate heterogeneity. We show that switching costs of switching <sup>fi</sup>rms are substantially lower than those of <sup>fi</sup>rms that do not switch. This suggests the presence of lock-in e<sup>f</sup>ects. Further distinguishing between deliberate stayers (e.g., because the <sup>fi</sup>rm was “successful” in renegotiating contractual terms with the current vendor) and non-deliberate stayers, we can quantify those e<sup>f</sup>ects more directly. We estimate that what prevents <sup>fi</sup>rms from switching vendors has a monetary equivalent of 70–210% of annual expenditure for professional services.

A second contribution of this paper is the decomposition of switching costs into market-speci<sup>fi</sup>c, <sup>fi</sup>rmspeci<sup>fi</sup>c, and relationship-speci<sup>fi</sup>c determinants. Our results show that the largest portion of switching costs is due to relationship- and <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c factors, while market-speci<sup>fi</sup>c variables are much less important.

This implies that <sup>fi</sup>rms can have substantial discretion in a<sup>f</sup>ecting the size of switching costs. As our results suggest, some 80% of switching costs can at least partially be a<sup>f</sup>ected by the <sup>fi</sup>rm. We show that <sup>fi</sup>rms can lower switching costs disproportionately by investing in vendor search and evaluation. On top of that, experience e<sup>f</sup>ects and scale economies reduce switching costs. The largest portion of switching costs is driven by relationship-speci<sup>fi</sup>c variables such as asset speci<sup>fi</sup>city and relationship duration. The speci<sup>fi</sup>city of the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s relationshipspeci<sup>fi</sup>c investments alone explains some 7% of switching costs but seems to play a minor role compared to relationship duration. The latter, however, has diminishing marginal returns to switching costs. We are therefore able to show that writing contracts with either very short or very long durations may provide a solution to the hold-up problem in the sense that it reduces switching costs. We believe our <sup>fi</sup>ndings provide important implications for the management of switching costs, both from a <sup>fi</sup>rm and vendor perspective. On the one hand, we can con<sup>fi</sup>rm normative statements in the practitioner literature, such as “spend some time researching vendors”, “draft tight contracts”, “cultivate the vendor relationship” (Barthélemy, 2001, p. 68), and “proactively reduce organizational complexity through simpli<sup>fi</sup>cation and standardization of internal operations” (Sia et al., 2010, p. 31). On the other hand, there is no clear theoretical reason why such factors should outweigh exogenous factors like vendor market power. The contribution of this paper hence is to provide large-scale empirical evidence for the relative importance of market-speci<sup>fi</sup>c, <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c, and relationship-speci<sup>fi</sup>c determinants of switching costs and their heterogeneity across <sup>fi</sup>rms.

A third contribution is a methodological one. The econometric model that we develop in this paper can be easily adapted to other empirical settings where the researcher has access to individual-level accounting data and information about vertical relations of <sup>fi</sup>rms, beyond the speci<sup>fi</sup>c case of IT outsourcing.

## Appendix B. Technical Appendix

## B.1 Econometric Model

Our model builds on Elzinga and Mills (1998). The approach takes the price mechanism and contractual hazards into consideration, while explicitly accounting for endogenous fee-setting due to renegotiations of contracts. In what follows, we use the term expenditure to refer to what the <sup>fi</sup>rm actually pays for using external IT services. Changes in expenditure can occur because the <sup>fi</sup>rm switches vendors which charge di<sup>f</sup>erent outsourcing fees.

## B.1.1 Set-up

In period t, <sup>fi</sup>rm i buys IT services from vendor a for a fee $\displaystyle { \mathit { P i } } , a , t ^ { . }$ . In the next period, <sup>fi</sup>rm i compares the fee it would pay when contracting vendor b and decides to switch given switching costs $s _ { i , a , t } ^ { * } .$ . The <sup>fi</sup>rm will switch vendors if

$$
p _ {i, b, t + 1} + s _ {i, a, t} ^ {*} <   p _ {i, a, t}.\tag{5}
$$

For notational convenience, we drop indices $a , b$ and write Equation (5) as ${ p } _ { i , t + 1 } + { s } _ { i , j , t } ^ { * } < { p } _ { i , t } ,$ where j indicates the vendor in period t such that the decision rule can be expressed as

$$
\Delta p _ {i, t} - s _ {i, j, t} > 0,\tag{6}
$$

where $\Delta p _ { i , t } = p _ { i , t } - p _ { i , t + 1 }$ is the change in expenditure of <sup>fi</sup>rm i from period t to period t 1. The left-hand side of Equation (6) can be interpreted as the latent value of switching:

$$
\nu_ {i, t + 1} ^ {*} = \Delta p _ {i, t} - s _ {i, j, t} ^ {*}.\tag{7}
$$

We assume that switching costs $s _ { i , j , t } ^ { * }$ are a linear function of observable <sup>fi</sup>rm and vendor characteristics and a constant included in the vector $\mathbf { X } _ { i , j , t }$ such that

$$
s _ {i, j, t} = \mathbf {x} _ {i, j, t} ^ {\prime} \beta + u _ {i, j, t},\tag{8}
$$

where $\beta$ is a coe<sup>fi</sup>cient vector and the error term consists of a <sup>fi</sup>xed e<sup>f</sup>ect for vendor j in t and t 1 such that $\begin{array} { r } { u _ { i , j , t } = \tilde { u } _ { i , j , t } + \tilde { u } _ { i , j , t + 1 } + \tilde { u } _ { i , t } } \end{array}$ , with $\widetilde { u } _ { i , t } \sim N ( 0 , \sigma _ { s } ^ { 2 } )$

## B.1.2 Economic Identi<sup>fi</sup>cation

Changes in observed expenditure for outsourced IT are likely to be endogenous due to unobserved renegotiations. We propose an approach in which we model these changes as short-run deviations from an exogenous long-run trend. Being able to empirically estimate parameters of a function that describes the endogenous part of the changes in expenditure allows us to distinguish between deliberate staying (i.e., contract renewal/modi<sup>fi</sup>cation) and non-deliberate staying (i.e., lock-in due to switching costs).

The central assumption is that <sup>fi</sup>rm i’s expenditure on outsourced IT services grows at a rate that is constant in the long term, but there are short-term <sup>fl</sup>uctuations induced by renegotiation, quality changes, or switching vendors:

<sup>Assumption 1</sup>. Expenditure on outsourced IT services in period t is a function of expenditure in period $t - 1$ and a shock, i.e., $p _ { i , t } = ( 1 + \lambda ) p _ { i , t - 1 } + e _ { i , j , t }$ . The variable $e _ { i , j , t }$ contains all other variables that afect expenditures and an iid. random component $\widetilde { e } _ { i , t } { \sim } N ( 0 , \sigma _ { p } ^ { 2 } )$

The left-hand panel of Figure 2, plotting aggregate producer price index data for “data processing, hosting, and related services”, suggests that this assumption is not overly unrealistic (see also the discussion in Section 5.2). There is some short-run <sup>fl</sup>uctuation, but there is also a clearly visible long-run trend over the period 2000–2009.

Solving the di<sup>f</sup>erence equation (see Appendix B.1.6), we can express $\Delta { p } _ { i , t }$ as a function of IT outsourcing expenditures in any year $\tau \in [ 0 \dots T ]$ , i.e.,

$$
\Delta p _ {i, t} = \alpha p _ {i, \tau} + \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {t} \alpha_ {k} e _ {i, j, k} - \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {\tau} \alpha_ {k} e _ {i, j, k} - e _ {i, j, t + 1}.\tag{9}
$$

We arrive at an empirical model of vendor switching when substituting Equations (8) and (9) in Equation (7) such that

$$
\begin{array}{c} v _ {i, t + 1} ^ {*} = \alpha p _ {i, \tau} - x _ {i, j, t} ^ {\prime} \beta + \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {t} \alpha_ {k} e _ {i, j, k} - \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {\tau} \alpha_ {k} e _ {i, j, k} - e _ {i, j, t + 1} \\ - u _ {i, j, t}. \end{array}\tag{10}
$$

Formulated in this way, we can control for endogeneity by including a vector of variables that potentially a<sup>f</sup>ect changes in outsourcing fees (e.g., bargaining power, competition, and quality) in a regression model. By assuming a certain structure with which observed outsourcing fees change over time (as implemented by assuming a process with a constant rate of growth and random shocks), we can model the changes in outsourcing fees using time-variant observables. This allows to control time-variant observables that a<sup>f</sup>ect fees but do not a<sup>f</sup>ect switching costs. Without this structure in the model, these would be omitted (i.e., part of the unobserved error term) and would therefore introduce estimation bias regarding the parameters of interest (the vector that de<sup>fi</sup>nes switching costs and the relative importance of its components). Under the assumption that the empirical measures of these variables absorb su<sup>fi</sup>cient variation that is correlated to the empirical measures of variables that determine switching costs, we can identify parameters of factors that determine changes in outsourcing fees because of switching, i.e., solve the endogeneity problem.

Before we can move on, we need to solve an additiona issue that is speci<sup>fi</sup>c to the dataset used in this paper. We cannot observe the outsourcing fees paid by each <sup>fi</sup>rm at each point in time. However, we can observe a time-invariant aggregate measure of expenditure for IT outsourcing. Let this variable be denoted $\bar { p } _ { \tau }$ . To be able to use this information as a suitable proxy for $\displaystyle { p _ { i , \tau } } ,$ , we need to make an assumption about the correlation between the aggregate and the individual-level data. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we assume that the correlation can be modelled as according to the process

$$
p _ {i, \tau} = \frac {1}{n _ {l}} \sum_ {l = 1} ^ {L} p _ {i, \tau , l} + v _ {\tau} = \bar {p} _ {\tau} + v _ {i, \tau},\tag{11}
$$

where $n _ { l }$ is the number of observations per aggregation unit $l , \_ L$ is the number of aggregation units, and $\nu _ { i , \tau } { \sim } N ( 0 , \sigma _ { \nu } ^ { 2 } )$ .

To make this more concrete, instead of a value of expenditure for IT outsourcing for each credit union in each year, we observe average values of expenditure for IT outsourcing for di<sup>f</sup>erent size classes of credit unions in our dataset. These data come from a survey carried out in 2008 (CUNA, 2008). We observe 10 averages for 10 size classes, which are de<sup>fi</sup>ned by the value of total assets.

Using this information, we can substitute Equation (11) in Equation (10) and arrive at our empirical model of the value of vendor switching:

$$
\begin{array}{c} v _ {i, t + 1} ^ {*} = \alpha \bar {p} _ {\tau} - \mathbf {x} _ {i, j, t} ^ {\prime} \boldsymbol {\beta} + \alpha v _ {i, \tau} + \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {t} \alpha_ {k} e _ {i, j, k} - \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {\tau} \alpha_ {k} e _ {i, j, k} \\ - e _ {i, j, t + 1} - u _ {i, j, t} = \alpha \bar {p} _ {\tau} - \mathbf {x} _ {i, j, t} ^ {\prime} \boldsymbol {\beta} + \mathbf {z} _ {i, j, t} ^ {\prime} \boldsymbol {\zeta} + \varepsilon_ {i, j, t}, \end{array}\tag{12}
$$

where $\begin{array} { r } { \mathbf { z } _ { i , j , t } = \sum _ { k = 1 } ^ { t } \alpha _ { k } ( e _ { i , j , k } - \tilde { e } _ { i , k } ) - \sum _ { k = 1 } ^ { \tau } \alpha _ { k } ( e _ { i , j , k } - \tilde { e } _ { i , k } ) - } \end{array}$ $( e _ { i , j , t + 1 } - \tilde { e } _ { i , t + 1 } )$ is a vector of price shifters with a corresponding coe<sup>fi</sup>cient vector ζ. The error term is $\begin{array} { r } { \varepsilon _ { i , j , t } = \alpha \nu _ { i , \tau } + \sum _ { k = 1 } ^ { t } \alpha _ { k } \tilde { e } _ { i , k } - \sum _ { k = 1 } ^ { \tau } \alpha _ { k } \tilde { e } _ { i , k } - \tilde { e } _ { i , t + 1 } - u _ { i , j , t } , } \end{array}$ with $\scriptstyle \varepsilon _ { i , j , t } \sim N ( 0 , \sigma ^ { 2 } )$

The error term speci<sup>fi</sup>ed in Equation (11) is modelling an individual speci<sup>fi</sup>c deviation from the group mean. We assume that this process is independent of the error term in Equation (12). Recall that this error captures all unobserved variation that is not yet controlled for by timevariant variables such as bargaining power, competition, and quality. When estimating the model, we further include year and vendor <sup>fi</sup>xed e<sup>f</sup>ects. Especially, the vendor <sup>fi</sup>xed e<sup>f</sup>ects should capture unobserved variation that could introduce estimation bias to the parameters of interest.

The value of switching $\nu _ { i , t + 1 } ^ { * }$ is of course a theoretical construct that is unobserved by the econometrician. However, like in a standard probit model, we can infer it from the observed switching decision. To see this, consider the decision rule (6) de<sup>fi</sup>ned as

$$
v _ {i, t + 1} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 1, & \text { if } v _ {i, t + 1} ^ {*} 0 \\ 0, & \text { if } v _ {i, t + 1} ^ {*} \leq 0. \end{array} \right.
$$

The probability that <sup>fi</sup>rm i switches can now be derived as

$$
\begin{array}{l} P (v _ {i, t + 1} = 1 \Big | p _ {l, \tau}, \mathbf {x} _ {i, j, t}) = P (v _ {i, t + 1} ^ {*} > 0 | \bar {p} _ {\tau}, \mathbf {x} _ {i, j, t}, \mathbf {z} _ {i, j, t}) \\ \qquad = P (\alpha \bar {p} _ {\tau} - \mathbf {x} _ {i, j, t} ^ {\prime} \boldsymbol {\beta} + \mathbf {z} _ {i, j, t} ^ {\prime} \zeta + \varepsilon_ {i, j, t} > 0 | \bar {p} _ {\tau}, \mathbf {x} _ {i, j, t}, \mathbf {z} _ {i, j, t}) \\ \qquad = P (\varepsilon_ {i, j, t} > - \alpha \bar {p} _ {\tau} + \mathbf {x} _ {i, j, t} ^ {\prime} \boldsymbol {\beta} - \mathbf {z} _ {i, j, t} ^ {\prime} \zeta | \bar {p} _ {\tau}, \mathbf {x} _ {i, j, t}, \mathbf {z} _ {i, j, t}) \\ \qquad = \Phi ((\alpha \bar {p} _ {\tau} - \mathbf {x} _ {i, j, t} ^ {\prime} \boldsymbol {\beta}) / \sigma), \end{array}\tag{13}
$$

where Φ is the standard normal cdf and $\sigma = \sqrt { \mathrm { V a r } \left( \varepsilon _ { i , j , t } \right) }$

Note that the ability to make inference about an underlying latent continuous variable from an observed discrete variable comes at the cost of parameter identi<sup>fi</sup>cation. Instead of $\beta ,$ the formula above implies that we can only estimate $\bar { \beta / \sigma } .$ . If we want to estimate switching costs in monetary units, i.e., predict $\begin{array} { r } { \mathbf { x } _ { i , j , t } ^ { \prime } \pmb { \beta } , } \end{array}$ we can only do that if we can estimate σ. We propose an appropriate estimator in what follows.

## B.1.3 Parameter Identi<sup>fi</sup>cation

The intuition for our estimator is similar to the standard two-stage IV estimator. Inspired by Leamer (1978, p. 245– 246), the idea is to obtain an estimate of α from a second regression on a variable that is correlated to $\Delta p .$ Now, we have an estimate of α and an estimate of $\alpha / \sigma$ from the probit model de<sup>fi</sup>ned in Equation (13). Combining both estimate, we can infer σ.

What could be a variable that is correlated to $\Delta p \ ?$ From income sheet data, we have information on yearly expen diture for outside and professional services, which we will denote by $c _ { i , t } .$ . By de<sup>fi</sup>nition, this includes expenditure not only for IT outsourcing but also for tax fees, legal fees, accounting fees, and consulting fees (let’s call those $o _ { i , t } )$ . In other words, we can de<sup>fi</sup>ne $c _ { i , t } = p _ { i , t } + o _ { i , t }$ . Because the expenditure for outside and professional services includes expenditure on IT outsourcing and expenditure for other professional services, by de<sup>fi</sup>nition, this variable is strongly correlated to expenditure on IT outsourcing. We can model the dynamics of other expenses $o _ { i , t }$ similar to $\mathbf { \nabla } \phi _ { i , t }$

<sup>Assumption</sup> <sup>2</sup>. Expenditure on other professional services in period t is a function of this expenditure in period $t - 1$ and a shock, i.e., $o _ { i , t } = ( 1 + \kappa ) o _ { i , t - 1 } + \epsilon _ { i , t }$ , where $\epsilon _ { i , t } { \sim } N ( 0 , \sigma _ { o } ^ { 2 } )$

The right panel of Figure 2, plotting combined price index for “certi<sup>fi</sup>ed public accountants-tax preparation and planning”, “business and commercial legal services”, and “management and technical consulting services”, again suggests that this assumption is not overly unrealistic. Also here, we see some short-run <sup>fl</sup>uctuation, but there is also a clearly visible long-run trend over the period 2000–2009.

We can again write $\Delta o _ { i , t }$ as a function of $\begin{array} { r } { o _ { i , \tau } \forall \tau \in [ 0 \mathrm { ~ . ~ . ~ } T ] \dot { : } } \end{array}$

$$
\Delta o _ {i, t} = \gamma o _ {i, \tau} + \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {t} \gamma_ {k} \epsilon_ {i, k} - \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {\tau} \gamma_ {k} \epsilon_ {i, j, k} - \epsilon_ {i, t + 1},
$$

Using this expression together with

$$
\vartheta_ {i, t} = \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {t} (\alpha_ {k} \tilde {e} _ {i, k} + \gamma_ {k} \epsilon_ {i, k}) - \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {\tau} (\alpha_ {k} \tilde {e} _ {i, k} + \gamma_ {k} \epsilon_ {i, k}) - \epsilon_ {i, t + 1}\tag{14}
$$

and $o _ { i , \tau } = c _ { i , \tau } - p _ { i , \tau }$ , we can write $\Delta c _ { i , t } = \Delta p _ { i , t } + \Delta o _ { i , t }$ as

$$
\begin{array}{c} \Delta c _ {i, t} = \alpha p _ {i, \tau} + \gamma o _ {i, \tau} + \mathbf {z} _ {i, j, t} ^ {\prime} \boldsymbol {\zeta} + \vartheta_ {i, j, t} \\ = (\alpha - \gamma) p _ {i, \tau} + \gamma c _ {i, \tau} + \mathbf {z} _ {i, j, t} ^ {\prime} \boldsymbol {\zeta} + \vartheta_ {i, j, t} \\ = (\alpha - \gamma) \bar {p} _ {\tau} + \gamma c _ {i, \tau} + \mathbf {z} _ {i, j, t} ^ {\prime} \boldsymbol {\zeta} + (\alpha - \gamma) v _ {i, \tau} + \vartheta_ {i, j, t} \\ = (\alpha - \gamma) \bar {p} _ {\tau} + \gamma c _ {i, \tau} + \mathbf {z} _ {i, j, t} ^ {\prime} \boldsymbol {\zeta} + \tilde {\varepsilon} _ {i, j, t}, \end{array}\tag{15}
$$

which can be estimated by OLS. Now we have an estimate for $\left( \alpha - \gamma \right)$ . Using the fact that $\bar { p } _ { \tau }$ appears in Equations (15) and (12), we can <sup>fi</sup>nally identify σ. Using the estimate of α from the probit model (13), i.e., $\boldsymbol { \hat { \alpha } } = \boldsymbol { \alpha } / \sigma _ { : }$ , we can write σ^ as

$$
\hat {\sigma} = (\widehat {\alpha - \gamma} + \hat {\gamma}) / \hat {\alpha} _ {v}.\tag{16}
$$

Note that a test of the hypothesis that $\alpha = 0$ is e<sup>f</sup>ectively a test of the assumption that $c _ { i , t }$ is correlated with $\mathbf { \nabla } \phi _ { i , t }$ . In a <sup>fi</sup>nal step, we will derive the variance matrix of the proposed two step estimator such that we can carry out such hypothesis tests.

## B.1.4 Variance Estimation

The assumption that the error components in Equations (12) and (15) are independent may or may not hold in the data. Hence, we treat the equations as seemingly unrelated when estimating the model to account for an unobserved correlation that may exist empirically and adjust the standard error estimates accordingly. To do this, we derive an estimate for the joint variance matrix of the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed parameters, Var Θ<sup>^</sup> .

The model is estimated in two steps. First, we obtain a parameter vector $\widehat { \theta } _ { 1 } = [ \widehat { \alpha - \gamma } , \widehat { \gamma } ]$ from an OLS regression of (). Maximum-likelihood estimation of Equation (12) results in parameter estimates $\hat { \boldsymbol { \theta } } _ { 2 } = [ \hat { \alpha } _ { \nu } , \hat { \beta } ]$ . A block matrix of traditional variance estimates for OLS and probit is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as

$$
\mathbf {D} = \left[ \begin{array}{c c} [ \bar {\mathbf {p}} _ {\tau}, \mathbf {c} _ {\tau} ] [ \bar {\mathbf {p}} _ {\tau}, \mathbf {c} _ {\tau} ] ^ {\prime} & 0 \\ 0 & - \mathbf {H} (\ln L) \end{array} \right],
$$

where <sup>H</sup> ln L is the Hessian of the probit log likelihood. Then, we can write the sandwich estimator of the joint variance matrix as

$$
\mathbf {V} _ {\hat {\theta}} = \mathbf {D} ^ {- 1} \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \mathbf {u} _ {i} \mathbf {u} _ {i} ^ {\prime}\right) \mathbf {D} ^ {- 1},
$$

where n is the number of observations, $\mathbf { u } _ { i } = [ u _ { 1 i } , \mathbf { u } _ { 2 i } ]$ includes the estimated OLS residual $u _ { 1 i }$ and the <sup>fi</sup>rst derivative of the probit log likelihood for the ith observation, $\mathrm { i . e . , ~ } { \mathbf u } _ { 2 i } = \partial \ln { \cal L } _ { i } / \partial \theta _ { 2 }$ . To account for the possibility that $\varepsilon _ { i , j , t }$ and $\tilde { \varepsilon } _ { i , j , t }$ are not independent within clusters (e.g., observations from the same <sup>fi</sup>rm), the middle term must be weighted accordingly. For illustration, consider the case of one-way clustering, where the estimator changes to

$$
\tilde {\mathbf {V}} _ {\hat {\theta}} = \mathbf {D} ^ {- 1} \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n _ {c}} \left(\sum_ {i \in c _ {k}} \mathbf {u} _ {i}\right) \left(\sum_ {i \in c _ {k}} \mathbf {u} _ {i}\right) ^ {\prime}\right) \mathbf {D} ^ {- 1},
$$

where $n _ { c }$ is the total number of clusters and $c _ { k }$ contains the indices of the observations in the kth cluster for $k = 1 , \dots n _ { c }$ . Below, we use the estimator for multiway clustering proposed by Cameron et al. (2011).

Finally, we can derive the variance matrix for the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed parameters, i.e., non-linear combinations of $\pmb { \theta } _ { 1 }$ and $\pmb \theta _ { 2 } .$ Therefore, let $\hat { \pmb { \theta } } = [ \hat { \pmb { \theta } } _ { 1 } , \hat { \pmb { \theta } } _ { 2 } ]$ . Using $\hat { \alpha } = \alpha / \sigma$ and Equation (16), we de<sup>fi</sup>ne $\hat { \Theta } = [ \hat { \pmb { \theta } } _ { 1 } [ \mathbf { 1 } , \mathbf { e } _ { 2 } ] , \hat { \pmb { \theta } } _ { 1 } \mathbf { 1 } ( \hat { \pmb { \theta } } _ { 2 } \mathbf { e } _ { 1 } ) ^ { - 1 } , \hat { \pmb { \theta } } _ { 1 } \mathbf { 1 } ( \hat { \pmb { \theta } } _ { 2 } \mathbf { e } _ { 1 } ) ^ { - 1 } \hat { \pmb { \theta } } _ { 2 } \mathbf { e } _ { 2 } ]$ with the same length , as $\hat { \pmb { \theta } } ,$ where $\mathbf { e } _ { k }$ is the kth unit vector. The variance matrix estimate is

$$
\mathrm{Var} (\hat {\mathbf {\Theta}}) = \mathbf {G} \tilde {\mathbf {V}} _ {\hat {\theta}} \mathbf {G} ^ {\prime},\tag{17}
$$

where <sup>G</sup> is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as

$$
\mathbf {G} _ {i j} = \frac {\partial \boldsymbol {\Theta} \mathbf {e} _ {i}}{\partial \theta_ {j}} | _ {\theta = \hat {\theta}, \Theta = \hat {\Theta}} i, j = 1 \dots \ell .
$$

B.1.5 Predicting the Size of Switching Costs The size of switching costs can <sup>fi</sup>nally be directly calculated as a prediction from the model, i.e.,

$$
\hat {s} _ {i, j, t} = \mathbf {x} _ {i, j, t} ^ {\prime} \hat {\pmb {\beta}}.\tag{18}
$$

The standard deviation of this prediction gives an indication of the heterogeneity of switching costs in the market, and the relative sizes of the individual coe<sup>fi</sup>cients in $\hat { \boldsymbol { \beta } }$ can provide insights about which determinants of switching costs are quantitatively more important than others.

## B.2.1 Measuring Quality

The number of standard features a vendor o<sup>f</sup>ers (More Features) in a given year is not necessarily the best measure of the quality of the service of a vendor. External data can provide a more direct proxy of vendor quality. The industry think tank Filene Research Institute published the results of survey carried out in 2001, asking a gross sample of 3000 small credit unions to evaluate the quality of their data processing vendor on a 5-point Likert scale (Sayles, 2002). Second, the survey speci<sup>fi</sup>cally only covers small credit unions (under \$20 million in assets). Third, the report only lists survey results for the top 25 most used vendors. ## B.2.2 Using a Measure of Relative Quality

One might be concerned that the measure of quality change for a focal vendor should also take into account the quality change of competing vendors. If some vendors add new services, while the current vendor does not, this will result in disutility for the client. In other words, it is possible that not only the absolute but also the relative quality of a vendor can be an important determinant of switching costs. To test this, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne Relatively More Features as the absolute change in the number of standard features of a given vendor (i.e., More Features) divided by the average absolute change in the number of standard features of all vendors in a given year. We then re-estimate our model using Relatively More Features instead of More Features. The results reported in Table B1 are very similar to those of our preferred speci<sup>fi</sup>cation reported in Table 3. All coe<sup>fi</sup>cients have the same sign and similar size – except the quality measure. It is worth noting that the relative importance of Relatively More Features in column (2) of Table B1 (13,555.2) is 1.9 times larger than the relative importance of More Features in column (2) of Table 3 (7013.9). Of course, this a<sup>f</sup>ects the quanti<sup>fi</sup>cation of the relative importance of market-, relationship-, or <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c factors in switching costs. Compared to Table 4, Table B2 shows that the di<sup>f</sup>erent de<sup>fi</sup>nition of vendor quality increases the contribution of market-speci<sup>fi</sup>c variables to overall switching costs by 6.8 percentage points. Nevertheless, the ranking remains the same. Relationshipspeci<sup>fi</sup>c factors are most important (49%), followed by <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c factors (28%) and market-speci<sup>fi</sup>c factors (23%).

We conclude that our results are robust to a di<sup>f</sup>erent empirical speci<sup>fi</sup>cation. The main reason why we choose the operationalisation More Features in the preferred speci<sup>fi</sup>cation is that those results appear to be more conservative estimates of the impact of quality. Given the limitations of our quality measure described above, we believe that this is an important consideration.

Because we are not only observing travel expenses that are directly related to vendor selection, but expenditure for all kinds of travel, the measure might overstate the true cost. Independent of which is true, however, the measurement error in the variable would be mostly of a systematic nature. That is, the unobserved part is likely to be a <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c constant (and should therefore also not lead to estimation bias in the parameters of interest). External information can help to understand which of the two is more likely in our data. The consulting company OSF estimates that the cost of selecting a vendor is 1–10% of the annual outsourcing fees (Globa Services, 2002). Interestingly, this describes our data very precisely. Looking at the distribution of travel expenditure relative to outsourcing fees shows that 98% of the observations are between 1% and 10% (see Figure B2). The median is 1% and the average is 1.9%. Hence, this seems to suggest that there is not much systematic measurement error in our data.

Random measurement error, if uncorrelated with other variables in the model, will lead to attenuation bias (the coe<sup>fi</sup>cient estimate is biased towards 0) and will not a<sup>f</sup>ect the coe<sup>fi</sup>cient estimates of other covariates. If correlated with other variables in the model, it will bias estimates of other parameters, the direction of which depends on whether this correlation is positive or negative. The correlation matrix in Table A2 suggests that this may be of minor concern, because the highest value is 0.11 across all other variables. It is of course important to acknowledge that this can only serve as an indication because it does not capture unobserved correlation. We conclude that it seems unlikely that the nonideal measure of search e<sup>f</sup>ort introduces substantial estimation bias. If at all, we’re likely to underestimate the impact of search e<sup>f</sup>orts on switching costs. However, when looking at relative e<sup>f</sup>ect sizes in Table 4, the estimate of 8% does not seem too far o<sup>f</sup> compared to other <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c factors, which account for 11–12% of the estimated switching costs.
