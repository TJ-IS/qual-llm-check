# Satisfaction with employee relationship management systems: the impact of usefulness on systems quality perceptions

Yang Yang<sup>1</sup>, Thomas F. Stafford<sup>2</sup> and Mark Gillenson<sup>2</sup>

## Abstract

The ability to effectively manage external customer satisfaction through ITbased Customer Relationship Management systems (CRM) is well documented in the literature. The concept of applying such technologies in Business to Employee relationships to manage the firm’s relationships with its employees is not. By extending the CRM paradigm to consider the service of internal customers, we characterize the emergent concept of Employee Relationship Management systems (ERM) for investigation. We find that employee satisfaction with ERM systems designed to provide access to benefits and other important employee services is largely a function of systems quality perceptions, as moderated by employee perceptions of system usefulness. Specifically, we believe our results suggest that high-quality systems implementations will not produce high degrees of employee satisfaction with ERM systems unless such systems are also found by the employee to be highly useful for their intended purpose. This highlights the critical role of accurate assessments of user requirements matched to specific needs from human resources-based systems support in the analysis phase of system design. ## Introduction

Employees, their skills, and their intellectual capital have become critical differentiators for modern businesses. Hence, traditionally tactical human resources (HR) functions in the firm have now taken an increasingly strategic role (Ulrich, 1997; Becker et al., 2001). The result is an effort to align HR strategy with business strategy, through the construction of a knowledge-centric employee service culture (Evans, 2003).

Organizations are now leveraging advanced technologies to enhance their HR efforts for competitive advantage. Such systems implementations free HR managers from mundane personnel administration activities, permitting a focus on strategic value-added personnel support and satisfaction activities (DeSanctis, 1986). Since people are the pre-eminent organizational resource, this is a critical focus (Delaney & Huselid, 1996).

Strategic beneficial outcomes arise from the corporate provision of high-quality services (Bolton & Drew, 1991), and at the conjunction of HR and employee service resides the emergent concept of Employee Relationship Management (ERM). As opposed to the well-known customer personalization strategy of Customer Relationship Management (CRM), when web-based self-serve systems are used to personally serve internal customers (i.e., employees), the resulting implementations can be characterized as ERM. Personalization is the hallmark of CRM (see Goodhue et al., 2002; Ross, 2005; King & Burgess, 2008), and as intuitive as it is to consider ERM an internally directed CRM implementation (e.g., Gillenson & Sanders, 2005), ERM is a distinct and differentiated system by comparison and the distinctions are not yet well understood in our literature. We begin that explication processes here.

This paper conceptualizes and operationalizes ERM in a way that more closely matches the actual realities of constituency-based, inwardly focused, HR benefitenabled IT systems designed for employee self-service delivery and production. ERM and CRM are distinctly and operationally different from each other, and these differences form the basis for the key background factor of this paper. If ERM systems are distinct from CRM systems, it is important to identify and understand the differences lest we inappropriately guide management in the construction and operation of such systems and mislead scholars in the operationalization of emerging constructs.

Bundling employee support services in electronic delivery channels is cost effective for firms (Hansen & Deimler, 2001; Ross, 2005; Mootheril & Singh, 2009). In many cases, electronic services delivery leads to higher satisfaction with services, owing to increased immediacy and access to desired benefits (Hwang & Kim, 2007). CRM provider Siebel Systems has developed a working prototype of an internal stakeholder support system (Panker, 2004). This ERM system prototype includes components for employee self-service applications, employee learning applications and employee performance management applications. A similar application has been developed by SRM provider SAP (Stein et al., 2005).

ERM systems that support employee services from a benefits perspective have been greatly anticipated (e.g., Crandell, 2005), but not forthcoming. Despite the emergence of the ERM concept in trade magazines (e.g., Kimsey-Sward, 2001; CRM Today, 2009), systematic research on critical success factors of electronically mediated employee self-service is nonexistent and this paper begins that process. The process of explicating ERM arises through the lens of two specific research questions:

1. Do employee perceptions of service quality delivery in self-service HR support systems affect the level of employee satisfaction with support systems?

2. Do IT services quality and subsequent employee perceptions of systems usefulness contribute to employee satisfaction with self-service support systems?

## ERM and CRM characteristics and differences

Some views of the emergent ERM concept liken it to CRM – essentially characterizing it as an inwardly focused CRM system designed for internal customers (e.g., Panker, 2004; Gillenson & Sanders, 2005). Others characterize ERM systems as instances of electronic benefits markets under varying characterizations of eHRM and Business to Employee (B2E) business models (cf., Strohmeier, 2007; Mootheril & Singh, 2009). The fact is that CRM systems have both explicit and subtle differences from ERM systems, despite their seeming similarities. The similarities of tactical iterations in the form of personalized web access to information are the common thread between the outwardly focused CRM and the inwardly focused ERM executions. The similarities between the concepts typically end at that point, however.

The best and most compelling conceptualization of the distinction between system types lies in the system deliverables. In the case of CRM, the system informs the organization about its customers. In the case of ERM, the system not only informs internal constituents about the organization (a reversed information flow in comparison to CRM), but also directly serves the users through the production and provision of informationbased products/services (a distinction from CRM, where the system supports the corporate marketing function that then provides products and services to the users). One implementation is a support function for important supply chain/marketing function (i.e., CRM). The other is a direct production/service channel though which constituencies are directly served with the desired products/ services they seek and desire (e.g., ERM).

## CRM characteristics

CRM systems are marketing support systems, plain and simple (Rigby & Ledingham, 2004; King & Burgess, 2008). Their purpose is specifically to enhance revenue production/profitability and to reduce marketing costs associated with customer transactions and relationships. As such, CRM systems are designed to support marketing functions such as the sales force and its interactions with key customers, and these benefits are realized through the collection and distribution of detailed information about customers since all other CRM functions rely upon this data stream (e.g., Shanks et al., 2009). The continuous customer information management process of CRM is designed to lead to improved value and profitability throughout the customer-focused supply chain (Goodhue et al., 2002; Kim et al., 2004).

## ERM distinctions

The scholarly view of ERM is sparse; absent the landmark work of Gillenson & Sanders (2005) in characterizing U.S. Navy HR systems in support of recruit job satisfaction as an instance of ERM, the best information about the emergent concept arises from corporate sources. Specifically, CRM provider Siebel Systems appears to have pioneered to use the ERM concept in system development contexts (e.g., Panker, 2004). While the initial scholarly view of ERM was essentially a retooling of the CRM concept for an internally focused implementation for internal customer (e.g., Gillenson & Sanders, 2005), the overly simplistic focus on the web-based personalization process that is, indeed, only one notable characteristic of CRM systems, gives lie to the important distinctions that exist between the two concepts. The best demonstration of the distinctions comes from considerations of how leading CRM providers in industry consider CRM vs their emerging ERM alternatives.

Most scholars think of CRM systems as modules of larger Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) systems (cf., Gefen & Ridings, 2002; Chalmeta, 2006). Hence, it would be useful to consider the characterizations that system vendors have for the emergent ERM concept. In the case of ERP giant SAP, ERM implementations are seen as facilitating employee self-service in automated interactions with the corporate HR department (Stein et al., 2005). A slowing in demand for CRM/SCM systems led such companies to consider developing internal self-service modules that would facilitate employee selfservice in attainment of organizational benefits, training and services. Such characterizations cast employees as increasingly responsible for managing their own data and services consumption processes. Having coined the ERM term, CRM provider Siebel Systems views ERM systems as e-business applications within the firm, the central components of which involve employee service, learning and performance management (Panker, 2004).

The key distinction seen in this view of ERM omits the all-important profit maximization goal of most CRM implementations, while including aspects of constituent service and support that are absent from CRM implementations, specifically the process of providing direct product/service provision (which would be analogous to CRM systems also serving as eCommerce portals), as well as interactive services provision/creation processes such as those involved in the training and benefits provision process in the corporate HR department. Such implementations can be characterized as eHRM, serving as an extension of benefits and services provision processes within the firm (Strohmeier, 2007; Mootheril & Singh, 2009). Indeed, CRM systems cannot always be characterized as serving customers directly so much as serving the marketing function of the firm, itself; the service of the customer flows from the marketing channels that CRM seeks to support and optimize (cf., Rigby & Ledingham, 2004; Chalmeta, 2006; Shanks et al., 2009).

Given that ERM systems serve their users in ways that are both subtly and explicitly different from the way in which CRM systems interact with customers, it is likely that system usage decisions by target constituencies may differ from conventional understandings and characterizations of technology user satisfaction and acceptance processes found elsewhere in the information systems (IS) literature.

## ERM usefulness and ERM quality: user satisfaction drivers

Nielson (2002) reports that 74% of workers believe that fringe benefits of employment compensation range from ‘important’ to ‘critical’ in affecting their loyalty to an employer. Such essential fringe benefits include health plans and employee-directed retirement plans, and the provision of online management for these benefits is the basis of the ERM concept. Graham & Meuse (1997) pointed out that we are moving into an era where employees increasingly take responsibility for the management of their own benefits. ERM systems simply enhance this critical benefits self-management process. The expectation is that giving employees direct control over highly desired and prized personnel benefits leads to higher employee satisfaction with their job and, subsequently, better work outcomes (Gomez & Rosen, 2001).

In the services literature, employees are characterized as internal customers (Berry, 1981), so the CRM/ERM perspective has great merit for increasing internal customer satisfaction. Kotler (2000) argues that internal marketing is more important than conventional external marketing, owing to the influence of employee job satisfaction on firm performance (cf., Tansuhaj et al., 1991; Rafiq & Ahmed, 2000; Conduit & Mavondo, 2001). Greene et al. (1994) point out that internal marketing is the basis for providing overall organizational services quality, which benefits firm performance in the marketplace (Pfeffer & Veiga, 1999). Wilkens (1971) was able to demonstrate significant differences in employee job satisfaction in a comparison between two plants of a large manufacturing firm, one of which had implemented a HR IS for managing information concerning work job satisfaction and HR investments. It has also been demonstrated that employee use of computer systems, in general, has positive employee job satisfaction benefits (Ghani & Al-Meer, 1989). It may be that the greater control over information by employees through computer systems use is the motivating factor (e.g., Rivard & Huff, 1984; Barker & Wright, 1997).

A close examination of the literature will quickly demonstrate the central roles of systems usefulness in determining positive user perceptions and attitudes toward system use in most technology acceptance contexts. Given the seemingly internal customer-centric focus of nascent ERM systems, it can be useful to review the body of work on acceptance and usefulness to determine specific subtleties that may pertain to the unique context of internally focused systems designed to serve organizational constituencies.

## Usefulness and IT services quality

System usage is one of the primary success-dependent variables of IS research (Delone & McLean, 1992, 2003; Straub et al., 1995). Among the theories of system usage, the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) stands out as an exemplar of predicting usage and usage intentions (Davis, 1989; Davis et al., 1989; Venkatesh & Davis, 1996, 2000). In studies of this model, Perceived Usefulness of IS has been identified as a salient belief that influences IS acceptance behaviors in a number of broad contexts (e.g., Davis et al., 1989; Mathieson, 1991; Taylor & Todd, 1995). Extensions of this model have been applied by marketers to explain acceptance of self-service technology (Dabholkar, 1996; Dabholkar & Bagozzi, 2002).

Since usefulness is one of the primary motivators of IS acceptance, it seems plausible that usefulness also influences user evaluations of IS. Previous research demonstrates that usefulness impacts attitude toward technology during several stages of IS use (Davis et al., 1989; Karahanna et $a l . ,$ , 1999). We generalize from this point to specify that ERM usefulness perceptions should influence perceptions of the quality of the ERM system, as a result. However, the ways in which usefulness combines with other usage-related constructs such as quality perceptions may well vary in novel and unexpected ways due to the unique contexts of inwardly focused constituency service systems as characterized by ERM implementations.

## Perceived usefulness and employee satisfaction with ERM systems

Technology supports HR practice as well as its responsibilities in the firm when core HR processes are redesigned around the emerging employee self-service concept (McCormick, 1998, p. 48). HR transactions performed by self-service are increasingly employee-centric, and the result of encouraging employees to direct their own HR encounters will serve to improve satisfaction in the workplace (McCormick, 1998). Hence, from an expectancy theory perspective (e.g., Vroom, 1964), enhanced performance in computing is seen as instrumental in achieving workplace rewards. Technology can also be used by employees to enhance their quality of work life through means related to their own self-governance, as iterated in Gillenson & Sanders’ (2005) ERM concept. Considering this idea of improving employee perceptions of work life by permitting them to serve themselves with regards to HR benefits and services provides the basis for several hypothetical expectations for testing in the workplace.

Service satisfaction in the workplace IS success fall into six major categories – systems quality, information quality, use, user satisfaction, individual impact, and organizational impact (DeLone & McLean, 1992). By contrast, IT quality includes three major dimensions: information quality, system quality, and service quality (DeLone & McLean, 2003). Although satisfaction arising from system use is theoretically distinct from systems quality dimensions, there are also synergistic interrelationships between services quality and subsequent user satisfaction (i.e., Bolton & Drew, 1991). This relationship is typically characterized in terms of the gap that potentially exists between user expectations and system services delivery.

The SERVQUAL instrument was developed to measure the gap between customer expectations and services received (Parasuraman et al., 1988; 1991), and has been successfully adapted to the measurement of IS service delivery (Kettinger & Lee, 1994; Zeithaml et al., 2000, 2002; Zeithaml, 2002; Parasuraman et al., 2005). The original SERVQUAL scale is a 22-item scale consisting of five service quality dimensions, including Tangibility, Reliability, Responsiveness, Assurance, and Empathy. When SERVQUAL is applied to B2C e-commerce domains, the omission of the dimension of tangibility is common (e.g., Devaraj et al., 2002). This is because the process of giving a physical presence to an intangible offering (what services marketers call ‘tangibilization’) often is irrelevant when the service is in the form of information.

Although SERVQUAL has come under increasing scrutiny and criticism over the course of its long existence (e.g., Carman, 1990; Cronin & Taylor, 1992, 1994; Pitt et al., 1995, 1997; Van Dyke et al., 1997; Babakus & Boller, 1999), the scale is still quite useful for measuring service quality in the IS context (cf., Yoo & Donthu, 2001; Wolfinbarger & Gilly, 2003; Parasuraman et al., 2005; Collier & Bienstock, 2006; Loiacono et al., 2007). For purposes of assessing the quality of an ERM implementation, four SERVQUAL dimensions are selected for use here: reliability, responsiveness, empathy, and assurance. Per Devaraj et al. (2002), tangibility is omitted. These dimensions of services quality inclusively and concisely define the ERM users’ experience in dealing with online employee benefits services.

Employee satisfaction Employee job satisfaction in the workplace has typically been assessed with the Perceived Work Environment (PWE) instrument. The theory on which this scale is based assumes that behavior is a function of the environment in which people work (Newman, 1997). The typical PWE scale measures 11 dimensions of PWE based on key aspects of organizational climate (Campbell & Beaty, 1971). In terms of envisioning employee satisfaction in the workplace, Smith (1992) made the intuitive analogy of a river with small tributaries (satisfaction with specific aspects of work life) that converged into ever-larger branches (satisfaction with general ‘facets’ of work life), eventually converging into a sea (general satisfaction with life). Adapting the analogy to the Information Age for developing a systems satisfaction measure, computers and interactions with computers have become a central aspect of work. To that end, Smith’s (1992) ‘river and tributaries’ conceptualization can be construed in the context of user satisfaction with workplace technology in the tributary role and the subsequent impact of technology on overall employee satisfaction as the analogy of the confluence to a river.

## Hypotheses

In our review, we have demonstrated empirical support for relationships between:

(1) Employee perceptions of IT usefulness and IT selfservice quality judgments, as regards an HR-based ERM system.

(2) IT usefulness perceptions and employee perceptions of satisfaction with ERM systems designed for providing HR benefits and support.

(3) IT users’ judgments of ERM services quality and subsequent perceptions of satisfaction with IT services provided by the firm.

Stated formally, these relationships provide the basis for three specific testable hypotheses. These linkages are demonstrated in Figure 1, but the specific path relationship predictions are as follows:

H1: Employee perceptions of ERM system usefulness are significantly and positively related to employee judgments of ERM system quality.

This hypothesis arises from the sensibilities of acceptance theory (cf., Davis, 1989; Davis et al., 1989; Venkatesh & Davis, 1996, 2000) in combination with research demonstrating the role of usefulness in forming system quality judgments (e.g., Yang et al., 2005). Perceived Usefulness of IS is a salient belief that influences IS acceptance (Davis et al., 1989; Mathieson, 1991; Taylor & Todd, 1995), and since services marketing scholars have leveraged acceptance theory from the IS literature to explain acceptance of self-service technology (Dabholkar, 1996; Dabholkar & Bagozzi, 2002), it is reasonable to expect a focal construct of the technology acceptance stream to also be indicative of user perceptions of system quality.

H2: Employee perceptions of ERM system usefulness are significantly and positively related to employee perceptions of satisfaction with ERM services.

In this hypothesis, we find the standard rubric of the TAM (Venkatesh & Davis, 1996, 2000): primary outcomes in the acceptance process are significantly linked to user perceptions of system usefulness on a reliable and ongoing basis in the IS literature. User satisfaction is also directly linked to usefulness perceptions of an IS (e.g., Ives et al., 1983). For that reason, it is sensible to expect that usefulness perceptions on the part of users of an ERM system will lead to increased system quality perceptions in the users.

H3: Employee perceptions of ERM system quality are significantly and positively related to employee satisfaction with self-service offerings.

As regards Hypothesis 3, the relationship between quality perceptions and system user satisfaction has been demonstrated in the services marketing literature (i.e., Bolton & Drew, 1991), but also occupies an important part of the IS success literature, wherein user satisfaction is posited to arise from a complex interplay of characteristics such as quality perceptions and usefulness perceptions that all converge upon the overall construct of success for a system (DeLone & McLean, 1992). Such a consideration suggests that a positive relationship should exist between user perceptions of systems quality and user satisfaction with the same system. Hence, the third hypothetical path in our model is specified for testing.

## Method

The hypotheses proposed here are tested using PLS Grapht structural equation modeling software (Chin, 1998). PLS Grapht (the trademarked name for a popular Partial Least Squares structural modeling package that is provided in a convenient graphical user interface) is a component-based structural modeling application that offers the advantage of making minimal demands on sample size and residual distributions. The target tech nology for the study is an ERM enterprise portal developed at a major southern university. In this system, employee benefits and compensation factors are reported to and managed directly by the university employees as a self-service application.

Four hundred eighty-seven university employees formed the sample drawn for testing, and were contacted for data collection by an email message delivered under the cover of the university Vice-President responsible for the HR function. An initial solicitation email was sent to potential respondents, with a second follow-up solicita tion sent out one week later. We collected 352 data points before the second email was sent, and an additional 135 responses were collected during a one-month window after the second email. Out of 487 returned questionnaires, 415 responses were found to be complete and usable.

Most employees (68.3%) reported accessing the selfserve portal either (a) once per month, or (b) a few times per month. Among the eight various activities of personnel records management that we listed in the questionnaire, five activities of online personnel records management have already been widely accepted: updating personnel information, vacation and sick day tracking, reviewing direct deposits, reviewing pay stubs, and reviewing benefits (see Table 1).

## Operationalization of measures

In order to assess our constructs, we used previously validated instruments whenever possible, in line with conventional practice (Straub, 1989). Measures for each construct were adapted from previously validated instruments, after alterations to fit with the ERM context of the study. In one case, we revalidated an existing measure of services quality for formative specification in line with emerging practice for formative revalidation for reflective measures of constructed judgments (Diamantopoulos & Winklhofer, 2001; Marakas et al., 2007; Petter et al., 2007). The basis of measurement for each construct was derived from previous research, and is detailed as follows.

Perceived usefulness The Perceived Usefulness construct (Davis, 1989; Davis et al., 1989; Venkatesh & Davis, 1996, 2000) has performed exceedingly well in research. Davis (1989) reported alpha coefficients of 0.98 for his use of the Perceived Usefulness scale (hereafter, PU). Hendrickson et al. (1993) examined the test-retest reliability of the perceived usefulness using two software packages, adding further evidence concerning the reliability of the scale. Subramanian (1994) also presented the results of a replication of the construct measurement of PU, as used for predicting future system usage. The original Davis PU scale items were adapted for our use here from a B2C electronic commerce context (e.g., Devaraj et al., 2002). PU is specified as a reflective construct in our study.

ERM system satisfaction User satisfaction is a critical construct serving as a surrogate of IS effectiveness. Definitions of satisfaction incorporate overarching constructs that range from IS appreciation (Swanson, 1974), user attitudes (Cheney & Dickson, 1982), and end-user satisfaction (Doll & Torkzadeh, 1988). Accordingly, there have been multiple scales developed and refined to measure user satisfaction, such as the shortened user information satisfaction scale (Ives et al., 1983), summary user information satisfaction (Galletta & Lederer, 1989), and End-User Computing Satisfaction (Doll & Torkzadeh, 1988).

Phelps & Mok (1999) developed a five-item ‘global’ User Satisfaction scale to measure user perceptions of intranet resources. The dimensions assessed include: (1) the extent of respondents’ intention to use intranet resources for their work, (2) their attitude towards encouraging other staff to use the company intranet, (3) their opinion on whether the introduction of an intranet is a positive move for their organization, (4) whether the respondents find themselves worse off or better off without an intranet, and (5) overall satisfaction. Of the available User Information Satisfaction and End User Computing Satisfaction scales, several contain dimensions that conceptually overlap the correlates of employee self-service satisfaction in our study (PU and SERVQUAL), and we are interested in the exogenous metrics’ influence on the user’s overall satisfaction with the ERM system. Therefore, we favor measuring ERM user satisfaction with an instrument designed to assess employees’ overall affective state with their ERM usage experience, which we adapt for reflective specification from Phelps & Mok’s (1999) instrument.

ERM services quality In line with Cronin & Taylor (1992) and Van Dyke et al. (1997), we conceptualize ERM services quality as being based solely on user perceptions related to the actual service consumption experience, removing considerations of pre-consumption expecta tions and service gaps. For purposes of assessing quality judgments for the self-service HR applications provided by the ERM system, we consider the SERVQUAL scale (Parasuraman et al., 1988). Though adapted from retailing and services marketing research, SERVQUAL has been demonstrated in IS contexts (Devaraj et al., 2002) and is theoretically well-situated to assess user perceptions of an IS services delivery channel.

Recent innovations in services marketing research have made the point that services quality assessments are formed judgments in response to service delivery performances, and the causative formation of the quality judgment in response to the service delivery is probably best conceptualized as a formative construct for model specification purposes (Collier & Bienstock, 2006). This consideration is entirely in line with emerging thought on model specification in the IS field (Petter et al., 2007), and will serve to avoid the methodological issues of statistical significance and predictive validity that arise with potential model misspecification in analysis ( Jarvis et al., 2003; Mackenzie et al., 2005; Marakas et al., 2007; Petter et al., 2007). In cases where constructs that have previously been modeled in reflective fashion are to be reused in situations that meet the threshold for consideration of formative specification, judicious revalidation can be engaged in (Mackenzie et al., 2005, p. 16).

Revalidating the reflective construct of SERVQUAL as a formative index is probably less risky than many other potential revalidation cases because of the robust history of domain development the SERVQUAL instrument has enjoyed (cf., Parasuraman et al., 1988, 2005; Cronin & Taylor, 1992, 1994; Parasuraman et al., 1991; Van Dyke et al., 1997).

## Formative specification of SERVQUAL

In line with emerging practice (Diamantopoulos & Winklhofer, 2001; Marakas et al., 2007; Petter et al., 2007), we modeled employees’ constructed judgments of ERM systems quality in formative rather than reflective fashion. In the instrument adopted for formative revalidation, there were four items for Reliability, three items for Responsiveness, four items for Empathy, and three for Assurance. From these, we produced a summed-score for each in order to model the SERVQUAL construct in formative fashion, in line with recent services marketing practice in formative modeling of the construct (Collier & Bienstock, 2006).

As noted by Diamantopoulos & Winklhofer (2001), formative indices do not exhibit the same reliability and covariance characteristics that reflective scales do. Often, an incremental selection process must be engaged in as part of index validation, to be certain that the resulting index is properly representative of the domain being assessed.

As an initial step in validation, confirmatory analysis was undertaken using structural equation modeling. As a formative construct was employed, PLS modeling was the method prescribed for purposes of a serial deletion of underperforming items (Diamantopoulos & Winklhofer, 2001). This was achieved through examination of the significance of the item paths in the PLS Graph outer model weights matrix.

Paths with insignificant t-values are typically deleted from the evolving index (Diamantopoulos & Winklhofer, 2001), before engaging in more advanced validation techniques. Hence, when assessing the model shown in Figure $^ { 2 , }$ it was found that the path for the SERVQUAL item ‘responsiveness’ did not meet the criteria for inclusion in the evolving services quality index (see Table 4), and it was removed from the model prior to further analysis. On reflection, it seemed reasonable to do this not only for methodological reasons (Diamantopoulos & Winklhofer. 2001) but for practical reasons of logic and intuition related to the domain sampling process (Marakas et al., 2007). Aside from the poor-performing significance level, an ERM system would not typically be expected to exhibit the sort of independent responsiveness that a human service provider would be capable of. Such systems are self-service interfaces that are controlled by and directed by the service recipient/system user. Hence, they could only, at best be effective in response to user commands as opposed to independently responsive to user wishes.

Convergent and discriminant validity for indices and scales The responsiveness dimension of the formative SERVQUAL construct was subsequently deleted and the model respecified as shown in Figure 3.

In the respecified model, all paths were significant – both for scales/indices and for structural components, in support of convergent validity (Gefen & Straub, 2005). Discriminant validity for the reflective scale items in the model is easily demonstrated by appropriate measurement item performance and Average Variance Explained (AVE) analysis (Gefen & Straub, 2005, p. 94). In AVE analysis, the square root of the AVE of each construct should be larger than the correlation of the construct in question with any other construct and should generally reach at least 0.50. As can be seen in the analysis presented in Table 5, this is the case for the scale items associated with each reflective construct in the model. Hence, evidence of convergent and discriminant validity for the reflective measures in the model is provided.

As regards to the formative construct in the respecified model, just as traditional internal consistency scores and confirmatory factor analyses do not adequately assess the measurement qualities of indexes, alternative methods must also be utilized to demonstrate discriminant validity (Jarvis et al., 2003; Gefen & Straub, 2005; Petter et al., 2007). This can typically be achieved with the construction of a modified multi-trait multi-method matrix (MTMM), as recommended by Marakas et al. (2007). This method is particularly useful, as it also provides additional support for discriminant validity of reflective items, as well (Gefen & Straub, 2005).

The process of constructing such a matrix requires the comparison of composite construct scores along with individual indicator scores. Composite construct scores are calculated by summing the product of each formative indicator score by its respective PLS weight. The resulting matrix permits a comparison of the indicators of formative constructs to other constructs in the model, for purposes of assessing convergent and discriminant validity (Marakas et al., 2007, p. 25). As would be the case for discrimination, index items indicators correlate more strongly with their own composite construct scores than scores of other variables, which supports discriminant validity for the index items (Marakas et al., 2007).

Convergent validity of both the formative index and reflective scale items is initially demonstrated in significant on-construct path loadings of the initial model (Gefen & Straub, 2005). Additional evidence of trait validity for the reflective scales in the model as well as primary evidence of discriminant validity for the formative SERVEQUAL index (e.g., Marakas et al., 2007) is provided by MTMM analysis. A final validation step for models in which formative indicators appear is collinearity analysis. In the validation of formative index items, the problem of multicollinearity must be addressed, as the validating model is based on multiple regression equations in the PLS approach (Diamantopoulos & Winklhofer, 2001) and unlike reflective scales in which controlled redundancy of indicators that reflect an underlying latent trait is considered desirable, index items are expected to uniquely contribute to the constructed causative factor (Jarvis et al., 2003; Petter et al., 2007). For this reason, the correlation of independent variables – multicollinearity – is particularly not desired. ## Results

Hypothesis 1 specified that user perceptions of ERM system usefulness would be significantly and positively related to subsequent ERM system quality judgments, and this expectation was strongly confirmed. The Usefulness/SERVQUAL path demonstrated a loading of 0.673 (t ¼ 13.418), and produced an equation that explained at least 45% of the variance in the SERVQUAL construct, alone. Hence, it can be surmised that user perceptions of the usefulness of an ERM system will substantially influence subsequent user judgments of system quality. Several important managerial implications can be derived from this finding.

Though the literature is replete with examples of how quality perceptions of a particular service were impacted by user perceptions of its usefulness, these examples generally pertain to product marketing scenarios (e.g., Bolton & Drew, 1991). By demonstrating a similar usefulness-quality link here, we advance the considerations of Technology Acceptance research (cf., Venkatesh & Davis, 1996, 2000) in addition to providing managers with a potential tool for use in managing employee expectations of ERM systems. Essentially, if the system can be engineered to support important activities (i.e., provide access to and control of critical employee benefits), the user will be far more impressed with the system.

Providing users with an impressive ERM system should then influence how happy they are, and how motivated they might be to excel in their work for the firm. Prior research in management has shown the value of employee satisfaction for advancing organizational goals and performance in the greater business environment (e.g., Pfeffer & Veiga, 1999). Other work provides support for the important link between quality perceptions and subsequent satisfaction (i.e., Bolton & Drew, 1991). Hence, our finding here does not just indicate that a useful system will be held in high regard. It suggests that a useful ERM system is a strategic asset for producing better degrees of organizational performance.

Hypothesis 2 specified that ERM system usefulness would be significantly and positively related to employee satisfaction with ERM systems. In the model, the Usefulness path exhibited a loading of 0.07 (t ¼ 1.835), which just reached significance. Though not expected, it appears that usefulness perceptions explain a relatively small percentage of the variance in employee satisfaction with ERM systems.

The strong explained variance component (0.817) for the Employee Relationship Management Satisfaction construct (ERMS) becomes clearer when respecifying the model with both the Usefulness/SERVQUAL path and the SERVQUAL/ERMS path present, but without the Usefulness/ERMS path. When this is done, the resulting change in explained variance for the ERMS construct (0.814 vs 0.817) suggests that Usefulness does contribute a small amount of the explained variance in the user satisfaction construct. This, taken together with the weak but significant path coefficient between Usefulness and ERMS, tends to suggest several alternative interpretations. One is that SERVQUAL may serve as a mediator in the causal relationship between Usefulness and ERMS, which is a presumption easily checked. Another alternative interpretation, particularly in light of the extremely weak yet significant path between Usefulness and ERMS is that the model is misspecified, and Usefulness actually plays a moderating role in the relationship between quality perceptions and user satisfaction with the ERM system, in keeping with the guidance of Baron & Kenny (1986) as to unexpectedly weak causal paths in potentially mediated models. Frequently, when there are unexpectedly weak relationships between predictors and criterion variables in a model (as is the case here), moderator variables can be suspected (Baron & Kenny, 1986). Were Perceived Usefulness of ERM systems found to be a moderator in the relationship between quality and satisfaction perceptions of ERM systems, important managerial implications could be gleaned from this interesting outcome; this is also a perception easily checked.

Hypothesis 3 specified that user judgments of ERM system quality would be significantly and positively related to employee satisfaction with such systems, and the SERVQUAL path exhibited a robust loading of 0.855 (t ¼ 26.508), strongly confirming this expectation. The managerial implication to this result is that a better benefit system will lead to happier employees, which fits with both management and marketing studies of quality and satisfaction relationships (Bolton & Drew, 1991; Pfeffer & Veiga, 1999; Shih et al., 2006). Happier employees, as noted in the management literature, do better work and have higher degrees of organizational loyalty, all of which are prized for enhancing company performance (Ostroff, 1992; Pfeffer & Veiga, 1999; Harter et al., 2002). In other quality-satisfaction relationships found in the management literature, the immediacy and impactfulness of work quality and organizational effectiveness arising from live-time ERM system use likely do not pertain. This differentiates this examination of quality effects from most others, in as much as it deals with quality of important instrumental services in the firm.

## Assessing mediator and moderator effects

The hypothesized model, as shown in Figure 3, represents the possibility of a mediation effect for SERVQUAL in the relationship between Perceived Usefulness and ERM Satisfaction. This possibility can be double checked with Baron & Kenny’s (1986) ‘causal steps’ approach (1986, p. 1177), wherein the proposed mediator (SERVQUAL, in this case) is regressed on the independent variable (PU, here), then the dependent variable (ERM Satisfaction, in this case) is regressed on the independent variable (PU), followed by regressing the dependent variable on both the independent variable and the proposed mediator. Mediation is demonstrated, as is the case in the results displayed in Table 8, when the independent variable affects the mediator in the first equation, when the independent variable also affects the dependent variable in the second equation, and, lastly, when the mediator affects the dependent variable in the third equation and the effect of dependent variable is less in the third equation than in the second. This is the case, here.

Even with mediation demonstrated, the fact that the influence of PU in the third equation is very weak – barely significant, in fact – provides the basis for asking whether the effect at hand might better be specified as a moderation effect than a mediation effect, since tests for moderation are typically called for when IV-DV effects are unexpectedly weak (Baron & Kenny, 1986). Moreover, when such equivocal results obtain, it may be best to investigate various combinations of mediation and moderation in an effort to determine the appropriate relationship and effect in operation (Edwards & Lambert, 2007). A sequential process whereby moderation is examined at the overall sample level, followed by similar tests performed after splitting the sample based on a putative moderator variable, can permit comprehensive testing of both moderator and mediator effects as well as potential combinations of the two (Edwards & Lambert, 2007, p. 2). In this incremental approach, samples are split based on an identified moderator and are then examined for potential mediation effects on the target variable. This was the analytical plan pursued here. Owing to the inexplicably weak performance of the PU–ERMS link in contrast to the robust link between SERVQUAL–ERMS, it seemed advisable to check for potential moderator effects for PU in the SERVQUAL– ERMS link as opposed to a direct causal link between PU and ERMS.

The subsequent test for moderation, using the predictor/moderator method in a regression framework (e.g., Baron & Kenny, 1986, p. 1174), was performed by fitting three separate regression paths: SERVQUAL predicting ERMS, PU predicting ERMS, and the SERVQUAL/ PU product predicting ERMS. Moderation for PU is demonstrated in the path for the interaction term (the SERVQUAL/PU product is significant), as does appear to be the case. The results of this analysis are displayed in Table 9.

What is found in this analysis is a strong negative effect for the PU/SERVQUAL interaction term, and in accordance with interpretive guidance (e.g., Baron & Kenny, 1986; Edwards & Lambert, 2007), this indicates that PU serves as a moderator in the SERVQUAL–ERMS relationship, and that it has a negative influence. This would mean that lower levels of perceived usefulness of the ERM system would reduce the beneficial impact of positive services quality perceptions on subsequent evaluations of the system.

The next step in the sequentially exhaustive process for testing possible combinations of mediation and moderation (e.g., Edwards & Lambert, 2007) involves using the moderator to split the sample, followed by individual mediation tests on each subsample. By dichotomizing perceived usefulness through a mean split, the sample was divided and mediation testing undertaken on both high PU and low PU samples. The results of this analysis are presented in Table 10.

What is seen in the testing is that mediation takes place in both subsamples, but more importantly, that the evidence for mediation differs between the two samples, which is taken as a sign that moderated mediation is taking place. Edwards & Lambert (2007) indicate that mediation is moderated by the subgroup variable if the evidence for mediation differs between the two split sample tests, and this is the case. The differences are small but important: there are clear differences in the performance of the PU–ERMS path in the third equation for both tests, with the low PU sample version demonstrating a small and non-significant Beta, while the high PU sample test produces a larger and significant Beta value for PU in the third equation, but generates a distinctly lower level of explained variance. Any difference in the evidence for mediation between the subsamples is taken as an indication of influence by the moderator, providing support for the conclusion of moderated mediation, and we conclude that SERVQUAL mediates the PU–ERMS relationship most effectively among users who have high perceived usefulness perceptions of the ERM system. Moreover, as a general finding, it can be concluded via the moderation testing and its subsequent negative interaction effect that low usefulness perceptions reduce the beneficial influence of system quality perceptions among ERM users. Important implications for future research and managerial practice, alike, arise from this interesting result.

## Discussion

Information technology provides increasingly effective delivery channels for important organizational services and HR benefits. The use of electronic services delivery channels in employee self-service configurations provides managers with synergistic outcomes. On the one hand, employees enjoy direct and immediate self-directed access to their personal benefits and compensation information; this will likely result in higher degrees of satisfaction, which can translate into important motivation, performance, and job tenure benefits for the firm. On the other hand, the ability to provide systemsmediated HR solutions provides managers with a means to reduce personnel costs and overhead by substantially automating what has traditionally been a critical but labor-intensive process in the firm.

While ERM systems have been described as ‘CRM for employees’ (e.g., Kimsey-Sward, 2001; Mootheril & Singh, 2009), the sort of B2E system characterized by ERM is designed to treat employees as important stakeholders of the firm, rather than simply track their activities, collect information and profile them for differential treatment as CRM systems do with customers. Hence, ERM is intended to ensure, through the direct provision of desired support services and benefit products, employees’ continued and motivated dedication to the organizational mission. As compared to the conceptually similar but operationally distinct CRM systems that firms operate, the ERM implementation described here is quite different in both operation and outcomes.

From an IT implementation perspective, key tenets of usability and system satisfaction might be expected to cohere across both sorts of systems. Yet, the resulting benefits from successful implementation for CRM and ERM are distinct and incomparable. In the case of CRM, the aim of system implementation is to build and maintain long-term transactional relationships with buyers with an eye toward maximizing revenues from high-potential customers (Sun, 2006). Even at that, the benefits of electronic management of the customer experience and their relationship with the firm via CRM are far from clear or established (Kotorov, 2002; Mithas et al., 2005; Hwang & Kim, 2007), while our results here show tangible system satisfaction outcomes can accrue from careful management of usability and quality of implementation. Hence, CRM systems will have different sorts of benchmarks for usability and customer satisfaction than will a system designed to enhance employee loyalty, performance and job satisfaction through the direct electronic provision of HR benefits and services. In short, CRM seeks to maximize different outcomes than does ERM, and may not even be as effective in doing so.

In the case of ERM, the aim of system implementation is to build, nurture, and sustain employee loyalty and commitment to the organizational mission (Delaney & Huselid, 1996; Gillenson & Sanders, 2005; Mootheril & Singh, 2009). Such systems are quite cost-effective, since they automate the provision of important employee benefits that would otherwise require more costly direct personal contacts (Hansen & Deimler, 2001; Ross, 2005; Mootheril & Singh, 2009). Unlike CRM, cost-effectiveness benefits notwithstanding, profit is not the immediate goal of employee-focused ERM; rather, it is organizational effectiveness (Mootheril & Singh, 2009). The sort of usability and satisfaction this type of inward focused system would engender are distinct from that arising from a customer facing revenue maximization system.

## Moderation and mediation effects

The fact that Perceived Usefulness of ERM systems moderates the quality–satisfaction relationship in a negative fashion is important and interesting. To the degree that well-built systems are perceived as possessing high levels of quality for users, their subsequent satisfaction with such systems will greatly depend not only on how well the system is built, from a quality standpoint, but how well the system meets the needs of users. This means, simply, that well-built computer systems which are designed to serve employees but which miss the mark in the user requirements phase of design in terms of providing services that users actually prize will fail to meet the goals underpinning most ERM systems. Such systems are intended to improve employee satisfaction with their jobs and employers by way of meeting their needs for employee services, information, and benefits. If such systems, even if built to exceedingly fine standards, are poorly designed in terms of user requirements, the goals of the firm in building stronger and more productive relationships with its employees will not be met.

To the extent that a case of moderated mediation exists, in which quality perceptions mediate the influence of usefulness on subsequent system satisfaction, we can still presume that the actually quality of implementation is a critical factor in meeting the employee service goals of the firm, even when such systems are well-designed in terms of meeting user needs. Hence, the key point of the study is that quality of systems implementation matters quite a bit, but only given the effective diagnosis of user needs and subsequent aspects of system usefulness for meeting such needs in ERM implementations.

## Implications

Managers concerned with effective provision of organizational services via IT delivery channels will find several important implications for decision making in this study. First, to the degree that employee satisfaction with organizational self-service provision is considered important, quality management processes are likely to maximize employee satisfaction outcomes in line with our finding that services quality judgments were strongly and significantly predictive of employee satisfaction. If ERM systems boost organizational effectiveness, then improving the quality of ERM implementations will have desirable effects for the firm, but only to the extent that such systems are well-targeted toward meeting user needs in addition to being well-constructed.

Secondly, to the degree that managers wish to proactively influence employee judgments of ERM systems quality, the usefulness of such systems will be a critical component, as suggested by the strong and significant link between usefulness and quality judgments. It is important for managers to bear in mind the instrumental aspects of ERM systems. These systems are designed to serve employees, and to the extent that they provide benefits and information prized by the employee, the organizational outcomes will be beneficial. This will require some dedication on the part of managers to discerning what benefits and information employees desire to have self-service access to through IT systems implementations. Moderation effects notwithstanding, usefulness is one of the determinants of quality perceptions, which is likely the reason for the moderated mediation effect of quality in the usefulness–satisfaction link in high usefulness groups of the sample.

Lastly, managers with systems implementation responsibilities, as well as managers with HR duties, must consider the synergistic effects that systems usefulness and systems quality considerations have in the specific and unique context of ERM. ERM may be conceptually similar to CRM systems, in terms of interface and operation, but its goals, operation and outcomes are quite distinct from customer-facing systems designed to support marketing operations. What is useful to an employee in the firm will differ from what is considered useful by an external customer. Hence, important satisfactions for each constituency will derive from different system qualities.

## Limitations and future research

This was a study of employee use of an enterprise portal for electronic self-service of HR benefits and compensation information in a university setting, and involved a study of university Internet users who accessed HR benefits online through an innovative ERM system. To the extent that universities are different from other sorts of complex organizations, the findings here should be generalized with caution to other large organizations in both the private and public sectors. For example, employees of educational institution may well have higher degrees of education than would be the norm in mainstream life, and this will differentiate this sample from industrial sample frames that could be envisioned.

For that reason, investigations of ERM systems must be extended to other contexts, particularly the corporate. Given that both Siebel Systems and SAP have ERM implementations, a good future research avenue would involve identifying and assessing these implementations in business contexts.

A good fraction of the sample (30%) reported being heavy Internet users, and this factor may certainly be a consideration in regard to the results encountered. Ongoing studies of ERM systems implementations should take care to either stratify samples or specify sample frames in which Internet usage is closer to the population norm, for specific external validity reasons.

The enterprise portal under investigation was subject to ongoing implementation, and results could be biased by either incomplete installations or incorrect performance, in as much as some systems were still under development. While many ERM systems will be under similar implementation circumstances, it will be important to investigate completed installations in order to ensure that user perceptions are stable across both early and mature stages of the implementation process.

Although 415 responses were available for analysis, this figure only represented about one quarter of the entire workforce of the organization, and we were not permitted to specifically identify respondents by name or job title, so it was not possible to differentiate between line, staff, administrative, and faculty roles in the survey. Generalization to specific university settings, as a result, should also be made with caution. ## Conclusion

ERM systems in the firm provide attractive opportunities for managers to increase organizational efficiencies and organizational effectiveness by way of improving employee job satisfaction. This is accomplished through the electronic delivery of important HR services to employees through an ERM portal. Employees of a major university were investigated with regard to their judgments and perceptions of electronic service delivery channels through an ERM implementation, and it was determined that the degree to which the system provided important services and benefits to employees and the quality with which such services were provided strongly impacted employee satisfaction, with subsequent implications for organizational performance.

As large organizations increasingly evolve toward enterprise-level systems solutions, the ability to integrate important service delivery options within the firm along with other important efficiencies that such enterprise portals offer will maximize the influence of, benefits from, and user responses to, such service technology implementations.
