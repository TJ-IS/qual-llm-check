# A systematic review on cross-cultural information systems research: Evidence from the last decade

Xinmin Chu<sup>a</sup>, Xin (Robert) Luo<sup>b,⁎</sup>, Yan Chen<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Jiangsu Normal University, Xuzhou, Jiangsu, 221116, China

<sup>b</sup> University of New Mexico, Albuquerque, New Mexico, 87131, USA

<sup>c</sup> Florida International University, Miami, Florida, 33199, USA

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Cross-Culture Systematic review Espoused culture Culture preference Information systems

## A B S T R A C T

Based on a review of 40 articles published in the information systems journals of the senior basket since 2006, this article presents refreshing and updated patterns of cross-cultural information systems (CCIS) research and suggests a roadmap for future research in CCIS. This 10-year systematic review contributes to the information systems community by unveiling three streams of transitions in CCIS research: (1) from national level to individual level with espoused and contextual cultural values, (2) from corporate users to end users, and (3) from West to East with the emergence of Chinese culture. The limitations and future research directions are presented.

## 1. Introduction

The applications of information technology and information system (IT/IS) have become ubiquitous across countries and regions world wide. Though the <sup>fl</sup>at world metaphor has gained momentum [28], cultural con<sup>fl</sup>icts are often blamed for failures in IT/IS projects [44] when IT/IS projects fail to circumvent unfamiliar cultural environments to succeed. Well-known unacclimatization cases have shown that eBay lost to Taobao in China [58] and Google frustrated and struggled in South Korea [62]. In IT/IS research, we have witnessed the paradigmatic switch from parochialization to internationalization, to globali zation, and then to glocalization [52]. In this trajectory, the key issue is what and how to settle the di<sup>f</sup>erences between native and foreign environments. This critical issue has piqued increased attention among IS researchers in the past decades.

Since the 1980s, cross-cultural information systems (CCIS) research has dominantly anchored on Hofstede’s cultural dimension framework. When corporations <sup>fl</sup>ushed IT/IS projects into foreign countries and regions in di<sup>f</sup>erent cultural environments in the 1980s, they soon realized that it was extremely necessary to be culturally aware of and to acclimatize to foreign situations for them to succeed. To examine and facilitate such acclimatization, IS researchers have intensively con ducted cross-cultural studies on IT/IS adoption, development, usage, and performance and proposed solutions for culture con<sup>fl</sup>ict and change [44]. Owing to the simplicity and parsimony of Hofstede’s cultural dimension framework, it has gained popularity. According to Hofstede [35], culture is “the collective programming of the mind distinguishing the members of one group or category of people from others” (p. 1). At the national level, culture can be viewed as a benchmark that distinguishes di<sup>f</sup>erent countries or regions. For this purpose, Hofstede developed four parsimonious cultural dimensions including power distance, uncertainty avoidance, individualism/collectivism, and masculinity/femininity. Two new dimensions, namely, long-term orientation and indulgence/restraint, were added to the dimension list a few years later [36].

Despite the broad acceptance of Hofstede's cultural dimensions, researchers and practitioners have raised criticism of them [3,4,7,26,29,32,55,56,89]. Many scholars made endeavors to make up for or correct Hofstede’s dimensions [36,44,77]. One noticeable example is Trompenaars and Hampden-Turner’ seven dimensions of national culture: universalism/particularism, individualism/communitarianism, neutral/emotional, speci<sup>fi</sup>c/di<sup>f</sup>use, achievement/ ascription, sequential time/synchronous time, and internal direction/ outer direction [77]. Scholars also attempted to develop some new dimensions from new perspectives, such as culture context [30], mono chronism/polychronism [31], Schwartz’s 10 universal values [64], locus of control [66], horizontal and vertical individualism [76], GLOBE expansion [37], and espoused cultural values [68], or to seek a new approach to explain cultural phenomena, such as ambiculture [2,12] (see Appendix A for a summary of cultural theories/values). Moreover, recent studies highlight the contextual di<sup>f</sup>erences and individual di<sup>f</sup>erences within a culture [2,13]. As suggested by Srite and

Karahanna [68], the cultural values endorsed by individuals or groups may play a more important role in IT/IS research, compared to the cultural values held by a nation as a whole. This is the inspiration for this study in which we review CCIS research in the past 10 years in an attempt to identify new paradigmatic trends in CCIS research based on di<sup>f</sup>erent contexts of IT/IS and to help IT/IS practitioners adapt to new cultural environments.

IT/IS research has unveiled the stage of di<sup>f</sup>usion and adoption as IT has become ubiquitous and IS is widely accepted. Additionally, cloud computing promotes the transition from system development to platform services, and big data enables a richer analysis of end-user de mands and behaviors. To keep up to date with this postadoption and ubiquitous use stage of IT/IS [52], CCIS researchers need to reexamine cultural factors and in<sup>fl</sup>uences within the IT/IS domain. It is necessary to go beyond the push-and-persuade models such as technology acceptance [45,69,79] in CCIS research. Signals of this emerging need might have appeared in the literature in the last decade, but a lack of systematic reviews on CCIS research in this period results in a knowledge gap with regard to such signals.

It is worth mentioning two prominent CCIS review studies focusing on research articles published before 2006. Leidner and Kayworth [44] reviewed 82 CCIS articles published from 1983 to 2004. Of these, 51 examined culture at the national level and 31 at the organizational or subunit levels. The review results point to six themes of IT-culture research: (1) culture and IS development; (2) culture, IT adoption, and di<sup>f</sup>usion; (3) culture, IT use, and outcomes; (4) culture, IT management, and strategy; (5) IT’s in<sup>fl</sup>uence on culture; and (6) IT culture. The six themes emphasize interweaving between IT and culture and lead to the development of the cultural con<sup>fl</sup>ict theory in the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld. Furthermore, Kappos and Rivard [39] reviewed 56 articles published between 1985 and 2006 by using a three-perspective conceptualization—integration, di<sup>f</sup>erentiation, and fragmentation. They then proposed a model of the relationships between culture, information systems, and their development and use. The theories synthesized by these two studies focus on addressing cultural con<sup>fl</sup>ict and acclimatization for IT/IS di<sup>f</sup>usion and adoption that were both a theoretical and practical concern for academia and industry.

How has the CCIS research progressed since then? Given the 10-year time lapse and the changed IT landscape, it is undoubtedly the time to revisit the evolving literature and to summarize and grasp the new trends of CCIS research. By doing so, we may be able to provide theoretical guidance for current CCIS practice and suggest a roadmap for future research in CCIS. Hence, we focus on the following three research questions: (1) what are the new changes in CCIS practice and research in the last decade? (2) is Hofstede’s cultural framework still prevalent and useful in CCIS practice and research in the evolving global environment? and (3) what are the trends in CCIS research and their implications for future research in CCIS? Di<sup>f</sup>erent from the review by Leidner and Kayworth [44], our review focuses on national cultural (or cross-cultural) values because, as Leidner and Kayworth pointed out, national culture research and organizational culture research are two traditionally separate streams, and there is a lack of consensus of organizational cultural taxonomies for comparison. With this single focus, we are able to present a clear picture of CCIS research tackling national cultural values.

This systematic review makes three contributions:

1) It <sup>fi</sup>lls the gap in literature review on CCIS research from 2006 to now. For a long time, CCIS research attention was on the acceptance of IT/IS and its execution performance in foreign environments. Furthermore, during the past decade, many new situations have emerged in the IT/IS application practice. However, little systematic review research was conducted to have an integrated examination on CCIS research and to bridge the gap between research and fast changing IT/IS practice since 2006.

2) This research synthesizes the new trends in CCIS research based on a systematic literature review. We have observed three transitions occurring in the CCIS research domain. The <sup>fi</sup>rst one is the transition from national level to individual level so that we could avoid ecological fallacies in CCIS research. The second one is the transition from corporate users to end users so that we could pay more attention to users’ perceived values rather than to the IT/IS di<sup>f</sup>usion di<sup>fi</sup>culty and application cost. The third one is the transition from West to East so that we could embrace a multidimensional world rather than just a single perspective.

3) We propose a new cultural value scale as “individual culture preference values.” When individuals face di<sup>f</sup>erences between a native culture and a foreign culture, they might not just have one alternative. The cultural value should be regarded as a continuous variable rather than a discrete one. This means that we should reexamine IT/IS users’ behaviors according to their culture preference. For example, using individual culture preference values, we can identify culture preference patterns at the group level. The analysis of the comprehensive e<sup>f</sup>ects of individual culture preference may identify a new path and broaden the horizon of CCIS research.

This article is organized as follows. In the next section, a systematic literature review is presented on CCIS articles published in leading IS journals since 2006. Based on this review, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed three trends in CCIS research in the last decade and discussed their theoretical and practical implications. We then discussed the limitations of the current research and pointed out future research directions before we concluded this study.

## 2. CCIS literature review

## 2.1. Journal and article selection

The journals selected for our review outlet are the eight IS journals listed in the o<sup>fi</sup>cial senior scholars' basket (AISNET.org). The eight journals have the highest impact factor (IF) scores among IS journals based on JOURNAL-DATA.COM, and their quali<sup>fi</sup>cation was con<sup>fi</sup>rmed by empirical research [8]. Thus, such journal selection can ensure the quality of our review outlets and the representativeness of our reviewed articles. The selection is also in line with the review outlet of prior research [9]. One of the authors examined the titles, abstracts, and implications of all the articles published in the eight journals since 2006 and then selected all the articles with a culture-related topic. By conducting a further review, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed 40 articles on cross-cultural research. ## 2.2. Review method

We adopt a systematic review approach [25,88]. The systematic review approach enables knowledge development based on the appraisal and synthesis of multiple studies by addressing the questions of “what,” “why,” and “how” [25]. “What” question explores the reality of existing studies with attention to study contexts and topics that de<sup>fi</sup>ne the theoretical boundaries of review; “Why” question addresses the theoretical reasons of studies by understanding theoretical lens adapted and consequent advances in theory development; and “how” question examines the operationalization of studies such as sources of data and research methods [25]. Hence, in the following sections, the systematic review on the CCIS research is presented considering three aspects: contexts and topics, theoretical lens, and research methods and data sources [25,88].

## 2.3. Analysis on contexts and topics

Being context and concept centric is important for literature review [88]. Relying on the basic assumption that culture would make no sense without di<sup>f</sup>erence, and being inspired by Leidner’s taxonomy (identi fying the di<sup>f</sup>erences, explaining the di<sup>f</sup>erences, and managing the di<sup>f</sup>erences) [43], we investigated the main di<sup>f</sup>erences in IT/IS practice in which culture plays an important role, given that disparity is the source of con<sup>fl</sup>icts.

In general, there are three key players in IT/IS practice. The <sup>fi</sup>rst one is business. Di<sup>f</sup>erent business contents have di<sup>f</sup>erent emphases on the needs of IT/IS. The second one is employee. Each employee comes from a di<sup>f</sup>erent background or culture, thus resulting in a di<sup>f</sup>erent attitude toward IT/IS. The third one is customer. Customers’ needs and re quirements for IT/IS can vary because of di<sup>f</sup>erences in background and cultural values. Accordingly, the CCIS articles can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into three contexts: (1) business related (corresponding to business), (2) organization related (corresponding to employee), and (3) end user related (corresponding to customer) (see Table 2).

## 2.3.1. Business-related CCIS research

Culture-related business disparity occurs when a corporation expands its business across the national border and faces a di<sup>f</sup>erent national culture. It is often observed in IT/IS outsourcing. Although a proxy company, which has IT/IS outsourcing demands, and an agent company, which holds the capability to serve the demands, are linked by a contract, cultural di<sup>f</sup>erences may lead to disparity in interpreting and executing the contract. Our review shows that business-related CCIS research was centralized on IT/IS outsourcing (six out of eight articles). The topics include outsourcing decision (two articles), o<sup>f</sup>- shore business organization (two articles), outsourcing performance prediction (one article), and dispute solution (one article). Cultural disparity was found when companies attempt to address IT/IS outsourcing questions such as whether and where to outsource? How to manage o<sup>f</sup>shore business? How to operate o<sup>f</sup>shore business? How to resolve cultural con<sup>fl</sup>icts and disputes?

Dibbern et al. [22] examined the systemic in<sup>fl</sup>uences on outsourcing decisions and found that systemic e<sup>f</sup>ects on IT/IS outsourcing decision are stronger in an individualist culture than in a collectivist culture. Tiwana and Bush [75] assessed the cross-cultural robustness (the United States and Japan) of three complementary theories in predicting project-level IT outsourcing decisions. Fang et al. [27] identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a set of coping mechanisms of dealing with rapid IT change in IT organizations and found the cross-national di<sup>f</sup>erences in using the coping mechan isms. Rai et al. [59] found that cultural di<sup>f</sup>erences at the organizational and team levels and relational factors (e.g., social embeddedness) a<sup>f</sup>ect o<sup>f</sup>shore IS project success. Mahnke et al. [50] found the emergence of brokers or middlemen and their intermediation capabilities of intermediating disparities in culture for o<sup>f</sup>shore sourcing. Ravishankar [60] found the role of reconciliation of cultural beliefs in the realignment of the o<sup>f</sup>shoring frame disputes and built a social-psychological process model to reconcile the o<sup>f</sup>shoring frame disputes. A synergy from the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of these studies is that organizations need to be culturally sensitive and be willing to reconcile cultural disparities and disputes in IT/IS projects to succeed when dealing with a foreign cul ture.

## 2.3.2. Organization-related CCIS research

Employee cultural disparity is often observed in multinational corporations (MNCs). Employees of MNCs come from di<sup>f</sup>erent countries and have di<sup>f</sup>erent cultural backgrounds, cultural values, habits, and work styles. These di<sup>f</sup>erences can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uence how employees work together in the same MNC. Employee cultural disparity is also observed in cross-national settings when employees work on the same IT/IS project together and behave di<sup>f</sup>erently toward the project. In this review, we found that employee disparity discussed in organi zation-related CCIS research focuses on the following areas: technology acceptance (three articles), management e<sup>fi</sup>ciency (six articles), and information beliefs and ethics (four articles).

Research on technology adoption has drawn tremendous attention since its debut [19,83]. Not surprisingly, technology adoption is still an important topic in the reviewed organization-related CCIS research studies. Our review shows that CCIS studies examined employee disparity in technology adoption from various cross-national perspectives. McCoy et al. [54] and Chen et al. [11] investigated it from a multinational perceptive, while Hsu et al. [38] studied it under the context of cross-cultural interorganizational information systems (IOS). These studies show that although IT/IS use has become ubiquitous, cultural di<sup>f</sup>erences still play a crucial role in IT/IS di<sup>f</sup>usion and adoption in corporate organizations.

The review of organization-related CCIS research also unveils organizational challenges and e<sup>f</sup>orts in improving management e<sup>fi</sup> ciency in multicultural settings. Zhang et al. [90] showed that the use of computer-mediated communication can reduce majority in<sup>fl</sup>uence in the cross-national group decision-making context. Su [72] studied the cultural sense-making activities of the supplier’s employees and the e<sup>f</sup>ects of such activities on employees’ collaboration with diverse and cross-cultural clients. Wake<sup>fi</sup>eld et al. [84] suggested that communication technologies and virtual team leaders’ speci<sup>fi</sup>c roles (e.g., the role of monitor) are important in reducing various con<sup>fl</sup>icts in virtual teams. Heikkilä [33] examined the impact of host-country institutional factors on strategic electronic human resource management (e-HRM) practices in foreign subsidiaries in an attempt to improve strategic potentials in a multinational corporation subsidiary setting. These studies suggest the need for multicultural-oriented strategies for management e<sup>fi</sup>ciency in multicultural organizational settings.

Our review also shows how culture in<sup>fl</sup>uences employees beliefs and ethics that could lead to di<sup>f</sup>erent behaviors among employees. Research found cultural disparity in reporting bad news [40], information ethics [21,53], and trust [48]. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that because of cultural di<sup>f</sup>erences, employees may behave di<sup>f</sup>erently in dealing with moral hazards, information privacy, and security, and employee con<sup>fl</sup>icts. As information security has increasingly become a managerial concern, we conceive that more cross-cultural IT/IS research on information security is needed.

Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, our review unveils that signi<sup>fi</sup>cant research attention has been paid to cultural disparity in end users’ requirements and demands. The assumption is that to adapt to users’ di<sup>f</sup>erent cultural backgrounds in a global environment, companies need to analyze, understand, predict, and prioritize users’ requirements and demands. As such, CCIS research has been on cultural disparity in consumer preferences, including preferences in usability testing [14], requirement prioritization [80], user interfaces [61], and personal communication technology [73]. We believe that CCIS research in this context will continue to evolve because although there is an argument on cultural convergence, users’ requirements and demands remain heterogeneous because of the existence of contextual di<sup>f</sup>erence and subcultural dif ference.

When business organizations move across the national border, they have to depend more on a trustful relationship between sellers and buyers to succeed. Thus, cultural disparity in trust is a crucial topic in the reviewed end user-related CCIS research. Martinsons [51] revealed the impact of nation-speci<sup>fi</sup>c, relationship-based commerce on trust in China. Several studies have explored the e<sup>f</sup>ect of culturally sensitive designs on trust. For example, Cyr [17] and Cyr et al. [18] investigated the e<sup>f</sup>ect of culturally di<sup>f</sup>erent web deigns on trust. Cross-cultural comparison of trust building is also a focus in end user-related CCIS research. Kim [41] examined cultural di<sup>f</sup>erences in trust of e-vendors, and Sia et al. [65] analyzed cultural customization to build consumer trust. Given the number of studies and topics covered in the reviewed literature, trust, we argue, is a relatively well-understood concept.

Security is another essential topic in our review on end user-related CCIS research. Dinev et al. [24] examined the cross-cultural di<sup>f</sup>erences between South Korea and the United States (US) in user behavior toward protective information technologies. Lowry et al. [47] explored the e<sup>f</sup>ects of culture on use of self-disclosure technologies. In addition, a polycontextual approach was proposed and applied it to study users online security perceptions and behaviors [13]. This approach suggests the use of polycontexts such as economic, legal, political, technological, religious, and other contexts to conduct cross-national research in the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld. We believe that this study points to a new direction for CCIS research: CCIS research needs to go beyond the cultural lens to include more contexts. Such polycontextual approach may enable a more hol istic understanding of cross-national disparities in any CCIS phenom enon by using many senses of knowing [13,78].

## 2.4. Analysis on theoretical Lens

There are several cross-cultural, cross-national lenses used in the reviewed articles. Hofstede’s cultural dimension framework is still a dominant one, despite it has been questioned and challenged by several new theories [3,4,7,26,29,32,55,56,89]. Fifteen of 40 articles (37.5%) used one or more Hofstede’s dimensions to conduct CCIS research (see Table 3). The share is less than the 60% utilization shown in Leidner and Kayworth’s [44] review. This indicates that researchers have moved beyond Hofstede’s cultural dimensions to conduct CCIS research through other lenses and perspectives.

Interestingly, all the reviewed articles except the one by Vance et al. [82] in Table 3 employ the IC dimension as a major index to analyze or bridge the cross-cultural gap between di<sup>f</sup>erent nations. The IC dimension refers to how individuals are integrated into their social clans (e.g., their immediate families) [36]. Nevertheless, espoused national cultural dimensions are still based on Hofstede’s cultural dimensions. Thus, we suggest that more research should explore other espoused national cultural dimensions to examine acculturation due to social media accompanied by the Internet culture and the impact of acculturation on IT/IS. For example, IT culture at the group level is a set of IT-related behaviors, values, and underlying assumptions shared by a group in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c IT usage context, while IT culture at the individual level is determined by the extent to which the individual interacts and a<sup>fi</sup>liates with the group [86]. Current CCIS research that examine and understand cultural layers and recursive and intertwined e<sup>f</sup>ects between them is scarce (e.g., how individual culture, group subcultures, and national culture shape and reshape each other)—this warrants more future research.

We also notice a trend that more CCIS research has focused on the cross-national comparison between China and Western nations. This is not surprising because China has become the second largest economy in the world, and Chinese traditions and culture have become more widely disseminated and recognized worldwide. For example, Guanxi (a Chinese type of social relationship and a manifestation of Confucianism) and its implications in di<sup>f</sup>erent IT/IS contexts were examined [20,21,51,91,92]. Our review suggests that researchers may need to develop more context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c and country-speci<sup>fi</sup>c cultural dimensions to have a more in-depth understanding of cross-national di<sup>f</sup>erence for speci<sup>fi</sup>c countries and contexts.

Finally, we found that some other cultural theories were employed in the reviewed CCIS research, such as GLOBE national culture theory [37] adopted by Fang, Benamati and Lederer [27], Trompenaars and Hampden-Turner’s cultural dimensions [77] adopted by Dibbern et al. [22] and Mahnke, Wareham and Bjorn-Andersen [50], and climato economic dimensions discussed by Chen, Hsieh, Van de Vliert and Huang [11]. Borrowed from psychology, sociology, anthropology, and economics, a large number of theories and methods have been used to study the IT/IS behaviors under cross-cultural circumstances. Additionally, some studies [e.g., 11,22,41,48] adopted Hofstede’s dimen sions along with some non-Hofstede’s dimensions to provide a broader angle to understand cultural di<sup>f</sup>erences in the IT/IS context. We encourage more of such endeavor to broaden our understanding of CCIS. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, expanding the polycontextual approach suggested by Chen and Zahedi [13], CCIS researchers may not only conduct polycontextual examination of multiple contexts such as cultural, social, political, economic, and technological but also investigate the interplay of them. This type of endeavor is possible within today’s data-rich/big data environment.

## 2.5. Analysis on research method and data collection

Positivism and interpretivism are two methodological schools in scienti<sup>fi</sup>c research [6]. Our review shows that CCIS research falls into these two schools while employing a variety of research methods, such as survey, experiment, <sup>fi</sup>eld study, case study, and secondary data analysis. In particular, 57.5% of the reviewed research studie employed survey, followed by experimental (22.5%) and case/<sup>fi</sup>eld studies (15%) (see Table 5). We found that few researchers employed other methods such as ethnographic study [60] and secondary data approach. We also found CCIS research on mixed-method studies is scant. IS scholars recently have been keen on mixed-method studies because such studies provide a broadened perspective by using more than one method to have a more thorough understanding of a research problem. Di<sup>f</sup>erent methods may compensate each other’s weaknesses and o<sup>f</sup>er less method-biased research results and <sup>fi</sup>ndings [5]. Thus, we call for more CCIS research using mixed methods. Such mixed-method research may help researchers tackle complex cultural issues in IT/IS (e.g., CCIS research on societal issues) that often involve problems with incomplete or contradictory information and changing environment.

## 3. Transitions of CCIS research

From the aforementioned review, we found three important CCIS research trends in the last decade. They are (1) transition from national level to individual level with espoused or contextual cultural values, (2) transition from corporate users to end users, and (3) transition from West to East with the emergence of Chinese culture. Based on three transitions identi<sup>fi</sup>ed, we propose a measure of individual culture preference value to evaluate the individual espoused level between his/ her native culture and the foreign culture.

3.1. From national level to individual level: espoused or contextual cultural values

Our review shows that Hofstede’s culture theory is still prevalent and important in CCIS research, but “its primacy is declining” [87]. The reason behind this decline is that more and more researchers have recognized that it is an “ecological fallacy” to use Hofstede’s national cultural values as a substitute for individuals’ espoused cultural values [68,87] to conduct CCIS research. Hofstede’ national cultural values stand at the national level and are the common and shared characteristics of people in a nation. Our systematic review also unveils that when we use Hofstede’ national cultural values to interpret the individual-level di<sup>f</sup>erences in technology acceptance and use and management of information systems, we may erroneously assume that the universal validation of Hofstede’ national cultural values is applicable across countries. Schwartz [64], however, argues that we cannot have such validation until we have validated these measures with equivalent meanings in all populations. Although it is understandable that the in<sup>fl</sup>uence from the national culture on an individual is potent, the extent to which an individual is espoused to the culture is di<sup>f</sup>erent for each individual [68,87]. This view leads to the transition from the national cultural level to the individual espoused cultural level in CCIS research [68,87]. In terms of individual espoused cultural dimensions, indulgence versus restraint is the sixth dimension in the latest development of Hofstede’s culture theory [36]. It has been adopted by Zhou et al. [93] as another individual espoused cultural dimension in addition to the four dimenisons proposed by Srite and Karahanna [68]. The transition also sheds new light on CCIS researh. Research found that the individual-level espoused values are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant for individualistic and idiosyncratic interpretations for user perceptions and behaviors such as security behavior [13] and privacy concern [47].

The shift of the CCIS research from the national level to the in dividual level is also shown as a research paradigm of individualism vs. collectivism (IC). Manifested as the private property rights, individualism has been a mainstream value for a long time in Western countries. Furthermore, collectivism-oriented nations, such as China, began to take individualism into consideration with the reform and “open-door” policy. Additionally, the IC dimension focuses on in dividual-level social clan [36]. Thus, the comparison between individualism and collectivism becomes a comparison of personal mindsets in groups or organizations rather than an ideology in societal political environments. In essence, the IC research paradigm respects individuals’ cultural values and ties, thus enabling a better understanding of IT cultural con<sup>fl</sup>icts in CCIS development, management, and operation.

Given the trend in cultural emergence, we call for more CCIS research at the individual espoused level. We argue that under such cultural trend, it would be more meaningful to investigate within na tion or within population subcultures and contextual-based cultures. Such more granular investigations may provide us with in-depth and granular knowledge on cultural disparities in di<sup>f</sup>erent subcultures and contexts. Equipped with such knowledge, business corporations can make more informed decisions to achieve IT/IS success when facing cultural con<sup>fl</sup>icts in IT/IS projects.

As IT/IS becomes ubiquitous and is widely employed in all businesses, more or less end users (internal users such as employees and external users such as customers) and their relationships determine IT/ IS success, though technology characteristics related to design and development of IT/IS are still critical.

This trend is fueled by rapid IT/IS di<sup>f</sup>usion, especially the Internet, in many countries. Because of the role of information-based transformation in producers, consumers, and markets, corporates have paid greater attention to information concerning end users’ perceptions and behaviors of IT/IS usage. Compared to the review by Leidner and Kayworth [44], individual users have been a new focal interest in our review. We project that this trend will continue because of the rising of using big data and business analytics. Business analytics and big data provide tools and data that enable researchers to conduct larger-scale research on end users by using a variety of data sources. Such end user research may not only provide businesses in-depth understanding of users to maximize the business values of current IT/IS products and services but also help them develop or discover new classes of products and services [1,15].

## 3.3. From west to east: emergence of Chinese culture

CCIS research was US-centric when American businesses expanded from the United States to other countries where the American culture and the foreign culture meet, and cultural con<sup>fl</sup>icts rise. For this reason, in our review, US culture vs. non-US culture comparisons are still prevalent. We also see a shift from US vs. Western country comparisons to US vs. non-Western country comparisons with China as the focal country (see Fig. 1). By analyzing the timeline of CCIS studies in Table 6, we found that from 2007 to 2009, the research focus was on the emerging economies at the time, such as South Korea, Hong Kong, and India [24,41,59]. Afterward, China vs. other country comparisons have become dominant in CCIS research. Of these, China vs. US comparisons have become a major focus [13,34,47]. Furthermore, some China-speci<sup>fi</sup>c cultural dimensions such as “Guanxi” have emerged as well [20,92]. This emerging China theme re<sup>fl</sup>ects the growing impact of Chinese economy and its rapid di<sup>f</sup>usion of IT/IS. This theme was not among the themes found in the review by Leidner and Kayworth [44].

We foresee this trend of CCIS research will continue as long as Chinese economy continues to grow. With an increase in China’s in-<sup>fl</sup>uence on the world and Chinese enterprises increasingly going broad, IS scholars will also <sup>fi</sup>nd the deep in<sup>fl</sup>uence of Chinese traditional culture on Chinese employees, Chinese enterprises, and socioeconomic structure. As such, Eastern culture, dominated by Chinese culture, will collide and interweave with Western culture dominated by the US culture. As a result, there will be more CCIS research on topics of “China” and “East” marked by Confucianism, Taoism, or Buddhism vs. “US” and “West” strongly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by Aristotelism and Judeo-Christian values. We encourage scholars to go beyond this “traditional” comparison scheme to explore new cultural and subcultural dimensions to capture cultural congruence and collision due to the Internet, social media, and fast information <sup>fl</sup>ow across the globe. Moreover, as abovementioned, we encourage more CCIS research involving African and south American countries. By doing so, researchers may <sup>fi</sup>nd IT/IS related issues and solutions as well as opportunities in these countries for businesses.

## 3.4. Individual culture preference value

The dimension style developed by Hofstede had been dominating for a long time. Dimension-style cultural values can be easily understood and applied to theoretical and practical activities with clear-cut values. However, culture is so complex that it would be arbitrary to view two cultures based on a high vs. low value. Additionally, in dividuals are often espoused to both the native culture and the foreign culture. Therefore, we argue that when a native culture meets a foreign culture and cultural con<sup>fl</sup>icts arise, an individual has to adapt to resolve the con<sup>fl</sup>icts. Through a process of cultural collision and congruence, he/she may reject or accept the foreign cultural values to a degree, while preserving or giving up his/her native cultural stands to some extent. To what extent he/she is going to adapt to the foreign culture depends on various aspects, and the adaption is a dynamic process. Thus, we posit that the cultural value should be considered as a continuous variable rather than a discrete variable at the individual espoused level of both the native culture and the foreign culture. We, therefore, propose the individual culture preference values and the group cultural heterogeneity curves (see the OL curve in Fig. 2) to ex amine the adaptation of the foreign culture and the native culture at the individual and group levels.

The individual culture preference values include the endorsed degree of foreign cultural value and the endorsed degree of native cultural value. A cultural preference survey based on the pairs of native and foreign cultural values may be developed to collect an individual s levels of the endorsed foreign culture and the endorsed native culture, e.g., 0.4 vs. 0.9 on a 0–1 scale. One vs. one on such scale indicates that the individual equally and fully accepts both the foreign culture and the native culture with no preference, while 0 vs. 1 shows that the individual is a thorough nationalist who is not willing to accept the foreign culture. Using the individual culture preference values collected from group members (e.g., employees of an organization), we then can draw the group cultural heterogeneity curves by the construction method of Lorenz curve [46]. As shown in Fig. 2, the OL curve in the box represents the cultural heterogeneity curve constructed from the cumulative percentage values of the ranked frequencies of individual culture preference values of a given culture (native or foreign) in a group (e.g., an organization). If the OL curve perfectly matches the OL line in the left (right) graph, it shows a totally equal distribution of native (foreign) culture preference values, indicating a homogeneous endorsement level of the native (foreign) culture. If the OL curve perfectly matches the OPL line, it illustrates a perfectly unequal distribution of native (foreign) cultural reference values, indicating a heterogeneous endorsement level of the native (foreign) culture. The Gini coe<sup>fi</sup>cient of A/(A + B), where A and B are the areas marked in Fig. 2, can be calculated and used as the group cultural heterogeneity index for a given culture.

Every individual has to adapt to the cultural con<sup>fl</sup>icts they face. We believe that by measuring the individual culture preference values over time, researchers can examine the dynamic change between the acceptance of a native culture and a foreign culture at the individual level. Businesses may use group cultural heterogeneity curves and Gini coe<sup>fi</sup>cients to predict and avoid cultural con<sup>fl</sup>icts. For example, if the right-side OL curve in Fig. 2 skews toward the OLP line with a large Gini coe<sup>fi</sup>cient and a small mean and the left-side OL curve approaches the OL line with a small Gini coe<sup>fi</sup>cient and a large mean, this indicates that employees largely and homogeneously keep their native culture and have low, but di<sup>f</sup>erent levels of acceptance of the foreign culture. With this <sup>fi</sup>nding, the companies operating o<sup>f</sup>shore may foresee cultural con<sup>fl</sup>icts in IT/IS projects and develop more culturally sensitive strategies to handle the con<sup>fl</sup>icts. They may also use group cultural heterogeneity curves to identify groups in a population that are the source of the con<sup>fl</sup>icts.

## 4. Limitations and future research

There are some limitations in this research that may be addressed through future research. As a systematic review, 40 articles published between 2006 and 2016 in eight top-tier IS journals in senior scholars basket were chosen. The source journals have high IF in IS research, and they can ensure the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance and the representation within the IT/IS domain. However, this might be a limitation in our sampling. Future research may address this caveat by using a larger sample to have a more exhaustive search for CCIS research in IS and other closely related academic disciplines such as management and marketing.

Our review focuses on CCIS research in the period from 2006 to 2016. Future research should conduct more comprehensive and holistic reviews based on a longer period. Moreover, this study is only a qualitative work focusing on research trends. Future studies may conduct a quantitative, even a mixed-method research to obtain more insights on CCIS research.

Additionally, we call for more follow-up studies to explore cultural dimensions other than Hofstede’s dimensions, e.g., subculture dimensions and individual espoused culture dimensions, though Hofstede’s culture framework still dominates the CCIS research. We also call for more CCIS research for a broader geographic coverage. Future research may also further explore the individual culture preference values, such as measurements, distribution, and formula.

Researchers’ nationality and cultural background may play a role in CCIS research. For example, researchers may tend to choose the country for CCIS research based on their nationality or culture. Future research may investigate how researchers’ demographics interplay with other factors and impact CCIS research trends.

## 5. Conclusions

The objectives of this study are (1) to provide a systematic literature review on CCIS research in the past 10 years, (2) to show the evolution of cross-cultural con<sup>fl</sup>icts and resolution among di<sup>f</sup>erent users, and (3) to provide a useful roadmap for future research in this area.

In this systematic review, three dimensions were used as follows: (1) context and topics, (2) theoretical lens, and (3) research method and data source. The comprehensive review identi<sup>fi</sup>es three major transitions in CCIS research: (1) from national level to individual level with espoused or contextual cultural values, (2) from corporate users to end users, and (3) from West to East with the emergence of Chinese culture. These three transitions show the changes in research focus and IT/IS impacts on businesses and individual users across cultures in the past 10 years. The review outcomes allow us to point out the current trends in CCIS research and directions for future research. The three transitions identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the current study would be able to bene<sup>fi</sup>t the future CCIS research by avoiding ecological fallacies at the pure national level, thus channeling the research attentions to individual users’ perceived culturalvalues and enhancing the CCIS research with broadened, diverse perspectives.
