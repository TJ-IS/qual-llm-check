# Leveraging IT Capabilities and Competitive Process Capabilities for the Management of Interorganizational Relationship Portfolios

Arun Rai

Center for Process Innovation and Department of Computer Information Systems, Robinson College of Business, Georgia State University, Atlanta, Georgia 30303, arunrai@gsu.edu

Xinlin Tang

Department of Management, College of Business, Florida State University, Tallahassee, Florida 32306, xtang2@cob.fsu.edu

irms are increasingly dependent on external resources and are establishing portfolios of interorganizational relationships (IRs) to leverage external resources for competitive advantage. However, the systems of information technology (IT) and process capabilities that firms should develop to manage IR portfolios dynamically are not well-understood. In order to theorize how key structural IT capabilities (IT integration and IT reconfig uration) and competitive process capabilities (process alignment, partnering flexibility, and offering flexibility) operate as systems of complements, we draw on the competitive dynamics perspective and resource depen dency theory and on the literature for IT business value, interorganizational systems, and interorganizational relationship management. We also theorize how a firm’s IR portfolio moderates the effects of structural IT capa bilities on competitive process capabilities and why a firm’s environmental turbulence moderates the effects of complementary process capabilities on competitive performance. We test our model using survey data from 318 firms in 4 industries. Our results provide broad support for the following: (1) structural IT capabilities and pro cess capabilities operating as a system of complements, (2) the effects of structural IT capabilities on competitive process capabilities being contingent on IR portfolio concentration, and (3) the effects of complementary process capabilities on competitive performance being contingent on environmental turbulence. We discuss the theoretical and practical implications of how firms should develop complementary systems of structural IT capabilities and competitive process capabilities to manage IR portfolios dynamically and leverage external resources.

Key words: competitive dynamics perspective; IT business value; interorganizational relationships; relationship portfolios; competitive performance; structural IT capabilities; competitive process capabilities; complementarities

## Introduction

Interorganizational relationship (IR) management has attracted significant attention both among academics and practitioners because IRs can serve as strategic assets in a firm and can impact its competitive performance (Johnson 1999, Srivastava et al. 1998). Traditionally, alliances have been conceived as ad hoc arrangements for specific needs, leading researchers to focus on understanding the successful management of individual alliances (Johnston et al. 2004, Larson 1992). However, firms are now simultaneously involved in multiple interorganizational relationships or IR portfolios to improve their endowment of external resources and to counter strategic uncertainty (Hoffmann 2007). Accordingly, strategic management scholars have started to examine how IR portfolios can enhance a firm’s competitive position (Lavie 2007). Thus, there is the need to understand the competitive capabilities required to manage IR portfolios dynamically and the information technology (IT) capabilities that are instrumental in this regard.

Past research has identified three process capabilities that impact a firm’s ability to leverage IR portfolios to (re)act competitively. First, a firm’s process capability to coordinate and improve interdependent processes with its IR portfolio (which we refer to as process alignment) enables it to collaborate with partners on competitive actions (Dyer and Singh 1998, Gulati et al. 2000). Second, a firm’s process capability to adapt its IR portfolio (which we refer to as partnering flexibility) enables it to enter into and terminate relationships and change the course of competitive action (Fjeldstad and Haanaes 2001, Malhotra et al. 2007). Third, a firm’s process capability to adjust its product and service offerings in collaboration with its IR portfolio (which we refer to as offering flexibility) enables it to respond to shifts in customer preferences, technological change, competitive rivalry, and global competition (Gosain et al. 2004, Young-Ybarra and Wiersema 1999). Possessing these three competitive process capabilities should help a firm achieve competitive performance, which we define as a firm’s attainment of its objectives in relation to its external environment (Ferrier 2001, Porter 1980b).

Though we have learned that each of the three competitive process capabilities affects a firm’s ability to leverage external resources and manage its IR portfolio, there is a gap in our understanding of how process alignment interacts with process flexibilities (i.e., offering flexibility and partnering flexibility). This is an important void to fill in our understanding for the following reasons: (1) when capabilities are complementary, firms compromise performance by fine-tuning them separately (Milgrom and Roberts 1995); (2) when interactions between capabilities are overlooked, firms increase the risks of partial or failed capability development initiatives (Sinha and Van de Ven 2005); and (3) when complementary capabilities are mistaken as substitutes, firms lose synergies and suffer from suboptimal long-run performance (Milgrom and Roberts 1995). We examine how process alignment and process flexibilities interact so as to address this gap in our knowledge and develop our understanding about the system of competitive process capabilities that firms should establish to manage their IR portfolios. Additionally, past research suggests that environmental turbulence plays an important role in determining the relative impact of different organizational capabilities on competitiveness (Koka and Prescott 2008). However, we have limited understanding on the emphasis that firms should place on process alignment or on partnering and offering flexibilities at different levels of environmental turbulence. This leads to our first two research questions.

Research Question 1. How does a firm’s process alignment interact with its partnering and offering flexibilities to impact its competitive performance?

Research Question 2. How does environmental turbulence moderate the joint effects of process alignment and offering and partnering flexibilities on a firm’s competitive performance?

We have made significant progress in understanding the relationship between IT capabilities and interorganizational process capabilities (Krishnan et al. 2007). Two structural IT capabilities have been identified in the interorganizational systems literature as key enablers of the process capabilities needed to manage IR portfolios successfully. First, a firm’s ability to achieve IT integration (through the timely and idiosyncratic exchange of information with its partners) promotes process alignment and partnering and offering flexibilities through mutual adjustment with its IR portfolio (Dent 2003, Grover and Saeed 2007, Rai et al. 2006). Second, a firm’s ability to reconfigure IT resources (through modular designs and standardized interfaces in applications and processes) promotes the dynamic alignment of processes with its IR portfolio while reducing the costs of switching partners and increasing the firm’s ability to offer products and services flexibly along with its IR portfolio (Gosain et al. 2004).

Though we know that IT integration and IT reconfiguration positively impact the three competitive process capabilities, we have a limited understanding of how they interact to affect these competitive process capabilities. This is an important gap in our understanding because firms risk misguided development of IT capabilities when their interactions are overlooked and because firms risk lost synergies and hampered business performance when complementary IT capabilities are mistaken as substitutes (Milgrom and Roberts 1995). This leads to our next research question.

Research Question 3. How do the two structural IT capabilities (IT integration and IT reconfiguration) interact to impact each of the three competitive process capabilities for IR portfolio management?

Additionally, we have a limited understanding of how the relationships between the two structural IT capabilities and each of the three competitive process capabilities are affected by the level of concentration of a firm’s IR portfolio (i.e., the density of the relationship portfolio in terms of the number of major partners and their relative importance). Indeed, this is an important gap for us to address because the network perspective of competitive dynamics notes that the effects of firm- and relationship-specific characteristics on a firm’s ability to engage in competitive (re)actions are moderated by the density of its partner network (Borgatti and Foster 2003, Brass et al. 2004, Gnyawali and Madhavan 2001). This leads to our final research question.

Research Question 4. How does IR portfolio concentration moderate the relationship between each of the two structural IT capabilities and the three competitive process capabilities?

We draw on the competitive dynamics perspective, resource dependency theory, and the literatures on IT business value, interorganizational systems (IOS), and interorganizational relationship management to develop our model and hypotheses. We used a survey-based methodology to collect data from 318 firms on how they leverage structural IT capabilities and competitive process capabilities to manage their supplier portfolios for major product lines. Our results provide broad support for our model and hypotheses. We contribute to the literature on competitive dynamics and IOS by extending the competitive dynamics perspective to the context of managing IR portfolios. We isolate three types of competitive process capabilities that enable a firm to engage in competitive actions: aligning resources with its IR portfolio, modifying its IR portfolio, and changing the offering of products and services in collaboration with its IR portfolio. We further reveal how environmental turbulence amplifies the returns to a firm’s competitive performance from its joint capabilities to align processes with its IR portfolio and to change the offering of products and services in collaboration with its IR portfolio. We identify how the two structural IT capabilities (IT integration and IT reconfiguration) jointly impact each of the three competitive process capabilities for IR portfolio management, how the capabilities to align processes with IR portfolios and to make changes to IR portfolios operate as complements for competitive performance, and how the capabilities to align processes with IR portfolios and to adjust the offering of products and services in collaboration with IR portfolios also interact as complements for competitive performance. Finally, we indicate how a firm should tilt the balance between IT integration and IT reconfiguration based on the level of its IR portfolio concentration.

## Theory Building and Hypothesis Development

The competitive dynamics perspective is grounded in Schumpeter’s (1934) theory of creative destruction, which frames competition between firms as “an incessant race to get or to keep ahead of one another” (Kirzner 1973, p. 20). In this race, absolute firm performance becomes a less relevant measure of success. Instead, competitive performance is a more relevant measure because it captures the dynamic outcomes from a firm’s actions to achieve greater competitiveness (Ferrier 2001). Awareness, motivation, and capability are the key drivers of a firm’s competitive actions to defend or improve its relative competitive position (Chen 1996, Smith et al. 2001). Awareness is the firm’s proactive attentiveness to information about the competitive environment and entails quickly sensing external challenges and opportunities (Sambamurthy et al. 2003, Zaheer and Zaheer 1997). Motivation is the firm’s intent to make moves against competitors (Chen 1996). Capability is the firm’s physical ability to (re)act with speed to generate customer-driven products and services, which requires the exploitation of the competitive bases of efficiency, flexibility, innovation, and quality through the integration and reconfiguration of resources (Chen 1996, Sambamurthy et al. 2003, Zaheer and Zaheer 1997). Though these three drivers are important for firms to launch competitive (re)actions, we focus on elaborating our understanding of how capabilities—specifically, structural IT capabilities and process capabilities—impact competitive performance together.

Previous studies suggest that a firm’s competitive capabilities are affected by both its internal and external resources. Internally, competitive capabilities emerge through new uses or unexpected configurations of resources (Penrose 1959) and through developing unique capabilities based on experiential learning (Helfat 2000). Externally, competitive capabilities are derived from a firm’s network of partners (Ahuja 2000, Kogut 2000). By establishing interorganizational relationships, firms access external resources that provide complementary capabilities to discover opportunities and respond to the market with customer-driven offerings (Baum et al. 2000, Gulati 1999). These resources that are embedded in partner relationships have emerged as an increasingly important source of a firm’s capabilities to develop, sustain, and renew competitive advantage (McEvily and Marcus 2005).

Given the importance of external resources, our theoretical framework describes a firm’s system of structural IT capabilities and process capabilities to manage external resources and impact its competitive performance (Figure 1). This framework is based on the premise that in today’s business context, it is rare for a firm to be able to launch effective competitive actions independently. Instead, a firm’s competitive actions are embedded in a network of interorganizational relationships (Granovetter 1985, Uzzi 1997) that not only influence its awareness of the competitive context and its intentions to act but also its ability to launch actions against rivals (Chen 1996). Our framework suggests that a firm’s competitive process capabilities to leverage external resources impact its competitive performance and that these process capabilities are enabled by its structural IT capabilities. Further, this framework incorporates the firm’s

IR portfolio concentration as a contextual factor that influences the relationships between the firm’s structural IT capabilities and its competitive process capabilities, and also includes the firm’s environmental turbulence as a contextual factor that influences the competitive performance benefits accrued from its competitive process capabilities.

There is evidence in best practices for the general premise of our theoretical framework that IT capabilities enable firms to develop competitive process capabilities that they can use to leverage external resources for competitive benefits. In general, a firm’s ability to develop competitive process capabilities and launch competitive actions has been shown to correlate with its IT capabilities to connect and collaborate with partners (Chi et al. 2007). Consider the following illustrations: American Airlines used the SABRE system to restructure its channel relationships and increase its competitiveness; Dell used an IT-based direct sales model to coordinate production and distribution across its network of supply partners and emerged as a price leader; and Cisco Systems used its IT-based platform to benefit competitively from both close collaboration and transactional exchange with its partners. Furthermore, by using IT and process capabilities to collaborate closely with Singapore-based Flextronics (a contract manufacturer), Microsoft launched the Xbox in record time. Microsoft streamlined its supply chain processes to reduce costs and Flextronics shifted the Xbox’s supply chain from Mexico and Hungary to China to cut manufacturing costs. This positioned Microsoft to match Sony’s aggressive discounts on the PlayStation 2 and to wrestle 20% of the video game’s market share from Sony by 2003 (Lee 2004).

## The Interaction of Process Alignment and Process Flexibilities

Process alignment provides a firm with the ability to collaborate closely with partners (Subramani 2004). It provides the firm the capability to establish routines and operating procedures to coordinate processes efficiently with its IR portfolio and to learn how to improve these processes. In terms of process dynamics, it reduces information distortion, provides visibility into processes that are distributed across the firm and its partners, facilitates the mutual adjustment of plans and actions, and enables the discovery of how processes can be improved. This tight collaboration with its IR portfolio enables a firm to launch competitive actions with precision and speed. In contrast, process flexibilities reflect the firm’s capability to capture emerging opportunities by adjusting its IR portfolio and its market offerings in collaboration with its IR portfolio (Gosain et al. 2004). In a dynamic environment, firms frequently need to adjust their IR portfolios because of changes in demand and to access valuable knowledge and resources that they themselves cannot replicate in a timely manner (Shapiro and Varian 1999). These process flexibilities position firms to exploit market opportunities because they can react to competitors’ action quickly and launch actions with variety and surprise.

Though both process alignment and process flexibilities impact a firm’s competitive performance, process flexibilities to partner or to offer products and services should each contribute more to performance when a firm is also able to align processes related to these changes with its IR portfolio. The positive effects that offering and partnering flexibilities as well as process alignment have on competitive performance should be mutually reinforcing because high process alignment capabilities should increase the focal firm’s effectiveness in competitive actions when it deploys the changed resources related to either its partners or its offering products and services in conjunction with its partners. These theorized complementarities between process alignment and the two process flexibilities reflect the dynamism that a firm requires to manage its IR portfolios effectively—aligning processes with its IR portfolio, adjusting offerings in collaboration with its partners, adapting its mix of partners, and realigning processes with the changed IR portfolio. Moderating Effects of Environmental Turbulence Environmental turbulence is defined as “general conditions of uncertainty” (Mendelson 2000) and emerges from three key sources (Jap 2001): (a) market turbulence—uncertainty in market demands or rate of change in the composition of customers and their preferences (Jaworski and Kohli 1993); (b) competitive intensity—the number of competitors in the field and their moves (Jap 2001, Schilling and Steensma 2001); and (c) technological change—the frequency of technical breakthroughs (Glazer and Weiss 1993, Jaworski and Kohli 1993). Environmental turbulence is a key variable that has been shown to impact a firm’s competitive performance, strategies, and capabilities. Furthermore, it can lead to the obsolescence of a firm’s capabilities (Burt 2000, Rowley et al. 2000) and influence a firm’s competitive performance (Porter 1980a). Finally, this turbulence can also influence a firm’s strategies and capabilities to manage inteorganizational relationships. For example, a firm is more likely to exploit relationships with existing partners (alliance reinforcing) than to explore relationships with new partners (alliance broadening) when market uncertainty is high (Beckman et al. 2004).

We recognize that environmental turbulence can have a direct effect on competitive performance and competitive process capabilities. As such, we focus on how it moderates the relationships between competitive process capabilities and competitive performance. Studies in strategic management have examined the capabilities that enable firms to leverage their resources and engage in competitive actions in turbulent environments. These studies note that firms require the ability to reconfigure resources (Teece et al. 1997) and the organizational flexibility to respond to technological change and volatile demand (Eisenhardt and Brown 1999, Hitt et al. 1998, Nadler and Tushman 1999). Studies in competitive dynamics have extended this line of work and have concluded that a firm’s ability to both make changes rapidly and execute changes efficiently contribute to its profitability and market share in turbulent environments (Ferrier et al. 1999). These studies’ core argument is that a firm benefits competitively from being able to manage the paradox between exploring opportunities by making changes and exploiting opportunities by efficiently capitalizing on opportunities. Following this logic, we argue that in a turbulent environment, a firm should perform better not only when it is able to make changes to its product and service offerings and its IR portfolios but also when it can implement these changes efficiently. Thus, we formulate the following hypotheses.

Hypothesis 2A (H2A). The positive interaction effect of process alignment and offering flexibility on competitive performance is moderated by environmental turbulence such that the positive interaction effect is further strengthened when environmental turbulence becomes high.

Hypothesis 2B (H2B). The positive interaction effect of process alignment and partnering flexibility on competitive performance is moderated by environmental turbulence such that the positive interaction effect is further strengthened when environmental turbulence becomes high.

## The Interaction of IT Integration and IT Reconfiguration on Competitive Process Capabilities

As the core infrastructure for a firm to transact and collaborate with its partners, the IOS affects the control and coordination mechanisms that a firm uses to manage its IR portfolio (Bensaou and Venkatraman 1995). The IOS literature has examined why and how the IOS structure influences a firm’s process capabilities to leverage external resources and to take competitive action, and it has identified IT integration and IT reconfiguration as the two key structural IT capabilities. IT integration reflects tight linkages between the trading partners’ IT infrastructures and has been identified as a key causal agent of the business value that a firm can realize from its interorganizational relationships (Rai et al. 2006). To achieve IT integration, the focal firm and its partners have to resolve differences in both the syntax and the semantics of the data, reconcile differences in the standards for data exchange and process coordination, and integrate disparate hardware platforms, communication technologies, and applications (Barua et al. 2004). An integrated IT infrastructure enables a firm to share information, coordinate activities, and align processes with its partners (Grover and Saeed 2007). The IT reconfiguration concept, also referred to generically as IT infrastructure flexibility by some researchers (Byrd and Turner 2000, Duncan 1995), is grounded in the literatures on modular systems (Schilling 2000) and platform organizations (Ciborra 1996). Drawing on modularized components and standardized interfaces, IT reconfiguration enables a firm to accommodate new applications and reduces the effort a firm needs to change or recombine resources to support evolving requirements to manage its IR portfolio. These requirements can emerge from a firm’s changing needs to coordinate exchange with its existing partners or with its new partners. Thus, IT reconfiguration can generate offering and partnering flexibilities for a firm by reducing the specificity of IT assets that are required to coordinate exchange with the IR portfolio (Bala and Venkatesh 2007).

We focus on the complementary effects of IT integration and IT reconfiguration because past research has examined their direct effects on process capabilities. IT integration and IT reconfiguration mirror the paradox between close collaboration and rapid adaptation in interorganizational relationships (Gosain et al. 2004). When firms operate in dynamic environments, they need to initiate action and counteraction continuously to capture emerging opportunities and respond to challenges (Lumpkin and Dess

1996). Such actions and counteractions require firms to make ongoing adjustments to processes as well as to align processes dynamically with evolving requirements for competitive action (Sabherwal et al. 2001). Though IT integration enables a firm to align processes with its IR portfolio at a given time, IT reconfiguration enables the firm to adapt processes with its IR portfolio as necessary over time. The two structural IT capabilities should operate as complements and generate synergies for process alignment, which leads us to hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 3A (H3A). IT integration and IT reconfiguration positively interact to impact process alignment.

Offering flexibility is the firm’s capability to respond to fluctuations in the volume and type of products the market demands. Achieving offering flexibility requires mutual adjustment between a firm and its IR portfolio in terms of their respective plans for sourcing, production, distribution, and logistics (Lee 2004). Such mutual adjustment should be facilitated by IT integration because it enables a firm to exchange rich information in real time (e.g., changes in plans for production or distribution) with its IR portfolio (Simchi-Levi et al. 2008). This adjustment should also be enhanced when IT resources are reconfigured and when IT functionality is extended rapidly to meet the emergent collaboration needs for scaling offerings and introducing new products and services. IT integration and IT reconfiguration should, therefore, be mutually reinforcing and should operate as complements with respect to offering flexibility, which leads us to hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 3B (H3B). IT integration and IT reconfiguration positively interact to impact offering flexibility.

Partnering flexibility is influenced by bridging and bonding capabilities. In the context of innovation alliances, Tiwana (2008) demonstrated that a firm’s bonding capabilities to collaborate closely with partners complements its bridging capabilities to establish ties with new partners. He concluded that bonding capabilities provide the complementary mechanism to integrate the diverse repertoire of skills and expertise that are accessible by bridging capabilities. IT reconfiguration provides a firm with bridging capabilities to accommodate the heterogeneity in IT and process standards across its base of existing and new partners, and also to recombine IT resources to support emergent collaboration requirements with its IR portfolio (Gosain et al. 2004). In contrast, IT integration provides a firm with the bonding capabilities needed to tie in its systems with the systems of its key partners and to exchange and aggregate information across these systems. When a firm develops both IT integration and IT reconfiguration capabilities, it should be positioned better to partner flexibly than when it develops one or the other alone. This leads us to hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 3C (H3C). IT integration and IT reconfiguration positively interact to impact partnering flexibility.

## The Interactions of IR Portfolio Concentration and Structural IT Capabilities

IR portfolio concentration is the density of a firm’s IR portfolio and represents the number of major partners and their relative importance in the IR portfolio. This concentration is high when a focal firm purchases a large proportion of its materials from a small group of suppliers or sells a large proportion of its products to a small group of customers. We draw on resource dependence theory and the literature on interorganizational knowledge sharing to develop the logic behind the moderating effects of IR portfolio concentration on the relationships between structural IT capabilities and competitive process capabilities.

Resource dependence theory states that a firm’s resource dependence on its partners and the pressures and constraints that arise from this dependence influence the focal firm’s plausible choices and actions (Pfeffer and Salancik 2003). The theory identifies the sources of these pressures and constraints that influence the resource dependence among firms (Burt 1983, Pfeffer and Salancik 2003). On one hand, resource dependence can increase with industry concentration because a few large and dominant players may control access to resources (Pfeffer and Salancik 2003). On the other hand, behavioral interdependence can increase with the concentration of a firm’s IR portfolio because a higher proportion of the firm’s transactions are concentrated within a smaller set of firms (Burt 1983, Thompson 1967). Some firms such as Cisco Systems have reduced the number of partners in their

IR portfolios (i.e., increased their IR portfolio concentration) to achieve high behavioral interdependence with their partners and to manage the dependence effectively (Rai et al. 2008, Simchi-Levi et al. 2007). Unlike the dependence that emanates from industry concentration and the dominance of players that have been widely examined in the strategic management literature, the behavioral interdependence that emanates from IR portfolio concentration has been less studied and is thus our focus.

As high IR portfolio concentration increases behavioral interdependence, it increases the need for a firm and its partners to learn about each other’s business processes and it also influences the dynamics of the relationships between a firm and its partners (Burt 1983, Pfeffer and Salancik 2003). Relative to a firm with low IR portfolio concentration, a firm with high IR portfolio concentration needs to coordinate a smaller number of separate social units, which should promote a high level of communication and the exchange of contextual knowledge (Ahuja 2000, Pfeffer and Salancik 2003). Because knowledge about the context in which IT artifacts are embedded influences their effective use (Kohli and Grover 2008), high IR portfolio concentration should create favorable conditions for a firm to leverage its IT capabilities for process integration. Accordingly, a firm with a concentrated IR portfolio should be better positioned to leverage IT integration (e.g., real-time information exchange) for coordinating and optimizing processes with its IR portfolio as well as to leverage IT reconfiguration (e.g., module recombination) for continually refining the processes that it uses to coordinate its IR portfolio. This leads us to the following hypotheses.

Hypothesis 4A (H4A). The relationship between IT integration and process alignment is moderated by IR portfolio concentration such that the relationship is stronger when IR portfolio concentration is higher.

Hypothesis 4B (H4B). The relationship between IT reconfiguration and process alignment is moderated by IR portfolio concentration such that the relationship is stronger when IR portfolio concentration is higher.

As discussed earlier, a firm with a concentrated IR portfolio should be better positioned to develop contextual knowledge about its partners’

IT resources and processes for production and distribution (Dyer and Nobeoka 2000, Nishiguchi and Beaudet 1998). It should also have a better understanding of its partners’ plans for production and distribution and how its partners’ plans impact its plans for offering products and services. Having such contextual knowledge about its partners should provide a firm with important complementary resources to leverage IT integration for offering flexibility. For example, some firms such as Cisco Systems have developed significant knowledge about their suppliers’ processes and have used this knowledge to leverage their data integration and interoperability capabilities to collaborate with partners when launching new products and when scaling the volume of offerings based on changes in customer demand (Rai et al. 2008, Simchi-Levi et al. 2007). Such contextual knowledge should also provide a firm with the complementary resources needed to leverage IT reconfiguration for offering flexibility. Consider the following example: Toyota’s deep understanding of the plans and capabilities of its concentrated supply base enabled it to avoid devastating results because it was able to reroute the production of valves when its only source of valve supply was destroyed in the Aisin Seiki fire (Watts 2003). With knowledge about its suppliers’ systems and processes, Toyota was able to recombine its technology and resources to reallocate the production and distribution of valves to other suppliers and to respond to the sudden shift in supply for an essential component. Based on our discussion, we propose the following two hypotheses.

Hypothesis 5A (H5A). The relationship between IT integration and offering flexibility is moderated by IR portfolio concentration such that the relationship is stronger when IR portfolio concentration is higher.

Hypothesis 5B (H5B). The relationship between IT reconfiguration and offering flexibility is moderated by IR portfolio concentration such that the relationship is stronger when IR portfolio concentration is higher.

Though behavioral interdependence in a concentrated IR portfolio facilitates coordination and knowledge sharing among partners, behavioral interdependence, closely coordinated processes, and extensive knowledge sharing also increase the switching cost of partners (Uzzi 1997). High switching costs of partners should, in turn, negatively affect the relationship between IT integration and partnering flexibility (i.e., make it more difficult for a firm to deploy IT integration when adjusting its relationship portfolio) (Tanriverdi et al. 2007). Though IT integration enables a firm to achieve visibility into partners’ processes (and to be aware of performance problems by monitoring performance relative to service level agreements), systems that are tightly integrated with partners also increase technological switching costs. As a result, total switching costs should be amplified when both IT integration and IR portfolio concentration are high, which would constrain a firm from adjusting its IR portfolios. In contrast, IT reconfiguration should be more important for partnering flexibility in concentrated IR portfolios than in sparse IR portfolios because it provides a firm with the plugand-play orientation required to connect with partners’ systems and reduces the switching costs of IT resources (Zhu et al. 2006), which should lead IT reconfiguration to have a stronger effect on partnering flexibility when IR portfolio concentration is higher. Accordingly, we formulate these two hypotheses.

Hypothesis 6A (H6A). The relationship between IT integration and partnering flexibility is moderated by IR portfolio concentration such that the relationship is weaker when IR portfolio concentration is higher.

Hypothesis 6B (H6B). The relationship between IT reconfiguration and partnering flexibility is moderated by IR portfolio concentration such that the relationship is stronger when IR portfolio concentration is higher.

## Control Variables

We control for the effects of industry, firm size, and average relationship length of the IR portfolio on a firm’s competitive process capabilities and on its competitive performance. We briefly explain our rationale for the inclusion of these control variables. Firm performance can also be affected by industry structure (Porter 1980a), dominance of a firm (Zaheer and Venkatraman 1994), and relationship strategy (to pursue short- or long-term partnerships) (Dyer and Singh 1998, Patnayakuni et al. 2006). Accordingly, we include industry membership, firm size, and average relationship length of the IR portfolio as control variables to account for these plausible effects on competitive performance. These variables also tap into factors that affect a firm’s dependence on external resources and, consequently, on a firm’s orientation toward the three competitive process capabilities. We control for the effect of industry membership on competitive process capabilities because a firm’s dependence on external resources can increase with industry concentration and the presence of few dominant firms in a particular industry (Pfeffer and Salancik 2003). We account for the effect of firm size on competitive process capabilities because large firms may be less dependent on external resources through greater availability of internal resources (Pfeffer and Salancik 2003). We also control for average relationship length of the IR portfolio because long-term partnerships may lead a firm to be more dependent on its IR portfolio than short-term partnerships (Ganesan 1994).

## Construct Measurement and

## Questionnaire Development

We developed measures to collect survey data on a firm’s competitive performance for a major product line and on its IT and process capabilities to manage its supplier portfolio (Appendix A). Based on the criteria identified by Jarvis et al. (2003) to determine whether a construct’s measures are formative or reflective, we specified the measures for all constructs (except IR portfolio concentration and the control variables) to be formative. Jarvis et al. (2003) note that a measure is formative when (i) the direction of causality is from the indicators to the construct, (ii) the indicators are not interchangeable, (iii) the indicators do not necessarily covary, and (iv) the nomological net of indicators is expected to differ. These conditions are met in the case of our measures.

Competitive performance is a firm’s ability to capture market share, remain profitable, keep growing, and be innovative and cost-efficient in comparison to its major competitors (Drew 1997, Lee 2003). Respondents were asked to evaluate their major product lines’ performances relative to that of competitors on the following five aspects: market share, profitability, growth, innovativeness, and cost leadership. Their responses were obtained on an 11-point scale with anchors from low (0%) to high (100%). Though self-assessed organizational performance scales may be criticized for their validity, these subjective scales have merit because objective indicators often cannot solicit a high level of specificity for industry, time horizon, or economic conditions. Moreover, selfreported measures are appropriate in our context because we are interested in the competitive performance of a major product line for which objective measures are hard to obtain. In such scenarios, subjective measures are suitable proxies for objective measures (Wall et al. 2004) and empirical evidence suggests that they are valid (Tallon and Kraemer 2007). To alleviate concerns about validity further, we took two additional steps. First, we captured two firm-level performance measures (i.e., percentage of market share and margin of profitability) using ratio scales. These two measures were used to triangulate the respondents’ assessments of the major product lines’ competitive performances. Second, we collected archival data about the firm’s annual revenue and triangulated it against the self-reported sales revenue for 2005. The results of these triangulations are reported in the section on common methods bias and provide evidence of the validity of our subjective measure for competitive performance.

With the exception of competitive performance, we measured all multi-item constructs on seven-point Likert Scales ranging from one (strongly disagree) to seven (strongly agree). To measure IT integration and IT reconfiguration, we asked the respondents to focus on the business-to-business (B2B) digital platform, which was defined as the base of IT capabilities to support information exchange with the portfolio of suppliers for the major product line. The definition of the B2B digital platform was specified in the survey prior to the questions for IT integration and IT reconfiguration. We chose to focus on the B2B platform (as opposed to a specific application or technology) for the following reasons: (1) an IT platform is a key enabler for multisided and multilateral processes (such as those required for IR portfolios) (Ciborra 1996), (2) implementing a B2B platform involves the substantial commitment of monetary and expertiserelated resources, (3) the life cycle for a B2B platform is longer than for an individual B2B application, and (4) the B2B platform has to confront higher uncertainty related to changes in both the technology and the supplier portfolio. Consequently, the structural properties of the B2B digital platform are likely to influence the range of a firm’s responses to manage its supplier portfolio dynamically, which leads to the B2B platform having a broader impact on the competitive performance of the product line than an individual B2B application alone.

IT integration was measured using four items that assess the ease with which the B2B digital platform can perform the following: transfer data between a firm and its suppliers (Goodhue and Wybo 1992), connect a firm’s systems with its suppliers’ systems, which allows for the sharing of real-time information between a firm and its suppliers (Bhatt 2000), and combine information across different suppliers to support decision making (Markus 2000).

IT reconfiguration was measured using four items to capture the relevant properties that we identified based on our extensive literature review. One item measured asset specificity because platforms with low asset specificity can be easily repurposed and do not lose much value if a relationship is terminated (Ciborra 1996, Young-Ybarra and Wiersema 1999). A second item measured the platform’s potential to grow to accommodate new applications or functions as necessary (Fichman 2004, Taudes et al. 2000). A third item measured the modularity of the platform architecture because modular architectures enable components to be disaggregated and recombined into new configurations, thereby giving flexibility to the system as a whole (Schilling and Steensma 2001). With modular architectures, adaptation at the subsystem level becomes easier because architectural constraints from certain parts of the system are integrated into the design of other parts (Galunic and Eisenhardt 2001). The fourth and final item measured the standardization of the interfaces, which is an indispensable aspect of modular design and makes the combination of components from different sources possible (Malhotra et al. 2005).

The four items for process alignment measured the firm’s capability to coordinate interdependency across suppliers, to bring visibility to processes and jointly optimize them, and to handle exceptions and errors in a timely manner (Malone and Crowston 1994, Subramani and Venkatraman 2003). Offering flexibility was measured using two items that are similar to those used by Gosain et al. (2004). These items assess the ability of the focal firm’s process to handle changes in volume and to phase in and phase out product and service offerings in collaboration with suppliers. The measure for partnering flexibility captured the focal firm’s capability to change its supplier portfolio. Three items were used to measure the firm’s process capabilities to establish new relationships, to replace current relationship with new ones, and to exit relationships when necessary (Gosain et al. 2004, Young-Ybarra and Wiersema 1999).

IR portfolio concentration was measured using the Herfindahl index,<sup>1</sup> which is based on the percentage of purchases from the firm’s top four suppliers. Because of the lack of objective measures for environmental turbulence at the product line level, four perceptual measures were adapted from Selnes and Sallis (2003) to capture the pace of customer and competitor changes, the difficulty to forecast demand, and the intensity of technological breakthroughs.

The initial questionnaire was refined in three steps as described next.

1. Two-Stage Q-Sorting. As suggested by Moore and Benbasat (1991), two-step Q-sorting is useful to determine if (1) all facets of a construct are measured (i.e., content validity) and (2) the measures for a construct belong together (i.e., convergent validity) and are distinguishable from the measures of other constructs (i.e., discriminant validity). Our eight sorters were Ph.D. students from the college of business at a large university in the southeastern United States. In the two rounds of sorting, they correctly classified 90% and 96% of the items into the intended constructs. These results suggest good quality measures.

2. Peer Review. The resulting questionnaire was peer reviewed by a panel of 11 academics with expertise in interorganizational relationships. They assessed content validity, format, appearance, and organization. In addition, five practitioners who were involved in managing IR portfolios for a major product line at their respective firms reviewed the questionnaire and commented on its content validity, terminology, clarity of instructions, and response formats. Based on their comments, some adjustments were made to the questionnaire.

3. Pilot Test. The instrument was pretested with 21 procurement professionals to assess if the measures sufficiently captured variances in the constructs. This process resulted in the refinement of some items and instructions.

## Data Collection

We tested our model using data that we collected in fall 2006 on the supplier relationship portfolio for business units’ major product lines. Our sampling frame included firms from four industries that have a broad presence in the U.S. economy (automotive equipment, chemicals, electronic equipment, and industrial equipment industries). We followed the key informant approach to collect data from one procurement professional at each organization because executives in these roles were most likely to be knowledgeable about the items dealt with in our survey (Phillips and Bagozzi 1986). Respondents were first asked to identify the major product lines (ones that account for significant portions of their firms’ total sales/revenue) and were then asked to answer the questions related to those. Because of the importance of the major product lines, firms are likely to treat their suppliers as strategic resources and to develop IT and process capabilities to manage those relationships.

To ensure the quality of the data, we contracted a market research company (e-Rewards) to access its prequalified base of business panel members.<sup>2</sup> Email invitations stating the purpose of the study were sent by the market research company to its panel members. ## Safeguards Against and Assessment of Common Methods Bias

We adopted four strategies to safeguard against and evaluate the presence of common method bias.

1. We applied the following procedural safeguards: developed concise and clear items, used different scale types, changed scale endpoints for independent and dependent variables, and controlled scale length (Podasakoff et al. 2003).

2. We triangulated the data by using different sources and scales as well as by comparing related measures. In addition to measuring each aspect of competitive performance at the major product line level on an 11-point scale, we captured two of the measures (market share and profitability) at the firm level through ratio scales. Significant correlations were observed between the measures at the product line and firm levels (market share for the product line and the firm: r <sub>=</sub> 0317, p < 001; profitability for the product line and the firm: $r = 0 . 2 4 1 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ In addition, each firm’s 2005 sales revenue that was provided by the respondents correlated strongly (r <sub>=</sub> 0692, p < 001) with the archival revenue information of the firms collected by e-Rewards when it recruited its panel.

3. We used Harmon’s one-factor test to assess common method variance (Podasakoff and Organ 1986). As expected, seven factors with eigenvalues >1 were extracted and collectively accounted for 74.8% of the variance in the data, with the first factor accounting for 30.2% of the variance. These findings suggest that common method bias is not a major concern.

4. We incorporated a method factor in the measurement model to evaluate the variance explained by substantive factors and the method factor (Podasakoff et al. 2003). On average, the substantive constructs explained 85% of the variance across the measures while the method factor explained only 0.4%. Taken together, we conclude that common method bias is not significant in our data.

Construct validity determines if the operationalized indicators actually measure the concepts that are intended to be measured (Bagozzi 1980). Convergent validity and discriminant validity are the two aspects of construct validity that are typically assessed postdata collection. Our focus was on discriminant validity because formative measures do not necessarily have to covary (unlike reflective measures that have to covary and must exhibit high internal consistency) (Jarvis et al. 2003). We obtained initial evidence of discriminant validity because the sorters in the two-stage Q-sorting process placed over 90% and 96% of the items at each stage, respectively, into the correct construct categories (Bollen and Lennox 1991, Rossiter 2002). With the collected data, we checked the patterns of item-to-construct correlation and item correlations with other constructs (Hair et al. 1998, Rozeboom 1979). We computed a weighted score for each construct using estimated item weights and evaluated the correlations among the items and construct scores, which is consistent with recommendations on how to evaluate the discriminant validity of formative measures (Diamantopoulos and Siguaw 2006, Petter et al. 2007) (see the Online Supplement, Appendix A). The items correlated more strongly with their constructs’ scores than with the other constructs’ scores, which provides evidence of discriminant validity. Cumulatively, the results suggest that our measures exhibit satisfactory validity. Following the validation of the measures, we computed the unit means of the items for each construct and used them as construct scores for hypotheses testing.

## Test of Hypotheses

We employed moderated regression analysis to test the hypotheses, all of which specify interactions.<sup>5</sup>

For the regression models, we assessed if the error terms followed a normal distribution, which is important for finite samples (Greene 2000). Based on the Shapiro-Wilks W test, our sample does not violate the normality assumption. For each regression model, the VIFs are well below 4.0, which suggests no harmful multicollinearity. The results of the post hoc power analysis suggest that the statistical power for each regression model is above 0.8 when the Type I error is 0.05 (Cohen 1988). Our sample size of 318 reduces the possibility of very erroneous coefficient estimates, which can occur when sample sizes are less than 100 (Starbuck 2006). Finally, in all regression models, we used a heteroskedasticity robust variance estimator for the robustness of the significance tests of estimates (Cohen 1988). As per the procedures for moderated regression analysis, we entered the variables in the following sequence: control variables (Model 1), direct effects (Model 2), two-way interactions between process alignment and each of the two process flexibilities (Model 3), two-way interactions between environmental turbulence and the process capabilities (Model 4), and three-way interactions among environmental turbulence, process alignment, and each of the two process flexibilities (Model 5). Table 4 presents the results.

H1A and H1B relate to competitive performance because of the complementarities of process capabilities. Model 3 (Table 4) includes the interaction terms of process alignment and each of the two process flexibilities. Both interaction terms are significant and positive, which suggests that the positive effects of process alignment and each of the two process flexibilities on competitive performance are mutually reinforcing (the Online Supplement, Appendix B, Figure B-1 shows these effects graphically). These findings support H1, which states that process alignment and the two process flexibilities positively interact to affect competitive performance.

H2A and H2B predict three-way interaction effects among process alignment, each of the two process flexibilities, and environmental turbulence on competitive performance. The interaction effect of process alignment <sub>×</sub> offering flexibility <sub>×</sub> environment turbulence on competitive performance is positive and significant (Model 5, Table 4), which indicates that the positive interaction effect of process alignment and offering flexibility on competitive performance is amplified when environmental turbulence is high (see the Online Supplement, Appendix B, Figure B-2). To probe the moderating effects of environmental turbulence further, we employed cluster analysis to derive meaningful clusters from the three process capabilities and then compared the competitive performance means of the clusters at different levels of environmental turbulence. These results also suggest that process alignment and the two process flexibilities are mutually reinforcing and complementary in terms of their effects on competitive performance and that these process complementarities increase in uncertain environments. ## Discussion

The competitive dynamics perspective has been applied to examine various issues related to a firm’s competitiveness such as competitive tension (Chen et al. 2007), multipoint competition (Baum and Korn 1996), competitive aggressiveness (Ferrier 2001), and competition in international markets (Yu et al. 2009). However, most studies have focused on firms’ internal resources and capabilities to take action against their rivals. Our study is one of the first that goes beyond the firm boundary and focuses on external resources and a firm’s capabilities to leverage those resources, and examines how a firm’s capabilities to manage its IR portfolio enable it to take action that enhances its competitive performance. ## Theoretical Implications

We extend the competitive dynamics perspective by adding to our understanding of (1) how competitive process capabilities can position a firm to leverage its IR portfolio dynamically and (2) IT capabilities’ role in establishing these process capabilities. Specifically, we identify four major theoretical implications that emerge from our work on which we now elaborate.

First, we extend the competitive dynamics perspective by developing the logic of how IT and process capabilities enable a firm to leverage external resources to re(act) competitively and improve competitive performance. The literature on interorganizational relationships has discussed how process alignment and process flexibility contribute to a firm’s competitive performance (Dyer and Singh 1998, Young-Ybarra and Wiersema 1999), but it has not examined their underlying dynamics in terms of competitive performance. We draw on this literature to isolate three competitive process capabilities (process alignment, partnering flexibility, and offering flexibility) needed to manage IR portfolios and develop our understanding on how they dynamically impact competitive performance. Our results suggest that for IR portfolio management, process alignment complements process flexibilities to provide a firm with the dynamism to align processes with its current IR portfolio, adjust its mix of product and service offerings as well as its mix of partners in its IR portfolio, and realign processes with its adjusted IR portfolio.

Indeed, the importance of such dynamic alignment is consistent with the literature on IT and strategic alignment, which notes that alignment is an ongoing process rather than a static state (Sabherwal et al. 2001). By surfacing the complementarities of process alignment with offering and partnering flexibilities, we safeguard against the misperception that these process capabilities are substitutes, demonstrate that process flexibilities and process alignment are mutually reinforcing in terms of their influence on competitive performance, and conclude that a firm should develop these process capabilities as a system of complements to leverage its IR portfolio strategically. Our findings suggest that the awareness-motivationcapability (AMC) framework that has guided a significant amount of the strategic management research on competitive dynamics should be elaborated on to include a firm’s complementary process capabilities for managing its external resources.

Second, we elaborate on the competitive dynamics perspective by examining how competitive process capabilities interact in different environments. Given the rich discussion in the strategy literature on why firm capabilities should be contingent on environmental characteristics (Eisenhardt and Martin 2000), we were prompted to isolate the critical complements of competitive process capabilities in turbulent environments. Our results revealed that the complementary relationship between process alignment and offering flexibility gains importance for competitive advantage when a firm operates in turbulent environments. We know from past research that a firm can respond to environmental turbulence by developing flexibility that enables it to increase the variety of its competitive actions (Eisenhardt and Brown 1999, Hitt et al. 1998). However, to outperform its peers, a firm also needs to align processes that facilitate the mutual adjustment and the efficient execution of activities in collaboration with partners (Ferrier et al. 1999). Jointly, the capabilities to launch and execute a variety of competitive actions related to product-market offerings collaboratively with partners can enable a firm to gain competitive advantage in the often-small windows of opportunity available in turbulent environments.

In contrast, we did not find the complementary relationship between process alignment and partnering flexibility to gain importance in turbulent environments. A possible explanation for this finding is that new partnerships involve greater uncertainty for the focal firm relative to its existing partnerships (Lavie and Rosenkopf 2006). It takes time for a firm to stabilize new partnerships and streamline interdependent processes with new partners. However, in highly turbulent environments, the window of opportunity for a firm to take competitive action is very short, making it difficult for a firm to extract additional marginal benefits from the complementary relationship between process alignment and partnering flexibility.

Third, we connect the competitive dynamics perspective and the IOS literature by developing the logic behind why complementary structural IT capabilities enable the process capabilities needed for competitive (re)actions. Though there has been progress in our understanding of the organizational assimilation of structural IT capabilities (Bala and Venkatesh 2007, Teo et al. 2003) and of the impact of IT capabilities on interfirm process capabilities (Gosain et al. 2004, Rai et al. 2006), there has been limited progress in uncovering the complementarities of IT capabilities that enable a firm to manage its IR portfolio dynamically. Overall, the significant positive interaction effects of IT integration and IT reconfiguration on process alignment, offering flexibility, and partnering flexibility support our theoretical position that the individual effects of these IT capabilities are enhanced when they are pursued together. Our findings challenge the traditional viewpoint that IT integration and IT reconfiguration are contradictory and that firms should pick resources for one or the other in order to develop either IT integration or IT reconfiguration. As complements, these two structural IT capabilities are an indispensable part of a technological system that needs to be established to support the dynamic management of IR portfolios. Recent developments in open standards, modular design, and service-oriented architecture make it possible for firms to balance the contradictory requirements of IT integration and IT reconfiguration. By establishing both of these IT capabilities, firms can enjoy the benefits of “electronic integration” and reduce the risks of “rigidity traps” (Bharadwaj 2000).

Fourth, and finally, we extend both the competitive dynamics perspective and the IOS literature by determining the changes in the marginal returns of the two structural IT capabilities on the competitive process capabilities at different levels of IR portfolio concentration. Our findings provide compelling evidence that IR portfolio concentration is an important contextual variable that influences the marginal benefits of the competitive process capabilities that firms garner from IT integration and IT reconfiguration. Interestingly, our results reveal differential moderating effects of IR portfolio concentration on the relationships between each of the two structural IT capabilities and the three competitive process capabilities. We found that IR portfolio concentration increases the marginal benefits of IT reconfiguration for all three competitive process capabilities while it decreases the marginal benefits of IT integration for these capabilities.

Our results support the role of switching costs in determining the marginal effects of structural IT capabilities on partnering flexibility at different levels of IR portfolio concentration, which is consistent with our expectations. IT reconfiguration becomes more valuable when IR portfolio concentration is high because its ability to lower the switching cost of IT resources has an added premium in this context where switching costs are high. In contrast, the marginal benefit of IT integration for partnering flexibility decreases when IR portfolio concentration is high because the technical costs to integrate systems with partners and the behavioral interdependence with partners have mutually reinforcing effects on the switching costs of partners.

Our findings are mixed for the remaining four hypotheses about the moderating effects of IR portfolio concentration on the relationships between structural IT capabilities and process alignment as well as offering flexibility. Our premise for these four hypotheses was that behavioral interdependence should create the conditions for a firm to develop complementary knowledge about its partners’ technology and processes, which should enhance the effect of each of the structural IT capabilities on process alignment and offering flexibility. Though this logic was supported by the results for IT reconfiguration (IT reconfiguration <sub>×</sub> IR portfolio concentration had significant positive effects on process alignment and offering flexibility), it was not supported by the results for IT integration (IT integration IR portfolio concentration had significant negative effects on process alignment and offering flexibility).

We believe that two mechanisms (the use of alternative channels and partners’ abilities to constrain a firm’s leverage of IT integration capabilities) can reduce the marginal benefits of IT integration for both process alignment and offering flexibility as the concentration of a firm’s IR portfolio increases. First, when a firm deals with a small number of partners, it is more likely to deploy multiple communication channels that also include rich face-to-face channels for information exchange (e.g., dedicated account managers, structured periodic reviews, steering committees, and task forces) and the transfer of complex information with partners (Coleman 1990, Dyer and Hatch 2006). Such channels can be more effective than IT-based channels when it comes to dealing with ambiguous problems and information and negotiating complex situations (Daft and Lengel 1986, Rai et al. 2009). For instance, UPS, which is known for its ability to establish digital connections with its suppliers and customers, also uses other channels (e.g., account managers and quarterly business review meetings involving relevant personnel from operations, technology, and marketing from both UPS and the partner firm) to manage its strategic relationships (Lewis et al. 2007). These other channels are used to evaluate the relationship’s goals and objectives, discover and discuss concerns, and evaluate opportunities. UPS has found these channels to be useful for sharing contextual information that can inform decisions on aligning processes and modifying offerings to address the changing requirements of its strategic customers such as Hewlett-Packard. Similarly, Toyota embeds its personnel in the facilities of its major suppliers and vice versa to exchange contextual information on how activities and resources can be coordinated better across the relationship and how product offerings can be modified jointly in response to changes in market conditions (Dyer and Hatch 2006). Second, a concentrated IR portfolio may be effective at constraining a firm’s behavior when it is deemed opportunistic by the major partners. High behavioral interdependence in concentrated IR portfolios can position these partners to constrain a firm’s initiatives in leveraging IT integration for process alignment or offering flexibility if they abstain in their cooperation or threaten sanctions. Because leveraging

IT integration for process alignment or offering flexibility can require relationship-specific changes not only in the firm’s business processes but also in its partners’ business processes and technologies, a lack of cooperation by major partners can reduce the benefits that the firm derives from IT integration (Bala and Venkatesh 2007). For example, a focal firm’s initiative to leverage its IT integration capabilities (e.g., radiofrequency identification-based visibility) to align processes more closely or to launch products more collaboratively with its suppliers can be constrained by major suppliers. These suppliers can refuse to make the necessary changes to their processes and technology or threaten sanctions (e.g., reallocation of their business to other firms) because they view the firm’s IT integration initiative as opportunistic or establishing unfavorable partnering parameters (e.g., loss of control on key resources and decision rights). We suggest that both of these mechanisms can operate in concentrated IR portfolios and can reduce the marginal benefits that a firm realizes from IT integration for process alignment and offering flexibility.

## Practical Implications

Firms should recognize that external resources impact their competitive performance and also realize that leveraging external resources requires a system of IT and process capabilities to manage their IR portfolios dynamically. They need to direct managerial attention to developing a complementary system of capabilities and not limit attention to individual IT and process capabilities. They should not view process alignment as the desired capability for static environments and process flexibilities as the desired capability for turbulent environments. Instead, they need to recognize that they can increase competitive performance in both types of environments by reinforcing partnering flexibility and process alignment and offering flexibility and process alignment. They stand to gain more in turbulent environments, which are characterized by rapid changes in customer preferences and technologies, when they develop the complementary capabilities of offering flexibility and process alignment. These complementary capabilities are especially valuable in this context because they enable firms to collaborate with their partners to adjust offerings efficiently.

Senior information systems managers should detect misperceptions that IT integration and IT reconfiguration should be managed as independent or substitute capabilities and should remedy these misperceptions by educating managers that the two structural IT capabilities should be managed as complements. Developing the complementarity between IT integration and IT reconfiguration is nontrivial and will require significant investments and initiative coordination. For instance, firms should not just structurally separate the function of standardizing processes and modularizing enterprise architecture from the function of integrating technologies and processes with their IR portfolios. Instead, they need to articulate a vision and establish the organizational mechanisms to coordinate the activities of these functions (e.g., communication processes and job rotation to exchange knowledge between the personnel responsible for evaluating process standards and developing enterprise architecture and those responsible for implementing solutions to manage the IR portfolio).

Once developed, the complementary structural IT capabilities will give firms the ability to establish linkages with new partners as well as to extend collaboration with existing partners, which will be hard for competitors to imitate. Firms should consider the structure of their IR portfolios when determining the relative emphasis that they should place on IT integration and IT reconfiguration. Though firms with concentrated IR portfolios may face partner expectations and develop managerial tendencies to make investments in IT integration, they should factor the role of alternative channels (other than IT systems) to exchange complex and ambiguous information with major partners. They need to safeguard against overinvesting in IT integration because these other channels may be more effective for such exchanges. They should also evaluate how the marginal benefits from IT integration to adjust product offerings and realign process risks can be reduced if relationship-specific complementary changes to processes or technologies are not made by the major partners or if sanctions are imposed by major partners in opposition to proposed IT integration initiatives. In contrast, firms with concentrated IR portfolios should have better knowledge of their partners’ IT infrastructure and over time may be able to influence their partners’ decisions with regard to IT and process standards, which should prove valuable to leverage IT reconfiguration for process capabilities. These firms should also be positioned better to adapt process standards to interface with their partners’ systems, establish middleware to enhance IT reconfigurability, and promote the assimilation of process standards among partners. By developing the capability to reconfigure IT, firms can reduce the partner specificity of IT resources, which should increase partnering flexibility in a context where such flexibility is likely to be rare.

## Limitations and Future Research

We identify some key limitations of our work and opportunities for future research. First, though we drew on the competitive dynamics perspective as the umbrella theoretical framework and assessed competitive process capabilities, we did not directly measure competitive actions. It would be useful to enhance and test our model with data on competitive actions, even if such data are collected in specific industries and contexts. Second, though we collected data from four industries that reflect a broad presence in the U.S. economy, they were all from the manufacturing sector. Our model should be tested in other industries including services to examine the generalizability of our findings. Third, though we evaluated the moderating role of IR portfolio concentration, we did not evaluate how other network properties such as network density, firm centrality, structural equivalence, and firm autonomy moderate the relationship between structural IT capabilities and competitive process capabilities. Thus, this would be a useful avenue to extend our work.

## Conclusions

Our study expands the competitive dynamics perspective and the IOS literature by integrating them to develop our understanding of the role of IT and process capabilities in leveraging external resources for competitive advantage. It informs how structural IT capabilities and process capabilities should be seen as complements to manage IR portfolios for competitive advantage. We find that process alignment and process flexibilities (partnering and offering)

amplify each other’s effects for competitive performance when they are pursued together and that the marginal benefits of the complementarity between process alignment and offering flexibility is enhanced in turbulent environments. We also find that IT integration and IT reconfiguration interact to enable the three competitive process capabilities. However, the marginal benefit from IT integration for competitive process capabilities decreases with an increase in IR concentration, and the marginal benefit from IT reconfiguration for competitive process capabilities increases with an increase in IR concentration. These findings provide insights into how firms should develop structural IT capabilities and competitive process capabilities to manage their IR portfolios for competitive advantage.
