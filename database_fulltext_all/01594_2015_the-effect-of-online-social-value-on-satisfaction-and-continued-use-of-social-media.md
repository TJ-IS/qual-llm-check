# The effect of online social value on satisfaction and continued use of social media

Tao Hu<sup>1</sup>, William J. Kettinger<sup>2</sup> and Robin S. Poston<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Department of Digital Media & Information Systems, King University, U.S.A.; <sup>2</sup>Management Information Systems, Fogelman College of Business and Economic, University of Memphis, Memphis, U.S.A.; <sup>3</sup>Management Information Systems, University of Memphis, Memphis, U.S.A.

Correspondence: William J. Kettinger, Management Information Systems, Fogelman College of Business and Economic, University of Memphis, Memphis 38152-3120, TN, U.S.A. Tel: +1 901 276 9272; Fax: +1 901 678 4151

## Abstract

Social media offers customers a unique service value proposition. Recognizing service value as a pivotal concept, this study develops an aggregate construct of online social value, whereby customers evaluate utilitarian and hedonic benefits relative to what they must sacrifice in effort and risk in deriving a value calculation of online social networking services which predicts satisfaction and continued use of online social media, such as Facebook. By empirically testing a model that explains online social value, this research contributes to information systems (IS) theory by introducing a customer value perspective in the social media context and helps service providers by identifying factors predicting satisfaction and continued use that might be employed to improve offerings to keep customers coming back. European Journal of Information Systems advance online publication,

Keywords: online social networking service; online social value; social media; satisfaction; continued use; services; longitudinal research; Social Exchange Theory

## Introduction

Online social networking services (OSN services, a.k.a. social media services), for example, Facebook, LinkedIn, and so on, are designed to help users share personal information, build and maintain social relationships, and have fun. While the online social media phenomena garners unprecedented popularity, endless alternative activities and other online services compete for users’ time and attention, begging the question: What leads online social media users to <sup>fi</sup>nd value in and continue to be satis<sup>fi</sup>ed and use an OSN service? Social media sites, such as Facebook with over US\$6.4 billion in advertising revenue in 2013, are especially interested in answering this question as Apple, Google, Amazon, and Facebook compete to dominate our limited online attention (Vance, 2011; Frommer, 2012; Anonymous, 2013). For example, Google challenged Facebook’s supremacy by advocating how Google+ offers the best features of Facebook and Twitter with an easier interface and better privacy controls (Beer, 2011; Bruno, 2011). Leadership is tenuous, as MySpace, SixDegrees, and Friendster illustrate how promising social media forerunners can lose their advantage (Beer, 2011).

OSN services offer a bundle of online socially oriented services (e.g., tools for pro<sup>fi</sup>ling, texting, posting on walls, blogging, and sharing) via a technology-enabled platform to support the creation and exchange of user-generated content to offer value in satisfying users’ social needs. Users are encouraged to be active participants interacting with, and contributing to, the online content, enhancing the value received (Vargo & Lusch, 2004). The longer and more deeply involved users participate in the online social experience, the more opportunities are available for them to expand their social networks and maintain their relationships (Li & Bernoff, 2008). However, these bene<sup>fi</sup>ts come at a sacri<sup>fi</sup>ce of expending more effort and taking on greater risks of exposing personal information. These pros and cons of OSN services usage require a conceptual and operational understanding of how users trade off social exchange bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces to calculate their online social value when making continued use decisions. Our study addresses this need by contextually operationalizing this trade-off calculation using a formative aggregate construct of online social value, which is nomologically positioned relative to how satis<sup>fi</sup>ed users are and their likelihood to continue to use the service. Conceptually, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne online social value as a person’s net positive or negative attitude based on his/her calculation of the weighted perceptions of the relevant bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces involved in using OSN services.

The marketing literature offers insights about how satis-<sup>fi</sup>ed service customers make re-purchase decisions, illustrating that higher customer value results in continued use directly, and through greater satisfaction (Yang & Peterson, 2004; Blocker et al, 2011). On a more limited basis and in different contexts, similar <sup>fi</sup>ndings have been found in information systems (IS) when the services marketing perspective has been adopted (Kettinger et al, 2009; Kim & Son, 2009). Given the prevalence of OSN services, there is a need then to examine the impact of service value on satisfaction and continued use customized to the unique attributes of socially oriented, relationship-driven, entertainment-providing, personal-information-sharing service environments. To de<sup>fi</sup>ne the dimensions of online social value calculations, we utilize Social Exchange Theory (SET) to theoretically derive the contextually unique bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces afforded by online social media settings. SET offers the psychological and sociological insights needed to de<sup>fi</sup>ne the processes of exchanges among people who follow a cost-bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis to compare social alternatives.

According to SET, to analyze social situations people consider dimensions of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts gained (sharing information, interacting, and staying connected) relative to the costs sacri<sup>fi</sup>ced (effort put into a relationship and the risks of sharing) (Emerson, 1976; Blau, 1994; Za<sup>fi</sup>rovski, 2003). Speci<sup>fi</sup>c to the unique attributes of an OSN setting, we examine user calculations of the value of social media situations based on perceived relational and informational utilitarian bene<sup>fi</sup>ts as well as enjoyment and curiosityful<sup>fi</sup>lling hedonic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, which are netted against the costs of information disclosure risk and effort-based sacri-<sup>fi</sup>ces accruing from using the OSN service. Rather than switching cost, which has been the focus of considerable past research in the marketing and IS literatures, our paper focuses on the value users perceive in continuing to use a preferred vendor’s OSN services. This study complements previous studies that examine contextual factors in predicting repurchase decisions (e.g., Zeithaml, 1988; Sirdeshmukh et al, 2002; Yang & Peterson, 2004; Kleijnen et al, 2007; Blocker et al, 2011) in other service contexts by incorporating a formative aggregate construct of online social value as a pivotal concept representing how users evaluate OSN service experiences and determine whether they should continue to use the service.

Next we provide a theoretical background of the study by incorporating perspectives of service value and usage continuance from services marketing and IS research, then propose a Value Model of Online Social Networking Services, describe online social value calculations grounded in SET, and develop hypotheses. The rationale and longitudinal research methods for testing the model are reported, followed by a discussion of the results and implications.

## Theoretical framework

Services are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a series of economic activities, processes, and performances that render bene<sup>fi</sup>ts given certain sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces in order to satisfy users’ needs (e.g., meals, entertainment, air travel) (Vargo & Lusch, 2004). This study focuses on technology-enabled services bundled into an online offering that allows users to manage relationships. Users turn to online social networks for information and relationship sharing services, for example, friending others and sharing news, to maintain associations and improve contact management (Enders et al, 2008). OSN services offer a larger pool of potentially interesting contacts and a social sense of interdependence and belongingness. In general, effort is needed to update contact data continuously so relationships do not expire over time and OSN services help to manage constantly growing numbers of contacts. Users have continuous access to valid contact data, with pro<sup>fi</sup>les acting as effective and informative address books. Users also take pleasure in using the OSN services to share music or photos, assume new or different identities, and participate in a virtual world of fun. For example, Facebook’s game services allow ubiquitous playing, for example, FarmVille, with its gift services allow giving presents with virtual balloons or stuffed animals. Google+’s community services allow artists to host small music concerts in Hangouts, and LinkedIn’s job link services make it easier for users to use their social network to <sup>fi</sup>nd jobs quickly. A network’s services may not involve a direct monetary exchange as users gain access to the platform for free in most cases. However, the services generate advertising revenues for the social network <sup>fi</sup>rm because of user involvement with the platform. The main service objective is typically to provide users with easy and continuous access to people and fun experiences. Thus, OSN services offer service bene<sup>fi</sup>ts by providing a platform that supports users in their relational and informational sharing in enjoyable and curiosity-ful<sup>fi</sup>lling ways, which must be assessed in light of the sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces made in effort and risks involved with sharing.

Given the services context of our study, we explain why, and the extent to which, users are satis<sup>fi</sup>ed and continue to use OSN services by making use of existing IS research and leveraging services marketing literature to gain additional insights. Prior research in IS acceptance offers insights into users’ intentions and behaviors of continued use of an IS (e.g., Bhattacherjee, 2001; Venkatesh et al, 2003; Wake<sup>fi</sup>eld & Whitten, 2006; Thong et al, 2011; Venkatesh et al, 2012). IS research examining non-workplace settings does predict technology usage by incorporating context-based factors, including facilitating conditions (Thong et al, 2011) and hedonic motivations (Venkatesh et al, 2012), with satisfaction and expectations leading to usage continuance (e.g., Venkatesh et al, 2011); however, it fails to examine value exchange in bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces as a driver of service use.

IS research has also begun to examine facets of service value exchange. These initial efforts contribute to our understanding, but they also have thus far not included service value as a calculation of its dimensions, nor do they directly assess risk perceptions or actual behaviors. In an internal IS functional service context, Kettinger et al (2009) lend support for the relationships of service value and service satisfaction and intentions to reuse the IS function’s services. However, the study did not examine the sacri<sup>fi</sup>ce (cost) antecedents of service value. Kim & Son (2009) recognize the importance of service value as a predictor of loyalty in the e-commerce context, and include usefulness and satisfaction as service bene<sup>fi</sup>ts; however, a service value calculation was not speci<sup>fi</sup>cally examined, and measures of the sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces of service use were not included, both of which are prevalent in the services marketing literature and re<sup>fl</sup>ective of social exchange.

Prior research in services marketing supports that value assessment calculations can positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence users’ continued use of a service. As part of a service usage experience, users <sup>fi</sup>rst evaluate service value netting out perceptions of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces, followed by the development of satisfaction attitudes, then make decisions about their intentions to continue using the service offering (Kleijnen et al, 2007; Blocker et al, 2011). Satisfacfl <sub>’</sub> lated overall impressions of the offering, which is shown to predict user repurchase and/or reuse (Mittal et al, 1999; Yang & Peterson, 2004). Service value also predicts repurchase and/or reuse intentions directly as well as indirectly through satisfaction. That is, satisfaction is positioned as an affect-oriented mediator that follows from value assessments and predicts intentions and/or behaviors to reuse a service. Several studies illustrate the partial mediating effect of satisfaction on the value–reuse relationships in traditional services contexts (e.g., Yang & Peterson, 2004; Brady et al, 2005). Our study extends this research by incorporating service value calculations in the online service context.

Past research does help us understand how people calculate service value. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, service marketing has built a rich integrated body of knowledge describing how users construct, perceive, and calculate the value gained from utilizing various types of services. The seminal work de<sup>fi</sup>ning service value comes from Zeithaml (1988), who adopted a rational approach espousing that users trade-off bene<sup>fi</sup>ts received relative to sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces incurred in using a service offering. Zeithaml (1988) illustrated how various types of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces in<sup>fl</sup>uence the way value calculations are made and how they in<sup>fl</sup>uence users’ product purchases or service use. In making the trade-offs in value assessments, users focus on the speci<sup>fi</sup>c attributes of the service they use, which can vary across offerings (Flint et al, 2002; Zeithaml et al, 2006).

Subsequent marketing studies support the approach that users consider bene<sup>fi</sup>ts gained and sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces incurred in a value trade-off calculation and the dimensions involved are de<sup>fi</sup>ned by the speci<sup>fi</sup>c services setting and context. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, bene<sup>fi</sup>ts can be utilitarian or hedonic gains received from using the service (Mathwick et al, 2001; Wang et al, 2007; Chitturi et al, 2008), while associated sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces can include monetary and non-monetary costs expended to use the service (Houston & Gassenheimer, 1987; Zeithaml, 1988). These value perceptions are dynamic as users continuously update their assessments after each service episode and aggregate assessments across multiple episodes (Ravald & Gronroos, 1996; Flint et al, 2002). Over time, users accumulate their experiences into an aggregated total service value calculation (Parasuraman, 1997).

## Online social value as a formative aggregate construct

Recognizing the need to extend early research, our study adopts the services marketing literature’s pragmatic economic perspective of combining bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces in a service value assessment within the OSN services context. By the trade-off de<sup>fi</sup>nition, both bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and sacri<sup>fi</sup>ce dimensions compose value, making it appropriate to conceive them as parts of a value calculation construct. The relationship between the dimensions of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces form the perceived value, thus a formative model of online social value is warranted.

Several additional reasons exist in support of utilizing et al <sup>fl</sup> model (Jarvis et al, 2003) causality runs from construct to items, which opposes the theoretical de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of how the dimensions of service value are traded off. Concep tually, users assess bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces, and cognitively factor both to form at overall online social value assessment. This suggests the value construct should be speci<sup>fi</sup>ed as formative in a higher-order model with the dimensions of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces included in a lower-order level as the de<sup>fi</sup>ning characteristics of value. Changes in perceptions at the dimensional level cause changes in overall value, but not necessarily vice versa. With OSN services, utilitarian bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, hedonic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, information risk, and effort are viewed as the de<sup>fi</sup>ning dimensions of online social value, where changes in any one dimension are expected to cause changes in value calculations. Second, in the re<sup>fl</sup>ective model items are expected to share similar content and be interchangeable (Jarvis et al, 2003). However, the dimensions of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces are distinguishable and not interchangeable. Dropping a dimension from the model changes the domain of the online social value construct, making it incomplete if a dimension is missing. For example, the utility gained from <sup>fi</sup>nding out the details of a friend’s party (utilitarian bene<sup>fi</sup>t) has little to do with curiously checking out a friend’s vacation pictures (hedonic bene<sup>fi</sup>t). The bene<sup>fi</sup>t and sacri<sup>fi</sup>ce dimensions do not all share a common theme, and excluding one dimension may lead to changing the meaning of the online social value construct (MacKenzie et al, 2005).

Third, a re<sup>fl</sup>ective model assumes correlations between items. However, the value dimensions are theoretically independent and correlation is not necessarily assumed. The relationship between bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and costs can vary and depend on the speci<sup>fi</sup>c facets of information, individual, and service category involved (Zeithaml, 1988). Thus, the constraint of assuming correlation among the dimensions of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces is not always necessary. Since utilitarian bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, hedonic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, information risks, and effort sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces can be incorporated in online social value calculations in different ways, these dimensions will not necessarily be correlated. Finally, in the re<sup>fl</sup>ective model the nomological networks of the items are expected not to differ, and thus are required to have the same antecedents and consequence (Jarvis et al, 2003). Bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces may in<sup>fl</sup>uence user satisfaction or continued use, but their antecedents differ. Effort sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces may result from what level of effort was expected, reference levels set via advertising, or observations of how much others have expended in using the services; while utilitarian bene<sup>fi</sup>ts may be caused by new circles of friends or learning about an upcoming event, suggesting that an aggregate construct would be appropriate when modeling online social value.

Given the formative nature of online social value, we next de<sup>fi</sup>ne the contextually relevant dimensions informed by SET. SET articulates an approach to ‘economic analysis of non-economic social situations’ (Emerson, 1976, p. 336). Through the economic lens, social actions and relations are viewed as an exchange phenomenon (Za<sup>fi</sup>rovski, 2003). Social occurrences are explained ‘by means of its bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and costs’ (Homans, 1961, pp. 12–13) based on rational choice theory which emphasizes that social exchange behavior is driven by maximizing gains and marginal utility through calculating and balancing the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and sacri-<sup>fi</sup>ces in maintaining relationships (Emerson, 1976; Blau, 1994). As described earlier, users turn to OSN services to pursue and enjoy social exchanges with friends and acquaintances suggesting SET is an appropriate lens to examine the dimensions of value calculations in this social setting. SET provides socially derived explanations about the dimensions and calculations involved in trade-off decisions informing a formative aggregate construct of online social value, which is then nomologically positioned relative to how satis<sup>fi</sup>ed users are and their likelihood to continue to use the service.

Analogies can be made in reference to other theories informing IS usage-related attitudes and behaviors. For example, the Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA) (Venkatesh et al, 2003) recognizes individuals make tradeoffs of feelings in their behavioral attitude. The Theory of

Planned Behavior (TPB) recognizes constraints on behavior (Taylor & Todd, 1995a) without directly de<sup>fi</sup>ning costs. The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) includes perceived ease of use, which is synergistic to service sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces, and perceived usefulness (Davis, 1989), similar to service bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. While TRA, TPB, and TAM provide similar fundamental notions used to explain IS usage phenomena, we believe the services marketing and SET perspective of value calculations employ a more direct and pragmatic assessment of both bene<sup>fi</sup>ts gained and costs incurred, offering valuable managerial insights of continued usage behaviors in the OSN context. Our study examines speci<sup>fi</sup>c positive and negative factors in<sup>fl</sup>uencing a calculation of exchanges in a more literal fashion than prior IS usage-related research.

## Utilitarian bene<sup>fi</sup>ts

The services marketing literature provides precedence for contextualizing the particular bene<sup>fi</sup>t and sacri<sup>fi</sup>ce antecedents that in<sup>fl</sup>uence the value calculations of a service, with some bene<sup>fi</sup>ts categorized as utilitarian. OSN services allow users the bene<sup>fi</sup>t of building and maintaining a network of friends supporting dynamic relationship management, and offer an appealing context for fun given sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces in effort to participate and exposures to risk (Trusov et al, 2009). Utilitarian bene<sup>fi</sup>ts provide instrumental payoffs of performing and achieving objective goals, including enhanced ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, convenience, and economic returns on investment (Mathwick et al, 2001; Sweeney & Soutar, 2001). Users gain utility from spending time on Facebook, Google+, and other social media sites by learning when and where their friend’s party is, meeting someone and friending them to discover who they are, <sup>fi</sup>nding out about a company’s latest product releases, and so on. The information and relationships gained fuels the user’s ability to join the party, hold more meaningful conversations with new acquaintances, and buy the trendiest products.

SET offers insights about behaviors and the way people are embedded in social networks in order to foster the rewards of relationship development and information exchange (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998), suggesting two key overlapping bene<sup>fi</sup>ts: relational and informational. Through building and maintaining social capital with others, people gain relational bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, creating a social sense of interdependence, belongingness, and identity typically manifested in friendships, peer support, and group af<sup>fi</sup>liations (Ellemers et al, 1999). People also gain informational bene<sup>fi</sup>ts re<sup>fl</sup>ected in interpersonal exchange and sharing of information within the reaches of their social networks (Granovetter, 1973). For the purposes of this study, OSN services support relational and informational bene<sup>fi</sup>ts increasing value for users by offering a service where users create and maintain social ties with others, enabling the ef<sup>fi</sup>cient spreading of information among members within their own network and virally spreading to others’ social circles within the site. People respond to social norms by building and maintaining social capital (Granovetter, 1973; Ellemers et al, 1999). In a socially oriented online context, social norms involve joining friends online leading to relational bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. In virtual communities, users reap the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of building wider and deeper networks by staying in touch with old friends and making new friends, maintaining their relationships, and keeping connected (Boyd & Ellison, 2007; Ellison et al, 2007). At the same time, people derive bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from searching for information about people and activities (Chen, 2007; Donath, 2007), and gain knowledge from reading friends’ postings, shared events, news, gossip, and ideas within their community.

Users determine their bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from a social services network with limited direct external in<sup>fl</sup>uence (as compared, e.g., with a corporate intranet), which may lead to stronger weighting in value calculations. People determine their own goals of using OSN services, and whether to share or <sup>fi</sup>nd information or friends, which can overlap in its objectives. Information exchange and relationship development occurs simultaneously making it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for users to separate their perceptions of these utilitarian bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. This suggests some elements of both relational and informational bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the overall utilitarian bene<sup>fi</sup>ts users perceive when using online networks. In this context, users are known to place high value on service capabilities that allow them to maintain and expand their relationships and improve searching for information and opportunities (Brown & Duguid, 2001). Thus, we include user perceptions of how well the online network provides opportunities to share information and build relationships as re<sup>fl</sup>ective indicators of the utilitarian bene<sup>fi</sup>t from service use, with the single construct of utilitarian bene<sup>fi</sup>ts included as a positive formative dimension of an online social value calculation.

## Hedonic benefits

Unlike utilitarian bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of utilizing OSN services which has an associated goal of building relationships and learning information, hedonic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are derived from the experiential feelings or emotional states experienced with using the services (Sweeney & Soutar, 2001), re<sup>fl</sup>ecting an affective appreciation of service activities and performance. SET suggests attributions of positive and negative emotions drive commitment to continued participation in a social exchange, which supports the inclusion of hedonic sensations as part of online social value calculations (Lawler, 2001). For example, users experience pleasure from spending time on Facebook, Google+, and other social media sites by ‘killing time’ when waiting in line at the grocery store, laughing at funny posts made by friends, enjoying a relative’s weight loss or exercise successes, and so on. Grati<sup>fi</sup>cation is gained by being distracted when bored, sharing a laugh with someone else, and encouraging others with their goals.

Unlike work-related intranet and commercial contexts where the goal is most likely to complete a task, OSN services could be used to pass the time and spontaneously react to content. Additional rewards of using OSN services are based on how a symbolic online system can create a surrealistic environment, where individuals indulge in interactive enjoyable experiences and gain hedonic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts: enjoyment and curiosity ful<sup>fi</sup>llment. Emotions and feelings involved when online include enjoyment of the esthetics and playfulness (Holbrook, 1994), and curiosity ful<sup>fi</sup>lling adventures (Agarwal & Karahanna, 2000). Enjoyment refers to individuals’ pleasurable experiences ‘for the sheer sake of doing it’ (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990), while curiosity ful<sup>fi</sup>llment re<sup>fl</sup>ects individuals’ cognitive exploration in seeking out experiences (Agarwal & Karahanna, 2000). OSN services offer outlets for enjoyment and curiosity ful<sup>fi</sup>llment as users can browse or search for others’ current status, explore events, review posted opinions, watch videos, play games, and vent their own thoughts and feelings. Enjoyment bene<sup>fi</sup>ts capture individuals’ perceptions of pleasurable experiences when users participate in online experiences. Online interactions offer users fun activities that are oftentimes novel and exciting, which people <sup>fi</sup>nd interesting and intriguing (Ghani & Deshpande, 1994). Users engage with the platform to see what others are doing, catch up with the latest buzz, express themselves in a variety of ways, and share their feelings and experiences. Curiosity ful<sup>fi</sup>llment re<sup>fl</sup>ects an individual’s perceptions of cognitive adventure in seeking out personal stories of friends, celebrities, organizations, or strangers. The environment offers socially created outlets that arouse users imagination and excitement about other people and activities.

Online social platforms support emotional enjoyment and curiosity ful<sup>fi</sup>llment at the same time, which creates an environment where it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for users to be aware of each of these hedonic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts separately. Accordingly, we include user perceptions of how well the network provides opportunities to enjoy interactions, participate in creative outlets, and browse others’ lives to satisfy his/her curiosity, as re<sup>fl</sup>ective indicators of the hedonic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. We model hedonic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts as a positive formative dimension of an online social value calculation.

## Information risk

As users increase their online network participation, they gain more bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, which come at a cost. In general, the services marketing literature examines two main types of sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces in service use: monetary and non-monetary (Zeithaml, 1988). Monetary sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces are measured as users’ perceptions of the prices actually paid (Varki & Colgate, 2001; Baker et al, 2002). Non-monetary sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces re<sup>fl</sup>ect users’ perceptions of the amount of risks and efforts devoted to the use of a service (Kleijnen et al, 2007). In some contexts non-monetary sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces can be more important than monetary sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces (Baker et al, 2002). In the online social context, monetary sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces may not be substantial. Beyond the need for an internetready device and internet service, monetary outlays are not considered signi<sup>fi</sup>cant as the main sites do not typically charge usage fees. Thus, we do not include monetary sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces in the online social value calculation in order to re<sup>fl</sup>ect the fact that users typically have insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant marginal monetary expenses.

Regarding non-monetary sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces in our context, we draw on SET, to illustrate how the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of social exchange come at the sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces users incur in taking on informational risk. Given the personal yet immediate and transparent nature of OSN services, users casually share content in highly informal ways. Users may share too much as, inhibitions are lower and, judgment is askew. This is problematic as content once posted online is out of the user’s control. Thus, informational risk is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as individuals’ loss of control over information about personal traits, actions, and attributes (Salton, 1980). Similar to the privacy calculus perspective of information privacy (Dinev & Hart, 2006; Xu et al, 2009), we expect these sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces to be expended in return for receiving the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts mentioned above. The privacy calculus perspective suggests that in online usage contexts users perform a privacy calculation before sharing personal information with users accepting the sacri<sup>fi</sup>ce as long as the risk is accompanied by adequate bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (Dinev & Hart, 2006; Xu et al, 2009). Other IS research has also found a negative relationship between users’ perceptions of informational risk and their evaluations of IS usage, with concerns about personal information being misused and made available to unknown parties (Pavlou & Gefen, 2004; Pavlou et al, 2007).

Within our context, a socially oriented participative environment encourages signi<sup>fi</sup>cant information disclosure. Users enter their personal pro<sup>fi</sup>le information and continue to make individual entries for others to access, which increases the risks of having their ideas, data, and comments be used nefariously by others (Rosen, 2007). With the pro<sup>fi</sup>le page created, users develop and join various online communities by displaying self-expressive interests publicly, exchanging personal stories, inviting and being solicited by others to share relationships and information. Information risks have become more problematic as online advertisers spam users and recruiters, prospective employers, college admission counselors, law enforcement of<sup>fi</sup>cers, and attorneys investigate people’s personal lives (Ward, 2007). Facebook and other sites offer privacy tools to help reduce information risks. For example, Google+ has ‘circles’ to de<sup>fi</sup>ne friends with varying levels of sharing allowed and Google Alerts offers automated tools to monitor social network content that sends subscribers noti<sup>fi</sup>cations of personal and family member name use. The more the user perceives the online network requires making personal information available for disreputable use, the more he/she realizes the information risks, leading to lower net value calculations. Thus, we model information risk as a negative formative dimension of an online social value calculation.

## Effort

SET also illustrates effort plays a role in social exchange situations. Mutual efforts by both parties to participate in and share personal stories are needed to sustain social networks (Bourdieu & Wacquant, 1992; Blau, 1994); otherwise, social relationships will be broken over time (Emerson, 1969). SET promotes that social actors are sensitive to key dimensions in social exchanges where participative efforts and information risks are involved, which may be viewed as acceptable costs of social exchange (Cook, 2000). Most users learn on their own how to share information, oftentimes following a trialand-error approach or asking friends for help given the lack of guidelines or user manuals suggesting what and when to share information. This suggests a wide variety of usage approaches will exist, some involving more effort than others. Effort in online network use is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the amount of time, work, and earnest activity used to participate in online social exchanges. IS research has found that the more users perceive it would take effort to achieve their tasks by using a system, the less they intend to continue using it (Kankanhalli et al, 2005; Olivera et al, 2008).

Within our context, a socially oriented environment can require considerable effort to keep personal pro<sup>fi</sup>les updated, comment on others’ posts, and navigate through dialogs needed to maintain the content and receive the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts outlined above (Boyd & Ellison, 2007; Ellison et al, 2007). The sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces of expending effort can make users feel a reduced sense of value in what they get out of using the service (Kleijnen et al, 2007). Facebook and other sites offer mobile applications and when combined with generally ubiquitous internet connections, this simpli<sup>fi</sup>es the effort needed to access and use OSN services. Furthermore, once an initial pro<sup>fi</sup>le is created and privacy settings have been established, the learning curve to understand how to use Facebook, Google+, LinkedIn, and other sites is incremental and self-deterministic as users decide whether to expand, maintain, or reduce their breadth of use. As users realize the effort they are devoting to OSN service use, the lower they calculate its net value. Thus, we model effort as a negative formative dimension of an online social value calculation.

## Value Model of Online Social Networking Services

As illustrated above, the services marketing literature and SET suggest a trade-off calculation of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts/rewards combined with sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces/costs. This calculation endorses the need to model a formative aggregate construct over a latent re<sup>fl</sup>ective construct of online social value. Thus, we view online social value as an aggregate, third-order construct formed by an algebraic combination of its four formative dimensions, with each of the bene<sup>fi</sup>t constructs included as latent models and each of the sacri<sup>fi</sup>ce constructs represented by several re<sup>fl</sup>ective manifest items. Modeling online social value as an aggregate, third-order construct is appropriate (Lawler, 2001; Jarvis et al, 2003) as it represents how users combine high or low levels of online social value. This parsimonious model is theoretically cooperative with online social value as a construct that may relate to other variables, while being practically relevant because OSN service providers can modify platforms to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the dimensions of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and sacri-<sup>fi</sup>ces. Utilizing the main constructs and relationships proposed by the services value perspective and SET, we propose the Value Model of Online Social Networking Services as illustrated in Figure 1.

While value can be assessed as positive or negative given trade-offs, a rational user of a service should continue to use a service as long as value is evaluated positively, with perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts greater than perceived sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces (Johnson et al, 2006; Blocker et al, 2011). Expectation–discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation models in prior studies indicate continued use is directly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by value and satisfaction; and IS studies have shown support for the relationship of service value and reusing an IS function’s service (Kettinger et al, 2009) and reusing online access portals (Kim & Son, 2009). In the online social context, Facebook, Google+, and other sites keep users informed about activities going on in their friends’ lives, allowing users when seeing their friends again to immediately ask about their latest activities, how new endeavors panned out, and how their latest issues were resolved. When users evaluate greater value in network use, they recognize that the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of socializing with others outweigh the sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces of expending effort to interact with the service and of taking on risks of exposing personal information to their social circles and beyond. However, as the sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces grow relative to the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts gained, value falls. If value is calculated low enough, users discontinue their use. Thus, we propose:

For online social networking services, calculations of online social value are positively related to continued use.

Satisfaction is the result of service users’ value perceptions (Sirdeshmukh et $^ { a l , }$ 2002; Yang & Peterson, 2004). As long as the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are greater than the sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces, value calculations are known to be one of the most salient evaluations in increasing users’ satisfaction with a service (Blocker et al, 2011). IS studies have shown support for the relationship of service value and satisfaction (Kettinger et al, 2009; Kim & Son, 2009). Satisfaction in the IS literature has been re<sup>fl</sup>ected in user feelings of being satis<sup>fi</sup>ed, pleased, and contented (Bhattacherjee, 2001). In the OSN context, value is gained from being able to keep current about distant friends, their kids, and social activities, and about admired organizations to learn about new products or the latest rumors. The value gained from integrating sizeable amounts of information from multiple sources may help form reasonable expectations about what is possible when using OSN services, which may lead to greater satisfaction, ful<sup>fi</sup>llment, and grati<sup>fi</sup>cation of the user’s desire, need, and appetite for social sharing. Thus, when users’ value calculations are favorable, this can positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence attitudes of satisfaction. When users evaluate greater (lower) value in online network use, they recognize that the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (do not) exceed the sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces leading to greater (lower) satisfaction with the service. Thus, we propose:

For online social networking services, calculations of online social value are positively related to satisfaction.

Services marketing studies illustrate the partial mediating effect of satisfaction on the value-continued use relationships (e.g., Yang & Peterson, 2004; Brady et al, 2005). The causal link between user satisfaction and continued use of a service has been empirically supported in non-online social contexts. Satisfaction as an affective attitude has gained empirical support in the IS literature as a predictor of IS usage (Taylor & Todd, 1995b; Karahanna et al, 1999), with satisfaction and expectations leading to usage continuance (Venkatesh et al, 2011) and users deciding whether to continue IS use based on their attitudes of satisfaction with prior use (Bhattacherjee, 2001; Kettinger et al, 2009; Kim & Son, 2009). These studies provide support for the satisfaction-continued use relationship. In the online social context, users return to OSN services to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll their need to stay informed and up-todate about social, collective events and to enjoy games, music, and sharing ideas. Rational users will take action returning to the OSN services to maintain their satisfaction. When users evaluate greater satisfaction in network use, they are gaining the valuable bene<sup>fi</sup>ts desired and accepting the sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces associated with service usage, and they will continue its use. ## Methodology and results

We conducted a longitudinal <sup>fi</sup>eld survey and collected data via two online questionnaires administered at two points in time. The <sup>fi</sup>rst questionnaire measured evaluations and attitudes about OSN services, and the second questionnaire measured subsequent continued use. As the target population for this study is OSN services users with internet access, we sampled from undergraduate and graduate students enrolled in a large university in North America as college students are considered a major online networking user group (Lenhart et al, 2010; Li & Bernoff, 2008). As we are interested in continued use, only participants stating that they used OSN services were provided the survey. The survey asked respondents to consider the one network they used most often during the prior three months, encouraging them to base their responses as an aggregate across multiple service episodes during this period.

## Data collection

Data collection in Time 1 measured respondents’ perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces as well as online social value, satisfaction, behavioral intentions to continue use, and control variables. Data collection in Time 2 (3 months later) measured self-reported continued use in terms of intensity, frequency, and duration. Participation in this study was voluntary. With con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality ensured, respondents’ email addresses were used to match answers across the two questionnaires. The response rates were 52.9 and 49.4% for Time 1 and 2, respectively. A total of 518 respondents answered both questionnaires. The sample was roughly evenly divided by gender, while relatively youthful in mix, 36.4% were above the age of 25, and 42.9% worked fulltime. Our sample is relatively similar to demographics of the predominant online site, Facebook, during the same time period of our study (Spring, 2009), where a majority of North American users (54.3%) were below the age of 25, with relatively equal gender participation (Istrategylabs.com, 2010). See Appendix A for respondent demographics.

## Measurement

Measures were primarily adapted from previously validated scales, and multi-item scales were used to improve reliability and validity of measurement. Appendix B illustrates the measurement scales and related literature sources. New scales were developed for relational and informational bene<sup>fi</sup>ts following standard procedures of instrument construction (e.g., Churchill, 1979; Straub, 1989; Boudreau et al, 2001; Straub et al, 2004). Given the survey was conducted at the individual level, control variables including age, gender, and ethnicity were captured, along with variables related to OSN services usage including life stability, work status, and usage experience. While not the major focus of this study, expectation–discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation theory from the marketing literature (Oliver, 1993; 1999) adopted in IS usage research (Bhattacherjee, 2001; Venkatesh et al, 2011) suggests users establish a level of service expectation that in<sup>fl</sup>uences their satisfaction with service usage; hence expectations was included as a control variable.

## Data analysis

Before testing the full model, we conducted detailed tests to examine common methods bias and non-response bias. Regarding common method bias, if the independent and dependent variables in a study are not obtained from different sources and are not measured in different contexts, common method bias can be a potential threat to the study (Podsakoff et al, 2003; Chin et al, 2012). First, we applied Harman’s one-factor statistical test to determine whether common method bias was a concern (Podsakoff & Organ, 1986). As suggested, we created the principal components analysis (PCA) model and included a common method factor that links to all of the singleindicator constructs that were from observed indicators. Because the method factor loadings were insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant and the indicators’ substantive variances were substantially greater than their method variances, we concluded that common method bias is unlikely to be a serious concern (Podsakoff et al, 2003). Second, an analysis of partial correlation was also performed (Podsakoff & Organ, 1986). In the analysis, the <sup>fi</sup>rst component emerging from the PCA was modeled as a control variable on the dependent variable. When common method bias exists the inclusion of the component would produce signi<sup>fi</sup>cant change in the explained variance in the dependent variable; however, the explained variance in the original model was not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different from the explained variance in the model with the control variable, indicating no threat of common method bias is exhibited in the data. Regarding non-response bias, as shown in Appendix A in Table A1, the demographic variables of the respondents who participated in the Time 1 data collection but not in Time 2 were compared with those of respondents who participated in both stages of data collection. No signi<sup>fi</sup>- cant differences across all demographic variables were found, leading to the conclusion that a non-response bias is not a threat to data analysis.

The measures were then submitted to a con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory factor analysis (CFA) using structural equation modelingbased partial least squares (PLS) to test the content, convergent, and discriminant validity, and reliability of the constructs. We used PLS to analyze our data, as it employs a component-based approach for estimation purposes (Lohmoller, 1989) and can test mixed-models with formative factors (Chin et al, 2003). To ensure the individual item reliability and convergent validity of the constructs, we examined factor loadings of individual measures on their respective underlying constructs, as well as the average variance extracted (AVE). All of the measurement item loadings on respective constructs were above the recommended minimum value of 0.70, indicating that at least 50% of the variance was shared with the construct (Chin, 1998), illustrated in Table 1. The AVE values for all re<sup>fl</sup>ective constructs were greater than the minimum recommended value of 0.50, shown in Table 2, indicating that the items satis<sup>fi</sup>ed the convergent validity.

Next, to ensure the discriminant validity of constructs in the research model, the square root of the AVE for each construct was compared with the other correlation scores in the correlation matrix. The square root of the AVE for each construct in the model, as reported in the diagonal of the correlation of constructs matrix in Table 2, was larger than the corresponding off-diagonal correlations of the constructs to their latent variables. We also performed CFA and examined the cross loadings of the items on other constructs and found that, as recommended, all of the measurement item loadings on the intended constructs were above 0.78 and were at least 0.1 less on their loadings on other constructs (Gefen & Straub, 2005), as illustrated in Table 1.

To con<sup>fi</sup>rm the scale reliability and internal consistency of the constructs in the research model, we calculated the composite reliability (Fornell & Larcker, 1981) and Cronbach’s α scores. A composite reliability and Cronbach’s α values of 0.7 or greater is considered acceptable (Nunnally & Bernstein, 1994); as reported in Table 2, the composite reliability values for all of the constructs in the research model were greater than or equal to 0.87 and Cronbach’s α values were greater than or equal to 0.77, demonstrating that all constructs had adequate reliability scores. All of the construct measures were deemed to be re<sup>fl</sup>ective of their indicators. We also assessed the content validity through an extensive literature review, theoretical validation, interviews with OSN services users, and discussions with OSN services experts and IS faculty members. These results illustrate that the measurement instrument possesses strong psychometric properties. The latent formative construct of continued use with multiple measures of duration, intensity, and frequency were tested and found to illustrate strong validity (see Appendix C). ## Testing the Value Model of Online Social Networking Services

To estimate the proposed third-order model of online social value, we modeled the coef<sup>fi</sup>cients $\left( \gamma _ { \mathrm { i } } \right)$ of each dimension (second-order factors for bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and <sup>fi</sup>rstorder factors for sacrifices) to the latent third-order factor using PCA (Diamantopoulos & Winklhofer, 2001; Wetzels et al, 2009):

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \text { Online   social   value } & = \gamma_ {1} \times \text { utilitarian   benefits } \\ & + \gamma_ {2} \times \text { hedonic   benefits } \\ & - \gamma_ {3} \times \text { information   risks } \\ & - \gamma_ {4} \times \text { effort } \end{array}
$$

First, analyses support the second-order re<sup>fl</sup>ective models of utilitarian and hedonic bene<sup>fi</sup>t dimensions, illustrating that all loadings on the second-order factors are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant on their respective constructs as shown in Appendix D. Then, analyses validate the coef<sup>fi</sup>cients of the <sup>fi</sup>rst-order factors (effort and information risk) and second-order factors (utilitarian bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and hedonic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts) with the latent third-order factor (Diamantopoulos & Winklhofer, 2001; Pavlou & Gefen, 2004; Wetzels et al, 2009), illustrating that all factors are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant formative dimensions of the online social value calculation construct as shown in Figure 2. Item loadings for <sup>fi</sup>rst-order constructs are reported in Appendix D, Figure D1.

The structural model was assessed resulting in a simultaneous analysis of (1) how well the measures relate to each construct and (2) whether the hypothesized relationships can be supported. The results of analysis with continued use measured as usage duration, frequency, and intensity several months after perceptions of the dimensions of value calculations were made are reported in Figure 2. As summarized Figure 3 and Table 3, all hypothesized paths are supported. The online social value calculation explains 50% of the variance in satisfaction and 29% of the variance in continued use. Data analysis also found that expectations was a signi-<sup>fi</sup>cant control variable. As predicted, online social value in<sup>fl</sup>uences satisfaction and continued use directly, and through the mediation of satisfaction indirectly. Testing of the mediation of satisfaction can be found in Appendix E.

## Findings

Our study <sup>fi</sup>nds that those who continued using an online network were not only satis<sup>fi</sup>ed but were making high calculations of value by trading off what they received compared with what they gave up in service use. By illustrating the pivotal role of value in explaining continued use, this study builds upon recent IS studies calling for additional context-based factors beyond usefulness and ease of use in predicting technology acceptance continuance (Thong et al, 2011; Venkatesh et al, 2012). Speci<sup>fi</sup>- cally, we exploit services marketing and IS usage literatures to integrate relevant concepts and then informed by SET create a calculation of value and a nomological network of factors predicting continued use of OSN services. Following the tenets of SET we observe that people consider socially relevant bene<sup>fi</sup>ts gained and costs sacri<sup>fi</sup>ced forming an aggregate construct of online social value based on contextualized lower-order dimensions. Grounded in SET, we put forward and tested four dimensions that form online social value calculations that were conceptually organized as two bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (utilitarian and hedonic) and two sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces (information risk and effort).

Relational and informational bene<sup>fi</sup>ts re<sup>fl</sup>ect the utility that users perceive from providing and receiving content for social purposes through maintaining and nurturing existing networks, as well as sharing with and learning about people and activities in existing or new networks. Enjoyment and curiosity ful<sup>fi</sup>llment bene<sup>fi</sup>ts signify the pleasure that users perceive from online networking usage that is fun and enjoyable, as well as its ability to satisfy curiosity, arouse imagination, and excite interest in such things as friends’ activities, games, and music. Online networking service providers should be cognizant that both utilitarian and hedonic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are positive dimensions of value, which in turn, spawn greater continued use.

Turning to the sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces of OSN services usage, information risk captures users’ perceptions of the exposure to having their personal information being misused, made available to unknown parties, and being used in unknown ways. Consistent with other IS studies (Krasnova et al, 2010) including studies based on the privacy calculus perspective (Dinev & Hart, 2006; Xu et al, 2009), users involved with socially created content recognize that their personal information could be subject to misuse; however, these sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces are not as strong as the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are in calculating value. Effort sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces are also a weaker but signi<sup>fi</sup>cant negative dimension of online social value. Users recognize work is needed to keep pro<sup>fi</sup>les up-to-date, maintain information, and participate in use of online networks illustrating negative dimensions of value.

As the sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces appear to be weaker than the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts as dimensions included in value calculations, future studies may want to address these <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Integrating Mental Accounting Theory with SET may help explain the relatively weaker exchange weights associated with sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces in value calculations in the online social setting. Mental Accounting Theory offers a framework of how people weigh various decisional dimensions, by potentially over-emphasizing and over-in<sup>fl</sup>ating bene-<sup>fi</sup>ts. When individuals stress greater importance on the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts than they do on the sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces, the gains can overwhelm the loss dimension (Thaler, 1985; 1999; Heath et al, 1995) to the point where individuals may ignore the sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces (Thaler, 1999). In OSN services settings, it appears that the reduced effort requirements and increased privacy controls for using the online service has diminished what is required to update and share personal information. Thus, consistent with Mental Accounting Theory and SET, our study suggests that users may in part perceive the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts marginalize the sacrifices in online social value calculations.

Perceptions of satisfaction partially mediated the relationship between value calculations and continued use. Our results illustrate that while users must assess greater value in the service experience, users must also feel satis<sup>fi</sup>ed, pleased, and contented in their experience in order to continue using OSN services. This suggests that service providers can encourage continued use by improving satisfaction, along with <sup>fi</sup>nding ways to build positive online social value and set achievable expectations. Finally, we also included a control variable for past experiences and uses as it has been used in models of IS continuance (Bhattacherjee, 2001; Khalifa and Liu, 2007; Venkatesh et al, 2011). We found that expectations did in<sup>fl</sup>uence satisfaction and continued use, suggesting the need to include this control variable when examining online service settings.

## Implications and extensions

The study’s <sup>fi</sup>ndings have several theoretical and practical implications. First, from a descriptive standpoint, the online social value construct represents key elements of users continued use decisions in social media contexts, which has largely been ignored in the literature. A major contribution of this study is the introduction of a new formative aggregate construct of online social value that predicts satisfaction and use in the social media context. Unlike re<sup>fl</sup>ective models of value where all components are treated as critical, our formative model illustrates the importance of expending scarce resources on <sup>fi</sup>nding ways to increase user bene<sup>fi</sup>ts while acknowledging the need to reduce user sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces to encourage continued online services use. Future research could adapt online social value measure to examine the dimensional nuances of how people calculate value of online blogs, communities of interest, recommendation systems, multiplayer video games, wish lists, or other OSN services based on their level of participation, size of networks, and other variables of interest.

By introducing online social value calculations, we have also extended the services marketing literature by illustrating the adaptation of service value in a social exchange context. drawing on SET to determine the contextually relevant social dimensions of value. Using a Value Model of Online Social Networking Services as a foundational framework, future research should consider adopting the contextualization process used in this study to identify the antecedents of value in alternative online settings, such as those enabling transactional e-commerce services, mobile commerce, governmental eServices, or intranetbased social networking. For example, in the ecommerce context, pricing theories might help identify sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces when the inclusion of monetary prices is warranted; or management theories might facilitate examining corporate online networks lacking voluntary participation and excluding hedonic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. We might anticipate under different contexts the types and strength of certain dimensions will differ in influencing value calculations and its consequences.

Our research <sup>fi</sup>ndings point to the merit in utilizing SET when examining socially oriented online exchanges. In OSN services contexts, people interact online by sharing news and supporting connections while regularly reassessing how much and what types of information to exchange, as well as the extent to which they will continue using the service. SET conceptually explains how selfinterested and mutually dependent parties calculate value when interacting with each other. SET research suggests individual self-interest combines with social economic and psychological needs to encourage interactions (Emerson, 1976; Lawler, 2001; Za<sup>fi</sup>rovski, 2003). According to SET, social behavior involving trust and friendship enables a satisfying social exchange processes when people receive reasonable returns for their efforts in building, maintaining, and growing relationships. Thus, additional factors supported by SET may in<sup>fl</sup>uence how people use OSN services. Future research should examine how other social economic and psychological needs of OSN service users in<sup>fl</sup>uence value calculations and continued use. For example, studies are needed to assess the role of user characteristics of sel<sup>fi</sup>shness, self-indulgence, and reciprocity, as well as OSN-supported task characteristics of social exchange through blogging and gaming vs gifting in value and usage decision making.

Our study responds to requests to better conceptualize and measure social media value (Aral et al, 2013). Calculating a formative aggregate construct of value offers a parsimonious model theoretically supporting that online social value relates to other variables (e.g., satisfaction and continued use). Calculating a higher-order value construct can also help OSN service providers identify which facets of their platforms to modify in order to generate higher value (e.g., facilitating information sharing and curiosity ful<sup>fi</sup>llment, while keeping information more private and making the services easier to use). For example, in developing value propositions, managers and designers of online services must determine what subset of offerings are most appropriate to invest resources in based on what matters most to major user segments. Today, OSN service users place great value on offerings that allow for creating deeper networks, searching for information about people and activities, being part of fun activities that arouse imagination and satisfy curiosity about others. While users want all of this, they appear to be less worried about costs involved with protecting their personal information from being misused and the effort it takes to update their information and to navigate through the platform. Some studies support this notion as Facebook users were found to disclose private information, accepting that their information would be visible to unknown people (Tunainen et al, 2009).

There are numerous areas to extend this research. First, our study focused on the most often used OSN services suggesting users were entrenched in one network inhibiting easy migration to a different network, thus reducing the need to incorporate switching costs in our study. Studies in alternate settings may <sup>fi</sup>nd switching costs are an important facet of satisfaction and continued use in certain online environments where set-up costs, usage entrenchment, or popularity play strong roles in the attractiveness of the service (Kim & Son,

2009). Related to switching costs would be perceived critical mass, which is the point at which users think an OSN service is suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently diffused for its use to continue (Van Slyke et al, 2007). Future studies could speci<sup>fi</sup>cally examine the point at which users discontinue to use communication platforms that fail to achieve critical mass. Trust in employees and management, brand reputation, owner/user communities, and other emotional hedonic feelings could be additional factors which may moderate the effects presented in the Value Model of Online Social Networking Services (Sirdeshmukh et al, 2002; Algesheimer et al, 2005). Future research should also consider incorporating these factors into the Value Model based on the unique characteristics of the contexts involved.

Second, this study asked survey respondents in Time 1 to identify the one online social network they had used most often in the prior 3-month period. We surveyed respondents in this manner to focus their perceptions, evaluations, and attitudes on that which was most readily available cognitively both regarding the particular site used as well as service episodes experienced. Selecting the one network used most frequently over the previous 3 months might be somewhat biased toward continued use behavior; however, we did not want to have respondents assess a network with which they had only a very short-term experience as they may not be able to adequately assess the antecedents of value. In the second data collection at Time 2 we gathered selfreported measures of usage behaviors. Unlike many studies in services marketing and IS that only examine behavioral intention in one cross section, our study measured usage across three dimensions (i.e., intensity, duration, and frequency) 3 months after perceptions of the dimensions of value calculations were made. We limited our study to a 3-month longitudinal period in order to reduce any problems associated with extinction in our sample resulting from collecting data over a prolonged period of time. While the study design was best suited to capture continued use, future research might investigate how short-term users (less than 3 months) assess value to determine if it is consistent with our study’s <sup>fi</sup>ndings, or measure several 3-month intervals to capture the longer-term impact of value on continued use.

Finally, we were interested in better understanding value calculations and continued use of OSN services from the consumer’s perspective. However, providers aim to offer value propositions that enable consumers to co-create value (Vargo & Akaka, 2009). Exactly how and through what means value that is co-created bene<sup>fi</sup>ts the provider is an open empirical question. Utilizing a services perspective, future research needs to address designing IS for participative experiences that engender continued use while converting user-created content into provider value. This research might explore the reciprocal side of the dynamic, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally addressing how value is passed back to the service provider.

## Formative construct of continued use

Three measurement indicators of continued use were usage duration, frequency, and intensity (Venkatesh et al, 2008; Aurier & N’Goala, 2010). On the basis of the decision rules outlined in Jarvis et al (2003) and our analysis of measurement characteristics of continued use, we utilize a formative construct conceptualization. With relatively high correlations for duration and frequency (0.57), duration and intensity (0.50), and frequency and intensity (0.55), the conceptualization of continued use allows us to examine the structural effect of the given latent construct as opposed to that of multiple individual variables, enhancing the theoretical parsimony of the research model (Cenfetelli & Bassellier, 2009), re<sup>fl</sup>ecting a richer, higher-order construct (Burton-Jones & Straub, 2006).

Following established guidelines for testing indicator collinearity and validity of formative constructs, we conducted PLS analysis and presented a validity test model for the measurement of continued use as shown in

Figure C1. We <sup>fi</sup>nd maximum variance in<sup>fl</sup>ation factor of 1.37 is below the recommended cut-off value of 3.33 (Diamantopoulos & Siguaw, 2006; Petter et al, 2007). Thus, no indication of multicollinearity exists. Figure C1 illustrates the measurement validity of the formative construct using the weight or partial effect of the indicators on the intended construct (Cenfetelli & Bassellier, 2009). Each indictor’s effect on continued use (β = 0.40 for duration, 0.41 for frequency, and 0.39 for intensity) are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant (P<0.001), supporting its formative nature (Barki et al, 2007). Finally, in validating the external validity of continued use, we examined the proposed nomological relationships (Diamantopoulos & Winklhofer, 2001), <sup>fi</sup>nding acceptable model <sup>fi</sup>t (APC = 0.349, P<0.001; ARS = 0.251, P = <0.001; AVIF = 1.372). The PLS results in Figure C1 demonstrate the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationships between online social value and continued use (β = 0.11, P<0.001), and satisfaction and continued use (β = 0.43, P<0.001), with online social value and satisfaction explaining 24% of the variance in continued use. This supports the external validity of continued use as a formative construct.

## Mediation of satisfaction

To illustrate the partial mediating effect of satisfaction on the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of online social value to continued use, we follow the three-step analysis espoused by Baron & Kenny (1986). First, we found that online social value alone had a positive direct effect on continued use $( \beta = 0 . 5 3 , R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 2 8 , P < 0 . 0 0 1 ) ;$ second, we found that online social value had a positive direct effect on the mediator, satisfaction (β = 0.67, $R ^ { 2 } { = } 0 . 4 7 ,$ P<0.001). Finally, we found that online social value and satisfaction jointly had a positive signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect on continued use (β = 0.37, P<0.001; β = 0.26, P<0.001, respectively; $R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 3 2 )$ . These results support that the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of value calculations on continued use is partially mediated by satisfaction.
