# Enabling knowledge diversity to benefit cross-functional project teams: Joint roles of knowledge leadership and transactive memory system

Lianying Zhang<sup>⁎</sup>, Haiyan Guo<sup>1</sup>

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Knowledge leadership Knowledge diversity Cross-functional project team performance Transactive memory system

## A B S T R A C T

Team members’ knowledge diversity has a “double-edged sword” nature within cross-functional project teams (CFPTs), showing an inconsistent relationship with team performance. For realizing this diversity’s potential benefits, leadership is usually an essential enabler. However, little is known about how knowledge leadership achieves this. This study proposed that knowledge leadership moderates the efect of knowledge diversity on team performance through a transactive memory system (TMS). By empirically testing survey data from 96 CFPTs, we found that knowledge leadership enables a positive linkage of knowledge diversity-CFPT performance by successively breaking down barriers to communication and cooperation in TMS development and functioning.

## 1. Introduction

To enhance competitive advantages and benefits of diferent viewpoints, organizations increasingly rely on cross-functional project teams (CFPTs) [1]. This refers to a temporary workgroup that aims to complete a project within a limited time frame, consisting of representatives of various functional units (e.g., departments and organizations) who possess diverse experience, skills, and knowledge relevant to com pleting projects [2,3]. To achieve shared team goals, members usually engage in a collective process and gradually develop a transactive memory system (TMS) to coordinate and utilize their distributed diverse knowledge efectively [4]. A TMS is defined as the cooperative division of labor for encoding, storing, and retrieving information based on a shared understanding of who knows what within the team [5–7]. Many scholars have pointed out that TMS represents an essential in tangible force within cross-functional context, yielding anticipated performance gains from diverse knowledge resources, such as improved team learning [8], team innovation performance [9], team decision making performance [10], or specific project outcomes [8,11]. There fore, team members’ knowledge diversity (team composition input), TMS (teamwork process), and CFPT performance (output) potentially form a team efectiveness framework within CFPTs, according to the input-mediator-outcome (IMO) model [12].

However, knowledge diversity in such teams is increasing as their organizations require a diversification in the knowledge economy, and it is not easy to ensure team efectiveness because, as research suggests, knowledge diversity has a “double-edged” nature [13–17]. Diversified knowledge resources enhance the epistemic and learning motivation to process information [18]. Such motivation stimulates the autonomous development of a TMS and thus leads to high performance. Inversely, this diversity also induces social categorization phenomenon [19], and emerging complicated cognitive and social interaction processes are likely to impair TMS development and its efect on team performance. Taking the example of the cognitive process in software development projects, diferences in knowledge depth and domain lead increasingly to knowledge boundaries among IT professionals. Designers and coders may not develop a shared understanding of their expertise because of discrepancies in programming language, design methodology, etc. Reconciling conflicts usually requires support from project managers or knowledgeable team members. In the social interaction process, shared project goals generally steer team members toward cooperation. However, competition can prevail over cooperation when representatives of divergent subgroup goals vie for scarce resources (e.g., physical space, equipment, manpower, and capital funds) during a project [2]. Unbalanced coopetition or the pursuit of competitive advantage considerably inhibits the efectiveness of knowledge activities [2,3]. Developing a well-functioning TMS to maintain team efectiveness is, thus, fraught with challenges unless leaders enact the interventions that value knowledge management [20,21].

In current popularity of the literature on both diversity and TMS, scholars have devoted increasing attention to how formal leadership in a cross-functional context as a contingency determines whether the benefits of knowledge diversity can be realized or not [15] or as a catalyst facilitates the development of a well-functioning TMS [9,10,22]. Indeed, knowledge leaders such as team members with exemplary knowledge or project managers are often also outstanding enablers or regulators. Knowledge leadership emphasizes leaders’ roles in information and knowledge management, aiming to realize the advantage of the intellectual capital [23]. Yet, notably less research has investigated how to accentuate the positive efects and attenuate the negative efects of knowledge diversity on both the TMS and team performance under knowledge leadership in a CFPT. This results in limited understanding of how knowledge leaders intervene in typical situations arising from knowledge diversity.

Knowledge leadership is defined as a process whereby an individual influences and supports other group members in the continuous learning processes necessary to achieve group or organizational goals [24,25]. As a type of development-oriented power, it incorporates ambidextrous knowledge management (motivations and support routes) and relationship management strategies (cooperation and trust) [23–25]. These strategies jointly ensure the achievement of anticipated teamwork processes and performance gains. Existing researches have typically advocated its roles in promoting knowledge-related activities such as knowledge sharing and application [26,27] or group learning [28]. Importantly, the implementation of these activities relies considerably on an efective TMS [8,27]. The motivational information processing in groups (MIP-G) model posits that epistemic and prosocial motivations for information processing facilitate formation of a superior TMS and team outcomes improvement; such motivations can be raised by various factors especially the leadership or members with power [18]. Accordingly, knowledge leadership is a potential contingency in the deployment of motivational mechanisms, enabling positive linkages from knowledge diversity to CFPT performance through the influence on TMS development and functioning.

Overall, this research aims to answer two questions. First, how does knowledge leadership as a contextual condition moderate the relationship between the knowledge diversity and team performance in CFPTs? Second, does the TMS reveal the moderating efect of knowledge leadership in typical situations arising from team members knowledge diversity? Guided by the IMO team efectiveness framework [12] and MIP-G theory [18], this study provides a mediated moderation analysis integrating knowledge diversity, leadership, and TMS literature. It empirically tested the moderating efects of knowledge leadership on the tripartite relationship among team members’ knowledge diversity, the TMS, and CFPT performance. Furthermore, the TMS was tested to mediate knowledge leadership’s moderating efects in the relationship between knowledge diversity and CFPT performance.

By identifying knowledge leadership as a novel contingent factor in the efectiveness of knowledge diversity, especially in CFPTs, this research further highlights the role of leadership in a project work context. It provides a conceptual and empirical integration of research to further underscore the role of knowledge leadership in a motivational work design related to knowledge characteristics. Moreover, it depicts how knowledge leadership enables diverse knowledge resources to be transited into team performance through developing a well-functioning TMS. This study thus offers new insights to the controversial discussion about knowledge diversity by further elucidating how and under what conditions knowledge diversity can improve team performance in a temporary project context with time pressure. Furthermore, our research delves deeply into the issue around how knowledge leadership coordinates both the development and the function of a TMS to achieve the potential of knowledge diversity. This broadens the understanding of how knowledge leadership creates an ideal environment for advancing team knowledge activities. Together, this research ofers guidance to leaders and managers involved in CFPTs and their organizations about efectively coordinating and utilizing diversified knowledge resources to enable the team efectiveness.

## 2. Literature review and hypotheses development

## 2.1. Knowledge diversity and CFPT performance

In contemporary organizational theory, team members’ knowledge diversity is often portrayed as a “double-edged sword” [29]. Extensive studies have demonstrated that this diversity is equivocally related to innovation and group performance [14,16,30]. Typically, two theoretical approaches, information/decision-making processing and social categorization process, have been introduced to explain the ambiguous associations [19].

From the perspective of information/decision-making process, knowledge diversity presents a positive relationship with CFPT performance [29]. This perspective posits that teams with heterogeneous knowledge are more apt to produce a broader range of task-relevant cognitive resources for making decisions, promoting innovation ability and solving problems than teams with homogenous knowledge [19]. Moreover, heterogeneous teams are better equipped to coping with changes and exploring new opportunities, especially in a dynamic environment. Inversely, the social categorization perspective holds that similarities and diferences are used as a basis for categorizing self and others into diferent groups [19,31]. Members usually identify more strongly with their functional unit than with their collective team [3]. The outgroup bias leads to low group cohesion, exacerbating relation distance and dysfunctional social interaction [19,31,32]. Even worse, members with divergent goals might compete with each other in the pursuit of scarce resources and strategic priorities [33]. Unsafe motivational climate threatens team members’ commitment to project goals. Meanwhile, members are usually partaking in complex cognitive processes because of knowledge boundaries and cognitive conflicts when jointly completing team goals [34]. Thus, knowledge diversity also yields a negative efect on CFPT performance.

Considering the ambiguous linkage of knowledge diversity-team outcomes, increasing number of scholars have shifted focus on how and under what conditions knowledge diversity may improve or inhibit team performance [15,31,32,35,36]. “How condition” refers to the mediating mechanism that explains how knowledge diversity can or cannot translate into team performance. Based on IMO team efectiveness framework, the mediating mechanism embodies the interacting processes whereby team members work interdependently to utilize various resources or the emergent states that describe team’s cognitive, motivational, and afective states [12,35]. Previous researches also focused on the two perspectives to open the black box of mediating mechanism. From the information/decision-making perspective that posits the positive efect of knowledge diversity, knowledge sharing [31,32], team learning [36], and elaboration of task-related information [19,35] are considered as information processing process to explain how diversity translates into team outcomes. Alternatively, the social categorization process predicts the negative efects of knowledge diversity. Based on this, team emergent states such as team identification [35] and team cohesion [31] can also be regarded as the mediating mechanism.

“Under what condition” reflects when either the positive or the negative efects of diversity are more likely to prevail. Increasing researchers have proposed motivational conditions that are cognitive and pro-social in nature. They function by controlling a specific team process or emergent state above. For the cognitive motivational condition, Kearney, Gebert, and Voelpel [35] found that team’s need for cognition moderates the relationship between educational diversity and team performance, and this moderating efect is mediated by both information elaboration and team identification. From the afective perspective, Cheung, Gong, Wang, Zhou, and Shi [32] demonstrated that the efects that function diversity exerts on team innovation via knowledge sharing are contingent upon afective trust in cross-functional teams.

Despite significant findings, researchers continue to ignore the focal conditions for reconciling the equivocal linkage of knowledge diversity and anticipated performance gains in CFPTs. Such teams are often under high time pressure and require a high degree of timely knowl edge coordination to fully utilize their diverse knowledge resources. In this context, it is dificult to autonomously foster an active cognitive or afective motivational condition within a limited time. Cheung et al. [32] called for more diversity-related research to consider the joint moderating efect of afective trust and formal intervention in multidisciplinary teams. Shin and Zhou [15] also indicated that transformational leadership motivated teams’ creative eficacy and then moderated the relationship between educational specialization heterogeneity and team innovation. However, little attention has been paid to defining means of intervention based on a more appropriate leader behavior to promote knowledge coordination and utilization in CFPTs.

In the next sections, the authors turn to more detailed theoretical analysis firstly of TMS as a potential teamwork process to dissect how members’ knowledge diversity can translate into team performance. Based on the process mechanism clarified, this study further explores how knowledge leadership can act as a potential moderator enabling knowledge diversity to be linked to CFPT performance through a TMS.

## 2.2. TMS as a teamwork process in CFPTs

The TMS is an emergent property of a team that describes a shared understanding of “who knows what” within the team [7]. This construct conceptualized and measured in this study follows the definition and measurement proposed by Lewis [5], and it refers to knowledgerelated transactive processes including encoding, storing, and retrieving information among team members [5,6].

In advancing TMS theory, numerous scholars have long explored its antecedents, components, and consequences of TMS [6,37]. Among them, team composition inputs such as member diversity are recognized as an essential factor that afects a TMS. Both Peltokorpi [38] and Ren and Argote [37] have called for further research to explore what types of diversity can hinder or facilitate a TMS in various settings. Beyond information processing, the notion of TMS reflects other multiple processes for developing and leveraging specialized knowl edge (e.g., social cognitive process [39] and knowledge coordination and utilization [4]). Grifith and Neale [40] noted that teams often transform their nascent knowledge into transactive memory and group outcomes through several processes. Reciprocal exchange and joint efort between individual team members depend on the extent to which team members get to know one another and establish routines for in teraction and task accomplishment [8]. A TMS thus represents the prerequisite for knowledge sharing, team learning, and elaborating task-related information. Moreover, team identification as an emergent state can also improve team performance through promoting TMS [41]. This all suggests that a TMS interacts with other mechanisms linking diversity to team performance. However, current researches have not systematically examined the mediating role of a TMS in knowledge diversity management. The “black box” linking inputs with outcomes (i.e., mediators) are one of the natures articulated in the IMO team efectiveness framework [12]. This research emphasizes this nature and adopts the TMS as a mediating mechanism to explain how knowledge diversity (input) can or cannot translate into CFPT performance (output).

From the information/decision-making perspective, TMS could be a necessary teamwork process. According to MIP-G theory, diferent new and usefulness knowledge within a CFPT can trigger members’ epistemic motivation for information processing [18]. Ghobadi and D'Ambra [3] and Lewis [42] have shown that members tend to utilize their knowledge and expertise in conjunction with that of other members; and they also learn more information in their own specializations if they believe that others possess diferent rather than similar expertise. As a result, transactive memory emerges. When solving taskrelated problems, members of CFPTs with high knowledge diversity are more likely to access knowledge inside teams rather than outside the teams [43]. Because there may be a low cost involved and a timely manner in seeking assistance from other team members especially the ones identifying with common goals or by the aid from knowledge leaders [43,44], benefits of the help from others can gradually increase cognitive trust among members. Gradually, a well-developed TMS is formulated spontaneously through intercommunication and interactive learning. Liao, O'Brien, Jimmieson, and Restubog [41] indicated that perceived a high-quality communication leads to a well-developed TMS. From this, greater amount of high quality and task-relevant knowledge is brought to bear on collective tasks [5,44]. Over time, members can also deepen their expertise in their areas. Correlative studies have demonstrated the positive efects of TMS on a variety of group outcomes such as group learning, team innovation, efectiveness, and member satisfaction [37].

However, according to the social categorization perspective, knowledge cooperation encounters great challenges in such processes as cognition and social interaction. As a result, there are negative or null linkages of knowledge diversity to TMS as well as TMS to team performance [45,46]. Nevertheless, Lewis and Herndon [6] argued that expertise diversity can benefit both the usefulness of TMS and group performance under some conditions (e.g., cooperative goal structure and intellective task output). Thus, the extent to which TMS as a teamwork process can link the knowledge diversity input to the output of CFPT performance depends on some specific conditions.

Related empirical studies have proposed these conditions in terms of promoting the development of TMS or the efect of TMS on team outcomes. Notably, social and communicative processes are underlying conditions for fostering a TMS [6,37,41,47]. As mentioned before, the shared team/project goals and the communication among members within teams have been considered to be fundamental factors. They ensure the positive efect of members’ knowledge diversity on developing a TMS in CFPTs. Despite this, their efects are limited and must be enabled by an active control or leading process especially in the tem porary project with time pressure and diversified members. Hsu, Chu, Lin, and Lo [21] suggested that the intellectual capital, especially the knowledge leader, exerts crucial roles in both spanning knowledge boundaries and reconciling the cognitive conflict between subgroups. Furthermore, cross-functional social interactions are often forced under the condition that competition and cooperation occur jointly [33,48]. Unhealthy motivational atmosphere threatens team members’ cooperative motivation and thus inhibits the development of TMS [2.37] Even worse, members may not identify with the shared goals but in stead seek their own interests at the expense of others’. Balancing and intervening in such interaction also require the support from knowledge leaders [20]. Cabeza Pulles et al. [22] found that the efect of network ties on the development of TMS was contingent upon leadership.

Regarding the TMS’ function of improving team performance, interventions from the knowledge leaders are also necessary. Key functions of TMS focus not only on a high quality of knowledge coordination but also on the proficiency of members’ specialization. The competitive advantage of knowledge could induce members to withhold their knowledge and then reduce the quality of accessing knowledge within teams [2]. In turn, this outcome induces ineficient and inefective knowledge coordination and hinders members’ commitment to attaining project goals. Although frequent communication presents, the quality of TMS cannot be guaranteed without a proper motivational climate fostered by the knowledge leaders. Prior empirical studies also noted that TMS function was influenced by some contingent factors such as trust level [47], membership exchange [37], and transforma tional leadership [9].

In summary, interventions by formal or informal knowledge leaders underscore active cooperation and communication by pursuing shared team goals. Although unlikely to have a direct efect, such knowledge leadership is nonetheless an indispensable force in promoting TMS development and function. Yet, it remains unclear about how knowledge leadership afects TMS to achieve efective knowledge coordina tion and utilization in CFPTs.

## 2.3. The roles of knowledge leadership

Along with the demand for efective information and knowledge management, knowledge leadership is increasingly recognized as an essential power of organizations and teams [25]. Of the two types of knowledge leaders, one consists of those with formal authority and the other comprises informal leaders who may not possess any oficial authority but are generalists with well-rounded knowledge [23,24,26]. No matter what their rank and title, these two types of leaders share the common feature of preferring to work as coaches or advisers who develop a collective energy to the advantage of intellectual capital and knowledge value. Such leaders usually value knowledge management and team learning [49]. Moreover, they foster a relational context emphasizing openness to experience, cooperation, and trust in support of information and knowledge management.

More specifically, knowledge leadership focuses on three central aspects: basic leadership elements (communication and motivation), relational strategies, and means of realizing efective knowledge management. First, as Donate and Sánchez de Pablo [49] elaborated, knowledge leaders in CFPTs also primarily play a crucial role in allocating areas of expertise, subsequently acting as advisers enabling team members to recognize project knowledge management initiatives and their job. As the project continues, it is essential for knowledge leaders to communicate clearly team’s expectations in terms of each member’s work. Second, knowledge leadership involves both implicit and explicit motivations (e.g., charisma, role modeling, and rewards). A knowledge leader’s main goal is to act as a role model and design strategies to motivate members’ continuous learning and participation in knowledge activities [24,28,49]. Third, as Zhang and Cheng [26] elaborated, knowledge leaders usually adopt relational strategies to foster a cooperative and trust-oriented relational context [50]. Last, knowledge leaders value the establishment of efective socio-cognitive and tech nological routes to achieve knowledge integration and creation [23,51]. Building socio-cognitive routes involves a variety of operational components, including job rotation and personnel transfers, organizing forums or meetings among functional units, ongoing training and development eforts, continuous learning in projects, and knowledge sharing through written documents [23,51]. Extensive use of e-mail networks, information systems, and knowledge management systems is essential to develop technological networks [23]. Guidance by knowledge leaders and the use of such routes break down communication barriers and increase communication eficiency among team members. Under all of these foci, members also perceive a mastery motivational climate reflecting the extent to which the team context values specialization development, cooperation, and learning [24,52,53]. Unlike the above researches focusing on a single strategy. knowledge leadership in this study underlines the interplay of knowledge management and relational strategies.

When team members receive the influence, guidance, and support in a high-knowledge-leadership context, they become committed to collective goals and active in learning and knowledge activities [49]. They also become more equipped to exchange information, learn across knowledge boundaries, and contribute their knowledge and time to ward task completion. Moreover, frequent discussions and interactions facilitate superior or unique solutions to problems or novel means of task performance [54]. Accordingly, high knowledge leadership can stimulate members’ need for cognition and epistemic motivation, causing them to search and acquire information and thus make more eficient use of the vast pool of information and knowledge resources [28,55]. The combined eforts of knowledge leaders and team members can refine product and decision-making quality, shorten project schedules, and ultimately promote CFPT performance. Additionally, the relational context fosters a comfortable and reciprocal interaction environment [56]. MIP-G theory also suggests that cooperative social interactions within teams can stimulate members’ prosocial motivation, which can enhance members’ epistemic motivation [18]. Such an environment is thus beneficial to seek and share information and mitigate dysfunctional interactions such as outgroup bias, tension, and conflict [32,56]. Creation, sharing, integration, and utilization of knowledge can be promoted when this climate is high [57]. Accordingly, CFPTs with a higher degree of knowledge leadership can realize the positive efect of knowledge diversity on CFPT performance. Therefore, this research proposes the following hypothesis:

## H1. Knowledge leadership positively moderates the relationship between knowledge diversity and CFPT performance.

As explained in the preceding section, a TMS helps to uncover how knowledge diversity translates into team performance. Cabeza Pulles, LLorens Montes, and Gutierrez-Gutierrrez [22] and Hammedi, Riel, and Sasovova [10] have indicated that leadership is crucial in directly or indirectly predicting TMS development. Based on them, this study argues that knowledge leadership can also enable the spontaneous development of TMS triggered by CFPT members’ knowledge diversity. Specifically, knowledge leaders who take the initiative in social settings can enable the allocation of expertise more visibly [10]. Establishing socio-cognitive networks can increase members’ awareness of both what expertise other members possess and where to access the knowl edge they require. In particular, the available technological networks developed under high knowledge leadership enable members to communicate frequently and leverage their specialization in CFPTs with high time pressure and low team proximity [27]. Moreover, Chiang, Shih, and Hsu [58] found that implementing a set of coherent human resource management practices could predict a TMS through incentive mechanisms in new product development teams. This implies that high knowledge leadership that designs reward system can also facilitate members’ interaction and participation in knowledge activities, thus enhancing their need for cognition and thereby promoting both their specializations and the team’s knowledge coordination ability [56]. Overall, the joint interaction of knowledge leadership behaviors re inforces members’ epistemic motivation deriving from knowledge diversity. The higher degree of knowledge leadership is more likely to achieve the positive efect of the knowledge diversity on developing a TMS.

Moreover, a high degree of knowledge leadership can attenuate the twofold adverse efects of knowledge diversity on the development of a TMS. Specifically, when knowledge boundaries or cognitive conflicts emerge among team members, knowledge leaders can work as a boundary spanner or catalyst to provide reasonable explanations or intellectual support [21,28]. Beyond such support, the socio-cognitive and technical routes established by knowledge leaders also provide active communication channels whereby members can deepen their understanding of who knows what and how to develop a common knowledge base and common specialized terminology [21,43]. All of these support mechanisms can alleviate the adverse efect regarding the cognitive aspect of knowledge diversity on developing a TMS. Meanwhile, Hansen, Mors, and Løvås [43] found that members channel knowledge-related activities toward rather than outside the team if a satisfactory relational context has been created. Relational strategies enacted by knowledge leaders are reflected in 1) acting as a model for valuing cooperation in work; and 2) increasing social interdependency among members when designing job [2]. Therefore, a high degree of knowledge leadership enables members to approach pro-social motivation and complete tasks together with members of other functional units in a cooperative or benignly competitive manner [20]. A climate with a high degree of psychological safety can also increase trust in other members’ expertise and the willingness to exchange knowledge [44]. Accordingly, members can deepen their specialization when both a trust climate and efective knowledge coordination emerge. Conversely, when knowledge leadership is low, dysfunctional interaction and a competitive motivational climate prevail over knowledge cooperation. Therefore, this study proposes the following hypothesis:

H2. Knowledge leadership positively moderates the relationship between knowledge diversity and TMS.

Furthermore, knowledge leadership can enhance the efect of a TMS on team performance. High knowledge leadership devotes to culti vating a mastery motivational climate, under which members tend to diminish their unhealthy competitive motivation and enhance pro-social motivation [18,52,53]. Their tendency to hoard knowledge is therefore reduced, whereas their commitment to achieving project goals strengthens. Černe, Hernaus, Dysvik, and Škerlavaj [52] found that members in a mastery climate do not engage in reciprocal knowledge hiding but actively contribute their expertise, as the more beneficial option. Consequently, more high-quality task-relevant knowledge can be applied to collective tasks. Through socio-cognitive and technological routes, tasks can be allocated to better match with members expertise while the problems can be better matched to the person with the necessary information [23,59]. Such enhanced matching capability promotes the efectiveness and eficiency of knowledge coordination; thus, problems encountered during task completion can be solved in an efective and time manner. Moreover, members can continuously deepen their expertise when interacting with others. Therefore, this research proposes:

H3. Knowledge leadership positively moderates the relationship between TMS and CFPT performance.

H2 suggests that both knowledge diversity and knowledge leadership are conditions for the development of a TMS. Under the influence of knowledge leaders, members with diverse knowledge resources may gradually come to identify with the shared team goal. Meanwhile, TMS development intangibly integrates distributed knowledge. H3 indicates that knowledge leadership enables useful knowledge to be applied in problem-solving and decision-making, thus achieving team goals. Accordingly, knowledge leadership can devote more eforts to enhan cing the indirect path. That is, developing a well-functional TMS that potentially links knowledge diversity to performance. However, the initial moderating efect of knowledge leadership on the association between knowledge diversity and CFPT performance subsequently becomes less crucial. Hammedi, Riel, and Sasovova [10] argued that leadership is of great influence in the coordination process within crossfunctional teams. Thereby, TMS as a mediating variable causes the di rectly interactive efect of knowledge leadership and knowledge diversity on CFPT performance, denoted in H1, to be eliminated. Overall, this research predicts that a TMS is responsible for the initial moderating efect of knowledge leadership (H1). Therefore, this study proposes the following hypothesis:

H4. TMS mediates the moderating efect of knowledge leadership on the relationship between knowledge diversity and CFPT performance.

## 3. Methodology

## 3.1. Survey sample and data collection

The quantitative data in this research were collected by a questionnaire survey conducted in 96 CFPTs in China, and this survey lasted for 11 months. The nonprobability convenience sampling method was employed, which was an efective approach to improve the eficiency of data collection and response rates [60]. Accordingly, the authors purposely selected the organizations in which CFPTs were relatively pre valent (e.g., new product development teams), or there was more than one case using CFPT in the last three years. The diverse samples used in this research increased the generalizability of our results.

With respect to respondents, most of them were receiving education or training from our college’s center of professional degree and executive development program (accounting for 59.4%). Others were selected to directly contact the HR department by phone or e-mail (accounting for 40.6%). The authors ensured that the respondents were willing to take part in the survey and met our requirements. All the targeted respondents selected are based on three criteria: (a) possessing suficient knowledge to answer the questionnaire; (b) an adequate level of involvement with regard to the issues under the investigation; and c) who had participated in two or more project teams. Great care was taken with collecting data to reduce the potential efect of common method bias. For example, the instrument measuring CFPT performance adopted the data completed by the team leader or organization’s human resources department.

Before the data collection, a pre-test for the clarity and practicability of items was conducted. Six team leaders and 18 team members, who were involved in CFPTs, were invited to ofer comments related to items. And then minor formatting adjustments were made based on their feedback. In the phase of data collection, paper-and-pencil questionnaires were administered to 500 individuals of sample CFPTs. Respondents filling questionnaires were connected by a face-to-face talk. For respondents, average year of experience was 9.61 years and most respondents (89.9%) received a bachelor’s degree or higher level (master’s degree, 56.6%; doctoral degree, 20.4%) (see Table 2).

## 3.2. Construct measurement

The survey instrument was composed of three sections: 1) personal information, firm and team information, and the marker variable; 2) items for knowledge diversity, TMS, knowledge leadership; and 3) items for CFPT performance. Each item was measured by 5-point Likert scale, with values ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree).

Knowledge diversity of team members $( r _ { w g } ^ { } = 0 . 7 9 , \mathrm { I C C 1 } = 0 . 3 1 _ { \ : \mathrm { z } }$ $\operatorname { I C C 2 } = 0 . 8 2 )$ is a construct in team level and articulates the nature of input informational element that drives team efectiveness. The diferences in members’ educational, functional, and occupational background, their wide range of industry experience, and their skills reflect the diversity of knowledge among the members [61]. Although Blau [62] index of heterogeneity has been widely cited to measure a specific diversity, it is not easy to measure the knowledge diversity with multiple diversity types integrally. Beyond this, another recent measuring method of knowledge diversity is the three-items scale regarding content, processing, and tacitness of the knowledge [14,16]. But this method is a bit abstract and theoretical that it may increase the dificulty for team members in understanding the questions. To ensure the measuring validity, this research adopted a broader scope of manifest diversity types, which are available to know in work practices to assess the knowledge diversity with a five-item scale based on the elaboration in Milliken and Martins [61].

TMS $( r _ { w g } \ = 0 . 7 5 , \mathrm { I C C 1 } = 0 . 3 0 ; \mathrm { I C C 2 } = 0 . 8 1 ) \colon$ : The empirical review of Ren and Argote [37] concluded the divergence in the measurement of TMS despite the convergence on TMS’s definition. Among these measurements, the one proposed by Lewis [5] has been the most widely employed in the laboratory and field. He demonstrated that TMS contains three key subdimensions as specialization, trust, and coordination. Each dimension was measured by five items. This study also adopted these items to measure TMS.

CFPT performance reflects the extent to which the project is suc cessful. It is usually denoted by indicators regarding the efectiveness and eficiency in completing project tasks, quality of innovation, and team satisfaction [8,32,63]. To evaluate CFPT performance integrally, this research chose external and internal performance as two dimensions both with four items. The former dimension included project effectiveness and eficiency while the latter consisted of members attitude toward team satisfaction. All items were adopted from the research of Zhang and Zhang [63], the context of which was also cross functional teams and appropriate for our study.

Knowledge leadership $( r _ { w g } \ = 0 . 7 2 , \ \mathrm { I C C 1 } = 0 . 2 4 , \ \mathrm { I C C 2 } = 0 . 7 6 ) \colon$ For measuring knowledge leadership, there exist three kinds of scales. The origins of them are the researches by Viitala [24], Donate and Sánchez de Pablo [49], and Yang, Huang, and Hsu [25], respectively. Among them, the first two are addressed in organizational context while the last one has been validated in project context and is more consistent with our research topic [26]. Thus, this study adjusted the scale with 11 items developed by Yang, Huang, and Hsu [25] to measure knowledge leadership in CFPTs. The construct of knowledge leadership included three dimensions: leadership skills, cooperation and trust, and knowledge integration and innovation.

Control variables: As suggested in previous researches (e.g., [8,25]), the authors selected project complexity, team size, team age, and average education level as the control variables in this study.

## 3.3. Analysis tool and data quality

## 3.3.1. Nonresponse Bias

This study did chi-square diference test by comparing the characteristics of respondents with the non-respondents, the results of which suggest that nonresponse bias was of no importance.

## 3.3.2. Common method bias

First, our research design avoided this bias as much as possible when collecting data. For example, the authors first sorted all the items in the multiple-scales randomly to refrain from respondents conjecturing the research aim. Second, the analysis data of the dependent variable (CFPT performance) in team level was from team leaders or organization’s human resources department while team members assessed other variables. Third, following Chin et al. [64], the authors also used a measured latent marker variable approach to detect the common method bias in this research. The marker variable has no significant correlation with the endogenous latent construct (TMS). Thus, common method bias might not present a problem in this study.

## 3.3.3. Aggregation analysis

Considering that the scales were measured by personal interview, the individual surveys should be aggregated at the team level. This research calculated the $r _ { w g }$ coeficient [65] for each scale (ranging from 0.72 to 0.79) $( r _ { w g } ~ \geq . 6 )$ . Intra-class correlations (ICC1 and ICC2) were also estimated and their results satisfied the aggregation criteria [66].

## 3.3.4. Analysis tool

Partial least squares-structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) has been regarded as an emerging methodological choice in quantitative research for models with latent variables [67,68]. PLS-SEM sets up no parametric assumptions and addresses the significance of the relationships between variables, instead of analyzing the overall model fit, as in covariance-based SEM [67]. When the sample size is relatively low, PLS-SEM can serve as an efective tool to measure the latent constructs [67]. Also, it allows for the estimation of the confirmatory factor analysis and the structural relations simultaneously. Considering relatively low sample size (96 sample teams), PLS-SEM was employed to examine both the measurement model and structural model, through which hypotheses test can be completed.

Research on diversity has indicated that cognitive or prosocial motivational conditions (e.g., afective trust [32], teams’ need for cognition [35]) can reconcile the equivocal efect of knowledge-related diversity on outcomes in general cross-functional teams. However, a single motivational condition is easily undermined in typical CFPTs. Although high group longevity can ensure the stability of motivational states [31], the limited time for project completion requires that sta bility be developed through team interventions as soon as possible. Moving beyond the aforementioned research, our results from H1 reveal that a high degree of knowledge leadership in the typical CFPTs is a vital force behind accentuating the benefits and attenuating the downsides of members’ knowledge diversity in promoting team performance. This force depends on the interaction between knowledge management strategy and relational strategy, which drives both epistemic and pro-social motivation for information processing. This finding is in line with that of the preceding researches that emphasize the necessity of motivational conditions for diversity management. However, our study goes beyond these researches by empirically demonstrating that knowledge leadership, a concept focusing on knowledge management initiatives, leverages the potential inherent in members’ knowledge diversity by deploying twofold motivation for information processing. This assists in filling the knowledge gap on the joint moderating efect of afective trust and formal team intervention in multidisciplinary teams, a productive direction for future research proposed by Cheung et al. Furthermore, Shin and Zhou [15] corroborated the moderating efect of transformational leadership on keeping the benefits of educational specialization heterogeneity consistent with our finding concerning the role of a formal leader. Furthermore, our study advances the leadership literature by emphasizing the roles of a formal or informal knowledge leader in realizing the potential benefits of knowledge diversity. Knowledge leadership can explain the inconsistent findings of the relationship between diversity and performance. Together, this ofers a better understanding of the interplay of leadership and team diversity and complements previously studied contingencies regarding cognitive or prosocial motivational conditions in broader diversity literature.

Besides, another point diference from other research, specifically that of Kearney et al. [35], is our finding that knowledge diversity tends to have a positive efect on CFPT performance even without the intervention of knowledge leadership (see Fig. 3). This is because knowledge diversity, as a type of cognitive diversity, may induce fewer social categorization cues than demographic heterogeneity and may also be attributable to the specific geographical setting (China) in which this study was developed. This suggests that a climate, regardless whether one of leadership or collectivism, may be viewed as the force that reconciles the equivocal efect of knowledge diversity on team performance.

Second, the findings for H2˜H4 deepen our understanding of how knowledge diversity translates into CFPT performance by suggesting the crucial mediating role of a TMS. Taking a further step, our study also identifies the TMS as a new teamwork mechanism that explains the efectiveness of knowledge leadership in accentuating the benefits of knowledge diversity for CFPT performance.

The results in H2˜H4 indicate that a TMS can intangibly integrate distributed knowledge resources and gradually transfer the efects of knowledge diversity. However, a TMS can link the knowledge diversity to team performance only in teams with a high level of knowledge leadership. This supplements the finding in H1 with new insights on how knowledge leadership enables members’ knowledge diversity to translate into CFPT performance mainly through promoting knowledge coordination and utilization. This finding agrees with those of Kearney et al. [35] and Shin and Zhou [15], namely that team processes or emergent states can reveal the moderating efect of its corresponding moderator around diversity issues. Nevertheless, broader diversity studies have refrained from opening the “black box” linking the knowledge diversity to team performance in CFPTs. This represents a specific process of integrating distributed diversified knowledge resources for prompt realization of knowledge coordination and utilization within teams. This study addresses this knowledge gap by providing empirical evidence that a TMS revealed the interactive efect of knowledge diversity and knowledge leadership on CFPT performance.

Research on knowledge leadership has emphasized its roles in teamwork processes such as knowledge management (e.g., knowledge creation, sharing, and application) [26,49] or group learning [28]. Viitala [24] suggested that knowledge leaders support learning in their units by creating an ideal environment. Our findings point to a novel interpretation of recent studies indicating that knowledge leadership’s role in reconciling the “double-edged” efect of members’ knowledge diversity may be the key to attain such an environment. This study thus extends the role of knowledge leadership by endeavoring to empirically link it to a TMS. In this way, CFPTs can indeed drive knowledge management and learning initiatives and thus achieve shared goals.

Third, to develop a comprehensive understanding of how leadership accentuates the performance benefits of knowledge diversity, it is essential to examine leadership’s role in the synergies between TMS development and functioning. Another core contribution of this study thus consists in further identifying how knowledge leadership coordinates the development and the function of a TMS, initiating a dynamic and process nature in the temporary project context (H2 and H3).

Specifically, Fig. 4 illustrates how members’ knowledge diversity turns its negative efect on the TMS into a positive one through a high degree of knowledge leadership. Considering this result together with the findings for H1, the negative efect of knowledge diversity on TMS may stem primarily from cognitive misalignment and knowledge boundary spanning rather than from categorization processing. Therefore, this study reveals that the main barrier in developing a TMS is communication breakdown. The participation and support of knowledge leaders in a social context focus mainly on guiding communication among members expertly. By doing this, members can accurately locate and retrieve information and knowledge. As an indirect impact, a trustoriented relational context is also fostered by knowledge leadership, thereby enhancing members’ epistemic motivation for information possessing. Frequent social interaction within teams ensures an eficient TMS. This not only indicates why knowledge diversity as a predictor did not influence TMS but also reveals the key function of knowledge leadership on TMS development.

Skilton and Dooley [46] argued that a TMS under repetitive colla. boration situation can be detrimental to creative performance. Our finding for H3 reveals that such adverse function is also produced by a TMS in competitive condition. It appears that frequent social interaction deepens the understanding of not only who knows what but also potential conflicts of interest among members of diferent functional units. In this case, a mastery motivational climate shaped by knowledge leadership encourages members to donate rather than hide knowledge, enabling mutual development and thereby achieving team goals. With regard to the results in Fig. 5, the specific geographical setting (China) of this study also plays a certain role in this process. Thus, the role of knowledge leadership in enabling TMS’s function on team performance lies in balancing knowledge cooperation and competition.

Together, the findings from H2 and H3 provide a detailed explanation of why knowledge diversity predicts CFPT performance; namely, knowledge leadership successively breaks down barriers to communication and cooperation in TMS development and function within CFPTs. As such, TMS can ensure that knowledge diversity translates into team performance. Research on TMSs has confirmed transformational leadership’s direct efect on predicting a TMS in top management teams or its moderating efect in research teams [9,10]. Comparing with them, this study ofers evidence that knowledge diversity does not always hinder the development of a well-functioning TMS because of the motivation intervention from both formal and in formal knowledge leaders in a temporary project context. This further broadens the understanding of how knowledge leadership creates an ideal environment for advancing team knowledge activities and meanwhile extends the research on the relationship between leadership and TMS by identifying a dynamic and integrated efect of knowledge leadership on a TMS that realizes the potential of knowledge diversity.

Lastly, this study articulates the stimulating efect of knowledge diversity and the multiple contingent efects of knowledge leadership by borrowing the MIP-G theory. This research enriches the application of this theory and contributes to its development, by suggesting that factors such as knowledge leadership, with its focus on enhancing epistemic and prosocial motivation, play an essential role in team members’ information processing.

## 5.2. Managerial implications

The increasing demand for making full use of members’ knowledge diversity to enhance performance has gained shared cognition among practitioners in CFPTs. Despite the fact that knowledge diversity may induce competition and dysfunctional social interaction, it also engenders great benefits if project managers play efective roles in enabling an efective teamwork process (TMS). Consequently, it is crucial for project managers or knowledgeable members to realize their behaviors’ in propelling ambidextrous strategies (knowledge management and relationship management). By leveraging such influence, communication barrier should be first broke down in the development of TMS. For example, project managers should increase the necessary meetings and training, build efective electronic networks, or use social media (e.g., e-mail, telephone, or Wechat) to familiarize members with each other earlier. With today’s development of business globalization, knowledge leaders should also value the use of technological networks. They can develop advanced information technology to support both social interaction and knowledge integration and application, especially in diverse-intensive or high geographically distributed CFPTs.

Then, knowledge leaders should balance the knowledge coopetition regarding the knowledge diversity level. In teams with homogeneous knowledge, they can more prone to encourage members to upgrade their education and specialization by exploratory learning and to share new knowledge with other team members timely. Alternatively, when in heterogeneous teams, knowledge leaders should motivate members to enhance trust and collaboration and develop healthy competition with members in other functional units. Especially for those CFPTs with high knowledge diversity, knowledge leaders should act as teachers to help members span knowledge boundary, reconcile cognitive conflict, realize the benefits of learning from competitors, and encourage them to learn continuously. Meanwhile, they should accentuate a mastery motivational and psychological safety climate to attenuate unhealthy competitive motivations through leveraging their influence and power. These measures propel members in functional units to enhance epistemic and prosocial motivation and to engage in mutual cooperation, learning, and development.

Additionally, a team leader or some members with knowledge lea dership skills can be selected when the team builds. With the devel opment of professional careers, project managers should gather knowledge leadership experience and then practice adopting knowledge leadership in CFPTs. Beyond knowledge leadership, this research also implies that cultural factors can also afect the benefits of knowl edge diversity in CFPTs. Chinese culture values collectivism, cooperation, and harmony. CFPT members in China usually believe that “harmony is priceless.” In this cultural orientation, despite the universal existence of conflicts and competition, members inspired by task motivation tend to develop harmonious relationship with other members and learn from their diferent knowledge or ideas. Information processing may prevail over the categorization process. Overall, CFPTs are encouraged to develop or value a collectivist context.

## 5.3. Limitations and future research

While this study ofers significant insights, the following limitations should also be taken into consideration. First, our research was undertaken in numerous Chinese CFPTs. The results of this research may be diferent in other cultural contexts (e.g., individualism), as a national culture can be a crucial factor that impacts the team environment in which CFPTs utilize diverse knowledge resources. Thus, future researchers are encouraged to extend our study in other cultural or crosscultural contexts. Second, the function of TMS and leadership are dynamic, especially at diferent stages of the CFPT life cycle. However, this study focused on their functions in information and knowledge management at a certain stage of a project, without considering their impacts at diferent stages of projects. A longitudinal study is en couraged to discover their dynamic functions more comprehensively. Furthermore, our research also raises critical unanswered questions that the authors hope future researchers can examine. First, there exist al ternative conditions or mediators such as ethical climate, psychological safety, or perspective taking in CFPTs. Future studies could investigate how these alternative conditions or mediators afect the potential of knowledge diversity comprehensively. Particularly, they can also test in particular whether TMS moderates the relationship between knowledge diversity and outcomes under alternative conditions. Second, considering the typical condition of CFPTs regarding the coexistence of cooperation and competition in knowledge activities, the authors encourage further researchers to empirically test 1) the efect of knowledge leadership on followers’ competitive or cooperative motivational orientation; and 2) whether knowledge leadership can promote knowledge boundary spanning to foster a well-functional TMS.
