## Abstract

IT governance arrangements refers to the patterns of authority for key IT activities in business firms, including IT infrastructure, IT use, and project management. During the last 20 years, three primary modes of IT governance have become prevalent: centralized, decentralized, and the federal mode. These modes vary in the extent to which corporate IS, divisional IS, and line management are vested with authority for the key IT activities. While a significant volume of research has examined the influence of contingency factors on the choice of a specific mode of IT governance, most of this research has examined the singular effects of the contingency factors. The assumption underlying these studies is as though the organizational contingencies act in isolation in influencing the mode of IT governance. However, in reality, business firms are subject to the pulls and pressures of multiple, rather than singular, contingency forces. Therefore, to acknowledge this reality, this study applies the theory of multiple contingencies to examine how contingency forces influence the mode of IT governance. The theory argues that contingency forces interact with each other by either amplifying, dampening, or overriding their mutual influences on the IT governance mode. Three scenarios of multiple, interacting contingencies are identified: reinforcing, conflicting, and dominating. Each of these scenarios of multiple contingencies is hypothesized to influence a particular mode of IT governance. Utilizing rich data from case studies of eight firms, empirical evidence is presented to support these hypotheses. Implications of the multiple contingencies theory for research and for practice are presented.

Keywords: IS organization design, multiple contingencies, federal governance, qualitative research

## Introduction

Today, information technologies (IT) present many opportunities for firms to enhance or transform their products, services, markets, work processes, and business relationships. Such initiatives, however, demand the intertwined efforts of technology and business specialists (Boynton et al. 1994). Therefore, research attention has begun to focus on the nature of IT capabilities and organization designs that will enable firms to exploit the business potential of IT (Broadbent and Weill 1997; Henderson and Venkatraman 1992; Rockart et al. 1996; Ross et al. 1996; Sambamurthy and Zmud, 1994).

Prior IS research suggests that three distinct spheres of IT activities underlie IT-based innovation: IT infrastructure management, IT use management, and project management (Clark et al. 1997; Cross et al. 1997; Sambamurthy and Zmud 1994; Weill and Broadbent 1998). IT infrastructure management consists of decisions that address the nature of hardware and software platforms, annual enhancements to these platforms, the nature of network and data architectures, and the corporate standards for procurement and deployment of IT assets (LaBelle and Nyce 1987; von Simson 1990; Weill and Broadbent 1998; Wilder 1990). IT use management consists of decisions that address applications prioritization and (short-term and long-term) planning, budgeting, and the day-to-day delivery of operations and services (LaBelle and Nyce 1987; von Simson 1990; Wilder 1990). Project management involves blending knowledge of IT infrastructure capabilities and capacities with knowledge associated for the conceptualization, acquisition, development and deployment of information systems applications (Curtis et al., 1988; Slevin and Pinto 1987; Walz et al. 1993).

The above three spheres of activities are directed, controlled, and coordinated through IT governance arrangements. Governance arrangements, hence, represent an organization's IT-related authority patterns. Typically, three sets of stakeholders are provided varying degrees of decision authority in designing IT governance arrangements: corporate IS, divisional IS, and line management (Brown and Magill 1994; Sambamurthy and Zmud 1993).

Over the last 20 years, three primary modes of IT governance arrangements have emerged (Table 1). With the centralized governance mode, corporate IS has the authority for all three of the spheres of IT activities. With the decentralized governance mode, divisional IS and line management assume authority for all IT activities. However, as illustrated in Table 1, five different variations in decentralized IT governance are observable in practice. These variations depict different patterns of allocation of authority for IT infrastructure management, IT use management, and project management decisions among the divisional IS staff and line management. Finally, with the federal governance mode, both corporate IS and the business units (either the divisional IS or line management) assume authority for specific spheres of IT activities (Brown and Magill 1994; Dixon and John 1989; LaBelle and Nyce 1987; LaPlante 1991; Rockart 1988; von Simson 1990; Zmud, 1988). As Table 1 illustrates, three primary variations of federal governance mode are observable in practice. Further, within each of these primary variations, there might be even more variations depending upon whether divisional IS or line management is the primary authority. For example, Table 1 shows the first federal governance pattern as allocating project management authority to either divisional IS or line management. Similar variations can be observed in the other federal governance patterns.

Observations of practice indicate that considerable diversity exists in the patterns of IT governance arrangements across contemporary firms. Understanding how and why a firm has adopted a specific IT governance arrangement is important in order to advance knowledge about the effectiveness of alternative governance arrangements for sustaining IT-based innovation. Prior research has examined the influence of a variety of factors: industry (Ahituv et al. 1989; Clark 1992), firm size (Ahituv et al. 1989; Brown and Magill 1994; Clark 1992; Tavakolian 1989), corporate strategy (Brown and Magill 1994; Tavakolian 1989), and corporate structure (Ahituv et al. 1982; Applegate et al. 1996; Brown and Magill 1994; Tavakolian 1989). Although such research has increased our collective understanding of the factors influencing a firm's choice of IT governance arrangements, the vast majority of prior studies have focused on singular impacts of specific contingency factors. For example, Olson and Chervany (1980) examined the influence of corporate structure (centralization vs. decentralization), whereas Tavakolian (1989) examined the impact of business strategy orientation (prospectors, defenders, or analyzers).

However, in reality, the evolution of firms' IT governance arrangements depends on the confluence of many contingency factors. To add even more complexity, these contingency factors (in practice) interact with one another in shaping firms' IT governance arrangements. The advancement of our knowledge of why and how firms exhibit particular IT governance arrangements requires accounting for the effects of complex interactions among the multiple contingency forces that are likely to simultaneously prevail in practice. This research draws on the theory of multiple contingencies (Gresov 1989) to provide explanations of how several contingency forces actively influence firms' IT governance arrangements. Through conceptual argumentation and evidence across eight case sites, the research demonstrates that firms' IT governance arrangements are the outcome of specific interaction patterns.

This paper is organized as follows. The next two sections present a literature review of IT management governance modes and their determinants. Next, the paper presents its theoretical model based on the application of the theory of multiple contingencies and articulates three hypotheses. The subsequent section details the research methodology. Then, results are presented. Finally, the paper discusses implications for research and practice.

## Determinants of the Modes of IT Governance Arrangements

Prior studies of IT governance arrangements have identified a number of contingency factors (see Table 2). These earlier findings are built upon by categorizing them into three sets of forces—corporate governance, economies of scope, and absorptive capacity—as determinants of a firm's adoption of a particular IT governance arrangement.

## Corporate Governance

Business firms seek to mirror their corporate governance arrangements in the activities of their key subunits in order to lower their organizational coordination costs (Blau and Schoenherr 1971; Galbraith 1967; Lawrence and Lorsch 1969). IS researchers have found that the mode of corporate governance significantly influences the mode of IT governance: firms that have centralized their corporate governance also tend to centralize their IT governance, whereas firms that have decentralized their corporate governance tend to decentralize their IT governance (Applegate et al. 1996; Brown and Magill 1994; Ein-dor and Segev 1982; King 1983; Leifer 1988; Olson and Chervany 1980; Tavakolian 1989).

Further, the size of the firm influences the mode of IT governance through its influence on the mode of corporate governance. Smaller firms tend to be functionally structured (i.e., the H-form), with individual subunits responsible for key functions, such as production, marketing, finance, and accounting. Such organizations utilize centralized corporate governance arrangements in order to facilitate coordination of interdependencies among the activities of the individual subunits. Further, such firms utilize integrated information systems in order to manage the subunit interdependencies. As a result, small firms are more likely to adopt a centralized governance arrangement. Growth in the size of a firm, however, typically leads to the divisionalization of its operations and a resultant decentralization of the corporate governance (Blau 1970; Blau and Schoenherr 1971; Grinyer et al. 1980). Divisionalization occurs in the form of segmentation along lines-of-business or geographic regions. In these larger firms, a significant amount of the information systems requirements tend to be customized to the needs of individual business or geographical units. As a result, large firms are likely to decentralize aspects of their IT governance arrangements in order to be more responsive to individual subunits' IS needs (Ein-Dor and Segev 1982).

## Economies of Scope

Economies of scope are benefits accruing to a firm when it is able to share its resources across multiple products/services. As a result, the synergistic costs are less than the sum of the costs of providing each product or service by itself (Panzar and Willig 1981; Teece 1980).

Economies of scope often arise from the sharing of knowledge and distinctive managerial competencies (Ginsberg 1990; Hamel and Prahalad 1994; Nelson and Winter 1982; Winter 1987). However, for a firm to realize economies of scope, administrative mechanisms or operating relationships must be established among its units to keep track of opportunities for synergies and to ensure exploitation of the associated economies of scope (Hoskisson and Hitt 1988; Reed and Luffman 1986; Teece 1980). In the present study, economies of scope are the benefits arising from sharing of appropriate IT-related expertise, know-how, and investments across the enterprise.

Research in strategic management suggests that the feasibility of leveraging economies of scope is largely determined by diversification mode and diversification breadth. Diversification mode refers to whether the enacted growth strategy of a business is driven by internal expansion or external acquisitions (Simmonds 1990). During internal expansion, a firm “grows its business” through internal capabilities and product-market expansions. Two considerations guide the expansion of such firms into areas that leverage their existing core capabilities and knowledge. First, these firms possess an inherent familiarity with the new entry markets and adopted technologies and are thus able to extend their current administrative and operating systems to the new business venture (Jemison and Sitkin 1986; Lubatkin 1986). Second, the pace of internal expansion tends to be gradual in nature, thereby enabling the firm to experiment with governance mechanisms that permit exploitation of synergies and economies of scope (Pitts 1976; Simmonds 1990). In the IT governance context, firms pursuing internal growth would expect to leverage existing technology investments and existing technology competencies throughout their enterprise. This would be easiest to accomplish when IT resources and decisions are centrally orchestrated.

On the other hand, firms that grow through acquisitions often experience problems in their efforts to integrate newly acquired units with their existing businesses. The strategy literature advances three reasons why acquiring firms might conclude that the costs of integrating organizational relationships and governance structures might exceed the achievable economies of scope. First, the desired organization design conflicts so much with that existing in the acquired businesses that attempts to implement the "new" design are simply not undertaken (Ghoshal 1987; Kazanjian and Drazin 1987). Second, organizational inertia in the behaviors of the senior management of the acquired units may forestall efforts to transition toward a unified organization design (Bastien 1987; Hannan and Freeman 1984; Napier 1989). Third, circumstances surrounding the acquisition itself often create a lack of harmony, goal incongruence, and competitive, rather than cooperative, behaviors on the part of the managers within the acquiring and acquired units of a business (Jones and Hill 1988; Williamson 1975). In the IT governance context, firms that grow through acquisitions might discover that the acquired units have disparate IT infrastructures, different histories and cultures of interactions and relationships among the line and IS managers, and different levels of reliance on IT in their business strategies and value chain operations. Further, the circumstances of the merger of acquired units could raise concerns among the managers of the acquired units that they might lose the responsiveness and service quality of their IS units, particularly if efforts are initiated to tightly integrate the IS activities across the acquired units. Overall, these arguments suggest that firms that grow through acquisition might judge the costs of integrating the different IT units into a centralized governance arrangement to outweigh the potential economies of scope. Therefore, such firms may very well adopt decentralized IT governance arrangements.

Diversification breadth refers to the degree of product/market relatedness of a multi-business firm. Firms that compete in related product-markets tend to possess considerable similarity in their production and logistics processes, consumers, distribution channels, and marketing strategies. As a consequence, such firms tend to exhibit numerous commonalities across their IT infrastructures and applications (Boynton et al. 1992; Brown and Magill 1994; Cash et. al., 1992). A centralized locus for IT decision making makes it possible to exploit the IT-related commonalties and leverage considerable economies of scope. However, when a firm's operating units are characterized by unrelated product-markets, these operating units often possess distinctive IT needs. As a result, local IS staff and line managers are generally much better positioned than the corporate IS staff to effectively apply IT in supporting the operating units' work processes and business strategies.

As discussed above, diversification mode and breadth influence the feasibility of attaining economies of scope through IT governance arrangements. In addition to these, a third important influence on the mode of IT governance is the exploitation strategy for economies of scope. Specifically, firms can seek to exploit the economies of scope either through development of enterprise-wide partnerships or through consolidation of enterprise-wide IT assets. Exploitation of economies of scope through partnerships reflects the realization that relevant knowledge and political authority for IT-based innovation is dispersed across the enterprise. Partnerships are required to integrate these dispersed pockets of knowledge and authority. Such partnerships enable the formation of trust among business and IS managers so that they are willing to jointly explore prospects for IT innovation in the firm, assume risk for IT innovation, and undertake leadership for the innovation projects (Cooprider and Victor 1993; Henderson 1990). However, such partnership relations are more likely to prosper when the decision rights for significant IT activities are located closer to the business units, for example, through decentralization at the divisional level (Rockart 1988).

On the contrary, when firms seek to exploit economies of scope through IT assets, the primary logic is that the dispersed IT assets across the enterprise must be consolidated and managed through a centralized entity, viz. corporate IS. The dispersed IT assets are consolidated through unified systems and procedures, planning approaches, and a focus on leveraging the IT assets across the enterprise's information needs (Keen 1991; LaBelle and Nyce 1987; Maglitta and Mehler 1992; Rockart 1988; Venkatraman 1994; von Simson 1990; Wilder 1990). Efforts to achieve such intentions generally benefit from centralized governance arrangements.

As a point of clarification, these exploitation strategies for economies of scope are only feasible when corporate diversification mode or breadth permits the leveraging of scope economies.

## Absorptive Capacity

As information technologies become a key element of the business strategies or core operating processes, firms are seeking to stimulate IT-based innovation by assigning more authority for IT decisions to line managers (Rockart 1988). However, the level of IT-related knowledge possessed by business managers across the enterprise and their willingness to accept IT decision-making responsibilities can significantly influence design choices about the location of authority for IT management decisions. The conceptual notion of absorptive capacity (Cohen and Levinthal 1990), which refers to the ability of a firm's employees to develop relevant knowledge bases, recognize valuable external information, make appropriate decisions, and implement effective work processes and structures, nicely captures these notions.

Absorptive capacity is reflected in the line managers' IT management experience in a firm. If line's IT management experience is limited, little IT-related absorptive capacity will be present outside of the knowledge possessed by the firm's IT specialists. A firm's capability to effectively implement and sustain a decentralized locus for IT decisions is largely predicated upon the extent to which line managers throughout its operating units possess the requisite level of IT-related absorptive capacity. Such an absorptive capacity is normally built over time through these business managers' interaction with IS staff and their participation in significant IT initiatives. Firms whose line managers lack an adequate understanding about IT and IT management practices are, stated simply, unprepared for and unwilling to support significant IT decentralization (Boynton et al. 1992; Brown and Magill 1998). On the other hand, when firms' line managers are knowledgeable about IT, they might seek greater authority for certain IT management decisions. Therefore, in such firms, there might be greater pressures toward decentralization.

## The Study's Theoretical Model

The previous section has identified three major sets of forces influencing the assignment of decision rights for IT activities. However, the current study recognizes the reality that organizations are subject to the pulls and pressures of multiple, rather than singular, contingency forces. A pragmatic theoretical perspective must acknowledge the simultaneous influence of these forces.

The theory of multiple contingencies provides a sound theoretical lens for viewing the influence of several contingency forces on the locus of IT decision rights. This theory is implicit in theories of organization design (Lawrence and Lorsch 1969; Thompson 1967) and has recently been used to study the design of work units (Gresov 1989). Researchers have recently drawn upon this theory to examine interdependencies between organizational contexts and the nature of the IS role and function (Fisher 1994; Rathnam et al. 1995; Sabherwal and Kirs 1994; Zerfane and Gul 1993). Essentially, the theory recognizes work unit design as being simultaneously influenced by numerous contingency forces, whose effects might complement or counteract one another. Thus, an organization's design is an evolving consequence of the interplay among different forces. Conceptually, individual contingency forces influence the choice of particular organization designs.

Multiple contingencies theory directs our attention toward two fundamental issues. First, in a given set of situations, the individual contingency forces will likely differ in their salience, i.e., relative strength, regarding work unit design. Organization designers or managers in one contextual situation will invariably perceive certain contingency factors to be highly salient, while organization designers or managers in other contextual situations are likely to perceive these same factors as being less salient. Second, the effects of individual contingency forces are amplified, dampened, or overridden depending on the nature and magnitude of the full set of contingency forces that apply to a given contextual situation. Based on Gresov's (1989) work, three mutually exclusive scenarios—reinforcing, conflicting, and dominating—are identified to characterize this interplay and provide a basis for positing the influence of multiple contingencies on the location of IT decision rights.

Reinforcing contingencies describe situations where the salient contingency forces produce similar influences on the locus of IT decision-making responsibilities. These salient contingency forces combine to reinforce a singular effect either toward a centralized or a decentralized IT governance mode. While countervailing dispositions might be produced by less salient contingency forces, the effects of these secondary forces are overwhelmed by the reinforcing contingencies. In other words, in such organizations, the most salient forces all tend to reinforce each other to produce a strong tendency toward either centralization or decentralization. This leads to the study's first hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1: Organizations facing reinforcing contingencies regarding corporate governance, scope, and absorptive capacity forces are likely to exhibit a centralized or decentralized mode of IT governance.

Conflicting contingencies refer to situations where multiple contingency forces are salient, but they have conflicting influence on the location of IT decision-making responsibilities. Some of these salient contingency forces would be expected to favor a disposition toward the centralization of IT decision making, whereas other forces would be expected to favor a disposition toward decentralization. With this scenario, these conflicting effects resolve themselves through the emergence of a federal mode of IT governance, where certain IT decisions are centralized and others are decentralized.

Hypothesis 2: Organizations facing conflicting contingencies regarding corporate governance, scope, and absorptive capacity forces are likely to exhibit a federal mode of IT governance.

Dominating contingencies reflect the situation where, despite the presence of numerous contingency factors, one or two contingencies impose prohibitively high opportunity costs on firms that ignore their effects. As a result, firms facing such dominating contingencies are forced to behave as if these dominating contingencies were the only ones present.

Two examples illustrate that dominating contingencies do not simply represent an academic exercise. First, consider a firm with related operating units that has primarily grown through acquisition and whose acquired units have disparate IT infrastructures. Although market relatedness could present prospects for leveraging economies of scope through a centralized IT governance mode, the disparate IT infrastructures of the acquired business units could present prohibitive costs to impede attempts at enterprise-wide integration of IT assets and, hence, the adoption of more centralized IT decision-making arrangements. Here, diseconomies of scope due to growth through acquisitions could become a dominating contingency influencing the decentralization of IT decision making, even though market relatedness might otherwise suggest the desirability of a centralized governance. As a second example, consider a firm with unrelated operating units whose line managers have limited IT knowledge and experience and, therefore, low absorptive capacity. The inability and (likely) limited willingness of these line managers to assume significant IT responsibilities might inhibit any efforts to assign responsibility for a significant portion of IT decisions to the operating units. Thus, even though low market relatedness might otherwise suggest the desirability of adopting a decentralized IT governance mode, the dominating nature of low absorptive capacity could dictate the reality of a centralized IT governance mode.

Given the prohibitively high opportunity costs associated with ignoring the effects of dominating contingencies, it is expected that firms will design IT governance modes in response to the singular effects of the dominating contingency. Such governance arrangements are, therefore, likely to be either purely centralized or decentralized. Therefore, the study's third hypothesis is stated as follows:

Hypothesis 3: Organizations facing dominating contingencies regarding corporate governance, scope, and absorptive capacity forces are likely to exhibit centralized or decentralized modes of IT governance.

## Research Methodology

Data for testing the three hypotheses were drawn from a research project that was initially designed to understand factors affecting the modes of IT governance arrangements in business firms (Rockness and Zmud 1989). The data were gathered through multiple case studies of the governance arrangements at eight firms. Eisenhardt (1989) suggests that researchers conducting multiple sites case studies should identify their theoretical unit of interest and carefully select the case sites so as to produce sufficient variation in the variables of greatest research interest. Accordingly, first, a field survey was conducted to gather data on IT governance arrangements in 35 firms. The data included the IT decision roles of corporate IT, divisional IT, and line management at 73 business units within the 35 firms. Details of this field survey and the manner in which the locus of IT decision making was measured are described in Appendix A.

These survey data were used to select the case sites as follows. First, data on the locus of IT decisions for infrastructure, technology use, and project implementation decisions across business units of each sampled firm were examined to detect if there were any significant variations across the units. In almost all the 35 firms, the locus of authority for IT decisions was found to be similar across the three sampled business units of the enterprise. This provided the justification for inferring an overall firm-level pattern in the locus of authority for IT decisions. Next, the locus scores were examined across the 35 firms to select firms that exhibit very different patterns. In addition, COMPUSTAT data were gathered on sales revenues over the last three years prior to the data gathering effort. These data were used as a proxy for the size of the firm. Together, the locus scores and firm size were used as the criteria for case site selection to select the eight case sites (Table 3):

\- Two firms (C and H) were selected because they reflected a centralization of infrastructure decisions, but moderate locus scores on IT use and project management decisions.

\- Firm A was selected because it reflected a centralization of infrastructure and project management decisions, but a decentralization of IT use decisions.

\- Two firms (F and G) were selected because they reflected a centralization of all IT decisions.

\- Three firms (B, D, and E) were selected because they reflected a decentralization of all IT decisions.

\- Four of the firms (A, B, C, and D) were large in size, two others (E and F) were medium in size, whereas the other two (G and H) were small in size.

The study's conceptualization scheme of centralization, decentralization, and federal modes of governance arrangements was developed subsequent to the completion of the case studies and the submission of a practitioner-oriented report to the study's sponsors. Table 4 portrays how the case sites vary according to the conceptualizations of IT governance developed for the current study. Four of the firms exhibit a federal mode of governance (sites A, C, G, and H), three exhibit a decentralized governance (sites B, D, and E), and one a centralized governance (site F).

During the case site selection, it was also considered important that all the case sites be characterized as competent regarding their IT practices. The questionnaire phase of the original study (Rockness and Zmud 1989) also provided measures of the firms' success in using IT; therefore, only those firms exhibiting better-than-average success in deploying IT within their business strategies and value chain activities were considered as potential case sites. If the firms were not competent at managing IT, one reason for that could be the selection of inappropriate IT governance arrangements. The selection strategy used here thus ensured the credibility of the governance arrangements as being effective in those firms and, therefore, worthy of examination in this study.

## Data Gathering Strategy

Telephone interviews at each case site were conducted by the second author of this paper in collaboration with another principal investigator (Rockness and Zmud 1989). At each case site, telephone interviews were conducted with the firm's CIO and the senior IS manager for the three business units involved with the field survey. At most of the case sites, the firm's CFO was also interviewed. Thus, a total of four to five interviews were conducted at each firm. On the average, each of these interviews lasted just under one hour. Transcripts for each case site were generated and returned to those interviewed in order to validate the researchers' interpretation. Appendix B provides the structured interview protocol used in conducting these interviews. As can be seen from Appendix B, the focus of the interviews was to understand how key IT decisions were made at a firm, the reasoning that underlay the pattern observed at a firm, and the historical basis of this pattern. At the time these transcripts were initially developed, the interviewers had not engaged in an extensive literature review of the type described earlier; consequently, the interviewers were reasonably blind to the theoretical model that frames the current study.

## Data Analysis Strategy

After the project was completed, a practitioner report was generated to report upon the trends in IT governance arrangements discovered through the study (Rockness and Zmud 1989). Subsequently, one of the original principal investigators (this study's second author) teamed with another researcher (this study's first author), who was not involved in the original data gathering analysis, to re-interpret the case studies and to produce the current study. Appendix C describes details of the process by which qualitative analyses were conducted.

Our analysis revealed that two case sites were categorized as exhibiting reinforcing contingencies (sites B and F). Four case sites were found to exhibit conflicting contingencies (sites A, C, G, and H). Finally, two firms exhibited this pattern of contingencies (sites D and E).

## Reinforcing Contingencies

Table 5 illustrates the contingency forces at the two case sites. As illustrated, all of the salient contingency forces produce similar dispositions toward centralization (Corporation F) or decentralization (Corporation B). Therefore, these firms are considered to exhibit reinforcing contingencies. Further, as might be inferred from knowledge of the likely effect of these salient contingency forces, Corporation B displayed a decentralized mode of IT governance while Corporation F displayed a centralized mode.

With Corporation B, the salient corporate governance and scope pressures reinforce one another in creating a strong governance disposition toward decentralization. Corporation B operated in a very dynamic marketplace (specialty markets), where market trends had to be identified and reacted to quickly, and where acquisitions, divestment, and joint venture activities had become the norm rather than the exception. As a result, the firm's overall corporate governance mode is characterized by high decentralization. In fact, the firm's operating philosophy envisioned each operating unit as being self-contained. Scope pressures emerged from low market relatedness among the operating units, with a result that these operating units had quite distinct information system requirements. Two of the other pressures (an "acquisition" diversification mode and a desire to build IT/line partnerships) also reinforced the governance disposition toward decentralization. Only a single, though weak, contradictory disposition toward centralization existed: leveraging economies of scope through the consolidation of IT assets. This factor produced a desire for the corporate IS group to serve a strong role in setting enterprise-wide strategic IT direction.

As a consequence of these reinforcing contingencies, Corporation B had decentralized the locus of its infrastructure and IT use decisions and highly decentralized its project implementation decisions. Most IT-related decision making was, in fact, being handled by the divisional IS staff. The corporate IS group served three major roles: development of corporate-wide infrastructure standards, with the divisions retaining the autonomy to conform to these standards; planning, development, and operation of corporate-wide applications; and approval of all major IT projects from the perspective of their fit with the corporate infrastructure standards. Overall, the qualitative evidence confirms the decentralized IT governance mode at Corporation B.

At Corporation F, the salient reinforcing contingencies involved corporate governance and scope pressures. Corporation F consisted of a number of small subsidiaries located in the same geographic area and the firm's management personnel were housed within a central facility. Additionally, the firm was characterized by high market relatedness: 95% of the sales revenues were generated by a single product category. Not surprisingly, given this context, Corporation F had adopted highly centralized decision-making governance—subsidiary management handled day-to-day operations, but most major decisions were made at the corporate level.

Two other contingency factors were observed at Corporation F: the desire to enhance partnership relations between IS and line managers and the fairly high IT absorptive capacity possessed by line managers throughout the firm. Although Corporation F was a late-adopter of IT relative to its competitors, it had vaulted ahead of these competitors in infusing IT within its value chain activities. Although corporate IT held ultimate decision authority for most IT decisions, line managers across the firm were very active in initiating and prioritizing application development requests, in defining the essential features of applications, and in software sourcing. In some cases, line managers undertook applications development without the involvement or awareness of the corporate IS group. Thus, applications are acquired in three ways: by line managers using a fourth generation language, by the corporate IS group, or from an outside vendor. The corporate IS group is always involved when applications are developed through the latter two means. In general, Corporation F's management views the IS function to be somewhat more decentralized than other staff departments.

However, during the interviews, respondents made it clear that they believed their firm's small size and the homogenous nature of their markets offered very real economies through the centralization of IT governance. Hence, even though line managers possessed high IT knowledge and there was a desire to build partnerships between the line and IS managers, the salient contingencies stem from size and market relatedness. These salient contingencies reinforce each other to influence the emergence of a centralized mode of IT governance at Corporation F.

Observations regarding the forces influencing the IT governance modes at Corporations B and F provide strong support for Hypothesis 1: reinforcing contingencies are associated with a centralized or a decentralized mode of IT governance.

However, Corporation A's senior management was also very concerned with improving economies of scope through consolidation of IT assets. This factor produced a strong countervailing disposition toward centralization. The firm had a history of building market share through aggressive cost reduction. As a result, considerable emphasis was placed on implementing common applications and sharing, where advantageous to do so, common resources and knowledge; and the corporate IT group was expected to play an influential role in identifying avenues for enhancing the efficient use of IT assets across the corporation.

These conflicting contingencies produced an IT governance mode where divisional IS had decision authority for IT use, but the corporate IT group had decision authority for both infrastructure and project implementation decisions. It is noteworthy that even the less salient contingency forces at Corporation A (an internal growth strategy and the desire to build IS/line partnerships) were conflicting, and hence impelled the movement toward a federal governance structure.

Corporation C, which had grown through a series of acquisitions and divestitures, had steadily decentralized its overall governance mode to enable each operating division to better configure its work and management systems to its specific market environment. While senior management recognized the benefits of aligning its IT governance mode with its overall governance structure, line managers across the firm had limited experience (and little desire to gain such experiences) with IT decision making. Thus, while line management knew where the business was going, the firm's corporate and divisional IS staffs were seen as being best positioned to react technologically to this business direction. As with these salient forces, conflicting dispositions were also observed regarding other less salient contingency forces. The fairly large geographic distances separating many of the firm's operating divisions induced pressures toward the decentralization of IT decision making, whereas the relatively high market-relatedness of these divisions suggested that centralization could be beneficial.

As a result of these countervailing dispositions, the IT governance arrangements at Corporation C reflected a federal governance structure: centralized for infrastructure decisions, yet decentralized for both IT use and project implementation decisions. Significantly, decentralization stopped with the divisional IS staff; line management played a fairly passive role regarding IT decision making.

Most of Corporation G's product lines had evolved from a closely related technology base. Therefore, the firm was functionally organized and had historically employed a centralized governance mode. This corporate governance mode has produced strong pressures to correspondingly centralize the firm's IT decision making. However, since it competed in a segment of the computer industry, Corporation G's managers were very knowledgeable about and interested in IT. Line managers often initiated IT projects, set IT priorities, and managed key IT projects. Therefore, the high IT knowledge of line managers produced a disposition toward decentralization, which conflicted with the effects of the corporate governance mode. These strong conflicting contingencies were accompanied by a number of less salient conflicting contingencies. The firm's history of internal growth and high market-relatedness of its operations induced moderate pressures toward centralization. On the other hand, a growing dissatisfaction among line management with corporate IS staff had led senior management to recognize the necessity of building better partnership relations between corporate IS and line managers (and, hence, decentralize IT decision making). Despite building a strong IT infrastructure and implementing critical applications, corporate IS had little credibility with line managers.

Thus, although infrastructure decisions were centralized at Corporation G, senior management believed that moving IT use and project implementation decisions to line management would both (1) enable line managers to become more deeply involved in the development activities and better understand the true costs and benefits associated with successful IT use, and (2) forge improved relationships between the corporate IS staff and the firm's line managers. Therefore, Corporation G's IT governance structure reflects a federal structure.

Corporation H's two principal areas of business were organized into corresponding business groups; one business group had two subunits and the other four subunits. Although Corporation H's relatively small size had created pressures for it to maintain (for reasons of cost) centralized IT governance, the firm as a whole had decentralized its corporate governance mode to enable its operating managers to more quickly respond to competitors' actions. In addition, there had been a growing belief at Corporation H that the scope and priority of IT applications were best determined by line managers. Correspondingly, the firm's line managers were becoming very knowledgeable about IT and were assuming increasing responsibility for strategic IT applications. Similarly, as some of the operating units grew in size and were able to support full-time IS staff, they had begun to take over an increasing number of IT decision responsibilities.

Thus, as it grew, Corporation H had moved from a centralized IT governance mode to a federal structure, where corporate IS handled most infrastructure decisions and line management handled most IT use and project implementation decisions. IS staff had been physically moved to the larger operating units while still reporting to a corporate IS manager. Such a movement, in turn, had spurred the line management to develop an interest in, and participate in, IT decision making. As line managers' absorptive capacity increased, they assumed greater responsibility for IT use and project implementation decisions.

The four case sites just assessed provide strong support for hypothesis H2: conflicting contingencies are associated with a federal IT governance mode. Interestingly, however, as evident from

Table 4, the four federal governance firms exhibited variations in how the specific responsibilities were allocated among corporate IS, divisional IS, and line managers. Corporate IS assumed responsibility for IT infrastructure management at all the four case sites. However, the locus of authority for IT use and project management varied across the firms. In particular, line management assumed responsibilities for IT use and project management decisions at both Corporation G and Corporation H. The high levels of line IT knowledge are responsible for the significant role of line management in these two firms with federal modes of governance. In contrast, Corporation C's federal mode of governance does not involve line management precisely because of their low IT knowledge. Thus, line IT knowledge and the consolidation of IT assets appear to be influencing the differentiation of governance forms within the federal mode of governance.

Table 7 illustrates the contingency factors felt at the two case sites exhibiting dominating contingencies: Corporation D and Corporation E. Both of these firms possess purely decentralized IT governance modes, thus providing strong support for Hypothesis 3.

The dominating contingency that exists for Corporation D arises from the firm's extremely low market relatedness: Corporation D competed, both domestically and internationally, in a number of distinct industrial markets, producing finished goods as well as component parts. Further, it operated manufacturing facilities in North America, South America, and Europe. Because of the firm's low market relatedness, divisional IS and line management were perceived to be much better positioned to determine the operating units' IT requirements than were the corporate IS staff. Therefore, the firm exhibited a very strong orientation toward decentralization of IT decision making. As might be expected, the firm's overall corporate governance mode was also quite decentralized: operating divisions were fully responsible for the vast majority of their strategic product-market and value chain operation decisions.

## Discussion

Scholars seeking explanations of the variance in firms' modes of IT governance arrangements have often restricted their search to the influence of a single variable. Even when researchers have focused upon the influences of several factors in a single study, they have examined these factors as though they operate in isolation. This study was motivated by the realization that, in reality, these contingency factors operate together in shaping firms' IT governance arrangements. The results of this examination of the forces in play at eight case sites indicate that the theory of multiple contingencies provides a useful frame for understanding how these contingency forces operate together in influencing the location of IT decision rights.

Prior to discussing the implications of these findings, first the study's limitations are summarized so that the findings can be placed in a proper perspective. Then, implications of these findings are provided for both research and practice.

## The Study's Limitations

The salience of individual contingency factors is subjectively assessed by retrospectively drawing upon managers' explanations for why their firms chose the existing IT governance arrangements. Two potential concerns may surface with such a strategy. First, managers could be applying faulty assessments of the salience of their firm's contingencies. However, the fact that four to five managers were interviewed at each firm and interpretations were based on this collective rather than on a single individual, we believe greater validity can be ascribed to the measures of salience. Second, the researchers' own biases may be confounding these measures of salience. However, as explained earlier, the researcher who assessed the salience of the contingency factors did not participate in developing assessments of the modes of IT governance. Further, the transcripts used for the analysis had already been read and validated by the interviewees.

Another potential concern regarding our methods relates to the relatively small number of case sites. However, as our case site selection process insured that considerable variation was observed in the modes of IT governance across the case site firms, we are confident that our implications are relevant to most large organizations.

A third concern might be raised regarding the age of this data set (collected in the late 1980s). Is this study relevant given today's IT management context? We would ourselves be concerned along these lines if the intent of this study was to document contemporary trends regarding the modes of IT governance. However, as our goal instead is to assess fundamental explanations underlying the design of firms' IT governance arrangements, we believe the data set to be reasonable and that findings similar to those obtained here would be observed with data collected from contemporary organizations. We strongly encourage such research efforts. A more relevant concern related to the age of the data relates to the changing landscape of the IT marketplace over the past few years. Today, external partners (e.g., vendors, systems integrators, and consultants) serve a very active role in many, if not most, firms' IT decision making arrangements. Therefore, our conceptualization of IT governance should be extended in future research to incorporate these external partners as an additional actor in measuring locus of authority. However, we are confident that the underlying explanations derived from the theory of multiple contingencies would remain valid given such an extension.

Further, another possible concern related to the age of the data might question whether or not the companies that constituted our 1988 study have remained exemplars of good IT management practice. Recall that we had assessed the appropriateness and effectiveness of these firms' IT management practice in 1988. While we do not have similar data about their contemporary performance, anecdotal evidence indicates that these firms do continue to be cited in the trade press for the quality of their IT management practices. Still, the absence of rigorous evidence regarding the contemporary performance of these firms might raise concerns about the generalizability of our findings. However, since this study focuses on the relationship between profiles of multiple contingencies and modes of IT governance, not on specific practices or tactics evidenced by these firms, we do regard our results as generalizable to contemporary firms.

Finally, the focus of the current study was to explain the formation of IT governance modes at an enterprise level of analysis. However, the eight case sites in our study exhibited relatively undifferentiated governance arrangements across their business units. Brown (1997) argues that when business units exhibit similar governance arrangements across the enterprise, it is appropriate to examine the influential contingency conditions at the level of the enterprise. However, if significant variations exist in the mode of governance across business units, Brown presents evidence that factors at the business unit level might provide stronger explanations of the governance arrangements within individual units. Therefore, we are satisfied that our focus at the enterprise level is appropriate for this study.

## Implications for Research

This study offers a robust conceptual model with theoretical linkages for understanding the location of a firm's IT decision responsibilities. Although previous studies have identified contingency factors that are thought to influence firms' modes of IT governance arrangements, explanations for their effects have been relatively sparse. One of the significant contributions of our work is the identification of corporate governance, economies of scope, and absorptive capacity forces as providing underlying explanations for the effects of specific contingency factors. These forces have strong roots in the contemporary research on organization theory, strategy, and innovation. We feel that these forces can serve as powerful theoretical lenses both in interpreting the results of prior investigations and in shaping rigorous research models for future inquiry.

The second major contribution lies in our application of the use of the Theory of Multiple Contingencies to provide a more robust explanation of the determinants of firms' IT governance arrangements. For example, the more sophisticated lens offered by the multiple contingencies perspective enables us to understand what otherwise would have been a perplexing observation, viz., why Corporation E exhibited a decentralized IT governance mode despite operating in highly related markets. We strongly encourage others to consider how this theoretical perspective may inform other IT research domains.

Further, the three types of contingencies identified in this work have interesting implications. Both the reinforcing and dominating contingencies produce similar outcomes in the sense that they lead to either centralized or decentralized modes of IT governance. Yet, the dynamics of how these contingencies produce alike outcomes are different. With reinforcing contingencies, all of the salient contingencies produce like dispositions on the mode of IT governance. In contrast, with dominating contingencies, the resulting governance arrangement is the result of a single, dominant contingency. If the effects of other salient contingencies are in conflict with the effects of the dominating contingency, as is the case with Corporation E, then it would appear that these firms might incur some structural maladaptation costs because they are being forced to ignore other salient contingencies. We would expect that the governance arrangements induced by reinforcing contingencies would inherently be more stable than those produced by dominating contingencies. Obviously, researchers might wish to examine some of the performance-related consequences of reinforcing versus dominating contingencies.

Conflicting contingencies appear to be a particularly interesting scenario for IS researchers since they often lead to a federal mode of IT governance. During the 1990s, observations of practice suggest that more firms are adopting the federal governance form (LaPlante 1991; von

Simson 1990; Wilder 1990). Our study provides theoretical insights into this phenomenon by linking this governance mode with conflicting contingencies. Specifically, most business firms are experiencing the simultaneous and competing pulls and pressures of their competitive strategy, desire to exploit enterprise-wide synergies, and the growing IT knowledge of their line managers. The federal mode of governance allows unbundling the authority for distinct segments of IT activities and vesting the locus of authority for those segments in response to the effects of distinctive, and conflicting, contingencies.

In terms of future research directions, there is a great need to better understand the dynamics of organizational adaptation of IT governance modes in response to changing organizational and industry contingencies. We believe that evolving business or technological forces regularly alter the nature and relative strength of the contingency factors and demand accommodation of their effects on the firms' existing IT governance modes. Thus, repeated observations by practicing IT managers that their firms regularly restructure their IT governance arrangements would be a direct reflection of the need to adapt to ever-evolving contingency forces. Since such periodic alterations of IT governance arrangements can be both disruptive and expensive, are there other means of adaptation? For example, Brown and Sambamurthy (1998) and Zmud (1988) have examined the role of coordination mechanisms as elements of IT governance. How are coordination mechanisms utilized along with the locus of IT decision rights to respond to the pressures of evolving multiple contingencies? We think that this might be a fascinating and fertile ground for future research.

Another direction for future research would be to examine the influence of multiple contingencies on the design of customized governance arrangements within individual business units. Brown (1997) identifies a variety of business unit level factors as influencing distinct modes of governance in individual business units. Brown and Magill (1998) provide conceptual arguments about impacts of enterprise and business unit level factors on modes of governance arrangements within business units. Applying the multiple contingencies perspective, Brown and Magill (1998) offer propositions about the mode of governance for IT use within business units under different scenarios of corporate and business-unit contingencies. Similar to our study, their propositions are directed at examining governance arrangements in firms where the patterns are similar across business units. Collectively, our study and the Brown (1997) and the Brown and Magill (1998) studies could help researchers empirically examine the influence of multiple contingencies on differentiated governance arrangements across business units in firms.

Finally, our conceptualization of absorptive capacity forces focused on the IT-knowledge of line managers. We also recognize the importance of the converse side of absorptive capacity—the business knowledge of IT managers (Boynton et al. 1992). We would expect low business knowledge of IT managers to influence pressures toward decentralization. Future research must examine how limited business understanding on the part of IT managers (either at an enterprise or divisional level) influences the nature of firms' IT governance structures.

## Implications for Practice

This study has provided robust descriptions of alternative modes of IT governance and the contingency conditions associated with their adoption. Further, the study has offered detailed explanations as to why particular IT governance modes may be appropriate when they may at first glance appear to “contradict” accepted practice. All of a firm’s senior executives (not just senior IT executives) must recognize the advantages of different IT governance modes and must understand the nature of the forces likely to become salient in directing the evolution of their firm’s IT governance arrangements. In particular, as existing contingency forces change in nature and salience, and as some of these forces become dominant in their effects, senior executives must realize the necessity of making adjustments in their IT governance arrangements in order to maintain a dynamic alignment with their ever-changing organizational and environmental contexts.

Similarly, senior executives must endeavor to accurately gauge the salience and the implications of individual contingency forces. For instance, in Corporation E, were it not for the dominating effects of growth through acquisition, the firm would have moved into a conflicting contingencies mode and would have found the federal governance mode to be more appropriate. What is the real cost of a maladaptive governance mode brought on by a dominating contingency? What would be the real benefit of resolving the dominating contingency and moving to the more appropriate governance mode? Senior executives responsible for design of their firm's IT governance arrangements must be vigilant in understanding the salience and implications (regarding both the mode of IT governance and the costs and benefits attributed to particular governance modes) of the multiple contingency forces impacting their firm at any given point in time.

## Conclusion

In practice, it is frequently observed that organizational IT governance moves through recurrent centralization/decentralization cycles—both for specific organizations and for all organizations in general. The theory of multiple contingencies, along with our identification of three core domains of contingency forces, offers the promise of richer conceptual and practical understandings of the complex dynamics underlying the evolutionary nature of organizational IT governance arrangements.

Our long-standing research program on IT governance has only served to reinforce our view of the critical importance of establishing appropriate IT decision rights to direct and coordinate an organization's effective use of and exploitation of IT. IT governance arrangements create the context, the rules, and the expectations that frame and filter the IT-related decision making occurring throughout all organizations. We anticipate that our conceptual ideas and our interpretations of the forces influencing IT governance arrangements at eight case sites will support, and ideally stimulate, the work of other researchers interested in these and related organizational design issues regarding the effective management of IT.

We also anticipate that our discussions of both the likely effects of specific contingency forces on IT governance arrangements and the dynamic nature of the salient forces at play at any point in time will prove useful to those executives faced with the responsibility of designing (and revising) their firm's mode of IT governance.

Finally, regardless of whether the focus is research or practice, IT governance issues invariably take the form of very messy and ephemeral phenomena. We are convinced that efforts to understand and direct these phenomena can not follow a “business as usual” stance. We hope that our application of holistic theoretical and methodological postures will encourage other researchers—and practitioners—to likewise innovate in their explorations of effective IT governance arrangements.

## APPENDIX A

## Details of the Field Survey

The field survey sample was drawn from 140 firms that were members of the Financial Executives Institute (FEI), the professional society for chief financial officers (CFO), whose research foundation funded the original study (Rockness and Zmud 1989). While manufacturers represented a majority of firms participating in the study, a few firms representing the insurance, financial services, and telecommunications industries were also included. At each sampled firm, the CFO was asked to identify three business units on the basis of the business units' overall performance—a high-performing unit, an average performing unit, and a low-performing unit. Five questionnaires were then asked to be distributed and returned to the researchers: one to be completed by the CFO, one to be completed by the chief information officer (CIO), and one to be completed by the senior executive of each of the three business units. Usable responses were obtained from 35 firms (response rate of 25%), yielding a total of 73 business units. This response rate is consistent with that observed in other field surveys aimed at understanding phenomena at both the firm and business unit levels (Gaedeke and Tootelian 1976). In order to verify whether the response rate produced a biased set of responses, secondary data on net revenue, net income, and assets were gathered from the COMPUSTAT database for both responding and nonresponding firms. Analysis revealed no significant differences between the two subsamples (p > .10), partially alleviating concerns about the problem of nonresponse bias.

Published material in the trade press and prior research were utilized for developing items $^{2}$ to assess locus and sharing on seven classes of IT decisions:

1. long-term hardware decisions about mainframes, minicomputers, and networks, including capacities, specific vendors, brands, and technologies;

2. long-term microcomputer decisions, including capacities, specific vendors, brands, and technologies;

3. long-term applications decisions about IT-enabled value chain activities (logistics, manufacturing, etc.);

4. annual hardware decisions about enhancements and upgrades;

5. annual applications decisions about enhancements or additions to existing applications supporting various value chain activities;

6. project management decisions; and

7. daily operations decisions, including service delivery priorities and overriding standard procedures when special IS service needs arose.

Senior business unit (non-IS) executives served as respondents in indicating the extent to which corporate IS, divisional IS, and line management assumed and shared responsibility for each decision. $^{3}$ The response cues ranged from “unilaterally makes decisions” through “shares equally in decisions” to “no role in the decision,” arranged on a five-point Likert scale. Factor analyses were used to confirm that the items tapping each one of these dimensions of IT decisions loaded together onto their corresponding factors.

Next, rule sets were devised to operate on the data collected through the field survey and compute measures of IT decision-making locus. $^{4}$ The locus of IT decision making was operationalized as follows:

\- If the decision scores indicated that corporate IS played a primary or unilateral role in a particular decision, and that divisional IS and line management had a minor or no role in that decision, then corporate IS was identified as the locus of that decision (low decentralization, score = 1). Similar logic was applied to identify cases where divisional IS (moderate decentralization, score = 2) or business management (high decentralization, score = 3) were viewed to be the locus of IT decision making. Such a rule set reflects a logic that business management as the locus of IT decision making represents the highest level of decentralization, a logic consistent with the literature (Rockart 1988).

\- If two stakeholders (invariably either corporate IS and divisional IS or divisional IS and line management) were reported to serve essentially equivalent roles, a mid-point score (1.5 or 2.5, respectively) was assigned.

## APPENDIX B

## Interview Protocol for Case Site Interviews

The following structured protocol was developed and used to guide the interviews:

1. Describe the distribution of IT management responsibilities between corporate IS and divisional IS groups in applying IT in your firm. What factors explain this pattern of distribution?

2. How does such a distribution compare with the overall corporate centralization/decentralization policies?

3. Describe the distribution of IT management responsibilities between divisional IS personnel and line managers in applying IT. What factors explain this pattern of distribution?

4. Have these patterns of responsibilities changed over the last three to five years? If so, how and why?

5. Do these patterns of responsibilities differ across business units? If so, how do they differ and why to they differ.

6. What are the critical factors that need to be in place for business units to apply IT effectively? Why?

7. What factors most limit a business unit's capability to apply IT effectively? Why?

8. Are these factors (both positive and negative) influenced by the distribution of IT management responsibilities?

The interviews were conducted with the firm's CIO and senior IS executives from the three business units identified by the CFO during the field survey. At certain sites, the firm's CFO was also interviewed. Extensive notes were taken during the interview, with the interview guide designed to accommodate note taking. At the end of interviews at each case site, the researchers generated descriptive summaries, which were then returned to the interviewees for their comments. Their comments were used to amend the summaries.

## APPENDIX C

## Details of the Data Analysis Approach

Given the qualitative nature of the data analysis across the eight case sites, we deemed it important to adopt an analysis strategy that would protect the results against potential biases due to the researcher's knowledge of the hypotheses. Therefore, one author undertook the analysis of the contingency forces, whereas the other author undertook analysis of governance modes at the eight case sites.

The study's first author read the transcripts of the individual case studies in order to develop profiles of the different contingency forces in play at each case site. Next, the author reread the transcripts to gauge the salience of the different contingency forces. The salience of individual contingency forces was assessed by both the tone and repetition evidenced by respondents in their identification of specific contingency forces and their discussion of the effects of these forces. For example, all respondents at Corporation E talked about the history of their firm's growth through acquisition. They emphasized that this diversification mode was a key characteristic of their business and that ignoring the influence of this factor in designing their IT governance arrangements would be both impossible and prohibitively costly. Thus, this factor was judged to be highly salient. As another example, respondents at Corporation C repeatedly mentioned that their line managers lacked the IT knowledge to take on IT decision responsibilities, even though other forces suggested that a more decentralized governance mode would be desirable. Thus, low line IT knowledge was judged to be a highly salient contingency factor in Corporation C. As a final example, respondents at Corporation A indicated the existence of a desire to build partnerships between the line and IS managers. However, during the discussions, respondents also pointed out that this desire was not as strong an influence as the desire to leverage economies of scope through the consolidation of IT assets. In fact, respondents indicated that significant concerns existed to obtain greater efficiencies through the enterprise-wide use of IT assets. Based on these conversations, we inferred that the desire to consolidate IT assets was a salient contingency force at Corporation A, whereas the desire to build partnerships was a less salient contingency force. Readers will recall that the transcripts serving as the basis for such judgements regarding salience had been read and validated by the respondents.

After identifying and assessing the salience of the contingency forces at play at each firm, the study's first author then diagnosed whether each force produced a disposition toward centralization or decentralization. Again, this analysis was based on the transcripts of the interviews. The analysis described so far was depicted through contingency-state diagrams for each case site. These diagrams reflected the following elements: (1) contingency forces producing a disposition toward centralization and their relative salience, (2) contingency forces producing a disposition toward decentralization and their relative salience, and (3) the strength of these overall dispositions toward centralization and decentralization. Figure C1 illustrates the contingency-state diagrams developed for one site, Corporation A; a full set of these diagrams can be obtained on request from the authors.

As a next step, the first author examined the different contingencies and classified the case sites into reinforcing, conflicting, and dominating contingencies. Reinforcing contingencies were defined as organizations where all the salient contingencies produced similar dispositions toward centralization or decentralization. Two case sites were categorized as exhibiting reinforcing contingencies (sites B and F). Conflicting contingencies were defined as organizations where the salient contingencies produced contradictory dispositions toward centralization or decentralization. Four case sites were found to exhibit conflicting contingencies (sites A, C, G, and H). Finally, dominating contingencies were defined as organizations where a single contingency appeared to influence the mode of IT governance. Two firms exhibited this pattern of contingencies (sites D and E). Thus, this process resulted in an exhaustive classification of the eight case sites into reinforcing, conflicting, or dominating contingencies.

Independently, the second author, who had participated in the original case data gathering, produced assessments of the modes of IT governance at each case site. This author examined both numerical scores from the questionnaire survey and transcripts of the cases to classify the case sites as to the modes of IT governance depicted in Table 4.

In summary, the two authors independently produced the profile of multiple contingencies and the mode of IT governance for each case site. These, then, became the data examined in assessing the study's hypotheses. Such an analysis approach provides assurance that the authors did not expose the study's results to their subjective research biases.
