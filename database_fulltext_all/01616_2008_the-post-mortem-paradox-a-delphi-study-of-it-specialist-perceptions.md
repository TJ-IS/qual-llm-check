# The post mortem paradox: a Delphi study of IT specialist perceptions

## Abstract

While post mortem evaluation (PME) has long been advocated as a means of improving development practices by learning from IT project failures, few organizations conduct PMEs. The purpose of the study is to explain this discrepancy between theory and practice. This paper integrates findings from a Delphi study of what experienced practitioners perceive as the most important barriers to conducting PMEs with insights from organizational learning theory. The results suggest that there are critical tensions between development practices and learning contexts in many organizations, and adopting PMEs in these cases is likely to reinforce organizational learning dysfunctions rather than improve current development practices. Based on these findings, we argue that the PME literature has underestimated the limits to learning in most IT organizations and we propose to explore paradoxical thinking to help researchers frame continued inquiry into PME and to help managers overcome learning dysfunctions as they push for more widespread use of PMEs. Keywords: PME; organizational learning; paradoxical thinking; Delphi study; post mortem paradox

## Introduction

In the U.S., information technology (IT) capital spending has reached an estimated 50% of nominal business capital spending (Benko & McFarlan, 2003). Yet, evidence suggests that 40% of IT investments fail to deliver their expected returns (Benko & McFarlan, 2003). The IT projects that fail cost U.S. businesses an estimated \$75 billion each year (Johnson, 1999). The high failure rate associated with IT projects is not new. Rather, it is something that we have known about and lived with for decades (Jones & McLean, 1970).

In the information systems (IS) literature, it has been argued that organizations should attempt to learn from their failures (Lyytinen & Robey, 1999). Yet, there is evidence that organizations not only ‘fail to learn,’ but also ‘learn to fail’ (Lyytinen & Robey, 1999; Wastell, 1999) in the context of IT projects. If true, this suggests that IT organizations must engage themselves in a process to change the negative climate that surrounds failure. Indeed, IT professionals and the organizations they work for must embrace failures as an opportunity for both individual and organizational learning.

Learning from failures is commonplace in other disciplines. Petroski (1994), who writes of civil engineering failures, argues that failure is central to the design process and that we can learn more from failures than from successes. In engineering, there is a tradition of examining major failures. From aeronautical and aerospace failures (e.g., airliner and space shuttle crashes), to nuclear reactor failures (e.g., Three Mile Island), to chemical engineering disasters (e.g., Bhopal), to electrical engineering and power grid failures, to civil engineering failures (e.g., Tacoma Narrows bridge), we see engineers systematically combing through the wreckage as it were, looking for explanations.

In civil aviation in the U.S., for example, there is the National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB), an independent Federal agency, charged by Congress with investigating every civil aviation accident in the U.S. and issuing safety recommendations to prevent future accidents. Investigations within aviation are, however, not limited to civil aviation accidents. Popper & Lipshitz (1998) describe how after-action reviews are used as a general and important tool for learning in the Israel Defense Force. During flights the pilots have limited possibilities for reflection. Decisions are based on intuition and experience and errors are inevitable. Through after-action reviews pilots are forced to always confront these errors in order to improve.

While we acknowledge that there may be important differences between IT projects and other types of engineering projects, we believe that there is still value from treating IT project failures as a learning opportunity that can help us from repeating the same mistakes in the future. Certainly, the manner in which other engineering disciplines embrace failure as a learning opportunity provides a compelling argument for the value of conducting post mortem evaluations (PMEs, sometimes referred to as post-project audits or post-implementation reviews) on IT project failures. In theory, PMEs can help individuals and organizations learn what worked and what can be improved upon so that lessons learned in one project can be applied to future projects. Prior research, however, suggests that PMEs are not used systematically within the IT industry, and when used, not performed adequately (Kumar, 1990; Norris, 1996; Seddon et al., 2002; Gwillim et al., 2005). In short, while there are considerable arguments in favor of conducting PMEs and while other disciplines have a history of examining their failures and learning from them, there appears to be less of a tradition of doing so in the IT field. Pedersen (2004, 2005) and others have suggested that there are barriers to performing PMEs, but there has been no systematic attempt to investigate these barriers from the perspective of experienced IT practitioners. In a recent case study of two organizations, Gwillim et al. (2005) suggest that some of the reasons why PMEs are not conducted include: (1) a view that there is nothing to be gained from such an exercise, and (2) managers’ perceptions of negative personal consequences that might result from such reviews if, for example, system failings are brought to light.

Given that PMEs are often advocated, but seldom performed, this study examines more systematically why PMEs have not been widely adopted in the context of IT projects. This paper integrates findings from a Delphi study of what experienced practitioners perceive as the most important barriers to conducting PMEs with insights from organizational learning theory to suggest that most organizations face a post mortem paradox (Cameron, 1986; Poole & Van de Ven, 1989; Lewis, 2000; Smith & Tushman, 2005). In general parlance, a paradox ‘is seemingly contradictory or opposed to common sense and yet is perhaps true’ (Merriam Webster’s Collegiate Dictionary, Tenth Edition); it therefore grabs our attention and presents a puzzle that needs a solution (Poole & Van de Ven, 1989). In organizational studies, paradoxical thinking investigates tensions and contradictions that stimulate more comprehensive insights into theory and practice (Cameron, 1986; Poole & Van de Ven, 1989; Lewis, 2000; Smith & Tushman, 2005). Applying paradoxical thinking to the results from the Delphi study suggest that there are critical tensions between development practices and learning contexts in many organizations, and adopting PMEs in these cases is likely to reinforce organizational learning dysfunctions rather than improve current development practices. We discuss how understanding this paradox can represent a critical step in helping organizations learn how to and how not to push for more widespread use of PMEs, with the ultimate aim of improving the success rate on IT projects.

## Background and research questions

## Post mortem evaluations

The literature on PMEs is related to the literature on IS evaluations. According to Smithson & Hirschheim (1998), IS evaluation imply ‘appraisal of the value, worth or usefulness of an information system’ and focuses ‘on the potential or actual costs and benefits of producing a new system’ (p. 160). In contrast, PMEs are systematic, formalized reviews of the quality of a product and the associated processes that produced it (Tiedeman, 1990). PMEs help team members recognize, remember, and document what they learned during a project (Birk et al., 2002). They help elicit and document tacit skills used by successful teams and developers, and they make lessons available (Statz, 1999). Despite similarities and a certain overlap, the emphasis in the IS evaluation literature is primarily on the resulting IS and its cost and impact, whereas the emphasis in the PME literature is primarily on the development process and the lessons learned.

PMEs can serve many different purposes. First, they lead to knowledge transformation by explicating knowledge from a project in a form that makes it available for a larger audience. Second, they result in knowledge creation through analyses of a project, for example, lessons learned and new risks (Statz, 1999; Kerth, 2000), root causes and their effects (Collier et al., 1996), critical success factors (Nolan, 1999), and suggestions for improvement (Tiedeman, 1990). Third, conducting a post mortem can lead to increased knowledge sharing within and across project groups (Statz, 1999; Kerth, 2000; Birk et al., 2002). Fourth, post mortems can have important side effects in terms of therapeutic benefits (Collier et al.,

1996), increased job satisfaction (Birk et al., 2002), and improved working relationships among participants (Kerth, 2000). Finally, post mortems can contribute to overall organizational learning, by enabling organizations to learn from their own experiences (Zedtwitz, 2002).

Project evaluation can be conducted at major milestones or, more commonly, after project closure. In either case, the literature refers to this activity as a post mortem (e.g., Tiedeman, 1990). While PMEs are most frequently advocated for projects with many participants in large organizations (e.g., Collier et al., 1996), Birk et al. (2002) emphasize the value of conducting post mortems in small and medium-sized companies as well.

## The PME process

Drawing on Pedersen’s review of the PME literature (Pedersen, 2004, 2005), we organize our discussion of the PME literature around the following four activities of the PME process: (1) design the organizational context, (2) focus the effort and collect the data, (3) analyze and interpret the data, and (4) share and exploit the resulting knowledge (See Figure 1.)

Design the organizational context Birk et al. (2002) suggest that post mortems should ideally be conducted on all projects, but only if conflicts do not remove the focus from learning, and if the organization is committed to improve project practices (Birk et al., 2002). Nolan (1999) suggests considering the performance difference between projects as a key reason to identify which projects to learn from. If some projects perform at an outstanding level, it pays to investigate why and spread critical success factors to other projects. A defined process for post mortems avoids having to invent a procedure from scratch for each project, and it provides participants with a practical approach for conducting evaluations and communicating experiences across projects (Collier et al., 1996). Projects are, however, different, and a defined process should always be adapted to the context (Collier et al., 1996). A skilled facilitator is essential and can help develop a plan for the post mortem (Kerth, 2000). It is important to allocate enough resources (Kerth, 2000). Often, engineers and managers are busy moving on to the next project and missing the opportunity to reflect on what happened on the project they just completed (Tiedeman, 1990).

Focus the effort and collect data Collecting data is time consuming and should not be done without clear direction (Tiedeman, 1990). Explicating objectives also help to focus PMEs on aspects that are considered relevant, create lessons that can be used in other projects, foster improvement within specific areas, or stimulate professional development among developers and managers (Tiedeman, 1990; Nolan, 1999; Kerth, 2000; Birk et al., 2002). Collecting data during a project (e.g., status reports, test reports, review documents) reduces the risk of historical revisionism and faulty conclusions (Tiedeman, 1990). Project documentation should be complemented by conversations with participants before the post mortem (Kerth, 2000), surveys (Collier et al., 1996), semi structured interviews, and facilitated group discussions (Birk et al., 2002). Before systematic analyses of data, Collier et al. (1996) suggest a debriefing where data are presented and commented on by all participants (Collier et al., 1996). This provides an opportunity to propose issues that are not covered, correct mistakes, and communicate directly on successes and failures.

Analyze and interpret the data Participants should look for ‘patterns, trends, and recurrent themes’ to identify the largest problems and recommendations for improvement that will have the greatest impact (Tiedeman, 1990).

(1996) suggest ‘Deming-like quality management methods to discover key insights into the project dynamics and its driving forces’ with the purpose of finding root causes that can be acted upon. Finally, Nolan (1999) suggests an elicitation step to uncover processes used in successful projects, a reduction step to isolate critical success factors in these processes, and a testing step to demonstrate that the findings are complete.

Share and exploit the resulting knowledge It is important that lessons are easy to access and use, that they are organized for several audiences (e.g., project teams, improvement teams, and managers), and that supporting evidence is described for each lesson (Statz, 1999). Concerning easy access, Tiedeman (1990) suggests that meeting minutes and a list of recommendations be published and Collier et al. ## Barriers to PMEs

The PME literature suggests that post mortems are important and put forth a portfolio of standard practices for conducting PMEs. Kumar (1990), however, concludes that PMEs are not used systematically, and when used, are not performed adequately. Unfortunately, very little is known about why PMEs are seldom conducted in practice. Pedersen’s (2004, 2005) work is the only study to the best of our knowledge that sheds light on the reasons why post mortems are seldom practiced. His doctoral dissertation explores some of the reasons why PMEs are not adequately performed, and lists them as barriers to PMEs.

Pedersen (2004, 2005) developed a list of potential barriers to PMEs based on a review of the PME literature (e.g., Kumar, 1990; Tiedeman, 1990) and organizational learning literature (e.g., Argyris & Schoen, 1996; Snyder & Cummings, 1998; Lyytinen & Robey, 1999; Panagiotidis & Edwards, 2001). Pedersen identified 27 barriers that he organized into the four activities of conducting PMEs described earlier: (1) design the organizational context, (2) focus the effort and collect the data, (3) analyze and interpret the data, and (4) share and exploit the resulting knowledge. After constructing his list of 27 barriers, Pedersen conducted a case study of a systems development organization trying to implement PMEs. Based on the case study, he was able to verify that 18 of the barriers identified from his literature review were present in practice. Additionally, through the case study, he identified four barriers that did not surface in the literature review, yielding a total of 31 possible barriers, as shown in Table 1.

While valuable, Pedersen’s list of 31 barriers have not been assessed or validated by practitioners beyond the confines of what could be accomplished in a single case study. Additional research is therefore needed to determine whether practitioners would judge Pedersen’s list to be a comprehensive one. Moreover, Pedersen’s work leaves open the question of which PME barriers practitioners’ view as being the most important.

In summary, the post mortem literature is quite optimistic about the prospect of conducting evaluations to support learning in and between IT projects. The literature provides, however, only anecdotal evidence of successful cases, for example, Tiedeman (1990) reports that one organization was able to adopt 50% of the recommendations on PMEs, and Birk et al. (2002) quote participants as being very satisfied with the process. There are no systematic studies of the impact of using PMEs in IT projects. The only systematic empirical studies focus on why post mortems are seldom used, not on the effect of using them. Kumar (Kumar, 1990) concludes that post mortems are not used systematically, and when used, not performed adequately. Zedtwitz (2002) reports that 80% of all R&D projects are not evaluated after completion, and when projects are evaluated it is done without using established guidelines. These findings are further supported by Glass (2002) and by organizational learning theory that is generally less optimistic on how easy it is to learn in organizational contexts.

## Explaining failure to learn

Organizational learning has been a topic of interest for decades and is recognized to play an important role in performance of organization (Fiol & Lyles, 1985; Ellmer, 1999; Love, 2001; Kalle & Gregory, 2006). Fiol & Lyles (1985) review the literature on organizational learning and define organizational learning as ‘a process of improving actions through better knowledge and understanding.’ In their review they find that although there are differences among various theorists on the topic, there is considerable agreement regarding a theory for organizational learning in several areas. There is an agreement on the four contextual factors that affect the probability that learning will occur: culture conducive to learning, strategy that allows flexibility, an organizational structure that allows innovativeness and new insights, and the environment. These four factors are said to have a reciprocal relationship with learning in that they create and reinforce learning and are created by learning (Fiol & Lyles, 1985).

Robey et al. (2000) review and assess the emerging research literature on organizational learning specific to IT. They relate organizational learning and organizational action by emphasizing the role of organizational memory. Thus, they define organizational learning as ‘an organizational process, both intentional and unintentional, enabling the acquisition of, access to, and revision of organizational memory, thereby providing direction to organizational action.’ They use organiza-

Although these findings show that learning can be useful and effective in IT project settings, the process of learning is, however, quite complex. To understand why learning can be difficult in the context of IT projects, Lyytinen & Robey (1999) draw upon organizational learning theory and two case studies to identify four reasons why organizations fail to learn from IT projects (see Table 2): (1) limits on organizational intelligence, (2) disincentives for learning, (3) organizational redesign, and (4) educational barriers to learning. They also provide advice to overcome these barriers.

Organizational intelligence is limited for a number of reasons. First, learning is bounded by the capacity to process information and make sense of experience (March et al., 1991). Information overload can hence be a barrier to building organizational knowledge. Second, the high turnover in most IT organizations taps them of relevant experience and knowledge. Third, organizational actors become blinded by pre-existing institutional arrangements and related thinking patterns. Fourth, theories in use lack adequate scientific validity because experimental controls are absent, measures are weak, and analyses lack rigor. Organizations can overcome limits on organizational intelligence by effective knowledge management that takes stock of the knowledge they already possess and ascertains what additional knowledge they need to acquire.

The second reason is absence of proper incentives for learning, especially from failure. Failures are often wiped from an organization’s memory out of fear that they will recur, and consequently valuable material for internal learning and theory testing is ignored. Failures precipitate disciplinary actions in which guilty parties are punished. To combat this barrier, organizations must create positive incentives for learning and remove customary penalties for mistakes.

Organizational design can also be a reason for failing to learn in IT projects. Departmental boundaries can limit access to relevant information, diminish testing and development of alternative theories in use, and retard the introduction of better methods. Even in situations in which organizational learning is valued, organizations often minimize the effect of learning by establishing special units that are walled off from core processes. Organizations can redesign their structure to remove boundaries among tasks and allow smooth flow of information from project start to finish.

Educational issues form the fourth reason. The IT discipline is characterized by specialized technical training with less focus on business strategy and operations. In order for IT professionals to exercise judgment in situations where technical and business values are inseparable, they need to be trained in organizational problem solving.

## Research questions

The post mortem literature provides, in summary, detailed advice on how to conduct PMEs. Apart from some anecdotal evidence of the benefits of PMEs we have little knowledge about the actual use and benefits of this approach to learning from IT projects, or the barriers that inhibit PMEs from being conducted. Empirical studies suggest, however, that post mortems are rarely adopted and executed according to the advice given. The purpose of this research is therefore to explain the discrepancy between our knowledge about and our practices related to PMEs.

PMEs are argued to be an effective means of organizational learning, as they enable organizations to learn from their own experiences (Zedtwitz, 2002). The organizational learning literature, however, indicates that organizations frequently fail to learn from their experiences (Lyytinen & Robey, 1999). Such failures to learn are discussed under the rubric of barriers to effective organizational learning. Pedersen suggests a rich set of possible barriers to post mortems in IT projects (Pedersen, 2004; Pedersen, 2005), but his list has not been empirically validated or checked for its comprehensiveness. Nor has any attempt been made to rank-order potential barriers in terms of their relative impact on establishing post mortem practices. By analyzing the underlying patterns by which experienced practitioners perceive and rank barriers to conducting PMEs, and by mapping the ranked barriers to the Lyytinen & Robey (1999) typology of why organizations fail to learn, we can gain insights as to why post mortems are not practiced and what might be done to overcome such barriers. Accordingly, this research integrates a Delphi study of what experienced practitioners perceive as the most important barriers to conducting PMEs with insights from organizational learning theory to address the following research questions:

RQ 1: What are the most important barriers to conducting PMEs of IT projects?

RQ 2: Why are PMEs seldom practiced to learn from failures in IT organizations?

RQ 3: Under which conditions can PMEs be successfully adopted in IT organizations?

## Research approach

Considering both our research questions and the exploratory nature of our study, we chose to use the Delphi method. One important aim of this study was to develop a list of the barriers to conducting PMEs of IT projects and to determine which of those barriers are most important. An obvious source for such information is an expert in the field with years of experience in conducting PMEs. But no single expert is likely to be in a position to produce a comprehensive list of barriers. In such circumstances, a reliable and valid data collection process involves gathering the opinions from a panel of experts and employing feedback-based convergence and closure to determine the barriers that matter most. Therefore, a ranking-type Delphi survey (Schmidt, 1997), designed to elicit the opinion of a panel of experts through iterative controlled feedback, was chosen as the research method for this study. We formed our panel of experts by recruiting project managers with experience conducting PMEs. We also used systematic procedures to elicit and rank barriers.

The Delphi methodology was developed by the RAND Corporation in the 1950s and has since been successfully used in over 470 studies to define important issues and reach consensus among groups of experts (Gupta & Clarke, 1996; Schmidt, 1997). According to Linstone & Turoff (1975), the Delphi approach may be effectively employed as a valid research method if ‘the problem does not lend itself to precise analytical techniques but can benefit from subjective judgments on a collective basis’.

The Delphi method is a structured, multi-pass group discovery process. A panel of domain experts is selected and each expert is asked to independently record what they think is important in dealing with the given problem. Individual responses are collected and aggregated, and a list of the factors uncovered is presented back to each panelist. The experts are subsequently asked to individually rank the factors identified by the group as a whole in terms of significance. These rankings are then collated and an overall ranking is computed. The reranked factors are then shown to each panelist along with their original ranking, and the panelist is invited to review their personal ranking in light of the aggregated group response. Many variations of the Delphi method have been used in the fields of IS, public policy, management, marketing, and group therapy (e.g., Dalkey & Helmer, 1963; Dalkey, 1969; Taylor & Meinhardt, 1985; Brancheau & Wetherbe, 1987; McLellan & Marcolin, 1994; Brancheau et al., 1996; Turoff & Hiltz, 1996).

We based our research on the procedure outlined by Schmidt (1997) to refine, add, and rank the barriers to PME of IT projects suggested by Pedersen. In addition to Schmidt’s three phase procedure, we included a fourth reflection phase, where the panel commented on the resulting list and our initial interpretation of the results. The adopted Delphi study approach is illustrated in Figure 2.

## Composition of panel

Our panel was comprised of 23 experienced practitioners working in IT organizations. To control for the effect of different cultures, the panelists were all drawn from a population of experienced IT practitioners working in Scandinavia. The demographic profile of the panel is presented in Table 3. All 23 of our panelists had direct experience performing project PMEs and more than half of them had performed PMEs on five or more projects. Thus the panelists were well equipped to serve as experts on our Delphi panel.

## Data collection and analysis method

Phase 1 followed the approach used by Keil et al. (2002) in that we began with a seeded list rather than having the panelists participate in a brainstorming session to generate the initial list from scratch. The initial list of barriers was based on Pedersen’s (2004, 2005) work. The purpose of beginning with a seeded list of 31 barriers was that prior research by Pedersen (2004, 2005) had identified these barriers through a combination of literature review and a single case study. Thus, in Phase I, the panelists were encouraged to provide open-ended feedback to the starting list of 31 barriers (Pedersen, 2004, 2005). The objective was two-fold: (1) validating existing barriers and (2) adding new relevant barriers. Data were collected via the World Wide Web (instead of paper-based questionnaires administered through the mail), which yielded the benefit of decreased data collection time.

By the end of Phase I, the panel (1) provided feedback on the title, description of existing barriers and (2) suggested 26 additional barriers. We first analyzed the feedback from the panel on the existing list of barriers and we systematically changed the text and description of existing barriers based on the input from the panel. Next, we analyzed each of suggested new barriers, to examine how similar or different they were from the existing barriers. If we found the suggested barrier very similar to an existing barrier, we changed the scope and text of the existing barrier to accommodate the suggested barrier under it. On the other hand, if we found the suggested barrier different from the existing barriers, we appended the suggested barrier to the list of existing barriers. This process was followed for all the suggested 26 new barriers. By the end of this analysis, the 26 additional barriers suggested by the panelists yielded seven additional unique barriers taking the list of barriers from 31 to 38.

In Phase II, the new set of 38 barriers was presented to the panel to narrow down the list of barriers to a manageable set for the ranking phase. Schmidt (1997) suggests 20 as a manageable number to rank. We therefore presented the panel with a randomized list of the 38 barriers and asked them to select the 20 barriers that posed most serious impediments to post mortems. Only those barriers that were selected by a majority (450%) of the panel were retained. Through this simple majority vote, the panel reduced the initial list of 38 barriers to 19.

Our data analysis addressed the two research questions. In response to RQ1, we identified the top-ranked barriers and analyzed them to see how they were distributed over the different activities of the post mortem process. In response to RQ2, we interpreted the findings to identify the types of barriers to effective organizational learning practitioners emphasize as explanations of the limited use of PMEs. First, we interpreted the final rank-ordered list of barriers to inductively (Mason, 2004) arrive at three overarching reasons as to why organizations fail to implement PMEs. Such inductive interpretations help researchers to develop theoretical explanations from the data by moving from the particular to the general (Mason, 2004). In Phase IV, we then asked the panel to reflect on the final rank-ordered list and our inductive interpretation of it. We also asked them to provide their views on the implications for post mortem practices. Second, we deductively (Mason, 2004) mapped the ranked barriers against the Lyytinen & Robey (1999) typology of why organizations fail to learn. In such deductive analyses ‘theory comes first’ as theoretical explanations are derived by applying predefined concepts or propositions to the data, hence moving from the general towards the particular (Mason, 2004).

## Results and discussion

In this section, we present and discuss the results of the study organized around our three research questions.

## Important barriers to PMEs (RQ 1)

The data from the Delphi study provide insights into how experienced practitioners perceive and rank barriers to PME and how they recommend approaching PMEs to learn from IT projects.

Ranking of barriers Phase I of the study resulted in a comprehensive list of 38 PME barriers based on Pedersen’s initial list (Pedersen, 2004, 2005) and inputs from the panel. These 38 barriers are shown in the Appendix along with a detailed description of each barrier. In Phase II of the study, the list of barriers was narrowed down to those that were viewed as being most important by allowing each panelist to pick what she/he viewed as the ‘top 20 barriers.’ Some of the 38 barriers from Phase I, like ‘Getting lost in current business,’ were suggested for retention by 95% of the panel. Others, such as ‘Lack of participatory design,’ were suggested by just 5% of the panel. As noted, we retained all barriers suggested by a majority of the panel. This allowed us to narrow the list to a manageable number of barriers that could be rank ordered. The resulting list of 19 barriers can be seen in Table 4. The study proceeded with three ranking rounds of the list of 19 barriers until the stopping rules in Schmidt (1997) were met. In the first round, the panel was required to rank order the list and give reasons for their top-ranked barrier.

The first round ended with a Kendall’s W of 0.26, signifying very weak agreement (Schmidt, 1997). We proceeded with the second round with two forms of controlled feedback: the mean rank for each barrier from the first ranking round together with the reasons given by the panel for their top selection. The second round moved Kendall’s W to a weak agreement of 0.32. This suggested proceeding with a third round. Here, the panel was provided with two forms of feedback: the mean rank from second round of ranking together with their personal deviation from the mean ranking for each barrier. The third round resulted in a Kendall’s W of 0.52, signifying a moderate level of consensus had been reached and that we could have some degree of confidence in the ranking (Schmidt, 1997). The standard deviations associated with the mean rankings were examined to determine if there were any that stood out as being particularly controversial. All of the standard deviations were between 2.9 and 4.6 with the exception of the bottom-ranked barrier (lack of procedure) that had a standard deviation of 5.3. Based on this, we do not believe that the moderate level of consensus was the result of a few items that exhibited greater variance (i.e., were more controversial) in terms of their ranking. To further test this, we conducted a sensitivity analysis in which we dropped barriers one at a time and re-computed Kendall’s W. We did not find that the consensus improved significantly by dropping individual barriers.

At this point, we discussed whether to conduct another round to obtain greater consensus. The trade-off between feasibility and potential gain was considered as discussed by Schmidt (1997). We decided not to proceed with another round of ranking as a moderate level of consensus had already been reached, and we did not want to put an extra burden on our panelists whose tolerance for the exercise appeared to be waning. The results of each ranking round are summarized in Table 4.

Below, we discuss some of the panelists’ explanations for the importance of the top two barriers. Both barriers indicate that PMEs are given low priority on the organizational agenda. ‘Getting lost in current business’ was the top rated barrier to conducting PMEs. As one panelist put it:

‘Getting lost in current business’ sums it all up. It’s easier to focus on the current projects, and problems lying ahead.

Indeed, many panelists indicated that the primary barrier to conducting PMEs was the tendency to become absorbed with new work-related activities after a project was completed. This tendency made it hard to focus the time and energy that would be required to conduct a PME. Another consideration is the fact that staffing levels typically decline near the end of a project, and project personnel are often assigned to other projects at precisely the time that they would need to be available for conducting a PME. As one panelist explained:

In a development project there is often a considerable time span from staffing intensive phases (e.g. construction phase) to end of project where post mortem usually is conducted. This means that at the time of post mortem the developers are engaged in new hectic projects. It also means that management focus is gone: ‘We delivered – everybody is happy’!

The second-ranked barrier to conducting PMEs was ‘No culture for inter-project learning.’ As one panelist put it:

When there is no culture, i.e. encouragement, for taking time for reflection and learning, nothing else matters. An organization can have plenty of nice procedures and methods for PME that probably never will be used.

It was also pointed out that the mechanics of conducting PMEs are not the central issue, but rather the sharing of what is learned. One panelist pointed out that by making project leaders responsible for conducting PMEs, an organization could begin to address the cultural barriers:

The most important barrier is the lack of culture for interproject learning. If you have an organization where the project leader is responsible for the post mortem evaluation, you will experience, that it is much easier.

## Why PMEs are seldom practiced (RQ 2)

After the ranking phase of the Delphi was completed, we interpreted the top-ranked barriers to identify why organizations seldom practice PMEs. We engaged in both inductive and deductive interpretations (Mason, 2004). First, we inductively interpreted patterns across the ranked barriers. Second, we deductively interpreted the ranked barriers in relation to the basic PME activities. Third, we deductively interpreted the ranked barriers based on organizational learning theory (Lyytinen & Robey, 1999). These insights subsequently supported our response to the third research question below.

Three Specific Reasons First, we categorized the ranked barriers into three specific reasons for why organizations seldom practice SMEs. The top-ranked group of barriers deals with organizational factors such as organizational emphasis and organizational commitment that inhibit performing post mortems. This cluster of barriers shows that organizational members are aware of post mortems, but they are too busy with other projects, the incentives are too weak, and bad project planning makes it unlikely that post mortems are performed. This interpretation of the panel’s ranking suggests lack of emphasis on or lack of commitment to PMEs as the primary reason that post mortems are rarely performed. Thus, the top sevenranked barriers (Barriers 1–7 in Table 4) all relate to lack of emphasis on or lack of commitment to PMEs. In other words, while the idea of PME seems to be on most actors’ radar screen, the activity is given too low priority to become actionable as a project unfolds and wraps up. Put differently, there is no culture for PMEs.

The second group of ranked barriers focuses on difficulties associated with performing post mortems. It can be difficult to acquire relevant information because of the situated and tacit nature of knowledge and because of time lag between decision, action, and outcome in IT projects. This interpretation suggests difficulties in acquiring and processing the information needed in a post mortem as the secondary reason that post mortems are rarely performed. This second cluster is comprised of six barriers in the middle of the ranked list (Barriers 8–13 in Table 4) that all relate to difficulties of acquiring and processing the information needed to conduct a PME. These barriers indicate that even if actors operate in an environment that encourages PMEs, they encounter severe difficulties in executing the activity.

The third group of ranked barriers focuses on the conditions for performing post mortems. This group suggests that the PMEs are difficult when there is insufficient funding, lack of standard procedures, insufficient integration with existing learning systems, and weakly supported recommendations. Thus, at the bottom of the ranked list, there are six barriers (Barriers 14–19 in Table 4) that relate to the conditions for performing PMEs. These barriers are ranked relatively low. While the inclusion of this group of barriers in the list indicate that the conditions for conducting PMEs is important, the ranking indicates that it is less important than the two other categories.

To provide some measure of validation for this interpretation of the major reasons why organizations seldom practice PMEs, we went back to the panelists for a fourth and final reflection phase of the Delphi study (see Figure 2). In this phase, the expert practitioners were asked to review and comment on our clustering of the barriers into the three reasons described above. A 7-point Likert scale was used to measure the panel’s agreement to this grouping and interpretation (1 ¼ agree and 7 ¼ disagree). The mean response was 2.8, indicating that panelists generally agreed with our interpretation.

Barriers to PME activities In further examining the barriers identified by the panel as being most important, it is interesting to note that they span all four activities of the PME process identified by Pedersen: (1) design a productive context, (2) focus and collect data, (3) analyze and interpret data, and (4) share and exploit knowledge. The panel put very high emphasis on barriers to sharing and exploiting knowledge resulting from post mortems: two of the three highest ranked barriers relate to this element (‘No culture for inter-project learning’ and ‘Lack of mechanism to encourage exploitation’). Five out of the seven initial barriers in this category were included in the ranking, and the average ranking for these five barriers was 7.8. The panel also put high emphasis on barriers to analyzing and interpreting data: the highest ranked barrier relates to this element (‘Getting lost in current business’). Four out of the initial nine barriers in this category were included in the ranking, and the average ranking for these four barriers was 8.25. Barriers associated with designing a productive context were also emphasized, although slightly less, by the panel: the fourth highest ranked barrier relates to this element (‘Lack of commitment and incentives’). Seven out of the initial 12 barriers in this category were included in the ranking, and the average for these barriers is 10.3. Finally, while the panel also included barriers associated with focusing and collecting data, they paid the least attention to this core element of post mortems: the fifth highest ranked barrier related to this element (‘Impractical to include all stakeholders’). Three out of the initial 10 barriers in this category were included in the ranking, and the average for these barriers is 10.6.

The experts suggest in other words that there are important barriers related to all activities of the evaluation process. However, the two areas that seem most problematic are analyzing and interpreting data coupled with sharing and exploiting knowledge.

Barriers to organizational learning As a third attempt to explain why organizations seldom practice SMEs, we mapped the 19 ranked PME barriers against the four reasons identified by Lyytinen & Robey (1999) for why organizations fail to learn from IT projects: (1) limits on organizational intelligence, (2) disincentives for learning, (3) organizational design, and (4) educational barriers. Each of the four authors independently performed this mapping and then compared their results. There was unanimous agreement on the mapping of seven of the 19 barriers and 75% agreement on the mapping of four of the 19 barriers. For the remaining eight barriers, there was 50% agreement on the mapping. Areas of disagreement were discussed until consensus was reached.

Table 5 shows the final mapping of the PME barriers against the four reasons why organizations fail to learn from IT projects. The results of this mapping revealed that 10 of the 19 PME barriers relate to limits on organizational intelligence. The remaining nine PME barriers were evenly distributed across the three other reasons why organizations fail to learn. Of particular interest is the fact that the four highest ranked barriers to PMEs were mapped to limits to organizational intelligence (first and third highest ranked barriers) and disincentives for learning (second and fourth highest ranked barriers). This indicates that while all four reasons for failure to learn in IT projects identified by Lyytinen & Robey (1999) help to explain why there are significant barriers to conducting PMEs, the most critical ones are limits to organizational intelligence and disincentives for learning. This is consistent with an observation made by Gwillim et al. (2005) who note that there was no recognition of the value of organizational learning in the two organizations they studied.

## Conditions for successfully adopting PMEs (RQ 3)

In the reflection phase of our Delphi, the panelists indicated that despite the many barriers to performing PMEs, they believed that organizations could overcome them. As part of the reflection phase, we asked panelists whether they agreed or disagreed with the following statement: ‘Organizations can overcome the barriers identified in the study.’ Using a 7-point scale (1 ¼ agree; 7 ¼ disagree), the mean response was 2.8 indicating that panelists were optimistic on this point.

Panelists were also asked: ‘What action(s) would you recommend to organizations that want to overcome the key PME barriers?’ One panelist recommended that PMEs be planned at the outset of a project. Such action might help to build time into the project schedule for conducting the PME. Another panelist recommended using PMEs earlier (e.g., when the detailed specification phase is completed) so that they can promote learning and improved communication during the remaining course of the project. One panelist recommended PMEs should only be executed ‘if you have a plan for how to do something about the findings.’ The notion here is that PMEs can often generate results that end up being put on the shelf, but never implemented, and therefore do not lead to improved practices within the organization.

Finally, we asked the panelists whether they agreed or disagreed with the following statement: ‘Organizations should conduct PMEs on selected projects’ and we recorded their responses on a 7-point scale (1 ¼ agree; 7 ¼ disagree). The mean response was 1.5 indicating that the panel of experienced practitioners strongly agreed with the notion that organizations should conduct PMEs on selected projects. We also asked panelists whether they agreed or disagreed with the following statement: ‘The benefits of performing PMEs on selected projects outweigh the costs.’ On the same 7-point scale described earlier, the mean response was 2.2 indicating that the panel of experienced practitioners agreed with the notion that the benefits outweigh the costs.

An interesting and somewhat puzzling pattern emerges when reviewing these recommendations from the panel of IT specialists in relation to the above interpretations of how they ranked barriers to PME adoption. While the IT specialists rank ordered 19 barriers to PME adoption, they basically remained optimistic. They believed that organizations can overcome the barriers identified in this study; they recommended that organizations should conduct PMEs on selected projects; and, they provided a number of specific recommendations for how organizations can overcome the barriers, for example, planning PMEs at the outset of projects, adopting PMEs earlier in the project life-cycle to facilitate learning within the project, and explicitly planning how insights from PMEs can be shared across projects.

However, a more pessimistic view emerges through the interpretations of the identified and rank-ordered barriers. All three interpretations suggest that it is fundamental barriers related to organizational learning in general rather than specific barriers related to effectively executing PMEs that constitute the primary reasons why organizations seldom practice PMEs. The first interpretation suggests that the primary reason for not practicing PMEs is lack of a culture in which learning is prioritized (secondary priority is given to barriers towards acquiring and processing information and to the conditions for conducting PMEs). The second interpretation suggests that it is barriers to analysis and interpretation of data and to share and exploit knowledge that are the primary reasons for why PMEs are not practiced (secondary priority is given to barriers to designing a productive context and to focusing on and collecting data). The third interpretation suggests that it limits to organizational intelligence and disincentives for learning that are the primary reasons (secondary reasons are organization design and educational barriers). Not only does the panel in this way give highest priority to general issues related to organizational barriers but by doing so, they also give highest priority to the barriers that are most difficult to overcome.

Rather than rejecting seemingly illogical findings like these or choosing between the opposing perspectives they offer, researchers have increasingly adopted paradoxical thinking in attempts to develop more comprehensive insights into the organizational realities they represent (Poole & Van de Ven, 1989; Lewis, 2000; Smith & Tushman, 2005). Lewis (2000) suggests a framework for such explorations of paradox that (1) identifies tensions as the underlying source for paradox, (2) emphasizes how actors’ defensive reactions can fuel reinforcing cycles, and (3) suggests that managers explore tensions and thereby ‘tap the potential energy, insights, and power of paradox that enable dramatic change’ (p. 762).

Following the first step of Lewis’ framework (Lewis, 2000), the puzzling pattern of positive recommendations towards PME from the panel combined with their rather pessimistic view of barriers towards PME, suggests underlying tensions between development practices and the learning contexts in most organizations. From the point of view of development practices, the panel finds plenty of reasons to adopt PMEs; however, from the point of view of learning contexts, most of their organizations are struggling to provide sufficient organizational intelligence and suitable incentives to support effective PMEs. The continued high failure rate related to IT projects suggests that these tensions are relatively widespread within the industry and that most organizations therefore fail to learn in the context of IT projects (Lyytinen & Robey, 1999; Wastell, 1999).

Considering the second step of Lewis’ framework (Lewis, 2000), giving priority to PMEs in these organizations will most likely not increase learning because the general conditions for learning are not in place. A likely outcome of pushing PMEs in these contexts is therefore defensive reactions: either practitioners will accommodate PMEs into current practices as another must-do activity or they will attempt to facilitate learning through PMEs only to experience limited or no impact. In either case, the adoption of PMEs will likely reinforce rather than overcome the organization’s existing learning dysfunctions. Put differently, the findings in this study suggest that adoption of PMEs is not a good tactic for developing appropriate organizational learning capabilities. Only when appropriate organizational intelligence and incentives for learning can be developed within the organization, can one hope to successfully adopt PMEs as an effective means to learn from IT projects.

Finally, following Lewis’ third step (Lewis, 2000), researchers and managers need to more directly confront the tension between development practices and learning context to overcome the barriers related to effectively exploiting PMEs. Based on the presented rank-ordering of barriers to PME adoption in the context of IT projects and the subsequent interpretations of the ranking from the point of view of organizational learning, we propose that the following PME paradox needs exploration:

Adopting Post Mortem Evaluations is likely to reinforce organizational learning dysfunctions rather than improve current development practices.

When asked about recommendations for how organizations could adopt PMEs, one panelist provided important clues that led in this direction. The panelist suggested that PMEs would only be effective if you can create a culture for learning from failures such as that, which exists within the aviation industry. Our analyses confirm that this is the challenge that must be overcome if senior managers want PMEs to become useful exercises in their organizations. Moreover, general studies also point in this direction suggesting there is evidence that organizations not only ‘fail to learn,’ but also ‘learn to fail’ (Lyytinen & Robey, 1999; Wastell, 1999) in the context of IT projects. Indeed, paradoxical thinking explains how actors’ defensive reactions to the critical tensions between development practices and learning contexts create reinforcing cycles that intensify rather than reduce tensions. Transcending current practices would require managers to not simply adopt PMEs, but to seek ways to overcome learning dysfunctions while at the same time pushing for more widespread use of PMEs. Eventually, this would allow IT professionals and the organizations they work for to embrace failures as an opportunity for both individual and organizational learning.

## Implications

Before turning to the implications of this research, it is appropriate to point out the study’s limitations. As with any Delphi-type study, the results are based on a limited number of subjects. While subjects were chosen for their experience with IT project management and PMEs, we can make no claim about a representative sample, as our panelists were not chosen randomly and were all working for Scandinavian organizations. Given the nature and size of our sample, one must be cautious in generalizing. Having said this, the sample is relatively diverse in terms of IS department size (25-2500 IS employees). Further research will need to be undertaken, however, to determine if the results obtained here would hold for other cultures. In spite of the aforementioned limitations, we believe that the results of the study have important implications for both research and practice. In terms of theory, we have interpreted the results from the Delphi study drawing upon a combination of organizational learning and paradoxical thinking. Other lenses, for example, reputation management (Scott & Walsham, 2005) or politics of blame (Brown & Jones, 1998) in IS research, could also have been applied and would have led to complementary insights. Exploration of these opportunities remains a challenge for future research.

## Implications for research

The chief objective in conducting this research was to explain why PME of IT projects is often advocated, but seldom practiced. In this research we therefore set out to explore why the practice of performing PMEs is not more common. While previous research has pointed to some of the barriers associated with conducting PMEs, this study represents the first systematic attempt to identify and rank these barriers. Thus, our research provides new and valuable insight into the barriers that make it difficult to establish or successfully conduct PMEs of IT projects. Our panel of experienced practitioners identified and rank ordered a list of key barriers. The panel initially commented on Pedersen’s proposed list of barriers and in addition they identified new barriers. That led to a list of 38 barriers in total. The experts subsequently selected 19 of these as being the most important and rank ordered them to arrive at the list in Table 4.

The two highest ranked barriers were ‘Getting lost in current business’ and ‘No culture for inter-project learning.’ Interestingly, we found empirical support for Pedersen’s (2004, 2005) notion that PME barriers exist within all four activities of the process: (1) design a productive context, (2) focus and collect data, (3) analyze and interpret data, and (4) share and exploit knowledge. However, the two areas that seem most problematic are analyzing and interpreting data coupled with sharing and exploiting knowledge. This is consistent with the other two interpretations of the ranking of barriers. First, we found that lack of emphasis on or lack of commitment to PMEs together with difficulties of acquiring and processing the information needed are the most important reasons why organizations fail to learn from PMEs. Second, we found that the perceptions espoused by the panel of experienced practitioners imply that IT organizations fail to learn from IT projects because of limits on organizational intelligence.

These findings, the attention drawn to the critical tensions between development practices and learning contexts and the proposed post mortem paradox, underscore the importance of a research agenda that addresses the learning dysfunctions in IT organizations (Lyytinen & Robey, 1999; Wastell, 1999). Given what we know about the IT context, the question may not be ‘can we get PMEs to be performed more routinely?’ but rather ‘can we create contexts and cultures in which PMEs are routinely performed on those projects that maximize organizational learning?’ We suggest that paradoxical thinking (Cameron, 1986; Poole & Van de Ven, 1989; Lewis, 2000) can be used to further explore how organizations can take appropriate steps towards becoming a learning organization to facilitate successful adoption of PMEs. Creating a culture that is receptive to conducting PMEs and learning from failures will not be easy. As Vaughan points out in her analysis of the launch decision behind the space shuttle Challenger disaster, organizations may become pre-occupied with the ‘culture of production’ and this may prevent them from engaging in the type of reviews that would prevent failures and promote learning (Vaughan, 1996). Ultimately, the demand for project evaluation as a form of risk management is likely to be a function of cultural demands for accountability. Thus, further research that builds on the concepts of culture, accountability, and risk may prove useful in extending our understanding (Douglas, 1992; Power 2004).

In terms of when organizations ought to be most concerned about conducting PMEs, the organizational learning and dynamic capabilities literature (Zollo & Winter, 2002) suggest that the value of PMEs to codify and share the causes of failure will be maximized when: (1) the particular mode of failure is infrequent or unusual in some way, (2) a firm has a high degree of heterogeneity in the projects it conducts and there is a need to understand what works and what doesn’t in different contexts, and (3) there is a high degree of causal ambiguity between the actions used to manage projects and project performance outcomes.

## Implications for practice

Our research has several implications for practice. First, the full list of 38 PME barriers identified and validated by the panel of expert practitioners can serve as a comprehensive checklist of barriers that managers can use to assess whether their organizations are conducive to the practice of performing PMEs. Knowing where the barriers are is half of the battle for the practitioner who wishes to implement PMEs on selective projects. There is, however, a cost associated with relying on a list that contains 38 separate barriers. Such a list may prove too cumbersome and complicated to use in practice both because of its size and its lack of a coherent structure. The ranked list of 19 barriers provides a more compact list that contains only the most important barriers. Practitioners can use the rankings to direct their limited resources toward removing those barriers that matter most.

For many managers, however, even a list of 19 barriers can be cumbersome to deal with. To address this issue, we have distilled the 19 barriers into three clusters: (1) lack of emphasis on or lack of commitment to PMEs, (2) difficulties of acquiring and processing the information needed, and (3) conditions for performing PMEs. In terms of relative importance, practitioners should first focus on overcoming the lack of emphasis on or lack of commitment to learning within their organizations. If there is no culture for learning, PMEs will not be likely to make any difference, and may actually reinforce existing organizational learning dysfunctions as expressed in the PME paradox. Instituting an effective PME process requires cultural change and ‘cultural changes require time and repetition before they are firmly embedded into the organization’ (Kwak & Stoddard, 2004, p. 920).

Finally, this study provides strong support to the statement ‘Organizations should conduct PMEs on selected projects’. Once the organizational culture is sufficiently learning oriented, the question that practitioners need to wrestle with is not, ‘Should we conduct PMEs?’ but rather, ‘Which projects should we conduct PMEs on?’ This research suggests that PMEs should be conducted on those projects that represent genuine learning opportunities for the organization. Projects that exhibit new failure modes or projects where existing dogma and project management wisdom were challenged and maybe transcended are certainly candidates for investigation. Moreover, PMEs should only be conducted if and when the organization is able to mobilize or develop sufficient organizational intelligence to identify and share the knowledge from these efforts.

## Conclusion

Based on the evidence from this study, most experts agree that PMEs are valuable and that they should be performed at least on selected projects. However, this study has identified the barriers to conducting PMEs and it has proposed a paradox that explains why most organizations fail to adopt PMEs despite their espoused advantages. The consequence of this paradox is that organizations need to develop sufficient learning capability before adoption of PMEs is likely to make a difference. At the outset we pointed out that other engineering disciplines seem to routinely perform PMEs, whereas the practice of examining and learning from failures is not routine in the field of IS. While we believe that there would be much to be gained from more widespread use of PMEs, we must, however, also point out that there are clear differences between the contexts in which PMEs are conducted routinely and the IT context.

The contexts in which PMEs have become routine are ones in which failure is clear and unambiguous and can lead to catastrophic outcomes (e.g., the collapse of a building, the crash of a commercial aircraft, and the release of radiation or toxic chemicals into the environment). Moreover, in many of these contexts, there are government agencies or regulations that trigger certain reporting or investigatory work whenever such failures occur. In the IT arena, there simply is no analogue to the

FAA and NTSB that serve as watchdog and investigator in the case of plane crashes and air traffic control failures. Moreover, in the IT context failures are seldom as clearcut as an airplane that comes tumbling out of the sky or a bridge that collapses. For example, a project may be delivered late and over budget, but may meet some, if not all, of the users’ requirements. Moreover, while IT project failures can lead to catastrophic outcomes or even loss of life (e.g., medical devices or automobile transmissions that fail due to programming errors), this is certainly not the norm.
