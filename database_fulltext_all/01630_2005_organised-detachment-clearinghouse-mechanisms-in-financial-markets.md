# Organised detachment: Clearinghouse mechanisms in financial markets

Yuval Millo <sup>a</sup>, Fabian Muniesa <sup>b</sup>, Nikiforos S. Panourgias <sup>c</sup>, Susan V. Scott <sup>c,\*</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Accounting, Finance and Management, University of Essex, Wivenhoe Park, Colchester, C04 3SQ, United Kingdom

<sup>b</sup> Centre de Sociologie de lInnovation (CSI), Ecole des Mines de Paris, 60, Boulevard Saint-Michel, 75006 Paris, France

<sup>c</sup> Department of Information Systems, London School of Economics and Political Science, Houghton Street, London WC2A 2AE, United Kingdom

Received 22 December 2003; accepted 4 February 2005

## Abstract

Bringing transactions to an end constitutes a crucial stage of market activity: the detachment between the counterparties engaged in a trade must be guaranteed. In financial markets, this operation relies on organisational technologies, such as clearinghouses, that can reach a high degree of sophistication. In this paper, we use financial clearinghouse mechanisms to explore how such detachment technologies are constructed. Based on several historical examples, our review shows that clearinghouse mechanisms developed on the basis of an increasingly IT-enabled organisational separation between the trading and clearing stages of market activity were a crucial factor that enabled clearinghouses to calculate the mutual obligations of the counterparties and perform the consequential steps. Our analysis goes on to reveal a paradoxical thread in the evolution of clearing: increasing informational and calculative capacity have lead clearing mechanisms to breach the separation on which their ability to operate was dependent – the boundary between trading activity and clearing processes. These findings shed a new light on the reflexive nature of IT-enabled market innovations and emphasise their role in re-introducing new forms of disorganisation back into contemporary financial markets.  2005 Elsevier Ltd. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Clearing and settlement; Financial markets; Economic sociology; Information systems; Calculation; Risk; Organisational change

## 1. Introduction

Clearing and settlement systems are an essential component of the organisation of financial markets. They constitute the -backstage without which present levels of trading activity would not be sustainable. These composite systems, which are both organisational arrangements and technological facilities, ensure the undisputed closure of financial transactions. While this is correct, this description is not exhaustive. Financial markets are also among todays prominent arenas of reflexive disorganisation – techno-social sites in which risk-reducing devices can generate new and unpredictable forms of hazards. This paper ofers an initial historical analysis of financial clearing and settlement, using a sociological framework. In so doing, we reveal the reflexive elements that turn modern financial clearing not only into a form of organisational technology, but to a potential new source of volatility and disorganisation.

The sociological framework used in this paper focuses on the materiality and spatiality of economic arrangements. It connects with recent developments in the sociological study of financial markets that have emphasised the role of material, socio-technical devices in the construction of markets ( Beunza & Stark, 2004; Knorr Cetina & Bruegger, 2002; MacKenzie, 2004; MacKenzie & Millo, 2003). These relatively novel developments are grounded in a science and technology studies (STS) perspective that has proved particularly useful in dealing with the technical sophistication of financial markets. Because it insist on the role of techniques in the construction of economic facts, this perspective is also relevant for the study of economic calculative practices at large (Miller, 2001; Vollmer, 2003), and of the organisation of coordination in economic activities (The´venot, 2001).

This paper focuses on the prominent organisational form among clearing and settlement systems – the clearinghouse.<sup>1</sup> We analyze several historical examples of clearinghouse mechanisms, which show how this organisational form evolved. In particular, we focus on the performance of detachment by clearinghouses: how the counterparties engaged in a trade are disentangled from the obligations embedded in the trading process so as to be in a position to continue trading while the processing of transaction is still ongoing. Drawing on current literature in economic sociology and anthropology, the first section of this paper presents the importance of the -detachment processes in markets in general, and in financial markets in particular. The second section introduces a conceptual account of the clearinghouse as an institution that constitutes an important step in the organisation of detachments and the achievement of overall finality and that by separating the clearing process from the trading process the clearinghouse provides an informational hub for the management of reciprocal obligations. The third section explores the emergence of a clearinghouse as an economic informational hub. Drawing on Charles Babbages description of Londons banking clearinghouse in the 19th century, we focus on the material and spatial aspects of the clearinghouse. The fourth section expands this analysis to the evolution of securities markets and shows how the separation of clearing from trading became central to the evolution of modern clearing. The fifth section explores the derivatives markets. Due to the increasing complexity of such markets and the time dimension embedded in derivatives contracts, clearinghouse procedures included more complex forms of calculation. The sixth section shows how new, risk-based, clearing methods have eroded the distinction between trading and clearing that was at the centre of the clearinghouse organisational form.

## 2. Finalising transactions in financial markets

Ending a trade is a key requirement in markets in general and in financial market in particular. The etymology of the word ‘‘finance’’ illustrates the centrality of this point. In Middle English the word takes on the meaning of payment or ransom, while in Middle French it derives from the word ‘‘finer’’, to end, to pay, which has its root in the word ‘‘fin’’, end. In financial markets, actors engage in a variety of, sometimes highly risky, entanglements with each other. From this perspective, clearing and settlement processes allow economic agents to unwind such entanglements, discharging them from reciprocal obligations. They constitute organisational technologies that aim at finalizing transactions.

The achievement of finality is an inherent aspect of any market procedure. In spite of multiple social factors that are involved in economic transactions, economic obligations contracted between the parties involved have to end. This point serves as one of the fundamental diferences with gift-based economies as economic anthropology has shown us. Godelier (1999) shows that, as opposed to market exchange, gift exchange keeps economic agents obliged to each other because the donor, as a person, is present in the gift. A similar point was made by Viviana Zelizer (1997) when she described the operations of -earmarking that are needed to extract money from the pure realm of economic alienation and transform it into a gift. Economic anthropology also provided us with the initial tools for the conceptualising of detachment. Thomas (1991) refers to market transactions as a series of entanglements and disentanglement. According to Thomas, markets are distinguished from other social institutions that facilitate transactions in maintaining institutionalised mechanisms that allow agents to disentangle from their obligations after they perform transactions. This point was developed further by Callon (1998) who stressed that the distinctive feature of economic markets is that in them, after the transaction, the economic agents are -quits.

Pioneering research in the sociology of financial markets (Abolafia, 1996, 1998; Baker, 1984) show how social ties make the market possible. Recent studies in this field have also explicitly explored how specific technologies make market connections possible in a globalised environment (Beunza & Stark, 2004; Knorr Cetina & Bruegger, 2002).<sup>2</sup> But, the -detachment side of market transactions is, at least, as important as the -attachment side. The fact that market participants frequently know each other and remain socially connected after the trades end does not contradict the fact that they require to be -quits, at least to some extent. It has to be stressed that the concept of disentanglement and detachment has been the focal point of an active debate in economic sociology in the last decades.<sup>3</sup> The main point of contention is that, as Granovetter (1985) has pointed out, markets are -socially embedded in existing cultural and social networks. Therefore, the argument continues, real detachment between any two economic agents cannot be performed, because they will continue to be attached to the overall social networks within which the market is embedded. We agree with the main thrust of this argument. However, we would like to qualify Granovetters approach by suggesting that in the case of financial markets detachment and the -embeddedness of the markets does not contradict each other, but are complementary. We claim that the evolution of the various detachment mechanisms was embedded in a strong institutional motivation to encourage and maintain active trading. Indeed, as the historical review in this paper shows, the evolution of clearing procedures was underpinned by a fundamental motivation to create a streamlined, operational detachment mechanism. That is, not only that the networks in which the markets were embedded did not prevent detachment, they took a crucial part in its development.

In spite of this lack of sociological attention, at least in the case of financial markets,<sup>4</sup> the clearing process has been at the centre of numerous other research projects, both in economic and practitioner literature. In particular, the study of systemic risk in financial markets highlights the role of the network of connections among market participants and, therefore, served as a basis for the development of alternative clearing models (Crockett & Cohen, 2001; Jackson & Perraudin, 2002; Fleming & Garbade, 2002). More generally, the development of the clearing and settlement sector in financial markets, and the concomitant enlargement and transformation of back-ofice departments in banks and other financial services entities, have also generated a consistent body of literature that describes and analyses clearing organisations (Karlin, 1997; Karyotis, 2003; Loader, 2002; Weiss, 2003).

## 3. Introducing the clearinghouse

In this paper, we focus on one of todays most popular forms of detachment-making: the clearinghouse. The clearinghouse is an institution that interposes itself between the counterparties and operates as a -third party in the disentanglement of trades and the resolution of obligations between counterparties: in its more stabilised forms, it thus works as a -central counterparty (CCP). This organisational form applies primarily to organised and centralised markets (stock exchanges, bourses) in which standardised products (stocks, listed derivatives such as futures and options, but also commodities) are traded in a multilateral and public matching of buy and sell orders.<sup>5</sup>

Clearinghouse methods developed historically as a way to cope with the increasing complexity of market activity. Let us briefly focus on the historical example of North-American futures exchanges (Moser, 2000). Early clearing practices where strictly bilateral – -direct clearing – and the terms of detachment where specified by the counterparties to each contract. In such markets, nascent clearing institutions served chiefly as mediators in cases of disputes. Since the bilateral resolution of disputes proved to be lengthy and costly, market participants created associations that were put in charged of handling them. As trading practices became more complex, the number of disputes also grew. For such a system to function, information relating to the agreed trades had to be provided to the association. Embryonic central information hubs thus emerged. This in turn resulted in the number of exchange rules increasing and in the induction of internal production of market statistics. This centralisation of information also facilitated the ability to ofset claims by, for example, agreeing to settle through payment of the resulting diference. The cost of maintaining open positions, coupled with the increasing complexity, volume, and speed of transactions, led to the development of a new method of clearing – -ring clearing – a method which allowed the counterparties to be substituted for each other. This further facilitated ofsetting by increasing the number of potential counterparties and reduced counterparty risk by reducing the accumulation of dependencies as contracts are ofset. The subsequent evolution of the clearing and settlement structures was the establishment of central clearinghouses, also known as -complete clearing (Moser, 2000),<sup>6</sup> a method that enabled the ofsetting, through aggregation and netting, of transaction claims among all market participants who were members of the exchange (and, therefore, of the clearinghouse).<sup>7</sup>

The clearinghouse, we see, appears as an organisational device that supports the detachment between the counterparties. From this perspective, the clearinghouse is a -normalising device, in a Foucauldian sense: it separates the -normal trades – the ones that can be finalised without dispute–from the -abnormal ones – the trades that require conflict management.<sup>8</sup> By so doing, the clearinghouse produces, in efect, an organisational environment within which price-setting can take place more eficiently. It does so by separating the resolution of financial obligations (after the trade) from the trading activity as such, i.e., by displacing the clearing process to a dedicated institutional space in which reciprocal duties are to be managed by a -third party. The clearinghouse moves the clearing away from the trading. It achieves a separation between the price-setting stage, in which an order is executed on the exchange, and the clearing and settlement stage in which the details of the transaction (identities of parties, respective obligations, settlement arrangements) are registered, aggregated and netted, the positioning of assets and payment are confirmed, and finally ownership is transferred. Finality (the complete detachment between the counterparties) can only be achieved when all obligations are met. Through this kind of clearing arrangements, technical and legal aspects of the detachment are -re-located. The market participants who take part in the trading activity are relieved from the technical and legal detachments needed to finalise the transaction, as these are postponed or relocated in a separate organisational space.

## 4. The clearinghouse as a calculative space

How does this separate clearing space operate? Enabling agents to detach in a -clean way involves calculative operations. In this regard, the clearinghouse provides an excellent example not only of an organisational space put also of a -calculative one. The notion of calculation employed here emphasises the spatiality and materiality of calculative practices: it refers both to the abstract manipulation of mathematical entities and to the concrete technological and organisational aspects of calculative activity. A calculative space, therefore, is an organisational apparatus through which information is collected, sorted, retrieved and then undergoes manipulations, in a material sense (Callon & Muniesa, 2003; Latour, 1987). As mentioned in the previous section, early developments in clearinghouse methods came from the development of information hubs that facilitated an -economy of means for reconciliation. This is where clearing meets calculation, in a quite literal sense, as the clearinghouse is often referred to as a fundamental step in the history of computer machinery (Campbell-Kelly & Aspray, 1996).

One interesting – if not very well known – episode in the history of computers is Charles Babbages discovery of the Bankers Clearing House in London. At the time when Babbage was struggling with the construction of his Diference Engine (a calculating machine which operated by breaking down complex arithmetic tasks into simple, mechanical operations), this central institution of Londons financial life was one of the rare examples of its time of a large-scale information management organisation and is often presented as a highlight in the history of computers (Campbell-Kelly & Aspray, 1996: pp. 10–20).<sup>9</sup> This institution was a clearinghouse facility used for the reciprocal payment of checks between the Citys banks. As a consequence of the rapid development of the use of checks in the $1 8 ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ century, before the existence of the Bankers Clearing House, an army of delivery boys had to run through the City to exchange checks against cash. Towards the end of the century, all these runners arranged to meet in a single place: the Five Bells Public House, in Lombard Street. This innovation saved time and prevented delivery boys from being robbed. In addition, it also enabled an important economic aspect to become apparent: if two banks had to pay checks reciprocally, the amount of money needed in banknotes to settle the operation was only the diference between the amounts due and claimed, which was consistently less than if payments were treated sequentially. On the basis of this clearinghouse principle, bankers decided to establish the Bankers Clearing House at 10 Lombard Street.

In 1832, little after the establishment of the clearinghouse, Babbage wrote to its then secretary, John Lubbock (influential financier, science lover and vice-president of the Royal Society), hoping to gain access to the clearinghouse (Campbell-Kelly & Aspray, 1996: pp. 10–20). Lubbock accepted, but warned Babbage sternly against any publicising of interesting procedures he might observe inside the clearinghouse: the public should never think that access to that -temple of finance is free. Despite the warning, Babbage could not refrain from providing a detailed, quasi-algorithmic description of the clearinghouse functioning in chapter 14 (‘‘Of Money as a Medium of Exchange’’) of his On the Economy of Machinery and Manufactures:

‘‘In a large room in Lombard Street, about thirty clerks from the several London bankers take their stations, in alphabetical order, at desks placed round the room; each having a small open box by his side, and the name of the firm to which he belongs in large characters on the wall above his head. From time to time other clerks from every house enter the room, and, passing along, drop into the box the checks due by that firm to the house from which this distrib utor is sent. The clerk at the table enters the amount of the several checks in a book previously prepared, under the name of the bank to which they are respectively due. Four oclock in the afternoon is the latest hour to which the boxes are open to receive checks; and at a few minutes before that time, some signs of increased activity begin to appear in this previously quiet and business-like scene. Numerous clerks then arrive anxious to distribute, up to the latest possible moment, the checks that have been paid into the houses of their employers. At four oclock all the boxes are removed, and each clerk adds up the amount of the checks put into his box and payable by his own to other houses. He also receives another book from his own house, containing the amounts of the checks that their distributing clerk has put into the box of every other banker. Having compared these, he writes out the balances due to or from his own house, opposite the name of each of the other banks; and having verified this statement by a comparison with the similar list made by the clerks of those houses, he sends to his own bank the general balance resulting from this sheet, the amount of which, if it is due from that to other houses, is sent back in bank-notes. At five oclock the Inspector takes his seat; when each clerk, who has upon the result of all the transactions a balance to pay to various other houses, pays it to the inspector, who gives a ticket for the amount. The clerks of those houses to whom money is due, then receive the several sums from the inspector, who takes from them a ticket for the amount. Thus the whole of these payments are made by a double system of balance, a very small amount of bank-notes passing from hand to hand, and scarcely any coin.’’ (Babbage, 1835: p. 173)

It is striking how the structure of the Bankers Clearing House, as described by Babbage, resonates with modern computer architecture: input and output, some kind of memory (the clerks books), and a proto-CPU organised around the inspector.<sup>10</sup> The clearinghouse is a calculative space in the sense that it organises entities inside a single frame in a way that calculative manipulations are possible and in the sense that it renders those entities governable (Callon & Muniesa, 2003; Miller, 1994, 2001). Netting payments between members, collecting payments, and debiting or crediting members accounts are among the calculative practices that such a space allows for. Because it calculates detachments, the clearinghouse imposes a discipline that lets the market evolve towards forms that are more complex. It is not merely an added facility, but an organisational tool that allows for the further development of markets.

## 5. Enabling markets through the separation of clearing

The example of the Bankers Clearing House shows how the application of industrial organisation to calculative activities provided a dedicated organisational support for calculating the obligations between financial players. This example, however, refers to a particular case of an inter-bank payment system and not to clearing and settlement of transactions of securities and other traded financial instruments and contracts. The picture becomes more complex if we consider the netting of payments relating to trading activities in a stock exchange or a future contracts exchange.

The role of a clearinghouse as a provider of calculative facilities for the detachment between counterparties is more visible when it is used to clear securities transactions. In the latter, the calculative apparatus is relied upon to supply the parties with the assurance to attach themselves to each other and then to detach themselves from each other without complications in an environment in which -taking a position (e.g., entering into a trade) is, explicitly, taking a risk. In financial exchange clearinghouse operations we witness a full deployment of the separation between the trading (or price-setting) and the clearing and settlement stage, as a way of performing the detachment between counterparties in a less conflict-prone manner.<sup>11</sup> The appearance of an organisational distinction between front-ofice and back-ofice activities in brokerage houses and investment banks can be seen as a material trace of this displacement.<sup>12</sup> The front-ofice activity has to live -in the market and, in order to do that, has to be discharged from the task of achieving of full finality, an operation that is actually moved -out of the front line of the market. This movement is not of an abstract nature. It is also materialised in the physical displacement of trade information (order tickets, confirmation documents, etc.) from the front-ofice desks to the back-ofice departments.

This materiality of the management of post-trade paperwork (i.e., the performance of detachment) has been at the centre of many organisational concerns in the recent history of financial markets. The physical transfer of paper documents contributed, for example, to the back ofice crisis (known as -the paper crunch) of late 1967 in the US. A booming stock market overwhelmed the clearing and settlement capabilities of many of the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE) firms. During the summer and autumn of that year trading firms experienced considerable backlog of transactions, loss of data and other problems. During several weeks the situation became so severe that trading on the NYSE was limited to four days a week in order to limit the flux of transactions (Seligman, 1982).

It is precisely in relation to these kinds of problems that clearinghouse procedures intervene. In 1968, and as a way to outline a solution to this -back ofice crisis, the NYSE established the Central Certificate Service, which was later at the origin of the Depository Trust Company (DTC), the US institution in charge of the holding of securities certificates, of the registry of ownership and of the management of the necessary administrative tasks. This was the first step toward a more radical solution: the dematerialisation of securities. Dematerialisation was performed through registration of ownership in a centralised depository facility.<sup>13</sup> Simultaneously, the NSCC (National Securities Clearing Corporation) proposed a multilateral netting protocol for the transactions performed on the NYSE, the American Stock Exchange (AMEX) and the NASD.<sup>14</sup> The CNS system (Continuous Net Settlement), introduced in 1974 by the NASDs National Clearing Corporation (one of the predecessors of the NSCC), allowed exchange members to clear their payments against a central counterparty (CCP). The use of CNS was associated with the register of an account at the DTC. The two institutional supports of both innovations (dematerialisation of securities and central counterparty) evolved in close relationship until their integration into a joint holding called DTCC (Depository Trust and Clearing Corporation) in 1999 (Depository Trust & Clearing Corporation, 2003).

Such arrangements permitted the institutionalisation of the central counterparty solution to clearing: the construction of a -third party that ‘‘interposes itself as buyer to each member seller and as seller to each member buyer in a defined trade’’ (Depository Trust & Clearing Corporation, 2000: p. 18). The central counterparty facilitates the multilateral netting of exposures: in a broader sense, it contributes to the economies of calculation that we analyzed through the example of Babbages -calculative space. This review shows us that clearing did not merely accompany the evolution of contemporary financial markets, but in many respects it facilitated it. By relieving market activity – at least partially – from post-trade entanglements, this organisational innovation contributed to the enlargement of market capacity.

## 6. The specificity of derivatives clearing

Clearing and settlement processes delineate the boundaries between the trading (or price-setting) stage of the transaction and the settlement stage, when finality is achieved. This stage is conceptually and practically important in securities markets. Yet, it is only in markets for derivative products that the full informational aspect of clearing and settlement is fully exposed. The fundamental diference between transactions in derivative contracts like futures (which we analyze in more detail below)

and in securities is the length of time between trading (price-setting) and finality. While in securities finality is achieved within several hours or at most a few days, in futures transactions finality may be achieved only after weeks or months, when the contracts expire or when the obligations of the transaction are negated by the purchase/sale of ofsetting contracts. This diference in time periods is not merely quantitative but highly relevant to the expected function of derivative contracts. Futures allow market participants to sell or buy the risks involved in holding assets for lengthy periods. As a result, in the futures market environment, the fact that finality is achieved a relatively long time after the initial price-setting stage takes place has a significant impact on the nature of the detachment process. As we will see in the following section, the well-defined boundaries between trading and clearing achieved in the securities world were challenged, and indeed penetrated, by the more sophisticated clearing and settlement procedures that the derivatives environment called for.

The history of derivatives exchange clearinghouses, such as BOTCC (Board of Trade Clearing Corporation) in Chicago, reveals that futures clearing and settlement procedures, as well as performing their traditional role of ensuring eficient detachment between counterparties, also used increasingly the transactional information available to them to measure performance and risk. In so doing, those procedures entered -market-wide analysis into the particular procedures they performed.

The initial reason for the development of the new informational role of the clearinghouses in the late 19th century was the existence of a structural flaw in the clearing method of the time. One of the major reasons for establishing a clearinghouse for CBOT (Chicago Board of Trade) in 1883 was to handle diference payments between the various traders. Williams (1986), quoting early descriptions, provides a compelling description of the early functioning of CBOTs new clearinghouse, (that resonates indeed with Babbages description presented above):

‘‘The new clearinghouses main contribution, which was considerable nonetheless, was to provide a central location for its members clerks [. . .] [where] at the close of business each day, every member of the clearinghouse submitted a list of his transactions [. . .] [and] the clearinghouse combined these lists into one grand roster, which it presented each morning to the clerks of the member firms who met together in a -settling room. [. . .] Aided by this roster, the clerk of a house with a bought contract not yet ofset looked for the clerk of the firm that had sold it. [. . .] The search expanded until the original buyer was found to have sold to someone on the circuit at which point the ring was complete [. . .] and the clerks would then mutually cancel the trades or -ring out the deals.’’ (Williams, 1986: p. 8)

The clearinghouses new role, combined with the ring system (see above), was successful in reducing counterparty risk. However, as Moser (2000) illustrates, potentially disastrous strings of defaults upon the failure of just one link in the ring were still possible. Such a situation occurred in 1902 when George Phillips, a CBOT trader, went bankrupt. His bankruptcy afected the accounts of 748 members, representing more than 42% of CBOT membership at the time. A 1903 change to the

CBOT rules required that losses or damages from defaulted contracts be paid though the clearinghouse (Moser, 2000). Although the determination of amounts to be paid was still left to the afected parties, a record of members defaults was kept in the clearinghouse (as it was acting as the mediator for payment). The clearinghouse used the default information to develop performance records of its clearing members.<sup>15</sup>

Such developments increased the importance of the market-wide data in the operations of the clearinghouses calculative space. One of the important developments following this trend is the growth of techniques for the calculation of margin requirements. These developments are also fundamentally important to the -reflexive turn that took place in financial markets in the last decades (Izquierdo, 2001; MacKenzie, 2001; MacKenzie & Millo, 2003). As we will see in the next section, the increased manifestation of risk assessment methods in clearinghouse procedures, in efect reintroduced the market (the trading or price-setting stage) to the clearing process of the transaction from which it had previously been eficiently separated. This process can be exemplified through the analysis of a particular risk-based clearing method, developed by the Chicago Mercantile Exchange (CME).

## 7. Bringing the market back in: SPAN

In 1988, the Chicago Mercantile Exchange (CME), a leading American futures exchange, implemented the Standard Portfolio Analysis of Risk (SPAN) system. SPAN is a risk-based system for calculating performance bond requirements or margins.<sup>16</sup> Generally, in the context of the futures world, performance bonds are -good faith deposits required by the futures exchange to insure that the obligations of both the market participants to the clearinghouse are met. These good faith deposits are updated daily and are estimates of a maximal daily loss to the traders portfolio. They represent the minimum amount of protection against potential losses at which the exchange will allow a clearing member to carry a position or a portfolio. There is a substantial diference between SPAN calculations and other types of futures margins. Unlike margin calculations that take into account only the specific market position for which a performance bond needs to be deposited, SPANs algorithm – and indeed the calculative space in which SPAN plays a pivotal role – calculates the estimated efect that market conditions would have on the overall portfolio of positions held by the trader. This calculation, in turn, is represented in the size of the performance bond deposit (margin) that the trader is required to pay.

In essence, SPAN calculations introduce a risk-based picture of the market into the clearing and settlement stage of the transaction. In so doing, the use of such a risk-based system blurs the boundary line between the price-setting elements of the transaction and the clearing elements of it. To understand how SPAN calculations re-introduce the market picture into clearing, it is necessary to describe the concepts on which the system is based in more detail.

The SPAN system is based on a pre-arranged array of market scenarios. Each of the scenarios assumes that a combination of price movements and price volatility can exist on the market and afect the contracts. Since SPAN margins are calculated daily, the starting point for the movements and the volatility levels are taken from the actual market prices of the previous day. An estimated loss/gain is calculated for each of the contracts composing the portfolio; the separate estimates are then summed up to produce the daily portfolio price changes under each of the scenarios. The worst-case scenario – the one producing the biggest daily loss – is then chosen as the daily performance bond payment.

This description highlights a feature of the SPAN structure that strengthens the argument about the blurring of the boundary between the price-setting stage of the transaction and the clearing and settlement stage. For example, the ranges of expected price movements are established periodically by the exchanges for each of the contracts.<sup>17</sup> These parameters determine, to a large extent, the level of influence that the risk-generated market picture has on the clearing process. Price ranges – one of the intermediating mediums through which information passes from the exchange to the clearinghouse – become, following the introduction of SPAN, a result of a socio-political process.

In spite of its historical contingency, SPAN has grown to become a de facto standard in the financial industry. A large majority on derivatives clearinghouses use SPAN-like models all over the financial world. This emphasises the purposeful nature of the erosion of boundaries within the clearing process. Although providing the means which enabled the finality between trading counterparties to become more eficient, the development of risk-based clearing systems resulted in the separation between the price-setting stage and the clearing and settlement stage to become less clear. In particular, risk assessment methods added informational input to the clearing process that had not existed previously. The dividing -membrane between the price-setting stage and the clearing and settlement stage, which previously allowed only prices, volumes and identities of specific trades to permeate it, now allowed a more general picture of the market to come through. As model-based risk assessment techniques were included into the clearing process, an interpretation of the whole market environment was incorporated into clearing. In other words, more elements from the price-setting stage of the transactions were represented in the clearing and settlement stage. Margins and capital requirements, such as the ones exemplified here, reflected the element of risk that volatile markets implied.

## 8. Discussion

As this paper sets out to show, financial clearing and settlement illustrates the relevance of the notion of -detachment for the better understanding of financial markets and financial risk. The modern financial markets clearinghouse is a prime example of organisational technologies that allow counterparties to clear their contracted obligations in an uncontested manner. Nevertheless, while essential, -detachment is far from a straightforward and easy operation. This is particularly visible in financial markets, since the academic and practical discipline of finance (as the very etymology of the word suggests) revolves, to a large extent, around the arrangements necessary to finalise a transaction and to allow counterparties to be -quits.

As the various historical examples reviewed in this article show, the clearinghouse method enabled the construction of a separate organisational space where a -third party could interpose itself between counterparties in the calculation and management of their reciprocal obligations. In addition, as we illustrate in the case of derivatives, the clearinghouse brought together data of diferent types (e.g., price and volumes, traders credit history, market risk measures) and incorporated them into the calculation of counterparties performance and the governing of collective risk. Finally, as shown by the case of risk-based clearing methods such as SPAN, those developments in efect eroded the boundaries that the clearinghouse had initially brought to financial markets: they introduced the market scene back again into the calculative space where post-trade management had been displaced. This growing re-entanglement of clearing and settlement activities with trading activities, partly due to their earlier separation, has now contributed to the transformation of risks in financial markets.

Are we facing a paradox? Are those very -detachment technologies that developed in order to organise and simplify financial transactions introduce new complexities to contemporary financial markets because of the additional calculative power? Is the gradual erosion of the boundaries within the practices of the market an unintended consequence of the development of clearing technology itself? We claim that this development is a result of a process of organisational and socio-technical innovation that is inherent to financial markets. The fact that sophisticated clearing blurs the boundaries between the trading aspects of market activity and its clearing and settlement stages should not be seen as an isolated risk factor that impinges only on financial markets. Instead, this should be seen as a manifestation of a larger trend that played, and still plays, a major role in the development of financial markets.

By examining the historical review presented here, we can see that the dialectical process that underlines the development of modern clearing refers to a more fundamental process of reflexive modernisation (Beck, Giddens, & Lash, 1994). The history of clearing shows that the development of financial markets marks a project aimed at transferring risks from the -social – and therefore unpredictable – trading arenas, to the bureaucratised spaces of clearing. This transfer, however, did not eliminate risks. It transformed and reintroduced them. The mechanised representation of the social activity of the markets – the information used for clearing and produced in the process – gradually became a constitutive factor in the social process of -making a market. The failure to maintain an efective boundary between trading and clearing points at the more fundamental failure of the technological society to rationalise risk (Boden, 2000; Giddens, 1990; Hutton & Giddens, 2000).

The growth of financial markets depends on the ability of the exchanges to create and then to maintain a flux of products to be traded. An inherent aspect of this process is the creation of organisational and technological mechanisms aimed at defining and managing risk. As this paper shows, the risk embedded in socio-technical systems is not a linear factor but a relational variable that is contingent upon several realms of activity. Unlike the common perception according to which markets, especially markets for financial derivatives, were created to allocate risk more eficiently and in a rational manner, the historical narrative of clearing reveals that financial risk was not -discovered and then managed by the markets, but to a large extent was manufactured in them. The example discussed in this paper about the clearing of the futures contracts shows that a sophisticated clearing mechanism, SPAN, which translated the overall market picture into the level of margins, introduced a new risk to the market, namely the possibility that demands for margins would render many of the traders accounts unprofitable. In times of extreme market volatility, as the events of 1987 in the US have showed, margin calls – the demands for collateral – carried as much systemic risk as price volatility itself.<sup>18</sup> The development of sophisticated clearing techniques and the blurring of clearing boundaries is only one manifestation of a process in which modern financial markets manufacture risks.

These questions obviously suggest opportunities for further investigation through empirical research. In this paper, we have aimed to provide a conceptual framework and a supporting socio-historical narrative we hope will highlight the complexity of the entanglements and disentanglements of financial life and the potential richness of the field as a topic for research. The initial analysis presented here also highlights a growing intensity in the relationship between various market participants, in particular the relationship between regulators and commercial market participants. While, from a regulatory perspective several aspects of mathematical risk assessment would be regarded as detrimental, for commercial actors the transition from organisation to disorganisation can open new opportunities and may revive some financial markets. The paper also makes a key methodological claim about the need to focus on the materiality of the organisational devices that support economic activities. Clearinghouses are spatially located settings where material displacements are performed. The fact that such settings and displacements are not always at a human scale (they evolve from buildings to servers, from paper to electronic flow) does not afect its material character. In this light, the notion of -detachment is not to be understood as an abstract metaphor but as a concrete movement. Perhaps this approach contrasts with a common perception of financial markets as -dematerialised and -virtual realms. For a position such as the one defended here, however, a sociological understanding of order and disorder cannot be reached without taking into account the concrete technologies of ordering and disordering.
