# Exploring complexity and contradiction in information technology outsourcing: A set-theoretical approach

## ARTICLE INFO

Keywords: IT outsourcing Configuration Set-theoretic Innovation paradox Portfolio-based governance

## A B S T R A C T

Efectively managing Information Technology outsourcing (ITO) requires integration of conceptually distinct and sometimes contradicting factors. For instance, ITO “best practices” for cost reduction can inhibit a firm’s innovativeness. To address these challenges, we build on governance and capability perspectives and employ a set-theoretic approach to examine ITO practices in 27 firms. Our exploratory study reveals four distinct ITO configurations, of which three lead to positive outcomes and one leads to negated-outcomes. Detailed contracts appear to be a necessary condition for cost reduction but can also be an impediment to strategic innovation; and firms can consider a “best-of-breed” strategy to overcome this tension. Our findings call for more attention to both transaction-based and portfolio-based governance to enable flexibility for innovation while retaining rigor and risk mitigation for cost reduction.

## Introduction

It has been more than two decades since Kodak shocked the world and became the first large enterprise to outsource all of its IT operations (Loh and Venkatraman. 1992b). Today, information technology outsourcing (ITO) has become an important strategy for organizations to reduce IT costs (Han and Mithas, 2013), focus on core competences (Quinn, 1999), and improve firm performance (Salimath et al., 2008; Thouin et al., 2009). Extant research has examined a wide range of ITO issues, such as “best practices” for ITO contracts (Beulen et al., 2011; Lacity and Willcocks, 2001) and risk mitigation (Currie, 1998; Willcocks et al., 1999), ITO success factors (Hodosi and Rusu, 2013; Qi and Chau, 2012), efects of ITO on firm performance (Gwebu et al., 2010; Thouin et al., 2009), and value creation by ITO vendors (Levina and Ross, 2003). A review by Lacity et al. (2010) identifies over 160 empirical ITO studies that span multiple disciplines. However, while the voluminous prior research has significantly advanced our understanding of ITO arrangements (e.g., Lacity et al., 2009, 2010), recent studies have suggested several issues that require further investigation.

First, ITO arrangements are inherently complex and require integration across multiple perspectives to fully understand (Lacity et al., 2009, 2011). The proliferation of theoretical perspectives in the extant literature (Cheon et al., 1995; Dibbern et al., 2004; Lacity et al., 2010) reflects this complexity. Because ITO takes place across organizational boundaries and can entail change in organizational structure and routines, managers need to consider the interplay among conceptually distinct factors to efectively manage the complex arrangements. Accordingly, researchers have called for more studies to explicitly examine how multiple causa forces operating in conjunction influence ITO decisions and their outcomes (Cheon et al., 1995; Lacity et al., 2009, 2010, 2011; Linden and Hovestadt, 2017). Yet, this call has not been sufficiently addressed as many researchers still utilize only one theoretical perspective to study ITO (Cheon et al., 1995; Lacity et al., 2011). Even among the limited number of ITO studies that adopt a more holistic approach to integrate multiple theories (e.g., the configurational perspective (Lee et al., 2004)), discrepancy between the integrative theory and the adopted empirical methods limits these studies to partial examination of the complex interactions among causal processes (Fiss, 2007). Subsequently, researchers have called for a better approach to more efectively integrate multiple theories in ITO empirical research (Fink, 2010).

Second, there are potential contradictions among factors in complex ITO arrangements (Aubert et al., 2015). Recently, firms are increasingly collaborating with their ITO suppliers to initiate strategic innovations—introducing new products and services for existing customers or entering new markets (Kotlarsky et al., 2015; Linden and Hovestadt, 2017; Oshri et al., 2015; Whitley and Willcocks, 2011). Under the right conditions, these vendors can help firms create business value through innovative initiatives that are not possible without the outsourcing relationships (DiRomualdo and Gurbaxani, 1998; Jean et al., 2014; Kotlarsky et al., 2015; Linden and Schmidt, 2016). For instance, KPN, a telecommunication company in the Netherlands, heavily relied on their suppliers to create innovative designs or products; or StatoilHydro, a Norwegian oil and gas company, used outsourced IT shared services to create innovative solutions in oil and gas exploration (Whitley and Willcocks, 2011). While this emerging practice gives firms a new way to innovate, it also reveals an antagonistic dynamic in ITO arrangements, known as the “innovation through outsourcing” paradox (Aubert et al., 2015).

On the one hand, successful ITO arrangements require carefully crafted contracts to ensure efective management of ITO projects and to safeguard against opportunism. Drawing from economics theories such as transaction cost economics, contract theory, and agency theory, prior studies have identified a plethora of contractual elements for “good” ITO arrangements: consideration for project duration and size (Gopal et al., 2003); adjustment of contract flexibility for organizational stability (Harris et al., 1998); use of partnership contracts with fixed price, variable time or materials (Oshri et al., 2015); combination with relational elements (Qi and Chau, 2012); or substitution between contractual and relational elements (Rai et al., 2007). Overall, the extant literature converges on the notion that carefully designed ITO contracts will likely lead to efficiency gains. On the other hand. Aubert et al. (2015) posit that many ITO contractual elements designed for eficiency gains can reduce the flexibility, openness, and shared resources needed to encourage innovativeness in an outsourcing relationship, thus constraining ITO suppliers from taking the necessary initiatives to pursue innovations. This paradox illustrates how individual ITO success factors can have antagonistic dynamics that negatively impact the outsourcing firms, thus calling for more research to understand how diferent elements of ITO arrangements interact, and what the efects of their dynamics are on various organizational outcomes.

In short, the need for deeper theory integration across multiple perspectives as well as the potential contradiction in ITO arrangements suggest a research opportunity to expand our current understanding regarding the boundaries of ITO causal processes as well as the efects of their interactions. In this study, we follow suggestions from recent studies (Aubert et al., 2015; Fink, 2010; Fiss, 2007; Oshri et al., 2015) and employ a configurational perspective using a set-theoretic approach to explore the complex causa relationships between ITO arrangements and various organizational outcomes. Our research question is: What does a set-theoretic approach inform us about ITO configurations and organizational outcomes? Drawing on two broad theoretical perspectives—the governance and capability perspectives—we identify five distinct factors that constitute ITO arrangements. We apply these factors to conduct fuzzy-set analyses on data obtained from surveying 27 companies on three sets of organizational outcomes: cost reduction, capability enhancement, and strategic innovation. Our findings show that having detailed contracts is a necessary condition for achieving cost reduction in ITO; and firms with a “best-of-breed” strategy—outsourcing to specific suppliers for particular needs—have a higher chance to achieve multiple desired outcomes. Furthermore, our findings also reveal one configuration that has a negative effect on strategic innovation. In this configuration, the combination of detailed and extendable contracts with flexible pricing can hinder strategic innovation.

Our findings ofer three contributions to ITO research. First, our study illustrates how a set-theoretic approach can help explore the trade-ofs between diferent causal factors as well as the underlying conditions under which these factors can work together. In light of recent theoretical discussions of potential contradiction in ITO arrangements (Aubert et al., 2015), our study is particularly useful as it reveals the existence of such tensions and the underlying factors. Second, our study illustrates the advantage of combining insights from multiple theoretical perspectives as it allows us to better identify ITO “best practices.” We directly answer the recent call for combining multiple theories (Argyres, 2011; Argyres and Zenger, 2012; Lacity et al., 2011) by integrating governance and capability perspectives to examine their interactions in ITO arrangements. Here, the set-theoretic approach is advantageous because it does not combine theories in an additive manner as do variance-based methods (Argyres, 1996; Poppo and Zenger, 1998). Lastly, our findings provide insight to the discourse on how to gain strategic innovation through outsourcing (Aubert et al., 2015). Specifically, we posit that to overcome this paradox, organizations need to move beyond transaction-based governance and incorporate portfolio-based governance to enable flexibility while retaining rigor in ITO arrangements

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. We first review extant research that attempts to study ITO holistically, i.e., the configurational perspective, and discuss how a set-theoretic approach is particularly suitable. We then present our research model as well as our data collection and analysis. Thereafter, we present our findings and discuss their implications.

Configurational perspective and set-theoretic approach

Thus, it difers fundamentally from the variance-based approaches regarding how causal relationships are conceptualized. Whereas the variance-based approaches reflect the view of a “general linear reality” (Abbott, 1988), marked by the implicit assumptions of independent, additive, and symmetrical causality (Delbridge and Fiss, 2013), the configurational perspective maintains that causality is complex in terms of (1) conjunction, that outcomes are often the results of the combination of multiple conditions; (2) equifinality, that there might be multiple causal paths to the same outcome; and (3) asymmetry, that the causal conditions leading to the absence of an outcome are not merely the mirror image of the conditions leading to the presence of the outcome (Fiss, 2007, 2011; Misangyi et al., 2017). Arguably, this conceptualization of complex causality is more consistent with how managers make ITO decisions holistically (Campbell et al., 2016; Tiwana and Bush, 2007).

Early configurational studies conceptually emphasized the theoretical implications of complex causality; however, their continued reliance on econometric methods presented a mismatch between theory and method that leaves the actual causal processes relatively unexamined (Fiss. 2007: Misangvi et al., 2017). This mismatch can be seen among the few ITO configurational studies to date where variance-based methods were used to examine the ITO configuration-performance link (Table 1). Consequently, while undoubtedly useful, these studies allow only limited peeks into the black box of configuration because these methods cannot tease apart the causal relations among the factors that form the identified configurations and specific outcomes (Fiss, 2007). In other words, they allow us to answer the question “do configurations matter?” but not the question “how do configurations matter?”

In recent years, the emergence of set-theoretic methods<sup>1</sup> (e.g., qualitative comparative analysis or QCA) has enabled a finegrained empirical investigation of complex causality (Misangyi et al., 2017). Empirical research in business disciplines, as well as adjacent fields such as sociology and political science, has successfully employed set-theoretic methods to study configurations (Fiss, 2007, 2011; Misangyi et al., 2017). Within the ITO literature, researchers have started to call for the use of set-theoretic approaches to fully explore ITO configurations (Fink, 2010). Following these recent developments, we employ a set-theoretic approach to explore ITO contradiction and complexity. Next, we present our research model.

## Research model

Our research model, presented in Fig. 1, comprises five core attributes and three organizational outcomes. To identify core attributes for ITO configurations. we review the extant literature from two broad theoretical perspectives (Cheon et al.. 1995: Lacity et al., 2010, 2011): capability perspectives (e.g., resource-based view, knowledge-based view) and governance perspectives (e.g., transaction cost economics, agency theory). Because we aim to integrate theories and examine ITO outcomes, an attribute must satisfy two criteria to be included: (1) the attribute is grounded in established theories, and (2) it is supported by empirical evidence to relate to ITO outcomes in focus. Our literature review produced 10 attributes, but we included only five factors in our fina research model for two reasons<sup>2</sup>. First, it helps improve the theoretical parsimony of our model as the five excluded attributes share common theoretical leanings with the five included factors. Specifically, we excluded the attributes Geographical Scope and Service Recipient because they were similar to the Service Level Strategy attribute Cullen et al. (2005) described these three attributes as part of a “Scope” category for ITO). Similarly, the excluded attribute Communication is captured by Contract Commitment, and the excluded attributes Resource Ownership and Equity Ownership are captured by Commercial Relationship. Second, these five attributes were excluded for practical reasons as they showed a lack of variance in our survey, suggesting that in contemporary ITO practice, firms are indiferent to how they organize these attributes.<sup>3</sup>

## ITO outcomes

Most ITO studies focus on two organizational outcomes: cost reduction (Gwebu et al., 2010; Han and Mithas, 2013) and capability enhancement (DiRomualdo and Gurbaxani, 1998; Levina and Ross, 2003). By outsourcing IT functionalities to a third party, organizations can take advantage of the economies-of-scale ofered by suppliers, utilize additional capabilities available in their services, and access external resources to focus on core business activities (Gwebu et al., 2010; Lacity et al., 2010; Levina and Ross, 2003; Levina and Su, 2008). In addition, recent studies also point out the possibility of using ITO services to create strategic innovation (Kotlarsky et al., 2015; Oshri et al., 2015). Strategic innovation (through outsourcing) is defined as using outsourcing capability in ways that significantly enhance a firm’s existing product or service or enable the firm to enter new markets (Oshri et al., 2015). A strong outsourcing relationship can enhance existing relational and contractual governance and allow firms to exploit new and innovative products, services, and markets (Whitley and Willcocks, 2011). Following these recent developments, our study examines three organizational outcomes of ITO: cost reduction, capability enhancement, and strategic innovation (through outsourcing).

## Capability factors

Capability perspectives maintain that a durable competitive advantage emerges from the unique combination of resources and capabilities (Aral and Weill, 2007; Bharadwaj, 2000; Grant, 1996b). One stream of research conceptualizes organizational capability as a mutually reinforcing system of practices and competencies (Aral and Weill. 2007), thus explaining superior performance in terms of complementarity among groups of organizational activities (Milgrom and Roberts, 1990; Richardson, 1972). A key implication is that the structure of complementarity afects the pattern of organization as well as performance (Langlois and Foss, 1999). It follows that the set of activities that a firm can coordinate better than others is an important source of competitive advantage (Dosi and Teece, 1998). Another stream of research views organizational capability as integration of individuals’ specialized knowledge (Grant, 1996a, 1996b). Accordingly, competitive advantage stems from combinative capabilities, a firm’s ability to generate new knowledge from existing capabilities (Kogut and Zander, 1992), or a firm’s level of eficiency in knowledge transfer and integration (Grant, 1996a). All together, capability theories suggest the following attributes for ITO configuration

Service level strategy describes what IT capabilities (e.g., application developments, operations activities, or management and support functions) and the degree to which these capabilities are accessed through outsourcing (Cullen et al., 2005). Considered to be one of the four fundamental parameters of an ITO arrangement besides outsourcing mode (discussed next), ownership, and time frame (Dibbern et al., 2004), service level strategy reflects managers’ perception of their organizations’ gaps of resources and capabilities or relative capabilities vis-à-vis suppliers (Argyres, 1996; Demsetz, 1988; Grant, 1991; Langlois, 1992; Teng et al., 1995), as well as the complementary assets that should be brought under common ownership (Milgrom and Roberts, 1992, p. 312; Teece, 2007).

Prior studies have suggested several outsourcing models based on service level strategy (e.g., total outsourcing, selective outsourcing, and insourcing), each with its own implications for performance. For instance, organizations with a total outsourcing mode are often outperformed by organizations with a partial outsourcing or insourcing model (Lacity and Willcocks, 1998, 2001); and Loh and Venkatraman (1992a) suggested that the degree of IT outsourcing is in fact negatively related to IT performance but not business performance. These studies suggest there is no simple causal relationship between degree of outsourcing and performance; and thus we include it in our model for further examination.

Supplier strategy, referred to as outsourcing mode by Dibbern et al. (2004), depicts how outsourced capabilities are provisioned and coordinated among various suppliers, as well as the number of suppliers involved. Proper supplier strategy can lead to enhanced IT capabilities (Cullen et al., 2005). better supplier fit (Levina and Su. 2008), or lower prices and increased innovativeness (Poston et al., 2009). Cullen et al. (2005) identified four supplier strategies: sole supplier, having one supplier provide all ITO services without subcontracting; prime contractor (also known as mediated or guardian vendor), having one head contractor as the single point of accountability to interface with any number of subcontractors; best-of-breed, having any number of suppliers with the client firm acting as the “head contractor." in effect; and panel, having a list of preferred suppliers in continuous competition. Other researchers use a broader category of single- vs. multi-sourcing (i.e., stitching together IT services from multiple vendors) to examine the delicate balance between supplier competition and cooperation (Aubert et al., 2016: Bapna et al., 2010: Cohen et al., 2006: Levina and Su 2008; Schlagwein et al., 2014; Wiener and Saunders, 2014).

These distinctions reflect some critical trade-ofs that an organization’s supplier strategy would eventually determine. On the one hand, single-sourcing, outsourcing to only one supplier, promotes the development of shared language, knowledge, and routines that enhance the eficiency of coordination (Grant, 1996b; Kogut and Zander, 1992); but it also increases the risks of vendor dependence and opportunistic rent appropriation (Bapna et al., 2010; Wiener and Saunders, 2014). On the other hand, multi-sourcing increases the number of suppliers and can increase the scope of specialized knowledge to be integrated, foster vendor competition, and enhance flexibility to adapt to changing conditions (Bapna et al., 2010; Grant, 1996a; Wiener and Saunders, 2014); but it also increases coordination costs (Cha et al., 2009; Dibbern et al., 2008). Thus, managing these trade-ofs requires appropriate governance me chanisms to mitigate the risks involved in accessing and integrating external resources and capabilities.

## Governance factors

Managing coordination usually requires motivation and control mechanisms, since participants in coordinated eforts do not bear the full consequences of their actions and therefore might behave opportunistically (Hoopes and Postrel, 1999; Milgrom and Roberts, 1992). Governance theories examine various risk factors of such opportunistic behaviors and prescribe governance structures to provide incentives or safeguards that mitigate such risks. Specifically, transaction costs economics (TCE) maintains that relationshipspecific investments increase the risk of opportunistic behavior that requires costly contractual safeguards to deter (Williamson, 1975, 1985). Alternatively, a firm can agree to make relationship-specific investments only if the transactional partner reciprocates, i.e., requiring mutual sunk-cost commitment or “financial hostage” as the safeguard (Kang et al., 2009; Kim and Mahoney, 2005; Williamson, 1983); or a firm can rely on informal or relational governance factors such as trust and reputation as motivation and control mechanisms (Gulati, 1995).

Besides risks of opportunism, governance theories also emphasize risks from the misalignment of economic incentives between the principal (i.e., outsourcing client) and the agent (i.e., supplier). To address this issue, agency theory prescribes contractual designs that help align economic incentives; however, if vendor contributions are dificult to measure, as is the case with outsourcing for strategic innovation, adequate contracts will be costly to craft (Barzel, 1982, 1997; Kim and Mahoney, 2005; Poppo and Zenger, 1998). Taken together, these theories suggest the following governance attributes for ITO configuration.

Commercial Relationship describes how economic exchanges between an organization and its suppliers are governed. One common governance structure is the formal legal contract that stipulates the obligations of the parties involved and allows access to the courts for enforcement (Dyer, 1997). Taking advantage of resource complementarity requires relationship-specific investments, which increase the risk of opportunistic behavior. Subsequently, the increased risk of opportunism leads to a higher degree of contract extensiveness as the need for safeguards increases (Anderson and Dekker, 2005; Benaroch et al., 2010; Chen and Bharadwaj, 2009; Poppo and Zenger, 2002). However, extensive contracts are costly to write and can increase ex-post monitoring and enforcement costs (Anderson and Dekker, 2005; Barthélemy and Quélin, 2006). Thus, a company needs to choose an appropriate contract type to account for these trade-ofs.

Diferent ITO contract types have been found to significantly impact ITO performance (Fitoussi and Gurbaxani, 2012; Oshri et al., 2015; Qi and Chau, 2012). We include the following four contract types from Lacity and Willcocks (1998): Generic contract, the ofthe-self, standard contract provided by either the supplier or the client without customization; Detailed contract, which includes specific and detailed clauses for ITO services such as service scope, service levels, performance measures, and penalties; Loose contract, which, in contrast, does not contain detailed clauses but does allow some customization as compared to a generic contract; and Mixed contract, which is a combination of detailed and loose contracts in which some detailed clauses are provided. We also include strategic partnership in which both the organization and its ITO suppliers pool resources to create, add to, or maximize joint value (Lacity and Willcocks, 1998). The partners share benefits and risks in this collaborative inter-organizational relationship, and their mutual resource commitment constitutes a self-enforcing agreement (Telser, 1980) which aligns the incentives of the parties involved without resorting to other formal governance mechanisms (Kang et al., 2009; Kim and Mahoney, 2006).

Pricing Strategy describes how payment to the supplier is determined. It characterizes the client-supplier relationship and directly impacts ITO performance (DiRomualdo and Gurbaxani, 1998; Domberger et al., 2000; Gopal and Sivaramakrishnan, 2008). Extant literature points to two prevalent pricing strategies: Fixed-Price (FP) contracts that specify a lump-sum payment for prespecified services; and Variable Price or Time and Material (T&M) contracts in which the client is charged based on the time and eforts incurred by the suppliers (Benaroch et al., 2010; Dey et al., 2010; Gopal and Koka, 2012; Gopal and Sivaramakrishnan, 2008; Gopa et al., 2003; Kalnins and Mayer, 2004; Mani et al., 2013). FP contracts are costly to write because they require ex-ante detailed specification of project requirements. They also entail higher ex-post adaptation costs if changes in requirements are needed. On the contrary, T&M contracts do not require ex-ante detailed specification of requirements and inherently provide for adaptation (Benaroch et al., 2010). Thus, studies have found that T&M contracts are preferred when a project is large or complex (Mani and Barua, 2015), or when perceived requirements uncertainty is high (Bajari and Tadelis, 2001; Gopal et al., 2003).

In addition, these pricing strategies also result in diferent risk partitioning between the client and the supplier. Under FP contracts, the supplier bears the risk of cost escalation, thus has incentives to underprovide quality (Benaroch et al., 2016; Kalnins and Mayer, 2004), e.g., by allocating less experienced personnel or using less expensive and lower quality resources. Thus, FP contracts can motivate the supplier to operate more efficiently to lower costs. Under T&M contracts, the client bears the risk of cost escalation. while the supplier has an incentive to increase billable hours. Accordingly, studies show that T&M contracts are preferred when the cost of measuring quality of vendor service is high (Kalnins and Maver, 2004) or when the client perceives greater project importance (Gopal et al., 2003).

## Method

To explore the complexity and tensions in ITO arrangements using a set-theoretic approach, we develop a questionnaire, which is the recommended approach for set-theoretical analyses across a medium to large sample size (Fink, 2010; Liu et al., 2015). This approach is also similar to prior set-theoretical studies (Dawson et al., 2016: Lee et al., 2004: Vergne and Depeyre, 2016): and we follow survey development principles to establish credibility and reliability for our instrument (Czaja and Blair, 2005; DeVellis, 2003). Furthermore, given our purpose to study patterns and diversity among ITO configurations (Ragin, 2000), our questionnaire does not use variance-based questions that aim to identify latent variables but rather uses questions to identify diversity in ITo configurations.

## Questionnaire development and data collection

Our questionnaire was developed in two stages (Czaja and Blair, 2005). In the first design stage, we developed questions for ITO attributes based on prior studies (Cullen et al., 2005; Grover et al., 1996; Oshri et al., 2015). All questions for attributes of ITO configurations were developed to capture various degrees instead of identifying latent variables. For example, to capture the ITO service level strategy, the survey question asked respondents to rank their outsourcing level for diferent categories such as IT application, operations, management, and support. For dependent variables, we used instruments for latent variables based on prior studies. We chose this approach for two reasons. First, these were well-established measurements for ITO outcomes in the literature; and second, the approach of using pre-developed measures for latent variables has also been used by prior configurational studies (Lee et al., 2004; Liu et al., 2015; Vergne and Depeyre, 2016). All questions were adjusted to better fit our purposes.

In the second pretest stage, we discussed the questionnaire with seven academics with expertise in outsourcing research. Their feedback was used to revise the questions. We then conducted a pilot study with four ITO professionals. The results were used to finetune the instrument. These pretest steps helped validate the instrument and establish its reliability (Czaja and Blair, 2005). In general, the results showed that the developed measures had a high face validity and consistency, and required only minor modifications to improve the general readability of the final version of the survey. Because our study is exploratory in nature, we aim to maximize diversity in the responses by distributing the questionnaire in waves with assistance from two organizations. The first wave targeted mostly large organizations, while the second wave targeted mostly small and medium organizations. In the first wave, between November 2016 and February 2017, the questionnaire was distributed to the members of the International Association of Outsourcing Professionals (IAOP). The IAOP population consists of large multi-billion-dollar companies with more than 10,000 employees. We distributed the questionnaire in two IAOP chapter meetings in order to increase response rate. In total, we received 31 responses from companies that have outsourced their IT ac tivities. Because we distributed the questionnaire through several anonymous channels such as social media and email, and because we do not know the exact number of outsourcing clients among IAOP members, we are unable to report an exact response rate. In the second wave, we targeted small and medium organizations. In April 2017, our questionnaire was sent to alumni of a northeastern US university. We received an additional 34 responses from this second wave, mostly from companies with fewer than 10,000 employees. Because the university did not want to disclose the exact number of recipients who received our invitation, we also could not calculate a response rate. In total, we received 65 responses from our data collection. After reviewing the responses and filtering out unusable or incomplete data, we retained 27 responses for further analysis. Because we examine three diferent outcome variables, our efective sample size is 81 observations (27x3). Discussion with our contacts in the two organizations suggested that our response rates were below 5%.

To test for non-response bias, we conducted t-tests to compare responses to the outcome questions between early and late respondents. The results were insignificant, suggesting that non-response bias is not a significant problem in our data. We also conducted independent sample t-tests on the response to outcomes questions between the two samples. The result was also insig nificant, indicating that we can combine the two samples (e.g., for Strategic Innovation outcome, t(25) = 0.376, p = 0.71). Table 4 reports the description of our sample. while Table 5 provides the descriptive data and correlations for dependent variables. which were developed using survey instruments that aim to capture latent variables. The scale for Cost Reduction outcome consisted of three items (α = 0.73). the scale for Capability Enhancement outcome consisted of five items (α = 0.73). and the scale for Strategic Innovation outcome consisted of four items (α = 0.82). The Cronbach’s alphas indicated that the scales were highly reliable (above 0.7) (Hair et al., 2010).

## Data analysis

We used the software fsQCA 3.0 (Ragin and Davey, 2014) to perform the analysis, and followed the stages specified by Liu et al. (2015). In the first stage, we transformed the collected data into Boolean sets (Liu et al., 2015). For each of the ITO attributes, we used cluster analysis to (1) descriptively make sense of the data and (2) transform multiple-scale questions into a single dimension that is suitable for set-theoretic analysis. All cluster analyses used the Ward’s method with squared Euclidean distance (Hair et al., 2010). The statistical description and interpretation of possible clusters are based on ANOVA analyses of the clusters’ dimensions. The interpretations were discussed with a senior MIS researcher who has ITO working experience. Through several rounds of discussion, the interpretations were fine-tuned to increase their validity. In the second stage, we used the five transformed variables (or sets) from the cluster analyses to conduct a set-theoretic analysis. Our analysis steps are described below:

Step 1: Calibrate the data. All ITO attributes were transformed into crisp measures with two types of membership while ITO outcomes were transformed into fuzzy measures. The details of data calibration using cluster analyses are presented in Appendix B.

Service level strategy: membership of 1 equals to majority outsourcing strategy while membership of 0 equals to conservative outsourcing strategy. Organizations with a majority outsourcing strategy outsource IT functions in all four categories—IT application, operations, management, and support—while organizations with a selective outsourcing strategy mostly outsource their IT functions in IT applications and operations.

Supplier strategy: membership of 1 equals to diversified supplier strategy while membership of 0 equals to flexible supplier strategy. Organizations with a diversified supplier strategy mostly use multiple suppliers for their ITO services while organizations with a flexible supplier strategy have a wide range of suppliers from one supplier to a pool of on-call suppliers.

Pricing strategy: membership of 1 equals to a fixed pricing strategy while membership of 0 equals to a flexible pricing strategy. The former sets a fixed amount for their ITO contracts while the latter uses various pricing strategies including fixed amount, per transaction, or mark-up on actual costs.

Contract commitment: membership of 1 equals to extendable contracts while membership of 0 equals to fixed-terms contracts. Organizations with extendable contracts allow their suppliers to extend ITO contracts while organizations with fixed-term contracts set time limits to their ITO contracts.

Commercial relationship: membership of 1 equals to detailed contracts while membership of 0 equals to mixed-type contracts. The former mostly use detailed clauses in their ITO contracts while the latter use various contract types with various degrees of detail.

ITO outcome—cost reduction, capability enhancement, and strategic innovation: a composite variable was first created to combine the corresponding scale items. We then converted each composite variable into a fuzzy measure using a 1–4–7 threshold that indicates the level of outcome a firm can gain through ITO. For example, firms with a Likert-scale value of 7 (Strongly agree) have the full membership of the cost-reduction set while firms with a Likert-scale value of 1 (Strongly disagree) have the full non-membership of the set.

Step 2: Construct a truth table. We constructed a truth table for each of the three organizational outcomes. To identify the most important and possible configurations, we used the coverage cut-of of 1 case and consistency cut-of of 0.8 as suggested by the literature (Liu et al., 2015; Ragin, 2008).

Step 3: Obtain the solution sets. In fs/QCA analysis, three solution sets are possible based on how strict the simplifying assumption is: complex, parsimonious, and intermediate (Ragin, 2008). Typically, the intermediate solution is preferred as it ofers more interpretable configurations.

Step 4. Interpret and evaluate the solutions. Using solution sets, we identified core and peripheral conditions for further interpretations of the solutions (Fiss, 2011; Ragin, 2008). Specifically, core conditions are those that appear in both parsimonious and intermediate solutions while the peripheral conditions only appear in the intermediate solutions. Thus, core conditions have a stronger causal relationship with outcomes than peripheral conditions (Fiss, 2011). The coverage and consistency measures of the final solution were also reported.

To test the robustness of our findings, we have conducted the set-theoretical analyses using diferent calibration results (e.g., 3- clusters instead of 2-clusters solution), and the results remain the same. To enrich our interpretations, following prior set-theoretical studies with a small sample size (Iannacci and Cornford, 2018; Vergne and Depeyre, 2016), we treated each data point as a case and performed post-analyses using a mixture of approaches: searching recent ITO news if we know the identity of the company, or reflecting on additional data that we collected from the survey. These post-analyses allowed us to construct rich case descriptions of exemplary companies to further support our interpretation and showcase the identified configurations.

## Findings

In this section, we report ITO configurations that lead to three desirable ITO outcomes: cost reduction, capability enhancement, and strategic innovation (Table 6). In addition, because the set-theoretic approach allows us to explore asymmetric causality (Fiss, 2007, 2011; Ragin, 2000, 2008), we also report ITO configurations that lead to negated-outcomes (Table 7). In Tables 6 and 7, we follow the conventional notation (Fiss, 2011; Liu et al., 2015; Ragin, 2008) and use black circles (“ ”) to indicate the presence of a condition, open circles $( " \textcircled { \times } " )$ to indicate the absence of a condition, and blank spaces to indicate “don’t care” or irrelevant conditions. Large circles denote core conditions while small circles denote peripheral conditions. We rely on these core conditions to theoretically make sense of the configurations. Furthermore. we use the core conditions to further classify solutions into first-order and second-order solutions (Fiss, 2011; Liu et al., 2015). Second-order solutions share similar sets of core conditions while first-order solutions have diferent sets of core conditions.

## ITO configurations to positive outcomes

## Cost reduction outcomes

Solution 1a, 1b, and 2 in Table 6 are ITO configurations that can lead to cost reduction outcomes. All together, they have a consistency value of 0.87, above the threshold of 0.75 suggested by Ragin (2008). Among them, solution 1a and 1b share the same core conditions, thus they are second-order solutions—within-type equifinality for one first-order solution 1. This group of firms outsource the majority of their IT services, and use extendable and detailed contracts as key factors to reduce IT costs. This strategy allows firms to satisfy their various IT needs while minimizing the heightened risks in majority outsourcing by having well-specified contracts with repeat business. The extendable contracts allow firms to award high-performing ITO providers, thus motivating their continuous improvements and eforts to reduce costs over time. We find this pattern is similar to cases in which firms lengthen their contracts to avoid repeated bargaining, often when there are significant investments in the outsourcing relationship due to specialized asset (Joskow, 1987). Inspired by those cases, we name this pattern asset-specific outsourcing in which firms have significant exante capital investments (e.g., specialized asset). Subsequently, they rely on detailed contracts with long-term extendable clauses to minimize transaction costs in repeated bargaining of new contracts. In fact, our case vignette of one such company reflects this pattern clearly (see Appendix C). As a major banking and finance company, Alpha needs specialized platforms for their fund and asset management operations, and it has to put significant investments in their ITO contracts. To leverage these specialized assets, Alpha wishes to remain in a strong and long-term relationship with its supplier. In recent news, Alpha has even acquired one of its ITO suppliers to gain complete control of a specialized banking platform.

In contrast, in the second configuration (first-order solution 2), firms only outsource a limited number of their IT services, but do so through a diversified number of ITO suppliers with flexible pricing and detailed contracts. While firms using this strategy do not rely on extendable contracts to minimize agency risks as in the first pattern, firms still can leverage the strength of several competing ITO suppliers using short-term contracts to minimize the risk from asymmetric information. In other words, in case of opportunistic behaviors from ITO suppliers, firms have the flexibility to terminate the contracts (Harris et al., 1998). Firms also do not use fixed pricing models but other models (e.g., performance-based pricing model) as the basis for evaluation of the fixed-term contracts. Coupled with detailed contracts, these firms will be able to choose the best possible ITO suppliers that meet their specific needs, allowing them to reduce costs while having good IT services. Thus, we name this pattern a best-of-breed strategy to describe the tendency of choosing the best possible ITO suppliers that match their limited needs. In our case vignette, Beta operates in a technology-communication industry; and it only needs to outsource its IT application development and support. Because there are no specialized requirements, Beta simply outsources to ITO suppliers that provide it the best deals, and uses detailed contracts with a fixed short term to control when and what to outsource. This strategy allows the company to focus on its core operations and reduce significant IT costs.

Overall, the configurations suggest that firms can use extendable and detailed contracts or use diversified suppliers and detailed contracts to reduce cost. Notably, across the ITO configurations that we observed. all three ITO configurations include detailed contracts. This suggests that having detailed contracts is a necessary condition for cost reduction in ITO configurations. Firms tend to have well-defined and specified contracts as a way to minimize agency risks and reduce the costs of outsourcing their IT services. This result is consistent with previous studies which suggest that having detailed contracts is a strong mechanism to a successful ITo experience (Lacity et al., 2009; Lacity and Willcocks, 1998, 2001) and in accordance with the agency theory which suggests that formal contracts are key to reduce opportunism in agent behaviors (Dawson et al., 2010; Jensen and Meckling, 1976).

## Capability enhancement outcomes

Solution 3 and 4 in Table 6 are two first-order solutions that lead to capability enhancement outcomes. Together, they have an accumulated consistency of 0.84, which is an acceptable value (Ragin, 2008). The two configurations are: 1) firms with majority outsourcing using extendable and flexible pricing, and standard contracts (solution 3), and 2) firms with a limited number of outsourced IT services through a diversified number of ITO suppliers with flexible pricing and detailed contracts (solution 4).

The first configuration (solution 3 in Table 6) fits what we call a majority outsourcing with flexible partnership strategy. Firms in this configuration outsource the majority of their IT services, but the number of suppliers is irrelevant (indicated by a blank space). Instead, they rely on flexible pricing models and extendable, standard contracts. This increases the robustness of ITO relationships, allowing firms to flexibly work with various ITO suppliers (Tan and Sia, 2006) and gain access to extra capabilities. Our exemplary firm, Gamma, exemplifies this approach (see Appendix C). Gamma has more than 100 ITO suppliers, and to mitigate risks and reduce costs from managing a large number of ITO suppliers, it remains flexible in its dealing with ITO suppliers. Gamma increases the ease of exiting ITO contracts through adjustment mechanisms such as incentive contracting (e.g., Gamma uses outcome-based pricing model) or flexible contract duration (e.g., using extendable contracts). Notably, Gamma actually uses longer-term standard contracts (three to seven years). This is probably to reduce the overhead in renegotiation with a large number of suppliers (Joskow, 1987)

The second configuration (solution 4 in Table 6) is identical to solution 2, describing two companies that use a best-of-breed strategy to gain both IT cost savings and additional IT capabilities. In this case, by limiting the scope of outsourcing and choosing the “best” providers from a large pool of suppliers, firms can gain access to IT capabilities that they desire. This pattern is aligned with prior studies that suggest ITO can simultaneously lead to multiple desirable outcomes when done right (Krishnamurthy et al., 2009; Levina and Ross, 2003; Poston et al., 2009).

A closer look across the two configurations suggests two distinct approaches to gain additional capabilities through ITO. In the first approach, firms outsource a majority of their IT services, thus leveraging the expertise, knowledge, and skills of ITO suppliers (solution 3). However, firms need to rely on additional governance mechanisms such as flexible partnerships to safeguard themselves from contractual hazards. In the second approach, firms outsource a small range of IT services, but use a diversified range of ITO suppliers to allow them to gain access to a variety of IT capabilities from ITO suppliers while limiting the risks associated with detailed contracts (solution 4). Interestingly, out of the possible configurations, solution 3 has the highest coverage, indicating that there are more firms that choose to use extendable contracts while outsourcing most of their IT services, thus gaining extra IT capabilities from outsourcing.

## Strategic innovation outcomes

Solution 5 in Table 6 shows the ITO configurations that can lead to strategic innovation. There is only one possible solution with a high consistency value of 0.95 but a low coverage value of 0.06. This means that there are only a small number of firms (1–2 firms in our sample) that seem to have gained strategic innovation through ITO services. This is not a surprising result given that using ITO for strategic innovation is a relatively new trend (Oshri et al., 2015). A closer examination indicates that solution 5 is identical to solution 4 and solution 2, suggesting that this best-of-breed strategy can lead to multiple desirable outcomes: cost reduction, capability en hancement, and strategic innovation.

While this pattern fits previous findings in terms of cost reduction and capability enhancement outcomes (Krishnamurthy et al., 2009; Levina and Ross, 2003; Poston et al., 2009), how does it deliver strategic innovation outcomes? Strategic innovation requires a deeper engagement level between the outsourcing clients and suppliers (Aubert et al. 2015: Linden and Schmidt. 2016: Oshri et al 2015). In other words, because strategic innovation is a knowledge-intensive activity, the buyer-seller relationship in outsourcing needs to be transformed into a co-production relationship in which both parties share knowledge and co-create new products or services (Jean et al 2014: Kaiser and Hawk, 2004: Poston et al 2009: Whitley and Willcocks, 2011). Similar to a client-consultant relationship (Dawson et al., 2010). here the client-supplier relationship needs to be transformed to allow both parties to leverage the capability and knowledge from the other to explore the unknown. Given the nature of this new type of relationship, having a smaller scope of outsourcing while engaging with “best” suppliers within a large pool of suppliers allows firms to lower coordination costs (e.g., avoid frequent searches for new suppliers). In addition, the flexible pricing models allow firms to use performance-based or outcome-based pricing models, which have been shown to encourage innovative solutions among ITO suppliers (Vitasek et al., 2010; Vitasek and Nyden, 2012).

## ITO configurations to negated-outcomes

To further explore the use of set-theoretic analysis in studying ITO configurations, we also examined IT configurations that lead to negated-outcomes. As noted in the theory section, this kind of analysis is a unique advantage of set-theoretic analysis over variancebased models. It allows researchers to examine asymmetric causality (Fiss, 2007, 2011), thus revealing deeper insights.

Out of the three asymmetric analyses that we conducted, only the analysis for negated-strategic innovation outcomes produced results with consistency values above the threshold (see Table 7). Specifically, there is one possible ITO configuration that leads to the lack of strategic innovation outcomes. In this configuration, firms outsource the majority of their IT services while using extendable and detailed contracts, but choose to have a flexible pricing model and use a small pool of ITO suppliers. Compared to solutions in Table 6, it bears the greatest resemblance to the asset-specific outsourcing strategy (solution 1a Table 6). This confirms that in pursuing cost reduction outcomes through ITO, firms may have used arrangements that can impede their strategic innovativeness (Aubert et al., 2015: Kotlarsky et al., 2015). Another closer look at the exemplary firm, Delta, suggests that this firm uses a configuration for majority outsourcing (outsourcing majority of IT services using flexible pricing models and extendable contracts) with an asset-specific strategy (small pool of suppliers with detailed contracts). However, because their services are generic, this approach is an overkill and backfires on their innovativeness.

## Discussion

In this paper, we follow Fiss (2007) and Fink’s (2010) suggestion to explore the use of a set-theoretic method to study ITO configurations and their outcomes. Based on a survey of 27 large and medium organizations, we identified six ITO arrangements that are related to both positive and negative outcomes. These arrangements can be grouped into four ITO configurations, summarized in Table 8. Among them, one stands out the most, delivering several desirable outcomes (best-of-breed outsourcing, configuration #2), while one presents an interesting case with undesirable outcomes (negated-strategic innovation in configuration #4).

Overall, our study makes three contributions: (1) we illustrate the use of the set-theoretic approach to explore contradiction and complexity in ITO arrangements, joining prior eforts in promoting this approach as an alternative method for exploring causal complexity in ITO arrangements (Fink, 2010); (2) we illustrate how to combine governance and capability perspectives in ITO research, heeding prior calls for additional research that extends beyond dominant outsourcing paradigms (e.g., transaction cost economics) (Lacity et al., 2011); and (3) our findings provide additional insight on the recent discourse on the ITO paradox—how to gain innovation through outsourcing (Aubert et al., 2015). To this end, we depart from the extant literature and suggest that in addition to transaction-based governance, organizations need to attend to portfolio-based governance. Next, we discuss each of the three contributions in detail

## Merits of the set-theoretic approach in ITO studies

Our study illustrates the strengths of the set-theoretic approach as an alternative method to explore trade-ofs and complexity in ITO arrangements. Compared to variance-based methods, this approach excels in exploring complex causality to identify necessary conditions (e.g., detailed contracts as necessary for cost reduction), revealing equifinality (e.g., configuration #2 and #3 in Table 8 both lead to capability enhancement), and examining asymmetric causality (e.g., configuration #4 in Table 8 leads to negatedstrategic innovation). By focusing on configurations of attributes instead of examining their causal efects in isolation, the settheoretic approach provides rich insight on how ITO configurations matter. To this end, our findings echo previous studies in suggesting the set-theoretic approach as an alternative method to fully explore the causal complexity in ITO decision-making and to identify underlying patterns across multiple ITO conditions (Fink, 2010; Fiss, 2007).

In addition, the set-theoretic approach readily lends itself to theoretical integration across multiple perspectives. In our study, we were able to combine capability and governance perspectives in explaining boundary choices (e.g., outsourcing decisions) (Argyres, 2011; Argyres and Zenger, 2012; Jacobides and Winter, 2005). While prior studies have seen these theories as competing ex planations for the make-or-buy decisions and use them cautiously in an additive manner (Argyres, 1996; Poppo and Zenger, 1998), our findings illustrate a much more intimate relationship between these theories, showing how governance and capability perspectives can complement each other to produce several positive outcomes (configuration #2 Table 8), but they can also work in a contradictory manner to produce negated-outcomes (configuration #4 Table 8). Such observations speak directly to the need for integrated theories (Cheon et al., 1995; Lacity et al., 2011) and echo studies that suggested a complex relationship between theoretical perspectives (Argyres and Zenger, 2012; Jacobides and Winter, 2005).

The set-theoretic approach has its own limitations, and we caution future studies about those challenges (Fiss. 2007: Ragin. 2000) In our study, one challenge stands out. It is the issue of determining core attributes in ITO configurations (Ketchen et al., 1997; Miller, 1996). Setting a narrow scope with an exclusive list of attributes leads to abstract configurations; but setting a broader scope with an inclusive list of attributes can lead to too many configurations that are difficult to interpret. The struggle to find the right balance between “zooming out” and “zooming in" is prevalent in configurational studies and requires careful consideration in research design, In our case, because of the exploratory nature of our paper, we first started with a list of 10 attributes in our initial design, but had to reduce them to five attributes in our final report for theoretical parsimony and the lack of variance observed in our sample Upon close examination, we found that the lack of variance of these attributes is not a surprise given contemporary ITO practices. For instance, we asked if our respondents stay in frequent communication with their suppliers, or their suppliers own equity in their organizations. It turns out most organizations in our sample have frequent communication with their suppliers, and do not allow their suppliers to own their equity. This is not surprising given the ubiquity of the Internet and the more mature stage of IT outsourcing (compared to the late 1990s and 2000s when it first surfaced). Similarly, the Resource Ownership attribute has little variance because in practice, to reduce costs, most firms do not retain ownership of IT resources in outsourcing; and the Equity Ownership attribute shows no variance because firms typically do not allow their suppliers to own an equity share. Thus, we strongly encourage future studies to reflect on the contemporary ITO literature and engage in multiple rounds of pre-tests to narrow down a meaningful list of attributes for their purposes.

## Implications to studies of strategic innovation through outsourcing

Our findings also add insights to the emerging literature on strategic innovation through outsourcing (Kotlarsky et al., 2015; Oshr et al., 2015) and the discussion of the inherent paradox of IT outsourcing (Aubert et al., 2015). Prior studies theorize that ITO arrangements for cost reduction can impede strategic innovations; and to overcome this tension, mechanisms for internal organization of people and activities should be considered (Aubert et al., 2015). Our study lends support to the claim that elements of cost reduction in ITO arrangements (but not capability enhancement) do indeed appear in configurations with the lack of strategic innovation outcomes. Specifically, having detailed contracts is a necessary condition for cost reduction but it also is a core element for the lack of strategic innovation. Thus, existing best practices for cost reduction likely create barriers to achieving strategic innovation However, while tensions do exist, these positive outsourcing outcomes are not mutually exclusive. In fact, the best-of-breed outsourcing arrangement was able to garner cost reduction, capability enhancement, and strategic innovation outcomes.

What is the root cause of the ITO paradox? One possible explanation suggested by Aubert et al. (2015) is the exploration-exploitation tension in outsourcing activities. On the one hand, gaining eficiency through outsourcing is exploitative in nature. Outsourcing clients exploit the knowledge, skills, and capabilities of suppliers to reduce costs and gain access to needed capabilities. On the other hand, achieving innovation through outsourcing is exploratory in nature. Outsourcing clients leverage suppliers’ expertise to explore new markets, create new products, and invent new processes. This explains why detailed contracts are a necessary condition for cost reduction but curtail innovation: detailed contracts specify terms and conditions to reduce risks and optimize ITO relationships; but they restrict suppliers’ flexibility in exploring new initiatives in the name of innovation. Drawing insight from the ambidexterity literature, Aubert et al. (2015) suggest two solutions: creating structural ambidexterity with teams focusing on exploration and exploitation, or a temporal ambidexterity in which firms switch between exploratory and exploitative outsourcing activities.

Another possible solution is to enable ambidexterity across multiple levels, for individuals and organizations (Raisch et al., 2009). While structural arrangements often enable ambidexterity at the organizational level, other arrangements such as socialization, teambuilding practices, or knowledge sharing initiatives allow individuals to think and act ambidextrously. Applying this lesson in the ITO context and based on our findings, we posit that one solution to ITO outsourcing is to create flexibility in outsourcing to enable ambidexterity across all outsourcing levels. Specifically, increasing flexibility both at the ITO portfolio level (organizational level) and transaction level will enable the clients to pursue innovative projects while maintaining operational eficiency. Next, we discuss in detail how this can be done.

## Flexibility in outsourcing: transaction-based and portfolio-based governance

The extant ITO literature has plenty examples of transaction-based governance to increase flexibility in outsourcing. Overall, there are three main approaches (see Table 9). First, research has suggested how contractual governance enables flexibility in outsourcing (Harris et al., 1998; Tan and Sia, 2006). Through contractual provisions such as pricing models, renegotiation clauses contract duration, early termination clauses, or incentive contracts, outsourcing clients motivate continuous improvements from outsourcing suppliers and thus can increase service satisfaction and improve service quality (Harris et al., 1998). Other contractual arrangements such as modular contracts (Goo et al., 2009; Tan and Sia, 2006) or reversible contract terms (Benaroch et al., 2010) also reduce contractual risks and encourage suppliers to innovate in their services. A second approach is to use relational governance to increase outsourcing flexibility (Gopal and Koka, 2012; Gulati, 1995; Sabherwal, 1999). In the observed best-of-breed configuration, several contractual and relational governance features at the transaction-level were observed. Specifically, the company used a flexible pricing model and pressured suppliers to continuously improve services by using fixed-term contracts; and it kept daily communications with its suppliers to foster a collaborative relationship. While transactionbased governance is useful, our findings also suggest the company has used a more holistic ITO governance approach. The best-ofbreed configuration is constituted through a combination of both project-level and portfolio-level factors. The exemplary firm had a mindful approach to outsourcing in which the company outsourced a subset of its IT services to multiple suppliers through carefu portfolio design. In doing so, the company was able to leverage the strengths of its small but diverse group of IT suppliers to provide application development services, helping the company pursue new products and services, and explore new market opportunities. Suppliers that were “best” in a category were selected, allowing the company to combine its in-house capabilities with externa knowledge in pursuing of innovative solutions. In other words, the company had a holistic management approach across its ITO projects, selectively choosing areas that need exploration and areas that need exploitation.

This holistic ITO approach is what we term a portfolio-based governance. The word “portfolio." adopted from Cullen et al. (2005). refers to a “collection of ITO deals in force at any one time” (p. 360). Here, the unit of analysis is not a dyadic, inter-firm transaction, but multiple transactions across a number of firms over a broad historical context. Lacity et al. (2011) suggested that “using the transaction as the unit of analysis overlooked the broader historical context which has significant implications for ITO decisions… [more interesting is] how capabilities are built over multiple generations of outsourcing, bundled sourcing and multi-sourcing” (p 148). From a theoretical standpoint, portfolio-based governance readily lends itself to theoretical integration—a central strength of set-theoretic methods. Leiblein (2003) stated that the transaction cost economics dominant view of organizations, which maintains that the individual transaction is the appropriate unit of analysis, can be usefully extended by including the efects of transactional dependence. This idea is consistent with a highly cited notion of governance inseparability by Argyres and Liebeskind (1999, 2002). They contend that a firm’s governance choices are influenced by its portfolio of contemporaneous exchange relationships because there are interdependencies between related governance decisions. In fact, Williamson (1999) himself has suggested that organizational capabilities can be fruitfully operationalized as “clusters” of transactions. Thus, approaches that consider multiple interdependent transactions can efectively facilitate theory integration.

What does portfolio-based governance entail? From a contractual perspective, a portfolio-based governance provides an outsourcing blueprint (Krishnamurthy et al., 2009), a portfolio-level guide for coordination across ITO projects in exploring new opportunities toward a strategic vision of the firm. For example. Krishnamurthy et al. (2009) reported how Cisco was able to outsource within a smaller scope, only outsourcing routine services to multiple service providers, and using a time-to-value pricing model to focus on performance rather than costs. Using this “Strategic Out-Tasking” approach, Cisco focused on a “portfolio of discrete managed services” and therefore encouraged innovation while yielding cost savings. Similarly, Tan and Sia (2006) suggested a strategic maneuver for flexibility by using multiple vendors for outsourcing, increasing vendor interoperability, and minimizing customization. This approach increases the flexibility for clients to easily switch between vendors for needed capabilities. Other contractual elements such as time-to-value pricing models or service-level agreements will strengthen the approach; but ultimately, portfolio-based governance requires a mindful understanding of what to outsource, when, and to whom, as well as potential com plementarity across individual ITO projects

From the relational perspective, portfolio-based governance focuses on establishing long-lasting relationships with suppliers that can foster collaborative initiatives beyond contract-bound transactions. This is what Tan and Sia (2006) called “quality partnerships” in which suppliers are enabled to quickly adapt to dynamic changes. Further examples can be found in the vested outsourcing literature (Vitasek et al., 2010; Vitasek and Nyden, 2012). Drawing insight from major successful outsourcing deals across various firms (e.g., Microsoft, P&G, US Air Force), Kate Vitasek and her colleagues at the University of Tennessee coined the term “vested outsourcing” as an alternative model to traditional outsourcing. In vested outsourcing, the outsourcing client attempts to form a partnership with suppliers by giving them flexibility and incentives to pursue innovative solutions that cut costs and enhance services outside the specifics of contracts. The rigid contractual controls are relaxed, while authority, autonomy, incentives, and trust are strengthened to encourage suppliers to step outside of contractual boundaries and create “win-win” solutions for all involved parties. For instance, Microsoft used a shared incentive program that turned cost savings into suppliers’ profits. By doing so, the suppliers are encouraged to step out of the ordinary contracts to innovate new solutions for process improvement. While our findings do not necessarily revea a configuration that resembles vested outsourcing patterns. it is a potential research opportunity for future research.

Taken all together, we posit that through both transaction-based governance and portfolio-based governance, firms are likely to increase flexibility in outsourcing and have a better chance to manage the tension between exploration–exploitation in outsourcing. ## Implications and future directions

Our findings have a number of implications for practice. For ITO managers, the multiple configurations we identified suggest different pathways to desirable ITO outcomes. Thus, we suggest that ITO managers should configure their outsourcing arrangements to best fit their strategic intents and level of maturity. Similarly, for ITO suppliers, our findings suggest the need to adjust to these configurations as they pursue business with diferent clients. In addition, our findings suggest that there are ITO configurations that can lead to multiple positive outcomes as well as configurations that can lead to negative outcomes. Thus, it is imperative for ITO managers to pay close attention to how these diferent ITO conditions work in conjunction to achieve optimal outcomes Furthermore, our discussion on the importance of transaction-based and portfolio-based governance mirrors the lessons raised by many outsourcing practitioners (Krishnamurthy et al., 2009; Vitasek et al., 2010; Vitasek and Nyden, 2012). Given the increased attention to the innovation-eficiency trade-of in outsourcing, our findings point to one possible solution to this paradox: through a “best-of-breed” strategy, companies can better manage these trade-ofs. Future studies are encouraged to explore other configurations.

Our study is not without limitations. First of all. the response rate to our survey is low and our sample size remains small, which limits the generalizability of our findings. However, our study follows a configurational perspective which is qualitative by nature and geared toward mid-size N (10 to 50 cases) (Ragin, 2000, 2008); thus, our aim is analytic generalization (Yin, 2009). We encourage future replication of our study to extend its generalization. In this study, following prior set-theoretical studies with small sample sizes (Iannacci and Cornford, 2018; Vergne and Depeyre, 2016), we performed post-analyses of selected cases using publicly available data. These additional insights provide richer description of the ITO configurations. thus increasing confidence in their applicability. Second, while we propose the set-theoretic approach as an alternative method to explore contradiction and complexity in ITO arrangements, it will be naïve of us not to recognize the several inherent limitations associated with this approach. For example, this approach might have a high risk of subjective bias, or might be highly sensitive to case selection (Liu et al., 2015). Despite these limitations, we contend that using the set-theoretic approach would better complement the goals of the configurational perspective than other approaches. Third, because of the exploratory nature of our study, only a handful of attributes were retained from our initial research design. Future studies are encouraged to consider other attributes to validate our conjectures here. Lastly, future research can explore to see if our conjectures hold in other contexts. Being able to identify ITO configurations that lead to multiple positive outcomes or to negative outcomes can shed light on how firms should configure their overall ITO practices.

## Concluding thoughts

Overall, our study reveals multiple ITO configurations that are related to both positive and negative organizational outcomes. It suggests that ITO managers should approach ITO from a configurational perspective, as opposed to managing ITO from the perspective of isolated factors. Our results contribute to a more holistic understanding of ITO, and take the first step toward greater integration of multiple theories and potential reconciliation of inherent contradiction in ITO arrangements. Furthermore, we join an emerging wave of outsourcing scholars to advocate for the need of both transaction-based and portfolio-based governance (Cullen et al., 2005; Krishnamurthy et al., 2009; Vitasek et al., 2010; Vitasek and Nyden, 2012). We hope our research encourages further studies to look into this important and underexplored research area.

## Appendix C. Case vignettes

Asset-specific outsourcing: Alpha is a banking and finance company located in the Chicago area. It has over 10,000 employees and annual revenues of more than \$500 million. Alpha has outsourced most of its IT services for application, operations, and support (more than 80% of their IT needs) for over 14 years. It has a small pool of five suppliers, providing and supporting some specialized technology platforms for fund and asset management. More than half the time, Alpha needs to put forth significant investment by investing in specialized assets in working with their IT suppliers. Thus, Alpha wishes to foster a strong relationship with its ITO suppliers by using detailed contracts and partnership strategies, and maintaining daily communications with its suppliers. In addition, it often uses extendable and short-term contracts to encourage continuous improvements from its suppliers and mitigate risks from opportunism

Best-of-breed outsourcing: Beta is a medium-size tech company located in California. It has over 5,000 employees and revenues of over \$100 million. The company has outsourced for over 10 years, but only outsources its IT application development and support, which accounts for 50% of its IT expenses. On average, Beta relies on five diferent suppliers, using mostly detailed contract types (detailed and strategic partnership) with fixed short terms. These contracts are mostly for Beta’s generic needs; thus, it hardly needs additional employees, training, or specialized assets to work with its ITO suppliers. There are daily communications between Beta and its suppliers.

Majority outsourcing with flexible partnership: Gamma is a large healthcare corporation located in the Chicago area. It employs more than 10,000 people, with an annual revenue of more than \$1 billion. In 2016, Gamma underwent a drastic strategical change when it laid of 70% of its IT staf and outsourced the majority of its IT services (75%), retaining only critical strategy and design work in-house. It has a multi-sourcing strategy in which the company outsources to more than 100 ITO suppliers through various contract types (generic contracts, detailed contracts, etc.). It has an outcome-based pricing model, and uses extendable contracts that last from three to seven years. Daily communication is maintained, and training is provided for diferent outsourced functions.

Asset-specific + Majority outsourcing strategy: Omega is a small size manufacturing company located in the state of New York. It employs fewer than 1,000 people and has annual revenues of less than \$50 million. Its IT budget is small, only about \$200,000 a year. More than four years ago, the company started to outsource almost all of its IT services (including IT management). Currently, it has only two ITO suppliers, using a fixed pricing model, and extendable and detailed contracts of medium length (less than seven years). However, most of the outsourced IT services are generic, requiring no specialized training and resources from ITO suppliers.

## Appendix D. Set-theoretic methodology

Conventional, variance-based approaches are marked by “net efects thinking” (Ragin, 2008) that implicitly assumes causal relationships are independent, additive, and symmetrical (Delbridge and Fiss, 2013; Misangyi et al., 2017). Accordingly, conventional approaches typically model outcomes, i.e., dependent variables, in terms of the additive efects of a set of independent variables. Empirical analysis is carried out through various econometric methods to estimate the isolated efect of each independent variable. While some interaction efects (i.e., conjunctural causality) between independent variables can be estimated through multiplicative terms, interpreting interactions bevond two variables is exceedingly difficult (Braumoeller. 2004: Vis. 2012) Thus conventional empirical approaches can only capture relatively simple causal relationships. Recently, the set-theoretic methods has emerged as an alternative method to study complex causality (Fiss, 2007; Misangyi et al., 2017). Figs. D1a–D1c depicts how the settheoretic approach fares against other methods

Fig. D1a illustrates a typical variance-based analysis in which independent variables A, B, C, and D will predict variables E and F, which in turn predict outcome O. In this simple scenario, the model captures theory-driven relationships between the diferent ITO predictors and ITO outcomes. It emphasizes their linear causal relationships and adopts a “net effect” estimation approach to estimate the efect size of each independent variable with reference to the dependent variable O. It is quite dificult to account for the possible interactions between diferent variables, especially if the interactions involve more than two variables. In addition, adding and removing a variable into this model would only control for the efect of such variable to the dependent variables but not necessarily to other variables, making it dificult to flexibly adjust the model and explore the diferent interaction efects.

Fig. D1b demonstrates eforts of some ITO configurational studies that attempt to explore the complex and highly interdependent interactions between ITO factors regarding performances (Lee et al., 2004; Salimath et al., 2008; Zimmermann et al., 2013). In this approach, what we term a partial assessment approach, authors rely on diferent categorization approaches (e.g., manual categorization or cluster analyses) to identify categories or types of ITO practices. These ITO types consider all possible factors and identify the dominant ITO types that are characterized by a small group of ITO factors. Compared to the variance-based approach, this approach is able to evaluate the complex interactions between ITO factors. It will be easier to identify the efect of adding or removing ITO factors to the mix. However, the partial assessment approach often relies on variance-based analyses to examine the relationship between ITO types and outcome (Fig. D1b). As a result, this approach can only assess whether the diferent ITO types difer in regard to the outcome O. It is not quite possible to assess how the diferent ITO factors contribute to the whole or how their combinations afect the outcome. The ITO types become a “black box,” and researchers are unable to discern whether other factors besides those that determine the membership of a case in an ITO type are relevant to the outcomes (Fiss, 2007). In other words, this approach assumes the presence of an ITO factor is important to the outcome but cannot empirically determine whether this is actuall the case.

The set-theoretic approach, illustrated in Fig. D1c, has been proposed by several researchers as an alternative that allows full assessment of the ITO configuration-performance relationships (Fink, 2010; Fiss, 2007). Compared to other approaches, this approach has three strengths. First, it focuses on conjunctural relationships and adopts Boolean logics to suggest prominent constellations of factors that lead to specific outcomes (Fiss, 2007; Liu et al., 2015; Ragin, 2000). As a result, the set-theoretic approach is more suitable to exploring complex interactions, letting the underlying patterns emerge from the data. The presence and absence of a factor would have certain meanings to other factors as well as to the observed outcomes in the set-theoretic approach. As can be seen from Fig. D1c, one can easily determine the impact of diferent ITO factors in conjunction with each other to the outcome O, something that both the variance-based approach and the partial assessment approach fall short on.

Second, the set-theoretic approach excels in exploring equifinality—the possibility of multiple paths to the same outcome (Fiss, 2007; Ragin, 2000). The variance-based approach focuses on the direct efects of independent variables regarding dependent variables, assuming that such efects are relevant for all cases under examination. Subsequently, they are less sensitive to the efects of other factors—enabling conditions—that make the efects vary. While interactions and moderations do account for some enabling conditions, their flexibility is sub-optimal compared to the set-theoretic approach. Adding or removing factors will automatically introduce diferent conditions to the analyses, allowing faster identification of diferent paths to the same outcome.

Finally, the variance-based approach assumes the relationships between independent variables and dependent variables are symmetric while the set-theoretic approach focuses on asymmetric relationships. Both the variance-based approach and partia assessment approach assume the absence of a variable will automatically have an efect on the dependent variable opposite to what occurs when the variable is present. The variable is both a necessary and a suficient condition for certain outcomes. In contrast, the set-theoretic approach assumes that a factor is either a necessary or a suficient condition for certain outcomes, and that adding and removing a factor does not have opposite efects on outcomes. Thus, for ITO relationships that are complex and highly interdependent, a set-theoretic approach can ofer better insights to understand how the presence and absence of specific ITO factors afect organizational outcomes.
