# PEER EFFECTS IN COMPETITIVE ENVIRONMENTS: FIELD EXPERIMENTS ON INFORMATION PROVISION AND INTERVENTIONS<sup>1</sup>

Zhuoxin Li Carroll School of Management, Boston College, 140 Commonwealth Avenue, Chestnut Hill, MA 02467 U.S.A. {zhuoxin.li@bc.edu}

Gang Wang Lerner College of Business and Economics, University of Delaware, 42 Amstel Avenue, Newark, DE 19716 U.S.A. {gangw@udel.edu}

Harry Jiannan Wang Lerner College of Business and Economics, University of Delaware, 42 Amstel Avenue, Newark, DE 19716 U.S.A. {hjwang@udel.edu}

Prior studies of social-norms interventions have focused on nudging behavior in noncompetitive settings. This research evaluates such interventions in competitive environments, for example, a class with a competitive grading policy. Field experiments on a learning management system show that providing descriptive infor mation about peers’ behavior has mixed effects in reducing procrastination and improving performance outcomes. Specifically, the effects are moderated by individual characteristics and contextual variables. First, peer information interventions are more effective for males, and the effects are stronger in a male-majority environment than in a female-majority environment. These findings differ from prior studies of social-norms interventions conducted in noncompetitive settings, in which females are found to be more responsive to interventions. Gender differences in our competitive settings can be explained by males’ and females’ differential preferences for competition: males are more competitive-oriented and thus are more responsive to peer infor mation in competitive environments. Second, we find that individuals who are in great need of interventions, that is, those with poor past behavior and performance, are also more likely to benefit from peer information interventions, suggesting that peer information interventions motivate positive change. This study highlights the heterogeneous effects of peer information interventions and has implications for targeted interventions.

Keywords: Peer information, peer effects, social norms, social comparison, gender differences, nudging, procrastination, digital interventions, randomized field experiment, learning management systems (LMS)

## Introduction

Individuals use a variety of personal applications and enterprise systems for work, learning, entertainment, and other activities. For instance, health and fitness apps such as Lose It! and MyFitnessPal help individuals to plan their nutrition and exercise programs, monitor and keep track of progress, and learn about the activities of other members. In an analogous fashion, online learning management systems (LMS) such as Canvas and BlackBoard create an IT-mediated environment for the teaching and learning process, where students get access to course materials, complete assignments online, and receive performance feedback once the teacher posts assignment grades. These information systems have led to the accumulation of detailed data on individuals’ behaviors and outcomes. There is an opportunity to analyze these data to learn about individual behavior, understand what behavioral patterns are associated with superior or unsatisfactory outcomes, and, ultimately, design targeted interventions to improve individual performance. For instance, such interventions may involve nudging behavior and performance improvement by disclosing information about peers (Cialdini and Goldstein 2004). Prior studies of social-norms interventions have focused on nudging individual behavior in noncompetitive settings. However, much is unknown about the effectiveness of such interventions in competitive environments; for example, individuals competing for a higher ranking on a fitness app’s leaderboard, students in a classroom with a ranking-based grading policy, employees within a department competing for performance-based bonuses, etc.

This research investigates targeted interventions in the context of online LMS. LMS are an integral part of higher education, with millions of students spending a significant amount of their time every week with these systems (Dahlstrom et al. 2014). The wide adoption of LMS in almost every college has motivated teachers to consider digital interventions to improve the learning process. Such digital inventions take advantage of rich behavioral data about students’ learning activities recorded in system logs (Lam et al. 2012). For instance, Canvas keeps track of students’ browsing histories, clickstream data, and assignment submission records. These fine-grained data are available at both individual and aggregate levels, which allow teachers to track each student’s academic performance relative to their peers. Furthermore, such detailed data are also immediately available for targeted experimentation or gamification to improve students’ learning behaviors and academic performance (Liu et al. 2017; Wang et al. 2017).

We focus on interventions of a specific type of individual behavior: procrastination. Poor study habits such as procrastination are among the major antecedents of unsatisfactory academic performance for college students (Ariely and Wertenbroch 2002). A recent survey indicates that over 87% of college students procrastinate, particularly when it comes to doing their coursework or assigned readings (StudyMode 2014). The absolute amount of procrastination is also significant, with students reporting that it typically occupies over one-third of their daily activities, often enacted through sleeping, playing, watching TV, or browsing social media feeds (Pychyl and Sirois 2016). Students recognize procrastination as being harmful and foolish, and over 95% of procrastinators wish to reduce it (O’Brien 2002).

Previous attempts to reduce procrastination in college classes have used different approaches. For example, Lamwers and Jazwinski (1989) design a number of intermediate deadlines and impose these deadlines for the completion of coursework. Ariely and Wertenbroch (2002) examine another approach of requiring students who delayed their work to meet with the teacher. Although demonstrated effective in experimental settings, these interventions are costly to implement because they require significant changes to the current curriculum design or require extra responsibilities for the teachers.

In this research, we designed peer information interventions through LMS to reduce procrastination in a classroom setting where students’ class ranking affects their final grade. Peer information reveals the prevalence of certain behaviors in a population, such as the number (or the percentage) of students in the class who have already submitted an assignment. This type of descriptive information may shape individuals’ beliefs about what others are doing and thus can motivate their own change to conform to socially appropriate behavior (Cialdini and Goldstein 2004) or to achieve a superior position vis-à-vis others (Garcia et al. 2013). Compared to existing procrastination reductions through teacher interventions (e.g., Ariely and Wertenbroch 2002), peer information interventions have the appeal of being able to be seamlessly integrated into LMS.

Despite a long tradition of effectiveness in laboratory tests, peer information interventions have had mixed success in changing behavior in field contexts, with some studies showing boomerang effects (Perkins et al. 2005; Schultz et al. 2007). The specific contexts and populations for which these interventions are effective are still not clear (Silva and John 2017). In the context of college classrooms, the critical questions of interest to educators are whether and under what conditions providing peer information will yield positive out comes, such as improved comprehension and higher academic performance. Answers to these questions, together with the detailed individual-level data from LMS, have the potential to improve student performance through targeted interventions.

We investigate the effectiveness of peer information through a build–evaluate approach (Hevner et al. 2004). Specifically, we first developed a computer program (an add-on for Canvas) that automatically retrieves real-time data about each student from the system logs of Canvas, computes descriptive summary statistics (descriptive peer information), and identifies individuals to receive treatments. We then conducted a set of field experiments with undergraduate students in the northeastern United States. Our computer program randomly assigned subjects into different treatment groups to receive interventions. At the end of the experiments, we collected data from Canvas to evaluate whether and when peer information interventions are effective in promoting behavioral change (i.e., reducing procrastination) and improving academic outcomes (i.e., achieving a higher grade).

Our field experiments reveal that peer information interventions reduce procrastination behavior and increase individual performance (i.e., achieving a higher grade). However, the effects of peer information interventions are moderated by individual characteristics and contextual variables. Surprisingly, peer information interventions are more effective for males than females. In addition, the effects are also stronger in a male-majority environment than a femalemajority environment. The experiments also show that individuals who are most in need of interventions, that is, those with poor past performance (previously receiving a lower grade) and bad past behavior (previously procrastinating more), are also more likely to benefit from the interventions, suggesting such interventions motivate positive change.

This research extends the literature of peer information interventions to competitive settings. Prior studies have focused on peer information interventions in noncompetitive settings (e.g., reusing towels in a hotel room), in which an individual’s performance does not interfere with others’ performance. Therefore, peer information interventions motivate behavior change due to social conformity (Cialdini and Goldstein 2004). Since females are more socially tuned, they would be more likely to respond to such interventions. In competitive settings, providing peer information may induce comparison concerns, enhance the perceived competitiveness of the environment, and thus motivate individuals’ own behavior change to achieve a superior position vis-à-vis others (Garcia et al. 2013). In contrast to the prediction from the social conformity theory, our results show that males are actually more likely to respond to peer information interventions due to social comparison. Our findings show that peer information interventions in a competitive setting motivates males more strongly than females because males are more competitiveoriented.

The findings from this study have implications for the design of data-driven, targeted interventions to promote healthy behavior and improve performance outcomes. Providing peer information may help reduce procrastination for college students, but the effects depend on individual characteristics and the contextual environment. To achieve effective interventions, educators may consider gender differences, gender composition, and the individual’s behavior trajectory and past performance. For instance, peer information interventions may target males in a male-majority environment because these individuals are more likely to benefit from such treatments. Peer information interventions can also target individuals with poor past behavior and performance, the individuals most in need of and benefitting more from interventions. The peer information interventions feature we developed can be built into LMS and can be used for classes throughout the entire institution.

## Literature Review and Hypothesis Development

In this section, we review prior studies on procrastination and its measurement. We also discuss the literature related to peer effects, gender differences, and interventions for behavior change. We then develop the hypotheses on the effects of providing peer information on procrastination mitigation.

## Procrastination and Measurement

Procrastination is defined as the delay in the beginning or completion of an intended task (Ferrari 1992). Prior studies have recognized procrastination as self-regulation deficiency or failure, for example, lack of motivation and weak impulse control (Arvey et al. 2006; Schouwenburg et al. 2004).

Often acknowledging procrastination as an irrational delay, individuals expect the outcome to be worse because of the delay (Steel 2007). Procrastination in academic contexts, in particular, is a severe problem among students (Kim and Seo 2015). For example, male students tend to use regulated strategies less effectively than female students, and are more likely to procrastinate (Karatas 2015; Özer et al. 2009).

Prior studies have mostly measured procrastination based on self-assessment questionnaires. However, in these selfreported surveys, students tend to over-report their grades and exaggerate their procrastination (Kim and Seo 2015). Thanks to the adoption of LMS, detailed data on student behavior (e.g., clickstream data) have now enabled researchers to create objective metrics for students’ learning habits. Prior studies have used the start time for specific tasks as indicators of procrastination (Cerezo et al. 2017; Rotenstein et al. 2009; You 2016).

## Procrastination Interventions

The prevalence of procrastination and its negative effects are well recognized. Educators and researchers have attempted to help students overcome procrastination. Extant interventions mostly take the form of therapeutic programs (e.g., helping procrastinators build self-regulatory skills and selfefficacy) to establish better habits and overcome procrastination (van Eerde and Klingsieck 2018). A few applied studies have attempted to reduce procrastination. For example, Lamwers and Jazwinski (1989) design different arrangements of deadlines and find that frequent deadlines reduce procrastination and improve student grades compared to a fully self-paced arrangement. Ariely and Wertenbroch (2002) discuss another approach to decrease procrastination by requiring students who delayed their work to meet with the teacher. Although proved effective to some extent, the interventions to reduce procrastination in the literature, are not without limitations. Typically, a deadline imposed by the teacher involves the threat of another punishment (e.g., failing an assignment or being forced to meet with the teacher) if the desired academic behavior is not performed by the specified date. Perrin et al. (2010) point out that “improved studying due to an intervention of this kind would appear to be maintained by negative reinforcement” (p. 465) in the form of avoiding some additional teacher-imposed punishment. Furthermore, these interventions can be costly to implement because they either require significant changes to curriculum design or demand extra responsibilities from the teachers (e.g., additional time to meet with students who procrastinated).

Our study investigates an alternative intervention that relies on the revelation of descriptive peer information to students, which does not interfere with a teacher’s course schedule. The feature of peer information notification we developed can be built into LMS and used for classes throughout the entire institution. Several field experimental studies have investigated student behavior on MOOC platforms, but their focus is on content contribution (Baek and Shore 2020) or assign ment completion (Huang et al. 2018) rather than learning outcomes in a classroom setting (the completion rate is around 97%, and thus less of a concern in our setting). The objective of our research is to identify when peer information interventions work (identifying moderators that determine heterogeneous treatment effects) and how they work (identifying the mediator that leads to grade improvement).

## Effects of Peer Information: Social Conformity and Social Comparison

Policymakers are increasingly interested in the potential of leveraging peer effects to nudge individual behavior (Peng et al. 2017; Qiu and Kumar 2017). While much is unknown about peer information interventions through LMS, several studies have looked into the effect of peer information in other contexts, such as stimulating prosocial behavior (Huang et al. 2019), reducing alcohol consumption (Lewis and

Neighbors 2004; Perkins 2002), and promoting sustainable environmental behavior (Schultz et al. 2007).

Prior studies have focused on peer information interventions in noncompetitive settings (e.g., reusing towels in a hotel room), in which an individual’s performance does not interfere with others’ performance. In noncompetitive settings, peer information interventions motivate behavior change due to social conformity (Cialdini and Goldstein 2004). In competitive settings, providing peer information may induce social comparison—the tendency to self-evaluate by comparing oneself to others (Garcia et al. 2013). The social cues from descriptive information may shape individuals’ beliefs about what others are doing, enhancing the perceived competitiveness of the environment, and thus can motivate an individual’s own behavior change to achieve a superior position vis-à-vis others (Garcia et al. 2013).

Evidence for the success of peer information interventions has been mixed. Although some studies appear to confirm the effectiveness of the peer information interventions, other studies have failed to produce substantial changes in behavior (Clapp et al. 2003; Granfield 2005). It is still not clear for which contexts and populations these interventions are effective (Silva and John 2017). In fact, some studies indicate that peer information interventions may backfire; they have actually increased undesirable behaviors and misperceptions they set out to decrease (Perkins et al. 2005; Wechsler et al. 2003; Werch et al. 2000). For instance, providing peer information (for example, the fraction of other employees in the company who participate in the 401(k) plan) may decrease the enrollment of nonparticipants due to discouragement from upward social comparisons (Beshears et al. 2015). Also, Hashim et al. (2017) find that random provision of information about others’ actions is no different than not providing information, suggesting the need for targeted interventions.

## Hypotheses Development

## Effects of Peer Information

Individuals use information about their peers to determine the most appropriate course of action in a given situation (Cialdini and Goldstein 2004; Cialdini and Trost 1998). Providing peer information may create a sense of urgency (Michael 1991) and produce a salience effect (Leland et al. 2019) that focuses the attention of participants on the targeted action. In competitive settings, disclosing descriptive information about other students’ progress may also encourage social comparisons, making the competitive environment more salient to those who received the information (Garcia et al. 2013). Competition effects may motivate students to start earlier (and spend more time on the assignment), hoping to achieve a higher grade compared to their peers.

Peer information about other students’ progress may motivate procrastinators to reduce procrastination and start working on an assignment earlier than they typically would. Such positive behavior change may be associated with performance improvement for several reasons. First, starting the assignment earlier grants students more time to understand expectations and to acquire the knowledge and skills needed to solve the problems in the assignment. Second, early starters may also seek help from teaching assistants or the instructor—they can go to office hours when they are stuck with difficult questions. In contrast, procrastinators may find themselves short of time and resources to complete a quality submission before it is due. Therefore, reducing student procrastination may lead to better performance outcomes (Ariely and Wertenbroch 2002). We thus hypothesize:

Hypothesis 1a (H1a). Peer information interventions reduce student procrastination.

Hypothesis 1b (H1b). Peer information interventions increase student performance.

## The Moderating Role of Gender and Gender Composition

The effects of peer information interventions are very likely to be individual- and context-specific (Lu et al. 2016). This section discusses how gender and gender composition moderate the effectiveness of peer information interventions.

Gender: Gender differences and their impacts on economic and social outcomes have attracted increasing attention (Ahuja and Thatcher 2005). The literature suggests that females are more socially attuned and thus more cooperative (Stocks et al. 2009). For example, Frank et al. (1993) find that females are significantly more cooperative than males in prisoner’s dilemma games. In contrast, males are prone to exhibit stronger individualism (Kashima et al. 1995). Males were found to exhibit significantly more aggressiveness than females (Brooks 1982). Furthermore, males prefer competition more than females. Gneezy et al. (2003) let participants solve computerized mazes in different competitive settings. They find that males are more strongly motivated by competitive incentives.

While prior studies suggest females are more likely to respond to social cues in noncompetitive settings, it is not clear whether males or females are more responsive to peer information in competitive settings. The two forces discussed above may drive the gender effects in opposite directions. On one hand, females may be more likely to respond to the social cues because they are more socially attuned (Gordon et al. 2000). On the other hand, males are more likely to be motivated by a competitive environment induced by peer information (Ruble et al. 2006; Weber et al. 2004). Given prior findings in the literature and the specific context we study, we lean toward the prediction that, in our setting, male students are more likely to opt into a competitive setting induced by the peer information they received (Gneezy et al. 2003). As a result, providing descriptive peer information can motivate them to work on the assignment early to achieve a better grade compared to others. We, therefore, propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2a (H2a). The effect of peer information interventions is stronger for males than females.

Gender Composition. The effects of peer information are very likely to be context-specific. In particular, this section discusses how the gender composition of the reference group moderate the effectiveness of peer information interventions.

Individuals’ behavior and performance depend not only on their own gender but also on the gender of people with whom they interact (Gupta et al. 2013). Groups of different gender compositions may reveal distinct idiosyncrasies emerging from the interactions among group members. For example, groups with a higher female-to-male ratio may reveal more “feminine” personality and dynamics. In a study of a business competition game, Apesteguia et al. (2012) observe that allfemale teams are less aggressive than mixed-gender or allmale teams in running their businesses. All-female teams compete less aggressively in pricing and invest more in social sustainability initiatives. In a dictator game setting, Dufwenberg and Muren (2006) find that female-majority groups are more generous and egalitarian.

Gender composition may influence the behavior of group members across gender. Lavy and Schlosser (2011) find that an increase in the proportion of female students in the classroom improves both male and female students’ cognitive outcomes. Such academic gains are mediated through decreased classroom disruptions and improved inter-student and student–teacher relationships. Lee et al. (2014) investigate the impact of school and classroom gender composition on student achievement. They find that male students attending single-sex classes within coed schools score below male students in mixed-gender classes. Interestingly, classroom or school gender composition does not affect the outcomes of female students (Lee et al. 2014), which suggests that gender composition has disproportional effects on male students’ behavior.

Following the findings in the literature, we expect an environment with more females to reveal more feminine personality and dynamics (Apesteguia et al. 2012; Dufwenberg and Muren 2006). Students, including male students, may perceive an environment of a higher female-to-male ratio less competitive and more cooperative. Thus, compared to a male-majority environment, male students in a femalemajority class may respond less aggressively to the peer information they receive. (Dewan et al. 2017; Lee et al. 2015; Zhang et al. 2017). We, therefore, hypothesize as follows:

Hypothesis 2b (H2b). The effect of peer information interventions is stronger in a male-majority environment than in a female-majority context.

## The Moderating Role of Past Behavior and Performance

The effect of peer information interventions is also likely to be moderated by an individual student’s past behavior and performance. Specifically, we expect the effect of peer information interventions will be stronger for students with relatively poor past behavior and low past performance. Descriptive information about desirable behavior by peers provides a reference point, with which people are likely to conform (Cialdini and Goldstein 2004). With such a cue in mind, an individual can measure the appropriateness of their own behavior by how far away they are from the descriptive information of their reference group (Schultz et al. 2007). The discrepancy between peers’ and his own behavior may motivate an individual to determine the most appropriate course of action in a given situation (Cialdini and Goldstein 2004; Cialdini and Trost 1998).

Providing peer information induces social comparison concerns (Garcia et al. 2013) and the desire to achieve or maintain a better relative position among others, which increases perceived competitiveness especially for those with lower prior performance. While high-performing individuals can also be responsive to interventions because they are more capable of improving themselves, the effects from peer information interventions are possibly weaker for these individuals because they are already more self-motivated, even without interventions. In sum, individuals who realize they are below peers are more likely to exert extra effort to catch up, whereas those who find they are above are less likely to respond positively. We, therefore, propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 3 (H3). The effect of peer information interventions is stronger for individuals who have poor past behavior and past performance.

## Research Method

## Experimental Setting

We designed and implemented a set of randomized field experiments with Canvas, a widely adopted LMS in the United States. The subjects are students of undergraduate courses in business analytics using Excel and other tools. Each course has multiple sections taught by different teachers. The reasons for choosing such courses are threefold. First, the size of each section of the class is relatively large so that we can assign different treatment conditions to students within the same section. Second, different sections of the course are highly comparable: they used the same core syllabus and curriculum, and all used the Canvas site. Third, assignments in these courses require considerable efforts from the students. More details related to the courses, the assignments, and sample questions are presented in Appendix E.

We developed a computer program that automatically retrieves real-time information about each student from the system log of Canvas, computes descriptive statistics of student progress in the reference group (descriptive peer information), and identifies individuals to receive interventions. Specifically, as an assignment deadline approached (e.g., due in a few days), the computer program compiled a list of students who had not started working on the assignment (i.e., not yet downloaded the data file for the assignment). The computer program then randomly assigned subjects into different treatment groups. Students received these messages via email and were also notified when they logged into Canvas. We designed and executed three complementary studies, summarized in Table 1.

Studies I and II were conducted in classes with a competitive grading policy where a student’s final letter grade depended on her/his class ranking (see grading policies in Appendix E). Standard experiments leveraging peer information consider all individuals in a class section as the reference group and provide descriptive statistics based on the observed number in the group. We adopted this design in Study I. However, this experimental design is subject to one identification issue when exploring the role of the contextual environment: there is no variation in gender composition within a section. Increasing the number of classes/sections could not fully address this issue because doing so would introduce an additional degree of heterogeneity at the section level. Therefore, we explored an alternative design. In Study II, students were randomly assigned to small study groups before working on the individual assignment. In the experiment, students still received descriptive peer information (as in Study I), but the reference group is the study group they were in rather than the entire class. All experimented assignments in this research are individual assignments. Students were required to complete them independently.

## Variables and Measurement

Dependent Variables. We constructed two dependent variables to measure the outcomes of experimental conditions (Table 5). The first dependent variable is related to a student’s procrastination behavior, which is measured by

StartTime, a continuous variable recording the length of time (hours) between two events: the time a student started working on the assignment (indicated by downloading the necessary data file for the assignment) and the time the assignment was due (Cerezo et al. 2017; Rotenstein et al. 2009; You 2016).<sup>2</sup> A smaller value of StartTime indicates that the student started late and had experienced more procrastination, whereas a larger value suggests that the student started early. The second dependent variable is Grade, which is the points a student received from the assign ment. We normalized this grade by the total credits for the assignment such that Grade is a percentage ranging from 0 to 100. Both StartTime and performance Grade are of interest to educators: StartTime captures students’ behavior change (the extent of procrastination) due to the treatments, and such behavior improvement may lead to a higher performance outcome as measured by Grade.

## Study II (Group-Level Peer Information)

Causal estimation of the effect of gender composition requires randomization in gender composition, which is challenging, if not impossible, at the class/section level because students self-select to enroll into a class/section. Randomization at the group-level, however, is possible because instructors often assign students to work on group assignments. In Study II, we explored an alternative design, in which students received descriptive peer information with their randomly assigned study group as the reference group. This experimental design creates random variations in gender composition within a class/section, which helps avoid having to conduct the experiments with a large number of classes, where we run into the identification challenge caused by cross-class heterogeneity.

The detailed experimental procedure works as follows. At the beginning of the semester, students were randomly assigned to a group of four to six members.<sup>3</sup> This randomization strategy generated 39 groups across four sections, with a group male composition in the range between 0% and 100% (see the details in Table B1 of Appendix B). Before participating in the experiment (an individual assignment), students had worked together and completed several group assignments. As shown in Table 3, students were randomly assigned to the control group to receive reminder messages without peer information or the treatment group to receive messages with the descriptive peer information calculated from their study group (i.e., “X% of students in your group have started working on the assignment”). Randomization tests in Table A2 of Appendix A shows that there are no significant differences between the treatment group and the control group with respect to preexperiment factors and individual traits.

Experiment results in Table 7 show that male students in male-majority groups are more responsive to peer information interventions and reduce procrastination (by 12.03 hours), whereas other individuals are not. This finding is consistent with the prediction from Hypothesis H2b. Consistent with previous results in Study I, female students did not show a treatment effect from the interventions. We formally test the H2b by pooling data from Study II and III, which allows us to test the interaction effect with a larger sample size. Empirical results (Table B2 in Appendix B) show that the treatment effect of peer information for male students in a malemajority group is stronger than that for male students not in a male-majority group (19.70, p = 0.06). Hence, H2b is supported.

## Study III (Manipulation of Competition)

## Effect of the Prevalence of Peer Behavior

The effect of peer information interventions may depend on how prevalent certain behavior is observed among peers (Sun et al. 2019).<sup>5</sup> To investigate the potential heterogeneous effects with respect to different percentage X, we created two dummy variables: StartPercent\_Low (1 if start percent < 50% and 0 otherwise) and StartPercent\_High (1 if start percent \$ 50% and 0 otherwise). Empirical results (Table C2 in Appendix C) show that for females, the effect of peer information is not significant (p = 0.36 and p = 0.18 for low and high percentages, respectively), although we observe some trend that disclosing a low percentage X may have a negative effect (not statistically significant, $p > 0 . 1 0 )$ . For males, the effect of peer information interventions is positive and statistically significant (for either low or high percentages), and the effect is stronger when the percentage X is high.

To ensure strictly exogenous prevalence levels, in Study IV we utilized an alternative design, in which we manipulated the prevalence level of peer behavior revealed to students.<sup>6</sup> We randomly assigned students from one single class to a control group to receive reminder messages without peer information or to one of the three treatment groups to receive messages with peer information at different levels (low = 21%, medium = 41%, or high = 61%) (Table C3 in Appendix C).

## Cross-talk with Other Subjects

One common issue in uncontrolled field experiments is the possibility of contamination, in which a subject happens to be exposed to multiple treatment conditions. In our setting, it is likely that such contamination occurs through cross-talk with other students in the class. For instance, a student in the control group happens to know the message intended for students in the treatment group.

To provide additional evidence that contamination is not a major concern, we conducted a post-experiment survey for both Study II and Study III, in which we asked subjects if they discussed the message they received with classmates. Survey results show that the likelihood of cross-talk and receiving different messages was only 6%\~8% (Table F2 in Appendix F). This result suggests that cross-talk does exist, but it does not seem to be a major concern in our setting. As a robustness check, we excluded those subjects who might have discussed their message with friends. The empirical results remain consistent (Table F3 in Appendix F).

## Discussion and Conclusion

## Social Conformity or Social Comparison?

The literature has suggested that peer information interventions influence individual behavior because people may choose to comply with peers (Schultz et al. 2007). However, social conformity alone would predict that females are more responsive to interventions, which contradicts the empirical findings from our study. We demonstrate that such inconsistency is due to the different contexts under which previous studies were conducted. Social conformity is more salient in noncompetitive settings, whereas social comparison can dominate in competitive environments. Prior studies have focused on noncompetitive settings, in which an individual’s performance does not interfere with others’ performance. Therefore, the primary effects of peer information interventions come from social conformity. In our competitive classroom setting, however, students may perceive a certain level of competition with their classmates and providing peer information may induce comparison concerns. Therefore, the primary effects of peer information interventions may come from social comparison, rather than social conformity. In competitive settings, males are more likely to respond to peer information interventions than females. Such differential effects are even more salient in a male-dominated environment. These results are consistent with the theory of gender differences in competitive environments (Gneezy et al. 2003), which predicts a significant increase in performance for males as the competitiveness of the environment increases, but not for females. Males have been found to exhibit significantly more aggressiveness than females (Brooks 1982). Furthermore, males prefer competition more than females. Therefore, males are more likely to opt into a competitive setting induced by the descriptive peer information they received (Gneezy et al. 2003).

To further dig into the competition effects induced by peer information, we pool the findings across multiple studies for more in-depth discussions. The empirical results suggest that peer information motivates behavior change by inducing comparison concerns, which enhances the perceived competitiveness of the environment (Gneezy et al. 2003). Gender differences observed in our experiments can be explained by the differential responses to competition between males and females. The direct support for the competition argument comes from Study III, in which we manipulated the competition conditions. Compared with females, males are more responsive to interventions in ranking based conditions. However, we do not observe such gender differences in nonranking based conditions. Another direct evidence comes from Study IV, in which we manipulated the prevalence of peer behaviors. For males, however, providing peer information helps reduce procrastination even when the prevalence level is low (21%), which cannot be explained by social conformity. Males’ response to low prevalence can be because peer information introduces comparison concerns and competition effects: males want to play ahead of the game to achieve a higher grade than their peers do.

## Theoretical Implications

Prior studies have focused on peer information interventions in noncompetitive environments, in which an individual’s performance does not interfere with others’ performance. We demonstrate that social conformity is more salient in noncompetitive settings, whereas social comparison can dominate in competitive environments. This research complements to the literature by investigating peer information interventions in environments with some degree of competition. In contrast to the prediction from the social conformity theory, our results show that males are actually more likely to respond to peer information interventions. Our results suggest males are more strongly motivated because they are more competitiveoriented (Gneezy et al. 2003). Our research highlights the role of environmental contexts in peer information interventions.

This research provides insights into the mixed results in the literature about peer information interventions (Schultz et al. 2007). Our research demonstrates that peer information interventions are moderated by individual characteristics and contextual variables. Individual differences such as gender and behavior trajectory play a significant role in an individual’s responses to peer information interventions. We also find that peer information interventions motivate behavior change, which in turn leads to a better performance outcome. This finding adds to the literature by establishing the mediation link between digital interventions and performance improvement. Our research, therefore, adds to the broader literature of digital interventions through social influence and peer effects by highlighting the possibility of targeted interventions based on individual characteristics and the contextual environment (Lu et al. 2016).

This research also points to the dark side of peer information interventions: widening the gender gap. The literature has documented the gender and performance gap in competitive settings. Our research suggests that peer information interventions can widen such gender gap because providing peer information enhances the competitiveness of the environment. In competitive environments, females are at a greater disadvantage due to peer information interventions.

## Practical Implications

This research has implications for the design of digital interventions. Prior research has discussed how alternative pedagogical designs such as imposing multiple intermediate deadlines may reduce student procrastination (Ariely and Wertenbroch 2002; Lamwers and Jazwinski 1989). Such interventions, however, are costly to implement because they require significant changes to the current curriculum design or impose extra responsibilities on the teachers. This research evaluates a data-driven approach to reduce student procrastination via digital interventions. Information systems have led to an aggregation of detailed data on individuals’ behavior trajectory and outcomes. Our research demonstrates how to analyze these data to study individual behavior, understand what behavioral patterns are associated with superior or unsatisfactory outcomes, and ultimately, to improve individual performance by targeted interventions.

The findings from this study have implications for the optimal design of data-driven, targeted interventions to promote healthy behaviors and outcomes. Providing peer information may help reduce procrastination for college students, but the effect depends on individual characteristics and the contextual environment. To achieve effective interventions, educators may consider individuals’ gender, behavior trajectory, past performance, and gender composition in the class. For instance, peer information interventions may target males in a male-majority environment because these individuals benefit more from such treatments. The peer information interventions artifact we developed for LMS can automatically extract real-time information from Canvas’ system log to implement targeted interventions.

## Limitations and Future Research

One important issue in peer information interventions is that the prevalence of peer behavior (“X% of students have started…”) depends on the timing of interventions and other factors. It is likely that the percentage X is small at the time of interventions. For males, our research shows that providing peer information is beneficial even when the percentage X is relatively low. However, disclosing a low percentage to females may backfire. To address the potential negative effect of low percentage value, future research may experiment with alternative framing of the message, for example, by hiding the percentage information in the message to females: “Students in your class/group have already started….” Future research may also leverage cross-group tournament, in which instructors disclose information about other groups in the class (“X % of other study groups have started…”). Such a design may cultivate a more cooperative environment within a group but promote competition between groups. It is likely that gender and gender composition may play a role in such settings (Delfgaauw et al. 2013). In addition, it is likely that some students delay on purpose. Future research may investigate whether peer information interventions are beneficial to these students. Finally, we have conducted field experiments on multiple business courses in the classroom setting, but future research can benefit from expanding the scale and scope of the experiments to test the generalizability of our findings to various contexts. For example, we may expand the study to different disciplines and different levels of courses and MOOC platforms. Future research may also look at the effects of peer information in other business settings, such as workplaces.

## Validation of StartTime as a Measure of Procrastination

In this appendix, we provide more support on the validity of using StartTime as a measure of procrastination. Specifically, we followed a procedure used in prior studies and evaluated the correlation between self-reported procrastination tendency and observed behavior (Michinov et al. 2011; Steel et al. 2001).

At the beginning of the semester for Study III, all students received a questionnaire regarding their basic information (e.g., cumulative GPA, grade of the prerequisite course, and degree of interest in the course) and procrastination tendency. A six-item procrastination scale was adopted and adjusted according to our classroom context (Michinov et al. 2011; Steel et al. 2001). Items include “I postpone starting in on things I don’t like to do,” “When I have a deadline, I wait till the last minute,” “I promise myself I’ll do something then drag my feet,” “Whenever I make a plan of action, I follow it,” “Even though I hate myself if I don’t get started, it doesn’t get my going,” “I always finish important jobs with time to spare.” Students respond on a five-point scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). We conducted a principa component analysis on the six items, resulting the first component explaining 48.12% of the total variance, which is comparable to the literature (Michinov et al. 2011). We used the value of the first component (FirstFactor) as a measure of self-reported procrastination tendency.

We calculated the correlation between self-reported procrastination tendency (FirstFactor) and observed StartTime, leading to -0.35, which is statistically significant (p < 0.001), which is comparable to that in the literature (Steel et al. 2001).

We further regressed the self-reported procrastination tendency (FirstFactor) on StartTime and other control variables. The results (Table F1) show that observed behavior (StartTime) is a valid indicator for procrastination.
