# Reconceptualizing knowledge networks for Enterprise Systems implementation: Incorporating domain expertise of knowledge sources and knowledge flow intensity

Sharath Sasidharan ssasidh@bgsu.edu

## ABSTRACT

Research on knowledge acquisition through informal social networks during enterprise system implementation has not accounted for the domain expertise of knowledge sources or the quality of knowledge flows. By using data collected from an enterprise resource planning system implementation, this paper reconceptualizes knowledge networks into subnetworks on the basis of the domain expertise of end users and analyzes knowledge acquisition patterns between subnetworks across workgroups having varying performance outcomes. Expertise-based knowledge patterns and their intensities had significant implications for performance outcomes, reiterating their role in the learning process and emphasizing the need to incorporate them into knowledge networking models.

## KEYWORDS

Enterprise systems; enterprise resource planning; social networks, knowledge acquisition, domain expertise, knowledge exchange

## INTRODUCTION

Enterprise systems integrate data across departments and functional work units through a unified software program structured around an organization-wide database (Ranjan, Jha, & Pal, 2016; Tarhini, Ammar, & Tarhini, 2015). They include enterprise resource planning (ERP), business intelligence, business process management, customer relationship management, sales management, and supply chain management systems. Their benefits are manifold as follows: improved financial management, faster information retrieval, streamlined business operations, value-added customer service, and e-commerce facilitation (Galy & Sauceda, 2014; Huang & Handfield, 2015). However, the large-scale, long-term, fundamental changes to business processes, functionalities, and work culture brought about by enterprise systems can be stressful for end users. The fear of skill obsolescence can generate considerable resistance and hostility toward the system, particularly for those ignorant about the changes it could bring to their work routines. Despite extensive research and practice, its implementation is viewed as risky by most organizations (Chung, Ahmad, & Tang, 2015; Jagoda & Samaranayake, 2017; Kini & Basaviah, 2013).

ERP systems in particular have faced significant implementation challenges. These arise out of combinations of factors, including end user resistance to change, ignorance of system features,

#

and a lack of awareness of underlying business processes (Chakravorty, Dulaney, & Franza, 2016; Chung et al., 2015; Ghosh, 2012; Ranjan et al., 2016). Hence, researchers and practitioners have emphasized the importance of disseminating system-related knowledge during and after implementation, primarily through training, involvement of end users in system implementation, and use of experts and technology champions within organizational departments (Abelein & Paech, 2015; Ahmad & Cuenca, 2013; Bano & Zowghi, 2015; Esteves, 2014; Ranjan et al., 2016). While these approaches represent institutionally mandated mechanisms for knowledge dissemination, research has also examined the role of informal social networks (SNs) in Santhanam, Brass, & Sambamurthy, 2012; Sasidharan, Santhanam, & Brass, 2017; Sykes, Venkatesh, & Gosain, 2009; Sykes, Venkatesh, & Johnson, 2014; Sykes, 2015). While executing their work responsibilities, employees may take advantage of their informal SNs to seek ERP system and business process-related knowledge from other employees to resolve their doubts and properly integrate the system into their work practices. Knowledge SNs do not rely on formal relationships reflected in job titles or organizational structure; rather, they are built around informal interactions between employees, forming a real-time, on-the-job, and context-relevant knowledge source for end users. Such a knowledge-sharing network can provide a supportive environment for integrating the system into work practices, especially for the use of more advanced and complex system features (Phelps, Heidl, & Wadhwa, 2012).

Existing research on ERP system implementation does not address the domain expertise of knowledge sources or the quality of resulting knowledge flows (Freeze et al., 2012; Sasidharan et al., 2012; Sasidharan et al., 2017; Sykes et al., 2009; Sykes et al., 2014; Sykes, 2015). In addition, knowledge interaction ties are often treated as binary: a knowledge tie is either present or not, thereby ignoring the intensity or frequency of knowledge interactions (Freeze et al., 2012; Sasidharan et al., 2012; Sasidharan et al., 2017). This results in end users and their knowledge flows within a network being treated as like and interchangeable. In general, these studies have found a positive association between knowledge ties and performance outcomes. However, the domain expertise of knowledge sources and the intensity of their knowledge flows may influence learning and impact performance outcomes (Allee, 2003; North & Kumta, 2014; Vorakulpipat & Rezgui, 2008). Knowledge sourced from those with domain expertise will likely be of higher quality than that from those lacking such expertise. In addition, the extent of knowledge acquisition will depend on the intensity or frequency of knowledge interactions. It is possible that high-intensity knowledge flows sourced from those with domain expertise have a greater impact on performance outcomes than low-intensity knowledge flows sourced from those lacking such expertise. In fact, the latter may even lead to the spread of misinformation, thus negatively impacting performance outcomes. Hence, to fully understand the knowledge dynamics at work within a network, it is imperative that research addresses both the domain expertise of knowledge sources and the intensity of end-user knowledge interactions.

The purpose of this study is to address these gaps in existing research by accounting for both the domain expertise of knowledge sources and the intensity of knowledge interactions. As opposed to the current practice of treating a SN as one cohesive entity with like and interchangeable end users, this study reconceptualizes the network as being composed of subnetworks of end users with varying levels of domain expertise. It analyzes the differences in knowledge-acquisition patterns between these subnetworks across workgroups having differing levels of implementation success while incorporating the intensity of knowledge interactions. Knowledge

flows regarding both system usage and business processes are examined. Results indicate that performance outcomes were influenced by the domain expertise of knowledge sources and the intensity of end user knowledge interactions. Knowledge sourced from those with domain expertise was associated with enhanced performance outcomes, especially when those knowledge sources had extensive knowledge interaction ties among themselves. On the other hand, knowledge sourced from those lacking such expertise was found to negatively impact performance outcomes. In addition, the densities of subnetworks dropped considerably when the intensity or frequency of knowledge ties was considered than when they were treated as binary, indicating that infrequent knowledge ties had been treated on par with more intense and frequent knowledge interactions. There were also differences in knowledge acquisition patterns between system usage and business process networks.

These results can have serious implications for both theory and practice. From a theoretical perspective, this study emphasizes the need for including both the domain expertise of knowledge sources and the intensity of knowledge flows while conducting network analysis. In this introductory study, it is not possible to definitively conclude that this is a better approach; however, the results are consistent with management research that theorizes knowledge exchanges among experts and the presence of high-quality knowledge flows as predictive of new knowledge creation and enhanced performance outcomes (Allee, 2003; Möller & Svahn, 2006; North & Kumta, 2014; Vorakulpipat & Rezgui, 2008). An extension of these findings to practice would open new techniques for creating co-location based “designer” networks that could introduce knowledge patterns identified as being predictive of enhanced performance outcomes. Such strategies could include workspace colocation (Song, Berends, van der Bij, & Weggeman, 2007) that would discourage those lacking domain expertise from acting as knowledge sources and help in promoting knowledge ties to those possessing domain expertise.

This paper first discusses the role of training, end-user involvement in system design, and SNs in knowledge dissemination during ERP system implementation. In addition, it assesses the impact of domain expertise on performance outcomes. It then details the knowledge dissemination activities for an ERP system implementation at a higher-education institution in the United States. The reconceptualization of both system and business process knowledge networks for ERP implementation into subnetworks based on domain expertise of knowledge sources is discussed. Data collection and analysis procedures are outlined and results presented, followed by a discussion of study findings and their implications for theory and practice. Future research directions are also presented.

## KNOWLEDGE DISSEMINATION FOR ERP SYSTEM IMPLEMENTATION

ERP systems reconfigure existing business operations to promote a cross-functional, crossdepartmental operating paradigm within an organization. This can represent a significant departure from an existing “legacy” system that is likely structured as discrete subsystems, each of which caters to the functional and business requirements of individual departments and operates independently of other subsystems. Hence, end users must unlearn what they already know, master the complexities of the newly introduced system, and gain an understanding of how their new work routines impact others within and outside their department, as well as across the organization (Sasidharan et al., 2012; Sasidharan et al., 2017; Shao, Feng, & Hu, 2017). Under such circumstances, training and education of end users become integral to the implementation process (Ahmad & Cuenca, 2013; Chou, Chang, Lin, & Chou, 2014; Esteves,

#

2014). While training can provide the foundation for system-related knowledge dissemination, it can be enhanced through end-user involvement in system design (Abelein & Paech, 2015; Bano & Zowghi, 2015). Such involvement can be facilitated by including organizational personnel in the implementation team. Organizational personnel should be knowledgeable about internal business processes and functions, cognizant of industry best practices as operationalized by the ERP system, and able to translate business requirements to technology artifacts. Their knowledge of internal business requirements assists with the implementation. This approach has added benefits, in that it can promote “buy-in” of the system among organizational personnel, which in al., 2015). In addition, when using the system, end users often turn to their informal SNs for knowledge support (Sasidharan et. al., 2012; Sasidharan et. al., 2017; Sykes et. al., 2009; Sykes et. al., 2014). The context-relevant knowledge sourced from SNs can enable end users to optimize their use of system features, solve technical issues, and effectively address business problems. The following section discusses the processes involved in knowledge acquisition through SNs.

## Knowledge Acquisition through Social Networks

SN theory asserts that understanding the social context of informal person-to-person interactions within a group can explain group outcomes (Borgatti & Cross, 2003; Brass, 2011). Research on SNs in the context of ERP system implementation has found that knowledge acquisition through informal contacts facilitates a better understanding of system functionalities, influences learning outcomes, and impacts job performance (Freeze et. al., 2012; Sasidharan et. al., 2012; Sasidharan et. al., 2017; Sykes et. al., 2009; Sykes et. al., 2014). Such networks have also been categorized into workflow and software advice networks: the former facilitating acquisition of business process knowledge and the latter facilitating acquisition of system knowledge (Sykes et. al., 2014). While SNs have traditionally considered person-to-person interactions, the term has subsequently expanded to include organizational support structures such as online support and the help desk (Sasidharan et al., 2017; Sykes, 2015).

The density of knowledge ties between end users in a departmental workgroup will be indicative of the speed of knowledge dissemination across its SN. Similarly, for individual end users in the workgroup, centrality will reflect the extent of their embeddedness in the network (Brass, 1984; Hanneman & Riddle, 2005; Freeze et al., 2012; Sasidharan et al., 2012; Sasidharan et al., 2017; Sykes et al., 2009; Sykes et al., 2014). Broadly, centrality refers to the number of ties that an end user has with other end users in the network. These ties can provide structurally advantageous positions that support enhanced learning and improved knowledge acquisition. Such structural advantages occur because of multiple reasons. For example, a larger number of direct knowledge ties to other end users can translate into greater opportunities for one-to-one knowledge acquisition. Further, if those direct end user contacts in turn have extensive knowledge ties with others, they provide increasing opportunities for indirect knowledge acquisition. In addition, those end users embedded in the knowledge path between other end users can benefit from their unique position as a knowledge conduit, acquiring knowledge in the process of transferring it between others. For these reasons, centrality positions within SNs have been associated with influence and power (Borgatti & Cross, 2003; Brass, 1984, 2011).

Network centrality can be a legitimate indicator of knowledge-acquisition capability. However, existing research implicitly assumes that all end users within a network possess the same level of

#

domain expertise (Freeze et al., 2012; Sasidharan et al., 2012; Sasidharan et al., 2017; Sykes et al., 2009; Sykes et al., 2014; Sykes, 2015). While this might be true in the case of standardized general-purpose software for which self-study literature and online support are widely and easily available (such as Excel, Access, and SPSS), it may not be the case with more complex ERP systems, especially those customized to meet more specific and contextual business requirements. As stressed earlier, ERP systems impose a cross-functional, cross-departmental operating paradigm on end users, exposing them to new business processes and system features radically different from their earlier technology environment. End users are expected to learn and adapt to the newly introduced system. However, some end users might acquire higher levels of domain expertise through their involvement in the development and implementation of the new system, by undergoing intensive training or having previously worked on similar technology. Hence, the network can include end users with varying levels of domain expertise, ranging from the novice to the expert. Obviously, knowledge acquired by end users from experts within the network can be expected to have a greater impact on performance outcomes than that acquired from novices. Even worse, knowledge acquired from novices could be incomplete and inaccurate, negatively impacting performance outcomes (Chi, 2006; Hoffman, 1998). Hence, it is imperative that research incorporates the domain expertise of end users while analyzing knowledge-based SNs.

When binary ties (i.e., a knowledge tie is either present or not) are used to determine centrality (Freeze et al., 2012; Sasidharan et al., 2012; Sasidharan et al., 2017), they reflect the breadth of knowledge acquisition but not its intensity or frequency. Strong high intensity ties have been found to increase the speed and ease of learning, promote bonding, trust, and reciprocity, reduce uncertainty, and provide comfort in a hostile environment. On the other hand, weak low-intensity ties have been found to slow the pace of learning and hinder the acquisition of newer concepts, thoughts, and ideas (Friedkin 1982; Granovetter, 1973, 1985; Lin, Ensel, & Vaughn, 1981). A new ERP implementation can create a hostile environment, especially for end users faced with steep learning curves and apprehensive of the impact of the new system on their work routines. Under these circumstances, the trust, reciprocity, and bonding provided by strong ties can reduce uncertainty and provide an environment more conducive to learning than one facilitated by weak ties. In addition, using binary ties may result in an end user having infrequent knowledge interactions with a larger number of users being considered more central to the network than an end user having very frequent knowledge interactions with a smaller group. The situation can be further complicated if this smaller group consists of end users with domain expertise. To address these potential confounds, this study incorporates both the domain expertise of knowledge sources and the intensity of knowledge ties to capture a broader and more precise picture of knowledge interactions between end users in the network.

## DOMAIN EXPERTISE OF KNOWLEDGE SOURCES

Experts have several distinguishing features. Apart from an accurate, deep, and complex conceptualization of problems, they can incorporate domain-specific constraints into their thought processes (Chi, Feltovich, & Glaser, 1981; Voss, Greene, Post, & Penner, 1983). In addition, they are capable of objectively analyzing their problem conceptualizations, detecting errors, and correcting them (Chi, Glaser, & Rees, 1982). Apart from being able to develop effective strategies for problem solving, they expend minimal cognitive effort in crafting optimal solutions for problem situations (Alexander, 2003; Lemaire & Siegler, 1995). However, these

#

capabilities tend to be confined within their respective operational domains. The limited research done in the context of enterprise systems indicates that experts tend to depend more on data provided by the system to make decisions, whereas novices communicate with their colleagues to complete their tasks (Leger, Riedl, & vom Brocke, 2014).

Expertise has been conceptualized as an absolute entity (as in the study of outstanding performers in their respective domains) and as a relative entity (by comparing their proficiency levels with nonexperts) (Chi, 2006; Hoffman, 1998). The latter conceptualization assumes that expertise is a function of domain knowledge, and nonexperts can become experts by acquiring relevant knowledge. It also assumes that experts and nonexperts have the same analytical and reasoning capabilities. Extending this approach, Hoffman (1998) views expertise as a continuum, ranging from the naïve (no domain knowledge), to the novice (minimal domain knowledge), to the initiate (introductory domain knowledge), to the apprentice (beyond introductory domain knowledge), to the journeyman (basic competence), to the expert (competent, experience with subdomains), and finally to the master (sets domain standards, expertise with subdomains).

Drawing on Hoffman’s approach, this paper reconceptualizes knowledge networks from an ERP system implemented at a higher-education institution in the United States into subnetworks of novices, apprentices, and experts, each having differing levels of domain knowledge proficiency. The “legacy” technology infrastructure of this institution consisted of several independent systems, each customized for their respective department. Their technical features and operating platforms differed to the extent that they lent themselves to minimal cross-organizational integration. The next section details the implementation processes and associated knowledge dissemination activities meant to facilitate the integration of the new ERP system into end-user work routines.

## RESEARCH METHODOLOGY

## ERP System Implementation and Knowledge Support Planning

Dating back to the 1980s, the technology infrastructure of the institution had obsolete operational processes and no active vendor support. To make matters worse, several paper-based, manual processes, with few or no established workflows, existed across the institution. Together, they exacerbated the integration problem. Thus, cross-functional and cross-organizational information could not be leveraged for institutional planning and long-term strategic decision-making, contributing to the institution’s fast-eroding competitiveness in the higher-education sector. The governing body for the institution recommended replacing the existing legacy systems with a single, integrated ERP system. The proposed system was the Higher Education and Research enterprise solutions portfolio from Systemanalyse und Programmentwicklung (SAP) with four modules dealing with financials (FI), materials, campus, and human resource management. Implementation partners were selected on the basis of their ability to deploy qualified consultants with experience in SAP higher-education implementation, availability for the full term of the implementation, and willingness to transfer technical knowledge to institutional personnel. The project team consisted of external consultants provided by the implementation partners, as well as technical and nontechnical personnel internal to the institution. A director led the team, supported by functional and technical co-leads.

## Execution

The execution of the four major modules was staggered, with FI being the first, followed by human resource management, campus management, and finally, materials management. The FI module was the most complex and had the largest end user base within the campus community, spanning senior and middle management, administrative assistants, secretaries, and staff associates, from both academic and administrative units. After baseline configuring, unit testing, debugging, installation, integration testing, and quality assurance, online access to the system was enabled, and the campus community started transitioning from the legacy system to the new SAP FI module.

The execution phase emphasized the training and education of end users of the FI system. All end users had to undergo role-based training sessions, to bring them to a knowledge level sufficient to pass a competency test, required to receive access to the system. They could take additional and more advanced training sessions if they so desired. The training curriculum was based on workgroups, a workgroup being a set of employees responsible for a distinct business operation. Employees within a workgroup had specific roles, each role encompassing welldefined tasks and functions. Workgroup employees were collectively responsible for their business operation and trained on their primary roles and closely related operational roles. Training goals included building two distinct knowledge bases in end users: use of system features and a conceptual understanding of the underlying business processes, both considered essential for optimal use of the system. Senior managerial personnel within workgroups were also trained in cross-organizational business processes and workflows.

Personnel internal to the institution were co-opted onto the project team with the intent of developing their allegiance to the system. Such institutional “buy-in” was expected to foster positive perceptions of the system. Moreover, such personnel could play the role of technology champions and domain experts upon return to their workgroups. Both the director and functional co-leads of the project team were senior faculty members in the institution. Technical personnel were recruited from the IT department, while nontechnical personnel were selected from departmental workgroups based on recommendations from supervisors. The project team also included senior personnel who had a cross-organizational perspective on business processes, workflow, information, and decision-making requirements. All institutional personnel on the project team received advanced training regarding FI system features and associated business processes. The project team was physically located in a rented building outside of campus, custom-furnished to suit the implementation effort. The implementation partners provided the external consultants on the project team. They led the implementation effort by collaborating with institutional personnel in mapping and calibrating existing business processes to match SAP system requirements. These consultants were also tasked with transferring knowledge to institutional personnel.

The release (i.e., go live) of the four major modules was staggered, with FI being the first, followed by human resource management, campus management, and finally, materials management. The FI module was the most complex and had the largest end user base within the campus community, spanning senior and middle management, administrative assistants, secretaries, and staff associates, from both academic and administrative units. ## Postexecution

The postexecution phase was a difficult one for the campus community as it struggled to come to terms with the FI system. The technical and nontechnical personnel, internal to the institution and part of the project team, returned to their workgroups, encouraged to perform the role of experts and technology champions there. Reflective of informal knowledge networking, there was frequent querying among end users regarding system features, informal gatherings around system terminals for discussion of operational routines, and impromptu, hands-on demonstrations of system use led by those considered knowledgeable about the system. While some end users transitioned to the FI system without much difficulty, others experienced problems attuning themselves to the new system and struggled with the new work paradigm. In some cases, these problems led to early retirements and resignations. Anecdotal evidence indicated dissatisfaction with the new system-driven business processes; work procedures perceived as different, cumbersome, and time consuming; system interfaces seen as unintuitive and not user friendly; and data flows perceived as not matching work requirements. These reactions were attributed in part to insufficient knowledge regarding proper use of system features and lack of understanding of underlying business processes. While end users had to pass a competency test for system access, it was more a sufficiency test for basic use of the system than a test for proper use of advanced system features.

## Data Collection

The specialized human resource management, campus management, and materials management modules impacted only a limited number of users across campus. However, the general-purpose FI module, being cross-organizational in scope, impacted almost every workgroup on campus and had the largest number of end users within the campus community. Because of these reasons, data for this study were collected from workgroups where the FI module had been implemented. These workgroups had been subject to the same implementation processes and training opportunities. However, workgroup supervisors indicated widely differing perceptions of system impact on workgroup performance. They rated the extent to which the FI system impacted their workgroup performance along seven aspects: ability to accomplish more work, generate new ideas, meet client needs, save time, improve productivity, facilitate innovation, and improve client satisfaction (Doll & Torkzadeh, 1998) (see Appendix for supervisor questionnaire). The mean of their responses on a Likert scale of 1–7 facilitated the categorization of workgroups into three categories: top performing (Likert score of 6 and above), medium performing (Likert score between 3 and 6), and low performing (Likert score of 2 and below). One workgroup selected at random from each of the three categories was used for subsequent SN analysis. With the intent of allocating postimplementation support resources to workgroups that were having performance issues with the new system, the implementation team had analyzed workgroup feedback regarding the new ERP-driven work processes and system features. This gave an indication of the extent to which workgroups were able to integrate the new system into their work practices. Senior members of the implementation team corroborated the study categorizations by benchmarking workgroup performance parameters against comparable implementations and using performance data sourced from help desk logs and workgroup feedback. These workgroups were responsible for the administration, accounting, and execution of campus-wide infrastructure projects; management, operation, and maintenance of campuswide physical assets; and the administration and management of campus-wide benefits and compensation policies.

## Social Network Analysis

In formal terms, an individual within a SN is referred to as an actor (depicted by a square node in Figure 1), and knowledge exchanges between actors are referred to as ties (depicted by connecting lines in Figure 1). SN analysis uses graph theory to generate metrics for both individual actors and the network in which they are embedded. The direct network in which an actor is anchored is called the ego network (in the figure, the ego network of A will be composed of B, D, and F). Larger ego networks are associated with power and influence, as such individuals control valued knowledge resources (Brass, 1984; Phelps et al., 2012). The central positions occupied by such advantaged individuals are reflected in the degree centrality measure, which is a count of the number of direct knowledge ties an actor has with other actors in the network. This can further be delineated into out-degree and in-degree centralities, depending on the direction of knowledge ties (i.e., outgoing or incoming). For example, in the figure, A has a degree centrality of 3, which can further be delineated into an out-degree centrality (ODC) of 3 (i.e., outgoing knowledge ties to B, D, and F) and an in-degree centrality of 2 (i.e., incoming knowledge ties from B and D). The overall density of knowledge ties across all end users reflects the speed of knowledge dissemination through the network (Brass, 1984, 2011; Hanneman & Riddle, 2005).

## Mapping Workgroup Social Networks

Prior research on SNs in the context of ERP system implementation identifies two distinct types of knowledge flows: one pertaining to system features and their usage and the other relating to underlying business processes (Sykes et. al., 2014). Adopting this approach, knowledgeacquisition patterns for both system usage and business processes for the three selected workgroups were mapped on the basis of data collected from end users through a questionnaire. The questionnaire listed the names of all FI users within that workgroup and asked each user to select the names of those from whom they had acquired knowledge related to system usage and/or business processes. This facilitated the development of both the system usage and business process knowledge networks. In addition, they were also asked to provide the frequency of their knowledge exchanges (referred to as tie strength), captured by using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “Seldom” to “Often.” This enabled the development of “valued” knowledge ties to reflect the intensity of knowledge exchanges (see Appendix for questionnaire).

Network matrices were developed for knowledge acquisition related to system usage and business processes by pairing all combinations of end users within the three workgroups on the basis of tie strength. The resulting knowledge social networks were mapped using NetDraw (Borgatti, Everett, & Freeman, 2002). Figures 2 and 3 depict the network diagram for the topperforming and low-performing workgroups, respectively. The squares represent end user nodes, lines represent knowledge ties, and line thickness indicates tie strength.

## Designation of Expertise Levels of End Users

Research on ERP systems in a classroom environment differentiated experts from novices based on prior experience with the software (Leger et al., 2014). However, such a categorization is not

#

suited for this study, as the FI module was wholly new to the institution and included system features and industry best practices that were unavailable in the legacy system. Knowledge dissemination took place through end users on the project team, who, upon return to their home workgroups, functioned as technology champions and domain knowledge experts to others. These end users had worked with the external consultants on the project team and possessed indepth knowledge of system features and business process functionalities. Because of their active involvement in system implementation, they were able to acquire specialized, in-depth domain and subdomain knowledge and were categorized as experts (Hoffman, 1998). The questionnaire had asked end users to rate the extent of their involvement in system development on a 1 (“very little involvement”) to 5 (“deeply involved”) Likert scale; those with a response of 4 or 5 were designated as experts (indicated by “E”s in Figures 2 and 3).

Knowledge regarding the FI system was also disseminated through formal training of end users. They had to undergo introductory role-based training sessions and pass a basic competency test that would enable them to access the system and perform their basic work routines. However, they also had the option of taking additional training sessions to understand the more complex and advanced features of the system. The latter group, possessing domain knowledge well beyond the introductory level, was categorized as intermediates<sup>1</sup> (Tabatabai & Shore, 2005). Other end users, exposed only to the introductory role-based training sessions and possessing minimal domain knowledge, were categorized as novices (Hoffman, 1998). The questionnaire asked end users the number of training sessions they had attended. Those who responded with five or more were categorized as intermediates (indicated by “I”s in Figures 2 and 3) and those with less than five were categorized as novices<sup>2</sup> (indicated by “N”s in Figures 2 and 3).

## Data Analysis and Results

A factor analysis questionnaire items using principal component analysis with varimax rotation resulted in the extraction of a single factor corresponding to supervisor’s perception of workgroup performance. It had a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.94 (see Appendix). At the workgroup (i.e., network) level, the densities of the top-, medium-, and low-performing workgroups were computed by summing the tie strengths of end users (i.e., frequency of knowledge acquisition by end users) and dividing it by its maximum possible value (by including all knowledge acquisition ties at their maximum frequency). This approach captured the speed and intensity of knowledge dissemination through the three knowledge networks, with the top-, medium-, and lowperforming workgroups registering densities of 39%, 35%, and 32%, respectively. If knowledge ties were to be treated as binary (Freeze et al., 2012; Sasidharan et al., 2012; Sasidharan et al., 2017), the densities of the top-, medium-, and low-performing workgroups would be 66%, 63%, and 56%, respectively.

At the end user (i.e., actor) level, outgoing knowledge ties represent an end user acquiring knowledge from other end users in his or her workgroup; hence, the number of such ties would reflect the breadth of knowledge acquisition by that end user. In addition, the tie strength (i.e., frequency of knowledge exchanges) would reflect the intensity of such knowledge acquisition.

Both the breadth and intensity of knowledge acquisition through network ties were captured by using the normalized valued ODC measure, defined as the sum of tie strengths of all outgoing ties, divided by its maximum possible value (computed by including all possible ties). On the basis of expertise level of end users, each of the networks corresponding to the randomly selected workgroups (i.e., top-, medium-, and low-performing) was grouped into subnetworks of experts, intermediates, and novices. The top-, medium-, and low-performing workgroups had 6, 7, and 7 experts, respectively; the corresponding numbers for intermediates being 8, 7, and 8 and for novices being 11, 12, and 13. $\mathrm { O D C } _ { \mathrm { E I } } { \cdot }$ ties of experts acquiring knowledge from intermediates

ODC<sub>EN</sub>: ties of experts acquiring knowledge from novices

ODC<sub>II</sub>: ties of intermediates acquiring knowledge from other intermediates

These ODC values would provide a comprehensive picture of knowledge acquisition patterns between end users having the same and differing levels of expertise within a workgroup. For example, a novice end user with the maximum ODC<sub>NN</sub> value of 1 (i.e., 100%) would indicate knowledge acquisition from all other novice end users in that workgroup at the highest level of intensity. The same end user with $\mathrm { O D C } _ { \mathrm { N E } }$ and $\mathrm { O D C } _ { \mathrm { N I } }$ values of 0 would indicate his or her isolation from expert and intermediate end users in the workgroup. In other words, that end user is acquiring knowledge exclusively from other novice end users. Existing research does not consider both the expertise level of knowledge sources and the intensity of knowledge ties. This could result in two end users having identical values for network centralities, thus reflecting similar levels of knowledge acquisition. However, the first user could be acquiring knowledge primarily from experts, and the other could be acquiring knowledge from novices. As knowledge acquired from experts is of higher quality than that acquired from novices (Chi, 2006; Hoffman, 1998), it could be argued that knowledge acquired by the first user would have greater impact on performance outcomes. The approach adopted by this paper enables a closer look at the expertise level of knowledge sources (and indirectly, the quality of knowledge flows) and facilitates a deeper understanding of knowledge-acquisition patterns between end users of differing expertise levels (i.e., experts, intermediates, and novices) across workgroups having differing performance outcomes (i.e., top, medium, and low).

## DISCUSSION

Prior research indicates that central actors within a SN are positioned to benefit from their extensive knowledge ties to others in the network (Brass, 1984, 2011; Hanneman & Riddle, 2005). In the context of ERP system implementation, knowledge acquisition through informal knowledge ties can provide the support needed for transition from existing legacy systems to the far more complex, cross-functional, cross-organizational ERP systems. Such networks provide the real-time, hands-on, context-relevant, on-the-job support that can translate to enhanced workgroup performance. On the other hand, fragmented networks with isolated end users bereft of these knowledge flows may struggle to leverage system functionalities to their full potential (Sasidharan, 2015; Sykes et al., 2009; Sykes et al., 2014). The results of this study broadly support and extend these contentions. However, it also emphasizes the need for a closer look at both the pattern of knowledge acquisition among end users with varying levels of domain expertise and the intensity of such exchanges. Extant research on knowledge networks in the context of ERP system implementation implicitly assigns the same level of domain expertise to all knowledge sources, thus leading to all end users and knowledge flows being treated as equal and interchangeable, irrespective of the actual domain expertise of end users and the actual quality of knowledge flows (Freeze et al., 2012; Sasidharan et al., 2012; Sasidharan et al., 2017; Sykes et al., 2009; Sykes et al., 2014; Sykes, 2015). In addition, treating knowledge interaction ties as binary (Freeze et al., 2012; Sasidharan et al., 2012; Sasidharan et al., 2017) fails to account for the intensity or frequency of knowledge acquisition.

The top-performing workgroup exhibited several interesting characteristics, including extensive knowledge acquisition by experts from experts and by novices from both experts and intermediates in both the system usage and business process networks. As experts are capable of accurate conceptualization of complex problems and are proficient in developing effective strategies for problem solving (Chi, 2006; Hoffman, 1998), intense knowledge exchanges between themselves would have helped enrich and reinforce their collective knowledge base. Novice end users in the workgroup while acquiring knowledge from experts tapped into this enriched knowledge base, thus facilitating the dissemination of authentic domain knowledge throughout the workgroup.

The low-performing workgroup was characterized by minimal knowledge acquisition by experts from other experts. Hence, their collective knowledge base stagnated. In addition, novices were hesitant to reach out to intermediates and experts in both the system usage and business process networks. For those novice end users who did reach out to experts for help, the quality of knowledge acquired would likely be lower than that in the top-performing workgroup. The other interesting aspect was that the low-performing workgroup had extensive knowledge acquisition by novices from novices for the system usage network. Compared with the top-performing workgroup, where novices had extensive knowledge acquisition from experts, here the experts seem to have been ignored in favor of other novices, thus leading to the dissemination of possibly unreliable domain knowledge negatively impacting workgroup outcomes. The medium performing workgroup was positioned between the top- and low-performing workgroups, with moderate knowledge acquisition by experts from other experts and by novices from both intermediates and experts.

#

Interestingly, knowledge acquisition by novices from novices in the system usage network was greater than the values for the business process network. This could be due to differences in the knowledge being acquired. Usage of system features tends to be factual know-how knowledge (for example, the sequence of menu clicks needed to generate a billing statement), whereas the underlying business processes will represent applied know-why knowledge (for example, understanding how each menu click contributes to the underlying billing process, the data needed as input and the data generated as output for the process, and its linkages with other processes). The more complex and theoretical nature of know-why business processes might not have lent itself to easy transfer through informal knowledge ties. The other notable pattern was the fragmented networks and relative isolation of intermediates in both system usage and business process networks. They were disinclined to approach other intermediates and experts for knowledge acquisition in all three workgroups. Hence, their collective knowledge base would have stagnated. This in turn would have negatively impacted the quality of knowledge flows acquired by novices from intermediates.

When the intensity of knowledge ties is taken into consideration, the densities of the three work groups had a substantial decrease in their computed values over when those ties are treated as binary (i.e., either a tie is present or it is not present)<sup>3</sup>. This would indicate the presence of numerous “casual” knowledge ties between end users. If the only criterion for recognizing a knowledge tie is whether it exists or not, occasional and infrequent knowledge exchanges get treated on par with more intense and frequent knowledge interactions. This can paint a misleading picture of the speed of knowledge dissemination through the network as well as the knowledge acquisition potential of end users. By using this approach, a workgroup with predominantly infrequent knowledge interactions may be viewed as more conducive to knowledge dissemination than one with fewer but deeper and more intense knowledge ties. The situation is further confounded if the domain expertise of knowledge sources is considered, especially if those deeper and more intense knowledge ties reflect knowledge exchanges between experts and knowledge acquisition by novices from experts. Hence, it is imperative that ERP research using SNs addresses both the domain expertise of knowledge sources and the intensities of knowledge ties to gain a more complete understanding of the knowledge subtleties at work within a network.

## LIMITATIONS OF THE STUDY

The study was conducted in a higher-education institution, which may not be truly representative of a business setting; hence, caution needs to be exerted while extending these results to other contexts. However, the tasks associated with the FI module examined in this study are to a large extent similar across business settings (such as in the generation of a pay slip or processing of supplier invoices). The knowledge required for performing these tasks using the ERP system is largely the same irrespective of the business context; hence, the results are to a certain extent generalizable to other business settings. As the ERP system was mandated by the institution and end users had no option other than to use it, the results may not be generalizable to a setting that provided for voluntary use of technology. Only informal knowledge acquisition among end users is considered; some end users could have reached out to the help desk and online technical documentation for clarifications, and these are not addressed (Sasidharan et al., 2017; Sykes,

#

2015). The categorization of workgroups was based on supervisor’s perceptions of performance outcomes, and given the prestige associated with the implementation, some supervisors could have been biased in favor of their workgroup. However, the implementation team had analyzed the performance of workgroups by using several measures, including reviewing workgroup feedback and benchmarking operational parameters against comparable implementations. A review by senior members of the implementation team concurred with the performance categorizations attributed to the three workgroups used in the analysis.

The grouping of end users into novices, intermediates, and experts was specific to the implementation context. While user involvement in the design and implementation of the system and extent of training were the basis for determining domain proficiency, such an approach need not always be reflective of expertise, which may depend on individual learning abilities and absorptive capabilities. It is possible that an end user with extensive training and hence designated as an intermediate, by virtue of superior learning skills and higher absorptive capacity has expertise on par or greater than an end user designated as an expert. Hence, a better approach might be through assessing the system-related cognitive mental map of users and the extent of its match with the actual system. The study analyzes the knowledge patterns and intensity of knowledge exchanges between three workgroups having different levels of performance outcomes. This does not necessarily prove causality, in that these knowledge factors were solely responsible for differing performance outcomes. However, it should be noted that all three workgroups belonged to the same organization, were subject to the same implementation processes, and were randomly selected from their respective performance categories. Hence, there would be an inherent control on alternate explanatory variables that could have otherwise impacted workgroup performance outcomes.

## IMPLICATIONS FOR THEORY

The current theoretical perspective that denser networks and higher centralities help facilitate knowledge exchanges implicitly assigns the same level of domain expertise to all end users — in other words, end users and their knowledge ties are like and interchangeable. While embeddedness in the knowledge network may promote knowledge acquisition, the domain expertise of knowledge sources can influence the quality of acquired knowledge, which in turn can influence learning and impact performance outcomes (Allee, 2003; North & Kumta, 2014; Vorakulpipat & Rezgui, 2008). If knowledge sources lack domain expertise, it may lead to flawed knowledge flows that could result in the spread of misinformation, leading to underutilization and misuse of the system. This study assigned expertise levels to end users and delineated them into subnetworks of experts, intermediates, and novices. It then examined the pattern of knowledge interactions between subnetworks across workgroups with differing performance outcomes. Results indicate that knowledge acquisition by experts from experts and by novices from both experts and intermediates was associated with enhanced performance outcomes in both the system usage and business process networks.

The relevance of tie intensities in impacting learning and knowledge acquisition has been well documented in the literature. Strong high intensity ties have been found to support knowledge acquisition particularly in hostile and uncertain environments (Friedkin, 1982; Granovetter, 1973, 1985; Lin, Ensel & Vaughn, 1981; Reagans & McEvily, 2003). The learning burden imposed on end users and work-related complexities associated with a newly introduced ERP

#

system can lead to a hostile and uncertain implementation environment. The presence of strong high intensity ties can help alleviate the problems faced by end users and promote an atmosphere conducive to learning and knowledge acquisition. Despite these findings, network research on ERP implementation often does not address tie strength and treats knowledge ties as binary (Freeze et al., 2012; Sasidharan et al., 2012; Sasidharan et al., 2017). This study accounted for tie strength and found the actual densities of subnetworks to be considerably higher for the binary approach, indicating that occasional and infrequent knowledge ties got treated on par with more intense and frequent knowledge interactions. Taken in conjunction with the domain expertise of knowledge sources, this could lead to end users having infrequent knowledge ties with sources lacking domain expertise being treated on par with those having intense, frequent knowledge ties with sources possessing domain expertise. Apart from having serious theoretical implications for learning and knowledge acquisition, such anomalies can lead to faulty analysis and misleading conclusions.

Admittedly, in this introductory study, there is no definitive evidence to suggest that incorporating both domain expertise of knowledge sources and knowledge tie intensities is preferable to the “one-size-fits-all” approach of treating all end users and their knowledge flows as like and interchangeable. However, it could be argued that the proposed approach facilitates a more nuanced understanding of the expertise-driven knowledge patterns at work within the network and their differential impact on performance outcomes. More importantly, it acknowledges and reiterates the well-established role of knowledge quality in furthering performance outcomes and being a source of competitive advantage (Allee, 2003; North & Kumta, 2014; Vorakulpipat & Rezgui, 2008).

## IMPLICATIONS FOR PRACTICE

Assigning expertise levels to end users and creating subnetworks based on those expertise levels have important implications for practice. Reflecting the current research approach of associating higher centralities with enhanced performance outcomes, organizations map their knowledge networks to identify less central, isolated users to provide them with additional training and knowledge support. By assigning expertise levels, such support can be prioritized and targeted at novice and intermediate end users rather than at experts. This approach can be further refined by considering the intensity of ties possessed by such users as reflected in the frequency of their interactions. If binary ties were used, end users having the same count of knowledge ties would be treated as being similarly positioned for knowledge acquisition. However, it could be the case that a novice end user has only occasional interactions and another has frequent, intense interactions. Incorporating the intensity of knowledge interactions can tease out this difference and facilitate increased allocation of knowledge support to the former. This approach could be refined further by factoring in the expertise level of knowledge sources and have additional support provided to novice and intermediate end users acquiring knowledge primarily from other novices and intermediates rather than experts.

The delineation of the knowledge networks into subnetworks facilitates a clear picture of knowledge acquisition patterns within and between subnetworks. Organizations must encourage and sustain knowledge interactions between experts and knowledge acquisition by novices from experts. One solution would be for organizations to purposefully modify existing networks to generate interaction patterns conducive to high-quality knowledge flows. Such “designer”

#

networks could be created by explicitly instructing end users to acquire knowledge from designated experts, and not from anyone else. However, SNs are by definition informal. End users usually gravitate into such networks over time, influenced by a variety of factors, including personal characteristics, co-location of workspaces, and outside interests. In addition, when an end user encounters a problem, despite instructions to the contrary, he or she will most likely just turn to their nearest knowledge network contact, rather than reach out to designated experts. Another approach might be to ensure that central individuals in the preimplementation knowledge network are the ones selected as technology champions and associated with the project team. Post execution, when novices seek support, they would naturally reach out to genuine experts who can guide them and sort out their issues. Central users in this preimplementation network might be drafted onto the project team and trained in the new system, so that this knowledge network continues to provide relevant support after transition to the new system. Mapping such a preimplementation network would also facilitate early identification of users with fragmented networks — the network isolates that are unable to tap into the knowledge flows facilitated by the network. Such end users could receive enhanced training and support beyond what other users receive even prior to “go-live” of the actual system. In addition, the intermediates in the network should be encouraged to reach out to experts to build upon their existing knowledge base, and transition to being experts in their own right.

## FUTURE RESEARCH

Future research should aim at validating the results of this introductory study. By delineating end users into subnetworks based on their expertise, research could examine the role of specific types of interactions within and across subnetworks that could result in enhanced knowledge value and new knowledge creation (Allee, 2003; Möller & Svahn, 2006; North & Kumta, 2014; Vorakulpipat & Rezgui, 2008). For example, would knowledge acquired by experts from other experts result in new knowledge creation or just knowledge validation and reinforcement or both? Would the value of knowledge to the recipient be minimal as both parties possess roughly the same levels of domain expertise? How would this compare to knowledge acquired by novice employees from experts? While it is unlikely that new and innovative ideas would arise out of such interactions, its value to the recipient may be high as the novice would be acquiring knowledge essential for proper system usage. Different combinations of knowledge interactions between subnetworks could be analyzed for new knowledge creation, validation, and value to recipients, and their impact on performance outcomes.

This study assumes that end users utilized knowledge that they had acquired through training equally well for the execution of system-related tasks and responsibilities. However, it is possible that individual characteristics related to learning skills and absorptive capacities might impact the extent to which they were able to internalize and utilize the knowledge acquired from training. Future research could examine the role of individual characteristics in impacting knowledge acquisition and its internalization during training and the extent to which such knowledge is actually used for system usage. The type of knowledge being transferred and its possible implications for knowledge value, new knowledge creation, and performance outcomes can also be examined. For example, in a subnetwork consisting of experts, would tacit,

#

internalized, and applied knowledge be transferred over explicit, factual knowledge that would anyway be available through technical documentation? Would this support new knowledge creation? Would these trends be the same or different across other combinations of knowledge interactions across subnetworks? Would it differ between system usage and business process networks? How would it impact performance outcomes? This study used the extent of training and employee involvement in system development to determine the expertise level of end users. Future research could utilize more sophisticated techniques such as associative network models (Schvaneveldt, 1990; Teichert & Schöntag, 2010) to map end user conceptualizations of the newly introduced business processes and assign expertise levels based on the extent to which such conceptualizations match the actual business processes.
