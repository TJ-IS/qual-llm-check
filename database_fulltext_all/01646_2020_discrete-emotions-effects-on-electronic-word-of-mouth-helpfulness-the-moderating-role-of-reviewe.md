# Discrete Emotions Effects on Electronic Word-of-Mouth Helpfulness: The Moderating Role of Reviewer Gender and Contextual Emotional Tone

Georgiana Craciun<sup>1</sup>, Wenqi Zhou<sup>1</sup>, Zhe Shan<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>: Palumbo Donahue School of Business, Duquesne University

<sup>2</sup>: Farmer School of Business, Miami University

## Abstract

Using frameworks from the literature on emotion, cognitive processing and gender stereotypes, we extend existing research on online product review helpfulness by demonstrating that reviewer gender moderates the relationship between emotional content and review helpfulness and that readers’ perceptions of reviewer credibility explain this effect. In the first study, experimental methods were used to demonstrate that the differential effect of angry vs. non-emotional reviews on review helpfulness was moderated by reviewer gender. Further, findings showed that the negative effect of anger on review helpfulness for female (but not male) reviewers was explained by lower reviewer credibility. In the second study, by analyzing product reviews from the Amazon web site, we found supportive evidence for the results of the first study on the relationship between female-expressed anger and review helpfulness. In addition, we explored the influence of gender stereotypes in terms of the relationship between anxiety and review helpfulness and compared it to anger. Study 2 also allowed us to explore the moderation effect of the contextual emotional tone of other reviews on the target product. Our findings highlight limitations in perceived helpfulness ratings due to gender stereotype biases and offer insights for various decision makers to better understand and manage review helpfulness.

Keywords: electronic word-of-mouth, review helpfulness, emotion, gender stereotype

## 1. Introduction

Eighty percent of Americans were online shoppers in 2016 compared to only 22% in 2010 [1]. This acceleration in e-commerce activities has been accompanied by a significant increase in the amount of online product and service research that consumers perform. Approximately 74% of the American consumers think it is important to be able to read reviews posted online by others who have purchased the item [1]. Past research has consistently demonstrated that electronic word-of-mouth (eWOM) has a significant impact on purchase intentions [2], product choice [3], product sales [4,5], and new product introductions [6]. Yet, the impact of eWOM is contingent on how helpful these online reviews are to consumers when making product evaluations and purchase decisions [7,8]. Potential customers are more persuaded by reviews that are perceived as more helpful [9] and websites that identify helpful reviews achieve greater consumer attention and traffic [10]. Therefore, understanding the determinants of perceived review helpfulness benefits review writers and readers as well as online retailers and review platforms. As such, the purpose of our study is to evaluate factors that affect the perception of eWOM reviews. Specifically, we test whether and how gender stereotypes influence perceived reviewer credibility and perceived review helpfulness.

Our research focuses on the connection between anger and perceived review helpfulness. Using two studies, a controlled experiment and a field study, we investigate the following questions: How do emotionrelated gender stereotypes influence the effect of anger on perceived review helpfulness? and How is this relationship explained in terms of perceived reviewer credibility? Second, we explore the differential effect of anxiety expressions versus anger expressions. Third, we explore the moderating effect of contextual emotional tone.

There are several reasons why these research questions are important. First, emotions communicate important information about reviewers’ experiences with products or services and, therefore, have a significant influence on eWOM helpfulness [11,12]. While ample research has focused on the quantitative determinants of eWOM helpfulness [5,7,13–16], only a handful of studies [8,12,14,17] have examined the qualitative determinants of eWOM helpfulness, including emotional expressions. Second, anger is commonly encountered in product reviews and has received significant attention in the literature on discrete emotions [8,12,18,19]. Third, anger has been contrasted with other negative emotions such as anxiety. Consider the

following hypothetical reviews, borrowed from the literature [8, p. 540], that describe the same unpleasant situation with different emotional tones: anxiety, “Very worried… I ordered the book two weeks ago, but still haven’t received it. At this point, I’m extremely concerned!” versus anger, “Very upset… I ordered the book two weeks ago, but still haven’t received it. At this point, I’m extremely angry!”. Some studies have found that anxiety-embedded reviews are perceived as more helpful than anger-embedded reviews [8], whil other studies have found the opposite [17]. We reconcile these mixed findings by demonstrating the moderating effect of reviewer gender stereotypes. We draw on prior studies in psychology that have demonstrated that the social influence of emotional expressions in in-person communication depends on the gender of the communicator and is attributable to emotion-related gender stereotypes [20–23]. Finally, recent eWOM studies have demonstrated the importance of contextual influences in examining online reviews [24]. A prominent but under-researched contextual factor is the overall emotion expressed in other user reviews on the same product, which we define as the contextual emotional tone. Akin to the star ratings provided in other reviews or the helpfulness ratings provided by peer reviewers, the contextual emotional tone can have normative influences on perceptions of review helpfulness [11,25]. Therefore, our research attempts to answer recent calls in information systems and marketing for studies that explore the interplay between the emotional content of the review, reviewer demographics, and the emotional tone of other reviews on the platform in shaping helpfulness perceptions [11,26].

The first study used an experiment to test the causal relationship between anger-related gender stereotypes and review helpfulness. This study showed that female-authored reviews that expressed anger (vs. no emotion) led to perceptions of lower reviewer credibility, which in turn led to lower review helpfulness evaluations. For male-authored reviews, anger had no effect on credibility and helpfulness. The second study replicated study 1 findings for anger in the realistic setting of Amazon reviews [27]. Additionally, it compared anger to anxiety effects on review helpfulness and explored the potential moderation effect of the contextual emotional tone.

Our contribution to the existing literature is threefold. First, this research demonstrates the influence of emotion-related gender stereotypes on eWOM evaluations. Second, we propose a new mediator, perceived reviewer credibility, which explains the effect of gender stereotypes on review helpfulness. Third, this study extends previous research on the influence of context on eWOM by providing evidence that the emotional tone of other reviews functions as another important moderator between emotional reviews and their helpfulness. Practically, our findings could benefit both e-commerce providers and customers. Online vendors and online review platforms can more readily identify and promote helpful reviews. Moreover, by understanding the biasing effect of reviewer gender, they can research ways to improve the algorithm for helpfulness calculations. Finally, e-commerce platforms could encourage reviewers to report facts that literature and section 3 presents our research model. Sections 4 and 5 describe the methods and results of our studies. We conclude our paper in Section 6.

## 2. Prior Literature

In this section, we review the literature on the antecedents of eWOM helpfulness and present our research model. We draw on three streams of prior research: 1) studies on discrete emotional expressions in eWOM, 2) studies on emotion-related gender stereotypes, and 3) studies on contextual influences in eWOM.

## 2.1. Discrete emotional expressions in eWOM

Emotions have been previously defined as valenced feeling states representing evaluative reactions to events, agents or objects [12]. Numerous studies in information systems, marketing, and psychology demonstrate that observers and readers use lay theories of emotions and stereotypes to interpret message senders’ feelings, beliefs and intentions and decide how to react to them [8,12,20,26,28]. In an in-person communication context, individuals make inferences about an interaction partner’s competence, status, professionalism, and decisiveness based on his/her emotional expressions [20,21,23,29]. In an online context, in the absence of personal ties or physical proximity between the product review writer and the review reader, the latter uses review-embedded emotions to make inferences about review helpfulness [8]. A number of studies have applied the attribution theory [11,12] to investigate how emotional expressions affect review helpfulness. In the eWOM field, attribution theory predicts that if consumers credit the product review to actual product performance, they will perceive the review as credible and be persuaded by it. Yet, if consumers credit the review to non-product related factors (e.g., reviewer’s predispositions), they will judge the reviewer as less rational and his/her review as less helpful [11,12].

Several prior studies have examined the impact of emotional valence (positive vs. negative emotions) on eWOM helpfulness. For example, in a field study using Amazon product reviews, Li and Zhan find that reviews are more helpful when they contain strong positive (vs. negative) emotions [30]. Using an experimental design, Kim and Gupta [12] demonstrate that negative emotions (vs. no emotion) in a single review decrease review helpfulness, but positive emotions have no effect. Ludwig et al. [31] find that increases in positive (negative) affective content are beneficial (detrimental) for conversion rates.

Departing from the valence-based approach, an emerging stream of research has begun to contrast the effects of distinct emotions on eWOM evaluations by using the cognitive appraisal theory of emotions [28,32,33]. According to this theory, discrete emotions (e.g., happiness, frustration, anger,) can be compared along several dimensions that characterize the events that triggered those emotions (e.g., valence, control, certainty). Prior studies have found that appraisal patterns (e.g., unpleasant event, high certainty, and high control) are correlated with specific consumption emotions (e.g., anger) and same valence emotions differ on multiple appraisal dimensions [32]. For example, the negative emotions of fear and anger differ on certainty and control. Fear is associated with uncertainty and situational control, while anger is associated with certainty and individual control. Further, these specific appraisals will differentially impact consumer decision making [28,33]. Using cognitive appraisal theory, researchers have shown that same valence emotions (e.g., anxiety and disgust) may have opposite effects on review helpfulness, while different valence emotions (e.g., happiness and disgust) may have the same kind of impact on helpfulness [17].

A few studies have examined consumers’ responses to online reviews containing anxiety versus anger with mixed results. Yin et al. [8] have showed that anxiety embedded reviews are more helpful than anger embedded reviews because of higher perceived cognitive effort attributed to the anxious reviewers. In contrast, Ahmad and Laroche [17] have found that emotions associated with uncertainty (e.g., anxiety) have a negative effect on review helpfulness, while emotions associated with certainty (e.g., anger) have a positive effect on review helpfulness. These conflicting findings suggest that other source or context factors moderate the effect of anger and anxiety on eWOM helpfulness. We contribute to this line of research by examining the moderation effects of reviewer gender and contextual emotional tone of target product reviews.

## 2.2. Emotion-related gender stereotypes

Prior findings from the psychology and management literatures have consistently demonstrated the existence of emotion-related gender biases in in-person interactions. According to this literature, one of the strongest gender stereotypes in Western cultures is that women are more ional than men are [20,34,35]. Furthermore, stereotypes are emotion specific. Women are believed to express more happiness, surprise, sadness, fear, and depression, while anger, contempt, and pride are seen as uniquely masculine emotions [20,36,37]. Women are seen as lacking emotional control and therefore lacking the ability to make rational and objective decisions [20]. Observers tend to attribute female expressions of emotion to internal causes (e.g., being emotional), and male expressions to th ernal environment or situation (e.g., having a bad day; [20,21]). Women, but not men, tend to be judged as less competent when displaying counter-stereotypical emotions (e.g., anger, contempt, pride; [21,38]). Remaining emotionally neutral in a specific emotioneliciting situation is perceived as a sign of dominance for men, but not for women [29]. For men, both anger and emotional neutrality lead to similar inferences of high competence [22]. These studies provide evidence that observers have strong expectations about what are appropriate emotional expressions for female versus male communicators in in-person interactions. Emotional expressions that contradict these expectations tend to lower evaluations about the communicator.

Prior studies have demonstrated that the norms and expectations that individuals use in in-person interactions persist in online communication [5,39,40]. In the eWOM context, past research has shown that decision makers use profile information (e.g., gender, expertise) to make inferences about reviewer

credibility and review helpfulness [5,16,41]. For example, one study has shown that the disclosure of a reviewer’s real name and geographic location leads to greater perceptions of review helpfulness and increased product sales [5]. Others have shown that the perceived or explicit gender of a blog writer or news source influences the perceived credibility of the message and its source [39,40]. We argue that the influence of emotion-specific gender stereotypes on in-person communication also extends to online communication.

## 2.3. Contextual influences in eWOM

In real life, consumers do not judge reviews in isolation, but as part of a set of reviews for the same product [42]. Websites such as Amazon, Epinions, or Yelp present multiple reviews simultaneously and consumers may consult this information prior to judging the credibility and usefulness of a target review. Normative factors such as the information about others’ opinions have a significant influence on review helpfulness [24,43,44]. Past studies have found that valence-consistent reviews are perceived as more helpful than inconsistent reviews [42,44]. Moreover, consistency in positive valence leads to higher conversion rates, while consistency in negative valence leads to lower conversion rates [31]. Kim and Gupta [12] have demonstrated that emotion- and valence-consistent reviews (e.g., multiple angry reviews) are seen as more helpful than same valence non-emotional reviews. Results provide strong evidence for an attribution theory explanation: review consistency is attributed to product characteristics, while inconsistency is attributed to reviewers’ dispositions [12,44]. Moreover, these studies have shown that the effect of review consistency on review helpfulness is moderated by reader involvement, reviewer expertise, review sequence, and the product sales rank [42,44]. Yet, these studies overlooked potential reviewer gender effects (i.e., gender was not displayed or only male-authored reviews were shown). Consequently, the present study extends prior research by exploring the interaction between reviewer gender and contextual emotional tone.

## 3. Hypotheses Development and Research Model

The main objective of our research is to examine the moderation effect of reviewer gender on the relationship between review anger and perceived eWOM helpfulness. Moreover, we explain this relationship through the mediation of perceived reviewer credibility. Our first argument is that readers will make different credibility inferences about men and women expressing anger in online reviews. As summarized in section 2.2, women are seen as more emotional, less rational and less objective than men. In particular, anger is seen as a counter-stereotypical emotion for women. Observers tend to attribute anger to a woman’s personal disposition and interpret it as a sign of irrationality or lack of self-control. Therefore, in the eWOM context, readers will see a female reviewer’s anger as unrelated to product performance and evaluate that reviewer as less credible than female reviewers who are emotionally neutral. In contrast, when judging men, anger is considered a stereotypical emotion and is attributed to external factors (e.g., he had a bad day). Hence, review anger will not affect evaluations of male reviewers’ credibility. Further, we argue that readers will perceive a review as more helpful if it comes from a credible source. This is consistent with prior findings in the eWOM literature that link source rationality and trustworthiness to review helpfulness [11].

To summarize our arguments, for female- (but not male-) authored reviews, anger (vs. emotionally neutral) expressions will result in a lower level of perceived reviewer credibility, which will in turn lead to lower perceptions of review helpfulness (see Fig. 1). Our hypotheses can be stated as follows:

H1: Angry (vs. unemotional) reviews will be perceived as less helpful when the review writer is a female, but not when it is a male.

H2: Perceived reviewer credibility mediates the effect of the interaction between anger and reviewer gender on perceived review helpfulness.

The second objective of our research is to explore the effect of another negative emotion – anxiety – on review helpfulness and compare it to the effect of anger. Past research on eWOM has shown that anxiety and anger trigger different cognitive appraisals and responses [8,17], but these studies did not consider the differential effect of reviewer gender. Moreover, to the authors’ best knowledge, no study in psychology has examined how people interpret and react to expressions of anxiety by men versus women. We draw on the attribution theory, gender stereotypes, and the cognitive appraisal theory of emotion to explore this question. According to prior studies, women are expected to express powerless emotions, which leads to lower competence evaluations [20,37]. In addition, women’s emotions are more likely to be attributed to internal predispositions [45,46]. Since anxiety is associated with uncertainty and lack of control, a woman’s anxiety expressions confirm the female stereotypes of lack of emotional control and powerlessness [45]. Therefore, anxiety expressions in eWOM will decrease the credibility and helpfulness of female-authored reviews.

In the case of male reviewers, the above-mentioned theories lead to two competing arguments. First, review readers may perceive anxiety as a powerless emotion and penalize men for expressing anxiety. Contrary to powerful emotions (e.g., anger, pride) that communicate certainty and control, anxiety is associated with lack of certainty and lack of personal control about outcomes [47,48]. Since men are discouraged from showing weakness and vulnerability [49], displaying emotions that convey these characteristics is stigmatized [50]. Men who express powerless (vs. powerful) emotions are perceived as having lower competence [23,51]. To the extent that lack of certainty and control are associated with powerlessness, expressing anxiety will diminish the helpfulness of male-authored reviews.

The alternative argument, instead, proposes the opposite, that is, expressing anxiety will enhance a male reviewer’s credibility and helpfulness. This argument relies on the fact that observers may experience incongruity when men, who generally occupy positions of high power, express uncertainty in their reviews [52]. Consistent with the attribution model of persuasion [53] and the informational incongruity hypothesis [54,55], incongruent information violates expectancies and, therefore, increases attention and elaboration, which can lead to enhanced persuasion. For example, Karmarkar and Tormala [54] have shown that when a message source has high (low) expertise, expressing more (less) uncertainty violates expectancies, which promotes elaboration and persuasion.

In summary, while anxiety expressions are expected from female communicators, they are not expected from male communicators. On one hand, if anxiety is perceived as a powerless emotion, it hurts the helpfulness of male sources. On the other hand, if the uncertainty associated with anxiety is more salient, uncertainty expressions by male sources may be surprising and therefore foster persuasion, leading to higher perceived helpfulness. We rely on our empirical analysis to reveal the direction of the moderation effect of reviewer gender on eWOM helpfulness for anxiety-embedded reviews. Hence, instead of formulating a hypothesis, we explore the following research question:

RQ1: How do reviewer gender and review anxiety, in conjunction, influence review helpfulness?

The last objective of this research is to explore the moderating effect of contextual emotional tone. As summarized in section 2.3, prior findings agree on the positive effects of emotional consistency in eWOM. Therefore, the expression of negative emotions such as anger and anxiety in the target review should increase perceptions of review helpfulness when the contextual emotional tone is also negative. In line with an attribution theory explanation, when reviewers express negative emotions such as anger or anxiety, readers will make more product attributions rather than dispositional attributions if the contextual emotion of target product reviews is negative. Prior studies have also shown that the impact of review consistency on review helpfulness depends on source or contextual factors (e.g., reviewer expertise [42,44]). We expect that the moderating effect of contextual emotion varies over reviewer gender. Because there is insufficient literature available to shed light on the specific gender difference in such moderation effects, we resort to our empirical analysis to unveil this potential mechanism under which target review emotion, reviewer gender, and contextual emotion interact to affect eWOM helpfulness. Hence, we pose our second research question as below:

RQ2: How do reviewer gender, target review emotion, and the negative contextual emotion, in conjunction, influence perceived review helpfulness?

To test our hypotheses and answer our research questions, we adopted two distinct methodologies including a controlled experiment and a field study. In Study 1, we manipulated anger and the reviewer’s gender while controlling for review content and contextual factors. Study 2 utilized a dataset of online user reviews from Amazon, which allowed us to examine and compare the effects of anger and anxiety, and to investigate the moderation effects of reviewer gender and contextual emotional tone.

## 4. Study 1

This study was designed to test the effects of reviewer expressions of anger and reviewer gender on eWOM helpfulness in an experimental environment. This allowed us to control for review characteristics while manipulating reviewer gender and the review-embedded anger. Moreover, this study allowed us to examine the hypothesized mediation mechanism through reviewer credibility.

## 4.1. Methodology

U.S.-resident MTurkers (N = 368; 51% male) were asked to imagine they were buying a new laptop and were reading online reviews on an electronic retailer’s website. They were randomly assigned to one of four conditions of a 2 (anger emotion: present vs. absent) × 2 (reviewer gender: male vs. female) betweensubjects design. The angry review provided a star rating of 1 out of 10 and stated: “I bought this laptop in January 2017. DOES NOT WORK WELL!!! Speed is not good enough for Internet browsing and regular work at the same time. I’M PLAIN MAD!!! ”. Emotion markers (emoticons, exclamation marks, capitalized bold font) and the sentence “I’m plain mad” were removed from the unemotional (control) review. Gender was presented through an abbreviated name (AndrewD vs. JennyD) and a grey avatar (to control for attractiveness). All participants read one review and then completed a questionnaire with the measures for the dependent and manipulation check variables.

## 4.2. Measures

Two manipulation check measures were included for reviewer gender and emotion. Participants were asked to indicate the gender of the reviewer (male, female, or I don’t know). Ten participants who did not identify reviewer gender correctly were dropped from the analysis (no significant differences across

conditions, $\chi ^ { 2 } < 1 )$ . We also checked participants’ perceptions of the reviewer’s anger by averaging three anger-related items (angry, mad, furious; $\mathbf { \boldsymbol { a } } = . 9 3 )$ on 7-point (1 = not at all, 7 = very much) scales. The angry reviewer $( M = 6 . 4 2 , \mathrm { S D } = 0 . 9 1 )$ was perceived as angrier $( F ( 1 , 3 5 4 ) = 1 0 2 . 4 4 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ than the unemotional one $( M = 5 . 0 3 , \mathrm { S D } = 1 . 5 9 )$ , supporting the anger manipulation.

The dependent variable, perceived review helpfulness, was measured with 3 items (the user review provided valuable information; the user review provided was useful; I think I learned a lot about the reviewed laptop after reading the user review) $\left( \mathfrak { a } = 0 . 9 5 \right)$ on 9-point scales (1 = strongly disagree, 9 = strongly agree) [11,12]. Perceived reviewer credibility was measured with 10 items (reasonable, reliable, trustworthy, honest, knowledgeable, experienced, qualified, expert, sincere and rational) (α = 0.96) [11,12].

When readers evaluate product reviews, the presence of anger leads to lower perceived credibility and lower perceived helpfulness for female reviewers, but not for male reviewers.

We tested for homophily effects due to gender similarity between review writer and reader [57]. The lack of interaction effects between reader gender and any of the other predictors failed to support homophily. This is consistent with recent results according to which homophily impacts eWOM effectiveness on social media, but not e-commerce platforms such as the one used in this study [58].

In conclusion, study 1 demonstrates the interaction effect of reviewer gender and anger on eWOM helpfulness. When readers were exposed to one online product review, their gender stereotypes about emotion had a significant influence on their evaluations of angry (vs. unemotional) reviews, even though reviews had the same objective content across the gender conditions. Female-authored reviews were perceived as less helpful when angry (vs. unemotional). Evaluations of male-authored reviews were not affected by anger. Moreover, results support the mediation through perceived reviewer credibility. Female reviewers who expressed anger (vs. no emotion) were perceived as less credible, which led to lower evaluations of review helpfulness. The main advantage of study 1 was the use of an experimental method that allowed us to manipulate the review emotion and the reviewer gender while controlling for other review characteristics (e.g., content, display). Nevertheless, this constrained the level of generalizability of the study. Therefore, we designed study 2, which uses real-world online product reviews collected from Amazon.

## 5. Study 2

The main goal of study 2 was to replicate our experimental findings for anger with real-world archival data. In addition, we also introduced a different emotion, anxiety. Finally, using a more comprehensive data set allowed us to measure the contextual emotional tone (CET) of target product reviews and investigate its moderation effect. Such context would otherwise be difficult to replicate in a controlled experiment setting (e.g., study 1). We were therefore able to examine the gender difference in the marginal impact of two discrete negative emotions, anger and anxiety, and the moderation effect of CET.

## 5.1. Context and Data

We constructed a panel data set based on a publicly available database called “Market Dynamics and User Generated Content about Tablet Computers” provided by [27]. This public database contains weekly data of tablets listed on Amazon over 24 weeks in 2012, including product information and consumer reviews. A tablet is a one-piece mobile computer, which typically equips a touch screen with finger or stylus gestures. There are more than 400 tablet brands listed in this data set, including Apple’s iPad, Amazon’s Kindle Fire, Barnes & Noble’s Nook [27]. This archival data provides a relevant foundation to create a data set to address our research questions. Amazon is one of the leading e-commerce platforms with 2018 sales accounting for 49% of all online retail spending in US [59]. Therefore, it has been adopted as the research context by many prior eWOM studies (e.g., [7,13,60]). For each product listed, Amazon encourages its consumers to share their experiences by posting a numerical rating ranging from 1 to 5 along with a detailed review comment. It also gives them the option of disclosing their real names. Consumers can vote others reviews as helpful or unhelpful. Therefore, the availability of online consumer reviews, reviewer name information and helpful votes make the data appropriate to test the proposed hypotheses.

We constructed our data set by utilizing the data of the most recent week (July 11, 2012) from the original public data. The main reason underlying this choice was that we ran our analysis at the individual review level, and the review, including its embedded emotions, did not change once posted. As a result, the time-series dimension cannot provide further information into examining the proposed relationships. Keeping the last week of the original data provided the largest sample of reviews that suited our analysis. Further, we excluded some observations based on three criteria. First, we removed all 5-star reviews. 5-star reviews are extremely positive by being at the highest end of rating range. They generally present little

negative emotion. In addition, they account for a very large portion in the data. Nearly half of the reviews in the data were 5-star. As a result, including them would dilute the variation of negative emotion and reduce the statistical power. Second, since gender was one of the key variables in our model, we excluded the reviews for which reviewer gender was difficult to determine. To detect reviewer gender, we matched the disclosed reviewers’ first names with the name corpus provided by CMU Artificial Intelligence Lab [61]. Some reviewers chose not to disclose their real names. Also, some disclosed first names were too ambiguous to infer the gender. In both cases, we removed those reviews. Third, we removed reviews that had no helpful/unhelpful votes. Therefore, our final data comprised 152 tablet computers and 2,288 reviews.

Readers may also trust more reviewers whose past reviewing experiences has been recognized. It is also worth noting that some products, by their nature, may tend to attract more helpful reviews. If a product had already received a large number of reviews, it was more difficult for an individual review to provide additional helpful information.

## 6. Discussion and Implications

Much of the prior research on eWOM supports a negativity bias according to which negative (vs. positive) reviews are more consequential to consumer attitudes and behavior. A handful of studies have examined this effect and demonstrated that it is partly due to the differential impact of the discrete emotions embedded in the eWOM [8,11,12,17,67]. Moreover, emotions that are similar in valence (e.g., anger, anxiety) can produce different effects on eWOM helpfulness due to the different appraisals they evoke ender moderates the relationship between review-embedded anger and perceived review helpfulness (H1). Further, using moderated mediation analyses, we demonstrate that the relationship between anger and review helpfulness is mediated via perceived reviewer credibility, while also depending on reviewer gender (H2). Our results show that, for female reviewers, anger (vs. remaining emotionally neutral) reduced their perceived credibility, which led to lower review helpfulness. For male reviewers, expressing anger did not have any effect on perceived credibility and helpfulness. In addition, this research extends the effect of gender stereotypes to another discrete emotion – anxiety. Results support a significant interaction between reviewer gender and anxiety, such that the effect of anxiety on perceived helpfulness is negative for female-authored reviews and positive for male-authored reviews in most situations. Taken together, these results are in line with prior research showing that the emotional content affects readers’ perceptions of review helpfulness [8,17,67]. Moreover, our findings show that readers have strong expectations about the appropriate emotional expressions of female and male reviewers, similar to in-person communication expectations [21,22]. Hence, gender stereotypes moderate the effect of emotion on eWOM helpfulness. Female reviewers are penalized when expressing counter-stereotypical emotions (e.g., anger) and even stereotypical emotions (i.e., anxiety)

compared to staying emotionally neutral. In contrast, men are not penalized for expressing stereotypical emotions (i.e., anger) and benefit from expressing counter-stereotypical emotions (i.e., anxiety).

Finally, this research extends prior findings by demonstrating that the contextual emotional tone of target product reviews moderates these effects. The above results hold when there is emotional consistency between the target review and the contextual emotional tone. Past research has shown that emotional consistency leads to more product attributions and thus, enhances review helpfulness [12,31]. In line with the attribution theory explanation and based on the present results, we argue that when male reviewers express anxiety (but not anger), readers make more product quality rather than dispositional attributions if the contextual emotional tone is negative. Yet, when female reviewers express either anger or anxiety, readers make dispositional attributions consistent with the view that women are more emotional and cannot control the biasing influence of emotions on their thoughts and behavior [21,45].

Results are reversed when there is little, if any, emotional consistency between the target review and contextual emotion. Hence, when the contextual emotional tone contains very little or no negative emotion, anger (and to a small extent anxiety) has a positive impact on female-authored reviews, while anxiety (but not anger) has a negative impact on male-authored reviews. Applying the attribution theory to these results, we argue that in the absence of emotional consistency, readers attribute female anger (and anxiety, to a smaller extent) to product quality, which makes the review more helpful. On the other hand, readers attribute male anxiety to some reviewer idiosyncrasy, thus making the review less helpful.

## 6.1. Theoretical and Managerial Implications

This research contributes to the existing literature in several respects. In recent years, an increasing number of studies in information systems and marketing have focused on understanding the effects of eWOM on consumer decision making and the business bottom line. While the majority of these studies have investigated the quantitative characteristics of eWOM [4,7], a few have started to pay attention to the review content, in particular the emotional cues [8,11,14,15,25,31]. Moreover, only a handful of studies have examined the interaction of profile characteristics (e.g., reviewer expertise) and emotional cues [11]. Our

work goes a step further and demonstrates the impact of an under-researched profile characteristic, reviewer gender, on perceptions of emotional reviews. Hence, we expand the literature on the effects of emotional expressions on eWOM helpfulness [8,11,12,26]. Second, this research demonstrates the effect of a new mediator, perceived reviewer credibility, which explains the relationship between emotion-related gender stereotypes and review helpfulness. Third, this research extends the literature on the influence of context on eWOM [24,44,68] by showing that the contextual emotional tone of target product reviews moderates the impact of emotional reviews on perceived helpfulness. Most of the prior studies on eWOM have examined factors related to product (e.g., price, popularity, brand name) and platform characteristics (e.g., third-party websites) [16,24,68]. Most studies examining review consistency have focused primarily on review ratings [42,44,69]. Our work, however, points out that normative influences also apply to the affective components of eWOM. Further, this study demonstrates that the effect of emotional consistency on review evaluations is gender specific.

According to a recent survey [70], nearly 90% of consumers who read online reviews agreed that they were influenced by online reviews before making purchasing decisions. Given the persuasive power of eWOM, it is extremely important for companies to understand how to use online reviews as a marketing tool. This research has significant practical implications for online vendors, online review platforms, and individual consumers. First, online vendors should be aware of the limitations of the helpfulness ratings and adjust their opinion collection approach to overcome gender biases, especially when female customers are one of their target segments. In particular, online vendors should be aware that anger- and anxiety-embedded reviews written by female customers tend to receive a lower helpfulness score due to gender stereotyping. Yet, those underrated reviews may carry insightful information for product improvement and innovation. Second, our findings suggest that online consumers should be aware of and refrain from using gender stereotypes in making inferences from emotional reviews. The emotional woman stereotype may bias readers’ perceptions of helpfulness in the presence of anger or anxiety cues. Therefore, e-commerce platforms should provide guidelines for review readers to raise their awareness of potential gender biases and encourage them to look for facts that explain product performance. Finally, many online platforms (e.g., Amazon.com, Yelp.com) rely on the collection and dissemination of online product reviews and, in order to provide better product quality information, they may need to adjust the calculation of helpfulness scores to eliminate potential gender biases. Alternatively, these platforms could use various website design characteristics (e.g., reviewer identification, review length instructions) to stimulate reviewers to write more concrete (vs. abstract) reviews [71]. In fact, many online platforms have already started to encourage reviewers to provide arguments to substantiate their opinions.

## 6.2. Limitations and Future Research

This study is not without its limitations. Using only one product category (laptops/tablets) limits the generalizability of our results. The literature on hedonic versus utilitarian consumption has demonstrated that making and perceptions of online reviews [26,72]. On one hand, since hedonic products are evaluated more holistically, while utilitarian products are evaluated based on detailed information about objective features, emotions may play a greater role in the evaluation of hedonic products. On the other hand, a recent study exploring the effect of emotional arousal in online product reviews demonstrates that expressed arousal has a greater effect on helpfulness evaluations of utilitarian rather than hedonic products [26]. Future research may benefit from considering the effects of gender stereotypes and emotion consistency on perceptions of emotional reviews for hedonic versus utilitarian products.

We also acknowledge the exploratory nature of our test of the effect of review-embedded anxiety on review helpfulness. Further research is needed to replicate this effect in a controlled experiment and to explain the underlying mechanism. Another promising research area is examining both positive and negative discrete emotions embedded in consumer reviews [8,11,26]. The present research has only focused on two negative emotions, anger and anxiety, because of a common finding in the eWOM literature whereby negative reviews tend to be more persuasive [8]. Nevertheless, the psychology literature has demonstrated that emotion-related gender stereotypes are present for both positive and negative emotions.

A review of past research on eWOM indicates that the helpfulness indicator may be skewed because only some readers provide helpfulness ratings online [13,72,73]. Since in study 2 review helpfulness was measured by only those consumers who engage in this behavior, those findings may not generalize to readers who did not vote. Nevertheless, the study 1 experiment measured the helpfulness ratings of all participants, thus strengthening our results. Further research on the helpfulness rating bias is encouraged to enhance our understanding of consumer decision-making on review websites. In study 2, we were not able to identify the review subsets each helpfulness voter actually read. Hence, the moderation effect of contextual emotion was likely underestimated. Moreover, the data set does not contain the most recent reviews on Amazon. Future research could use a more recent and more granular data set to investigate contextual emotion effects. Further research is needed to examine the moderation of contextual emo an experimental setting where the emotion of target product reviews [12] and the order of these reviews could be manipulated [42].
