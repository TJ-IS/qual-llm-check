# Attention Adjustment, Renewal, and Equilibrium Seeking in Online Search: An Eye-Tracking Approach

ABSTRACT: Using eye-tracking field experiments, we examine the dynamics underlying consumers’ attention-allocation behaviors in online search, with focus on attention adjustment, attention renewal, and equilibrium seeking. In particular, we probed into how consumers’ e-commerce search behaviors vary when they are exposed to an advertisement during a search and when they are not. The findings from the two separate experiments suggest that consumers’ attention span decreases exponentially, instead of linearly, as they maneuver from the top to the bottom of a search result webpage. The total number of available options significantly influences the speed and pattern of attention decay. However, attention decay does not simply move in the direction of depletion but can be refreshed and renewed upon encountering attention-diverting ad stimuli. Although ad stimuli are often considered distracting and worthless, they can produce positive effects when positioned in the middle of a search results listing, where a consumer’s attention resources are rejuvenated by ads. Finally, because of consumers’ propensity to seek equilibrium, attention decay occurs more rapidly after, rather than before, attention renewal. We extend the literature on the mere categorization effect by investigating how ad stimuli structurally separate search choices into mental categories and diminish ongoing attention decay patterns.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: attention allocation, visual attention, attention decay, attention renewal, eye-tracking, excessive product choice, e-commerce, consumer attention, online search.

## Introduction

Success in e-commerce is often attributed to the ability of vendors to “capture eyeballs”—an ability that factors importantly in a market characterized by information overload. A market research firm indicates that a person residing in a city 30 years ago was exposed to an average of 2,000 advertisements a day, but this figure has dramatically increased to around 5,000 with the advent of the Internet and e-commerce [54]. In an environment overwhelmed by a massive influx of ad messages, consumers have learned to tune out advertising. A large proportion of ad stimuli go unnoticed, and the messages that they carry dissipate into the cacophony of media noise. Capturing consumer attention continues to confound savvy online marketers, who spend millions of dollars per year in directing traffic to their websites [35]. The complex and arduous nature of these challenges have recently escalated, as the number of online retailers competing for eyeballs continues to dramatically increase and as companies invest heavily in marketing resources to differentiate their products.

Choice is often more a curse than a blessing for buyers. In electronic markets, consumers may incur significant search costs as they are besieged by a torrent of options. For example, a search for woman’s jeans on the search engine of ebay.com, presents consumers with numerous results pages, each listing several dozen selections. As in the case of ad exposure, most product options displayed on a company’s results pages become meaningless and futile, given that they fail to seize attention. The disparity between exposure (i.e., the number of options presented to consumers) and attention (i.e., the number of choices that capture consumer consideration) has substantially widened [18, 78, 84]. The integration of IT and marketing platforms has enabled marketers to display, at minimum cost, ad stimuli in every corner of a webpage in the e-commerce environment. Consequently, there is an immense increase in consumer exposure to commercial stimuli. Meanwhile, the physical information-processing capability of human beings, in terms of the number of neurons in the brain, remains relatively unchanged over time<sup>1</sup>[60], thereby, failing to keep pace with the increase in stimuli. In choice-setting environments, where many products are available, consumers adopt a choice mechanism either deliberately (e.g., through stopping heuristics [10]), or unconsciously, to reduce the volume of options and retain only relevant selections.

As competition in online retailing steadily intensifies, business managers and marketers must understand the search and browsing patterns [87], choice strategies [73], and information-screening heuristics of online consumers, particularly when these buyers are confronted with numerous product options. Studies have suggested that most consumers actively narrow down the search space and reduce the number of options for a variety of products; these strategies are part of the pre-choice decision-making process [42, 80]. For instance, through field experiments and survey instruments, Scheibehenne, Greifeneder and Todd [70] found that the size of a consumer’s consideration $\mathrm { s e t } ^ { 2 }$ is smaller than expected and depends on the quantity of available selections. Under exposure to classical music CDs, the survey respondents accumulated consideration set sizes of only 2.6 and 5.3 items in 6 and 30 available product options, respectively.

Existing studies delved into the relationship between consumer search behaviors and purchase propensities by measuring click-throughs and conversions. We extend this literature by examining the dynamics that underlie the relationship between visual attention and search patterns in the context of online shopping. We focus particularly on how consumers allocate, adjust, and renew visual attention when inundated with numerous available options while shopping online. To gain empirical insights into attention allocation dynamics, our study delves into how consumers respond to advertising stimuli that appear amid search results during engagement in search tasks. A common approach is the survey-based inquiry into consumers’ search behaviors and choice strategies. Although useful and enlightening, surveys are susceptible to structural biases and inaccuracies that stem primarily from participants’ cognitive constraints [29]. They reflect merely perception, not actual behavior, and imprecisely mirror consumers’ conscious behaviors and unconscious search mechanisms [14]. To analyze the patterns of visual attention allocation more accurately, we identified and substantiated the decay and renewal of consumer attention and equilibrium-seeking behaviors through eye-tracking methods. We supplemented our eye-tracking approaches with field experiments to directly monitor eye movements and search behaviors in e-commerce settings where consumers are provided a menu of options.

The eye-tracking approach helps us understand the dynamics governing the attention allocation behaviors of consumers in online environments. The implementation of the approach is guided by the following questions:

● How do online consumers allocate their limited attention across product choices?

● To what extent do consumers adjust their attention allocation in accordance with the number of displayed choices?

● Does attention decay discontinue when consumers encounter ad stimuli positioned in the middle of product listings? If so, to what extent is their attention renewed?

● Following attention renewal, how do consumers apportion the attention resources available to remaining choices? Does the state of disequilibrium caused by attention renewal motivate consumers to allocate and consume fewer mental resources for “residual” choices?

Addressing these issues with the aid of eye-tracking devices is worth pursuing in the context of e-commerce environments, where the presence of immense product options often overpowers consumers’ cognitive capabilities and discourages purchase intent [70]. Designing an effective e-commerce system and harnessing sales opportunities require online retailers to comprehend consumers’ actual search and browsing heuristics, pre-purchase choice decisions, and information-screening patterns. We use a unique neurophysiological approach on the basis of eye-tracking instruments to examine consumers’ attention adjustment, renewal, and equilibriumseeking patterns in e-commerce retailing.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. The second section surveys the relevant literature, and the third section presents the hypotheses on consumers’ attention decay, renewal, and balancing behaviors. The fourth section comprehensively describes the experimental design. The fifth section discusses the data analysis procedures and corresponding findings and the final section concludes the paper with the implications, contributions, and limitations of the study.

## Theoretical Background

This section describes previous studies that pertain to consumers’ attention adjustment, attention renewal, and equilibrium-seeking behaviors. We discuss studies on selective attention to elucidate the conceptual foundation of attention research and the theoretical underpinnings derived from cognitive psychology. Subsequently, we review the scholarly output on visual working memory to explain consumers choice-making behaviors under limited cognitive capabilities. Finally, we review studies that use eye-tracking mechanisms.

## Selective Attention

In his book The Principles of Psychology, William James described “attention” as the “taking possession by the mind, in clear and vivid form, of one out of what seem several simultaneously possible objects or trains of thought” [38; 404]. Attention is, then, focalization, concentration, and consciousness, representing selective perception and a focused line of information processing. Attention can be further divided into two distinct modes of action. Voluntary attention, due to their limited information-processing capacity, selectively processes sensory information on the basis of pre-analyzed, prioritized sequences [45]. In contrast to this endogenously controlled mode of attention, involuntary attention operates through exogenous, non-predictive, transient sensory cues, reflecting a spontaneous response to abrupt and salient stimuli and events.

Although research on the multifaceted nature of involuntary attention is long established, voluntary attention has been the central subject of intensive scientific scrutiny over the years, spawning a wealth of research and theorizing (see Posner [63] for a comprehensive review on voluntary attention). The essence of voluntary attention can be illuminated by understanding the perceptual selectivity required by the limited information processing capacity of humans. For this reason, voluntary attention is often considered akin to selective attention, which according to Shiffrin and Schneider denotes the “control of information processing so that a sensory input is perceived or remembered better in one situation than another according to the desires of the subject” [74; 4]. The concept of selective attention originated with Broadbent’s filter theory [8], by which human perceptual systems, when overwhelmed by information overload, are thought to process information through a selective filter. In the face of the vast amount of information to be processed, the selective filter permits certain stimuli to pass through for further processing but does not permit the entry of unattended or unwanted stimuli into the sensory buffer of the processing system.

## Visual Working Memory

The theory of visual working memory claims that when our eyes move from one fixation to another, the observed contents from previous fixations do not vanish immediately from our memory but are retained temporarily as part of the scene representation [6]. While studies on selective attention offer psychological insights into users’ attention-allocation dynamics, the theoretical tenet of visual working memory (e.g., [55, 62, 64]) provides more fundamental, neurological explanations of why people limit their attention to only a few alternatives among numerous options. Luck and Vogel [55] reveal that individuals can concurrently hold only a small, fixed number of objects in their short-term memory due to neurobiological limits. Moreover, Vogel, Woodman and Luck [81] find that individuals can store up to four or five objects in their working memory, independent of the number of features (e.g., color or orientation). Reporting similar experimental findings, Lorigo et al. [53] claim that only the first three search results draw attention, with the remaining usually receiving little or no attention. These findings collectively suggest that the human upper storage limit comprises several items (e.g., four or five) owing to the limited neurological capacity of users’ visual working memory, and that this limit lowers precipitously beyond a handful, as the complexity and visual information load of the items increase [2].

## Eye-Tracking Research

The application of neuroscientific knowledge to the design and use of ISs and the measurement of their effectiveness is becoming increasingly popular in various scholarly domains [3, 20, 34, 51, 82]. Psychophysiological and neuroimaging tools (e.g., eye-tracking instruments, skin conductance response tools, functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), electroencephalography) have emerged as reliable measurement schemes to assess unique aspects of human behaviors that are impossible or difficult to observe using conventional mechanisms, such as questionnaires or self-report measures [20]. Neuroscientific tools can detect the unconscious processes that involve human judgments and behaviors on the basis of direct body signals. These data are generally free from subjectivity and social desirability bias and serve as ideal reference for measuring IS constructs that people are unable, embarrassed, or unwilling to truthfully report [20].

Among available psychophysiological tools, eye-tracking devices can effectively capture and quantify users’ selective visual attention behaviors. Research based on eye-tracking trajectories in the IS field can be grouped into two streams. The first seeks to understand conscious and subconscious human judgments and behaviors. For example, Cyr et al. [17] used eye-tracking methods to explore the effects of different types of human images in websites on image appeal and perceived social presence. Léger et al. [47] demonstrated that eye-tracking methods can be used to observe and assess precise human reaction to stimuli (pop-up stimuli in the study), while reducing the problems of self-report bias encountered in traditional measurement approaches. Fadel et al. [27] measured the gaze duration and viewing order of participants to determine the performance of knowledge workers in the knowledge filtering process. Djamasbi at al. [23] identified the different viewing and aesthetic preferences of the baby boomer generation and generation Y through an eyetracking experiment on a set of home pages. In the e-commerce context, Lee and Ahn [50] addressed the effectiveness of Internet banner ads and demonstrated that animation in such ads triggers the ad-avoidance phenomenon. Ye et al. [86] measured eye fixation duration and determined that long eye fixation on historical sale records are significantly associated with purchase behaviors in online marketplaces. Wang et al. [83] employed an eye-tracking approach to confirm the moderating role of task complexity in the relationship among web complexity, eye fixation count, and eye fixation duration in online shopping.

The second stream of research centers on the design and performance evaluation of IS. Twyman et al. [79] used eye-tracking instruments to evaluate the performance of an automated screening system in deception detection and measured the degree of pupil dilation to test the hypothesis that countermeasures against the system cannot deceive it. Anderson et al. [3] applied eye-tracking to design systems that effectively deliver security messages. In an eye-tracking experiment, the authors quantified habituation— attention reduction due to frequent exposure—a phenomenon that occurs unconsciously and is impossible to measure with orthodox methods. Despite the effectiveness of eye-tracking methods in illuminating human behaviors and system performance in various environments, there is limited understanding of attention decay, renewal, and equilibrium-seeking behaviors in the context of e-commerce shopping, wherein consumer attention is often diverted by various commercial advertisements. This study intends to fill this void.

## Hypotheses Development

Having established the theoretical building blocks of selective attention and visual working memory, we investigate the following three aspects of user attention allocation dynamics in an environment of multiple alternatives: attention adjustment, renewal, and equilibrium seeking.

## Attention Adjustment

A phenomenon known as “attentional blink” [66], a failure of visual selection attention [59], provides neurological explanations for sequential attention decay and offers a more elegant theoretical framework for understanding consumer attention behaviors during product search and browsing processes. When two targets (e.g., T1 and T2) are shown in close temporal proximity (200 to 500 milliseconds), individuals tend to successfully detect the first of the two targets, but often fail to recognize the second accurately. This salient visual and attentional processing pattern is referred to as attentional blink. The first stimulus (T1) receives the highest weighting; it consumes the bulk of the attentional resources, hindering the identification of the second target stimulus (T2), that is, allocating attention to T1 leaves less attentional resources for T2, rendering the latter susceptible to decay [66]. When applying the attentional blink model to a multi-product search and browsing environment, consumers are expected to pay the highest attention to the first few stimuli in the top position and abruptly reduce the attention paid to ensuing alternatives.

The attention blink model is grounded in the underpinnings of primacy effects, which occur when individuals who suffer from limited cognitive capabilities disproportionately pay greater attention to the information they receive early on than the information presented later [5]. These position-driven effects are known to affect the attitude formation, purchase intention, liking of an advertisement, and brands [33]. Lohse [52] finds that Yellow Page users are inclined to view and choose advertisements that are placed at the top of the alphabetical list. Such positional advantages explain why managers place high margin products at the top of a menu at restaurants [22].

Although the fact that attention decays across alternatives positioned sequentially may not be surprising, little is known about how the pattern of attention decay and the concentration of attention differ in response to the number of available alternatives. How does the rate at which the attenuation of sequential attention varies depending on the search space (a contextual factor reflecting the size of the information-processing load)? Will attention decay for a given product occur more slowly (or more quickly) when a relatively small number of alternatives are available for searching, as opposed to when a large number of selections are presented? For example, do consumers facing 30 alternatives reduce their attention three times faster and quicker than consumers encountering 10 alternatives?<sup>3</sup> Of the two scenarios, which exhibits a more long-tail distribution of attention?

Although scholars in the domains of working memory and selective attention generally agree on the cognitive limits of human storage capacity, they differ in their interpretation of the “nature” of such capacity boundaries. Some researchers (e.g., Luck and Vogel [55] assert that working memory stores only a discrete, fixed number of items irrespective of their complexity or the size of the informationprocessing load. Others (e.g., [88]) suggest that working memory is a pool of various resources that can be distributed flexibly, according to the extent of the information-processing requirements. These two conflicting views on the flexibility of working memory have prompted calls for more nuanced inquiries into the modality of attention decay patterns in product search environments. According to the fixed working memory model, the non-linear attention decay pattern does not alter significantly, regardless of the scale of the search space. Individuals tend to pre-determine their memory allocation management. Thus, they are unlikely to dynamically tune their attention levels for alternatives contingent on search scale during their search. By contrast, the variable working memory model proposes that individuals incorporate the size of the search space into their information processing and proactively refine their attention-allocation behaviors according to its dimension. Given that choice overload is one of the main causes of consumer confusion, which leads to suboptimal purchase decisions [57], consumers dynamically change their search strategies contingent on search space, and pay attention to items listed at the top of search results due to primacy effects.

Research (e.g., Einhorn [25]) has shown that as the amount of information presented increases, decision-makers attempt to avoid complexity by simplifying the choicemaking and judgment process. Such simplifying processes lead to stronger primacy propensities, as individuals form judgments on the basis of information presented early in the process. Primacy effects become more pronounced as the number of alternatives increases. All else being equal, top-ranked items determined from a large pool of alternatives (e.g., 30 items) are perceived to be more valuable and trustworthy than topranked items drawn from a small pool of alternatives (e.g., 10 items). Consequently, consumers pay more attention to top-ranked items selected from a larger search space than to those selected from a smaller space. In addition, all else being equal, value variations between top-ranked items (e.g., between the first- and second-ranked) drawn from a large number of alternatives are generally smaller than those between similarly ranked items drawn from a small number of alternatives. This smaller value variation stimulates consumers to spend more time on highly ranked alternatives beyond the first-ranked item. Thus, attention decay for the top-ranked items occurs more slowly as the number of alternatives increases. Therefore, we posit the following<sup>4</sup>:

Hypothesis 1. Attention Adjustment Effect: The larger the number of alternatives, the greater is the attention placed on the top-ranked alternatives.

## Attention Renewal

Visual distractions impede users’ sustained attention, usually degrading their task performance. For example, in web environments, irrelevant visual stimuli, such as flashing banner ads, may interfere with user concentration on search engagement and often cause them to stop browsing [50]. However, visual interferences can also have positive side effects. From a working-memory perspective, visual distractions that interrupt actions such as information search or visual scanning may lead to involuntary attention switching [71], subsequently, reversing attention-decaying behaviors. Visual interventions, particularly those inciting stimuli that grab users’ attention (e.g., ads displaying big discounts), can halt or decelerate users’ attention-decaying practices, at least temporarily, and perceptually arouse them to renew attention. A momentary exposure to different stimuli may interrupt consumers’ routine cognitive thoughtprocessing trajectories (e.g., attention decay) and induce them to unconsciously refresh their working memory by freeing up the information stored in their brains [71]. When resuming their search tasks, users may then involuntarily recommence their attentiondecaying process at the onset of the search event.

To illustrate, consider the product search setting shown later in Figure 5. Here, a large, enticing ad stimulus is displayed in the center of the search results, abruptly altering its structure. Consumers performing a top-to-bottom sequential search will exhibit attention decay after inspecting the top-ranked items and will maintain low levels of attention thereafter. However, when unexpectedly exposed to ad stimuli during the course of a search, consumers are likely to temporarily discontinue their search process and consciously or unconsciously divert their attention to the visual distractions. The visual working memory framework suggests that consumers, when visually exposed to highly seductive stimuli during the course of their product search, may wipe out their prior visual and information processing and restart by redirecting their attention to the remaining alternatives.

Furthermore, visual distractions located in the center of search results may also produce categorization effects, positively influencing users’ attention renewal intent. This happens because such visual distractions spatially and perceptually divide the sequentially listed alternatives (e.g., products) into categories, and subsequently, change users’ mental cognitive structures [13, 58]. The perceptual categorization of alternatives may help consumers refine their set of alternatives [19]. According to studies on the mere categorization effects (e.g.,[58]), the presence of categories increases consumers’ perceptions of assortment variety and, subsequently, affects their satisfaction positively. That is, because consumers are more willing to choose among seemingly distinct alternatives, categorization is an effective way of increasing consumer satisfaction [68, 76]. Consequently, categorization through different visual stimuli may weaken consumers’ propensities to attenuate their attention during sequential searches.

Mogilner, Rudnick and Iyengar [58] observed the “mere categorization effect,” in which it is not the category label but the categorization itself that increases the perception of variety and consumer satisfaction. The presence of categories might serve as a perceptual cue signaling the differences among options, even when those options and their differences remain unchanged. Because categories allow consumers to perceive greater variety in the choice set, the increased number of categories may alleviate the detrimental effects of choice overload. Consequently, the mere categorization effect, driven by different visual stimuli, may change consumer perceptions of assortment structures and their attention-allocation mechanism, leading to attention renewal. Therefore, we posit the following, concerning attention-renewal patterns in e-commerce product browsing:

Hypothesis 2. Attention Renewal Effect: When consumers perceptually encounter a new category of alternatives due to exposure to different stimuli, they are inclined to renew their attention.

## Attention Equilibrium-Seeking

Previous conceptual frameworks and empirical findings collectively suggest that consumers facing multiple-choice situations in online product search environments will exhibit progressive, non-linear, top-to-bottom attention decay propensities for search outcomes. Responding to many available alternatives, consumers wisely devote the bulk of their attention to a few top-ranked items and pay limited attention to choices in the middle or bottom sections of search outcomes [53]. Prior research (e.g., Peterson and Merino [61]) indicates that users’ online behaviors when searching for information are more structured and systematic than any of their other forms of information gathering. Regardless of search engines and search results, consumers have a strong tendency to sequentially browse through the results based on the search rankings from top to bottom [30].

How will consumers’ propensity to reduce their attention, as they move from top to bottom through the search rankings, change after stimuli from advertisements cause them to renew attention? Will attention decay occur more rapidly or slowly after attention renewal in online search environments? Kahneman’s limited capacity model of attention [41] might offer valuable theoretical insights into the dynamics of attention decay and renewal. According to the limited capacity framework, the total amount of attention resources an individual allocates to engage in mental activities for a given task is limited. Similarly, Knowles [43] asserted that human agents have a “pool” of processing resources from which attention activities are funded; however, this pool is of limited capacity. Human operators tend to amend their attention allocation policy from one moment to another depending on several factors, such as momentary intentions, arousal intensity, and enduring dispositions [41]. If one activity demands more resources, fewer resources will be available for another activity.

The limited capacity perspective [41] suggests that attention decay occurs more rapidly after attention renewal than it does before. Since individuals have already allocated additional attention and cognitive efforts to such arousal events (i.e., ad stimuli), they deliberately diminish the “spare capacity” allocated to the ongoing task (i.e., search and browsing) following attention renewal driven by ad stimuli. To deal with limited attention capacity, individuals will consciously or unconsciously exhibit natural balancing or equilibrium-seeking behaviors using their cognitive feedback circuits [15]. In doing so, subsequent to attention renewal, consumers accelerate the search process and radically reduce the attention resources available to remaining alternatives. The equilibrium-seeking behaviors are their heuristics used to reduce search intensities for additional information [10], which would be manifested by the rapid attention decay.

From a neurological view, human agents are inclined to maintain “cognitive homeostasis” or mental stability in which the state of disequilibrium caused by attention renewal stimulates the cognitive reevaluation of the ongoing task and controls the allocation and consumption of mental resources. Kozloski defines cognitive homeostasis as “a process by which variables that change brain dynamics are carefully regulated so that properties of brain state transitions (and thus brain information processing and behavioral dynamics) remain relatively stable under constant neuromodulatory conditions” [46; 14]. In our brain systems, several control functions that support normal behaviors, percepts, affect, and cognition attempt to self-regulate behaviors and stabilize the unbalance or disequilibrium created by abnormal actions. In line with these individual traits, we argue that consumers seek to maintain mental stability after the occurrence of attention renewal, and as a result, attention decay occurs more rapidly after than before attention renewal. Therefore, we posit the following attention-equilibrium seeking pattern in e-commerce product browsing:

Hypothesis 3. Attention Equilibrium Effect: Attention decay occurs more rapidly after attention renewal than before.

## Experiments

To investigate attention-allocation patterns and test the corresponding hypotheses, we performed two separate experiments: Experiment 1 and Experiment 2. The goal of Experiment 1 was to validate Hypothesis 1 concerning attention adjustment, while the intention of Experiment 2 was to test Hypotheses 2 and 3 related to attention renewal and equilibrium-seeking behaviors. The experimental methods and results for the two studies are described in depth in the following section.

## Experiment 1

## Participants

Ninety-seven e-commerce users took part in the within-subject experiment, in which all the participants were exposed to the treatment (i.e., different numbers of alternatives).<sup>5</sup> The online retailing company that collaborated on this research hired a professional marketing agent who recruited the participants from a consumer database. To enhance the diversity and representativeness of the population, stratified random sampling (SRS) grounded in two major criteria (i.e., gender and age) was used following the distribution of actual user profiles in commercial e-commerce websites (see Table 1). As remuneration for participating, the users received a gift card valued at US \$10, redeemable at online commerce websites.

To validate Hypothesis 1, we chose different types of clothing and varied the number of alternatives available to each participant. We selected t-shirts and jeans for the experiments, as these are the most popular items in the clothing category.<sup>6</sup> We manipulated the number of alternatives presented to participants, each of whom was offered either 10 or 30 alternatives. These experimental settings resulted in four different conditions (2 types of clothes [T-shirts, Jeans] X 2 different number of alternatives [10, 30]). Simulation webpages were developed to reflect each experimental scenario. The pages reflected those of top e-commerce retailing sites; the similarities reduced the participants’ feeling of unfamiliarity and helped them to concentrate on the experiments. Information about each alternative included a product image, product description, benefits, price, delivery information, seller information, and user satisfaction score (see Figure 1).

## Experiment Procedure

In the within-subject experimental setting, we alternated the types of clothes to eliminate the effects of clothing type. For instance, after the participants were exposed to jeans in 10 alternative cases, they were given T-shirts in 30 alternative conditions, and vice versa. Online Appendix 1 illustrates the scenario with 10 T-shirts labeled from A1 to A10, top to bottom. To control for a potential order effect, we randomized the sequence of alternatives.

The experiments were performed in a quiet laboratory without distractions. When the participants entered the room, they were guided to chairs in front of a Tobii T120 eye-tracker (see Lamm, Meltzoff and Decety [48] for the technical details). The T120 eye-tracker requires no additional head-mounting equipment but does require a calibration check of approximately 30 seconds before an experiment can be initiated. We used Tobii Studio 3.0 software for calibration and recording. The calibration process is set by the software and takes about 30 seconds. The calibration mechanisms and rules are consistent with those described in Hansen and Ji [32]. These devices use infrared diodes to generate reflection patterns on the corneas of the user’s eyes. Image sensors collect these reflection patterns, along with other visual information about the subject. A sophisticated image-processing algorithm programmed by the T120 eye-tracker identifies relevant features, including the corneal reflection patterns. A complex mathematical procedure is used to calculate the three-dimensional position of each eyeball, and ultimately, the subject’s gaze point.

After 30 seconds of calibration, participants were instructed to browse alternatives and select the product they intended to purchase by adding it to their shopping cart, as they would in reality. The participants were allowed to control the time they spent on browsing, selecting, and clicking on the alternatives when additional product information was needed. To refresh the participants’ attention, pictures (i.e., three separate landscape images) were shown after the first task. Then, the participants proceeded to the second task, which presented other product types and alternatives. This refreshing procedure is necessary for minimizing the effects of the participants’ visual attention behaviors on the succeeding experiment. Each experiment lasted from 5 to 15 minutes. Data on all the participants’ eye movements were collected while they were searching for and choosing products.

## Measurement Items

Using an eye-tracking device, we measured attention intensity on the basis of two distinct variables, namely, eye-fixation and eye-saccade.<sup>8</sup> Eye-fixation is considered to occur when eyes are fixated stably on a specific area of the display for a certain period (100 milliseconds in our experiment) required for basic visual processing. Eye saccade is defined as rapid eye-movement between any two eye-fixations. Therefore, an eye-fixation represents a series of eye gaze vector data points when the target stimuli are stationary. During fixation, detailed visual information is processed, the data related to fixations serving as a reasonable proxy to measure consumer attention [49, 67]. Since information acquired through eyes is often too vast to be processed consciously, users tend to segregate the information requiring further processing and fixate their eyes on selected locations. The eye-tracking device can measure fixation frequency (i.e., total number of eye fixations on a target stimuli), fixation duration (i.e., total duration of eye fixation on a target stimuli), the location of the first fixation, and the time of the first fixation [24]. As our interest is in measuring online consumers’ attention to each alternative, fixation duration and fixation frequency were selected as measurement items [85].

## Results for Experiment 1

In total, 97 e-commerce users were recruited for the experiment with the help of a professional recruitment company. Among the participants, five failed to successfully complete the 30-second calibration check required by the eye-tracking system before its operation. Therefore, we excluded these five individuals to avoid any inaccuracies in eye-tracking data. Consequently, experimental data from 92 participants were collected for each case (a case of 10 alternatives and a case of 30 alternatives). In the case of 10 alternatives, 87 valid samples were used after five of them were excluded because of data incompleteness. In the case of 30 alternatives, 71 samples were used after removing incomplete data.

We expect that attention is unevenly distributed across the displayed alternatives and decays non-linearly from top to bottom. To test this intuition, we gathered and analyzed the fixation frequency and duration for each alternative. The data analysis in the case of 10 alternatives reveals that alternative location was negatively correlated with fixation frequency (−0.71 [p-value < 0.01]), as it was with total fixation duration (−0.76 [p-value <0.01]; see Figure 2).

These findings suggest that lower the alternative in the search outcome hierarchy, lesser the attention paid in terms of fixation frequency and fixation duration.<sup>9</sup> Similar analyses for the case of 30 alternatives revealed almost the same pattern. A negative correlation exists between alternative location and fixation frequency (correlation = −0.69, p-value < 0.01). Similarly, the location of alternatives was negatively related to fixation duration (correlation = −0.85, p-value < 0.01; see Figure 3).

To compare the patterns of attention concentration of the two scenarios, we used the Lorenz curve and Gini coefficient measurement schemes [12], both of which are statistical measures of inequality among the values of a frequency distribution. For example, the Gini coefficients used in this study represent inequality of attention distribution, and the stronger the coefficient, the more concentrated the distribution of attention. The derived Gini coefficients were 0.222 in the case of 10 alternatives and 0.428 in the case of 30 alternatives. Similarly, Figure 4 presents two Lorenz curves: the dashed and solid curves represent the 10 and 30 alternative cases, respectively. The Lorenz curve for the 10 cases lies above that for the 30 cases and this pattern suggests that the 30 alternative cases exhibit a more concentrated distribution of attention than the 10 cases. From the Lorenz curve, we can easily calculate the percentage of attention concentration by the bottom percentile (e.g., 80%). For the 10 alternative cases, the bottom 80% alternatives attract approximately 60% of the total attention. In contrast, for the 30 alternative cases, the same 80% elicit only 42% of the total attention. In other words, the top 20% alternatives attract 40% and 58% of the total attention for the 10 and 30 alternative cases, respectively. This empirical regularity suggests that consumer attention is more concentrated in the top-ranked options, as the number of alternatives increases. This pattern remains unchanged even after controlling for differences in the number of alternatives using proportional values.

Although Figure 4 suggests that differences exist between the two cases in terms of the attention concentration pattern, there is no assessment of whether such differences are statistically significant. To determine statistical validation, we fit the fixation frequency and rank data to the log-linear model specified in Equation 1, and then compare the rank coefficient $( \beta _ { 1 } )$ values for the two scenarios.

$$
\ln \left(\text { fixation } _ {i}\right) = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \ln (\text { rank } _ {i}) + 1\tag{1}
$$

The independent variable, ran $\mathrm { \zeta } _ { i } ,$ represents a percentile ranking of the alternatives sequentially listed. It should be noted that we used percentile ranks [16] rather than regular ranks to control for differences in the number of alternatives within each scenario. A percentile rank refers to the percentage of scores in its frequency that falls at or below a certain score [16]. For example, the top 10 percentile rank for the 10 alternative cases refers to only one alternative ranked 1. However, the top 10 percentile for the 30 alternative cases includes three alternatives ranked 1, 2, and 3. The curve derived from Equation 1 is often called a Pareto curve [12]. The coefficient, $\beta _ { 1 }$ , describes how quickly the frequency of fixation decreases as the rank increases. If the 10 alternative cases exhibit a longer tail than the 30 alternative cases, after controlling for the difference in the number of alternatives, the magnitude of the negative coefficient is expected to be lower in the former case (i.e., 10 alternatives) than in the latter one (i.e., 30 alternatives).

Table 2 shows the Pareto curve estimations for Equation 1. Both coefficients for percentile rank $( \beta _ { 1 } )$ in Models 1 and 2 are significant at the 99% confidence level and the high R-squares (97% and 94% for Models 1 and 2, respectively) indicate that the log-linear specification strongly fits our data. These results show that fixation decreases significantly as rank increases. Next, we pooled both the 10 and 30 alternative cases into one dataset to determine whether the $\beta _ { 1 }$ coefficient specified in Equation 1 is less negative for the 10 alternative cases than for the 30 alternative cases. A dummy variable, Case30<sub>i</sub>, was included to indicate whether an observation belongs to the 30 alternative cases (Equation 2).

## Methods for Experiment 2

Participants. Seventy online commerce users participated in the experiment. As in Experiment 1, we used SRS based on gender and age to recruit participants. The participants were 36 men and 34 women aged less than 20 years (4), 20 to 29 years (29), 30 to 39 years (23), and over 40 years (14). This composition is close to the actual user distribution. As a monetary incentive, a gift card worth US \$10 was given to each participant.

Procedures. The same product category (clothing) and alternative formats (10 vs. 30 alternatives) employed in Experiment 1 were used in this experiment, but the structure of the selections was changed to validate H2 and H3. The first segment at the top of the webpage consisted of 10 alternatives, and the second segment at the bottom of the page comprised 30 alternatives. An animated advertisement was placed between the two groups of alternatives, (Figure 5). Positioning an advertisement immediately after the 10 alternatives enabled not only an analysis of advertising effects, but also a comparison of 10 or 30 alternatives with ads (Experiment 2) and without ads (Experiment 1). Fixation frequency and total fixation duration were measured to investigate attention renewal (H2) and equilibrium-seeking (H3) effects.

Experimental data on the T-shirts and jeans were collected from the 73 participants. Among them, we excluded three data points due to incompleteness, leaving 140 samples. We analyzed the two groups separately and obtained attention decay patterns similar to those observed in Experiment 1. The attention decay patterns detected for groups 1 and 2 were analogous to those reflected in Figure 2 (case of 10) and Figure 3 (case of 30), respectively. ## Patterns of Attention Decay before and after Exposure to Ad Stimuli

The results for Hypothesis 3 point to another intriguing aspect of attention allocation dynamics in the form of attention equilibrium-seeking propensity. Subsequent to attention renewal, attention decay tends to accelerate at a much faster rate than before renewal (−0.809 before ad encounter and −2.537 after encounter, Table 3). Figure 7 shows a schematic of attention renewal and attention equilibrium-seeking patterns.

To gain additional insights into attention equilibrium-seeking behavior, we used fixation frequency and total fixation time as bases in validating the mean difference between two scenarios: one with and the other without stimulus ads. No statistical difference exists between the two scenarios $( p < 0 . 0 1$ , Table 4); fixation frequency and time spent are 69.82 (without ads) versus 66.79 (with ads) and 27.69 (without ads) versus 27.67 (with ads), respectively. Attention renewal driven by stimulating ads increased the extent of attention, but such increases are offset by rapid attention reduction after renewal. Consumers either consciously or subconsciously balance their overall attention resources, regardless of the presence of attention-diverting stimuli. Additionally, both the standard deviations of fixation frequency and total fixation time significantly decrease when ads are displayed, suggesting that on such occasions, consumers browse search results evenly. Although ads located in the middle of search results lists do not attract more attention, they compel the even distribution of attention, increasing the possibility of product exposure. This finding may pose important implications for the design and organization of retail websites.

## Robustness Check

We carried out an additional experiment as a robustness check to determine whether the observed attention renewal pattern can be attributed to an alternative explanation. One may argue that consumers can regard Group 1 (items 1 through 10) and Group 2 (items 11 through 30) as two separate lists, regardless of the presence of an advertising stimulus. To determine the plausibility of this explanation, we designed a new between-subject experiment for 51 participants, whose demographic characteristics are similar to participants in Study 2. In the experiment, they are exposed to the two product lists (T-shirt and jeans) sequentially, with or without advertisement. For example, if a T-shirt list without advertisement is exposed for participant 1, then a jeans list with advertisement is exposed later. For the next participant, a T-shirt list with advertisement is exposed, and a jeans list without advertisement is exposed later. Through this between-subject experiment, a participant is never exposed to the same product list with and without advertisements, while controlling the possible learning and familiarity effects.

We observed whether the extent of attention renewal varies depending on the presence of the advertisement. To better control for possible learning and familiarity effects, we asked the participants to read online cartoons between the tasks. Among the responses derived, four were unusable because of a calibration issue. Thus, the effective data for analysis were those obtained from 51 participants. The experimental design was analogous to those implemented in Study 2. Figure 8 presents the results. Regardless of the advertisement, the same attention decay pattern in terms of total duration and frequency occurred in the new experiment. However, attention renewal was detected only when the advertising banner was shown to the participants in the middle of the list. Specifically, the differences of the fixation frequency and duration were statistically significant for item 11 $( \mathrm { t } { = } { - } 4 . 3 9 , \mathrm { p } { < } . 0 0 1 )$ and item 12 (t= −3.49, p < .001) in Group 2. This finding rules out the alternative explanation and reinforces the validity of our main results.

## Discussion and Implications

The behaviors of consumers beset with multiple product options in the online and mobile marketplace continue to interest researchers and practitioners. In this study, we examined unique regularities in consumers’ visual attention allocation behaviors, such as attention adjustment, attention renewal, and attention equilibrium seeking, each presenting important academic and practical implications. Unlike self-reported data, the information derived with neuro-physiological devices is less susceptible to bias [20] because such tools capture the eye movements of consumers in real time, thereby, reflecting the patterns and trajectories of consumer heuristics and their evaluation of multiple product options.

Hypothesis 1 shows fine granularity concerning the specific patterns and trajectories through which visual attention decays in the presence of multiple options in an e-commerce search and browsing environment. Attention decays in a non-linear manner from the top to the bottom of a webpage; however, decay patterns differ significantly depending on the number of displayed options. With a greater volume of choices available for evaluation, the participants more intently concentrate on the top-ranked choices. Such focus rapidly dissipates thereafter. This selective attention confirms that consumers either consciously or subconsciously adjust attention to maximize their limited visual working memory.

While the literature indirectly demonstrates the value of positional advantage in search outcomes, this study seeks to quantify the extent to which visual attention is distributed across multiple options and the extent to which it declines as consumers vertically scroll through a search results list. For example, research on sponsored search engines (e.g., Agarwal, Hosanagar, and Smith [1]; Animesh, Ramachandran, and Viswanathan [4]; and Ghose and Yang [30]) have employed quantitative schemes (e.g., click-through rates, or “CTRs”) to measure the effectiveness of advertisements. Although CTRs reveal the level of attention paid to all search outcomes in aggregate form, they may not precisely capture the attention devoted to each option displayed or illustrate how users distribute attention across a variety of options. While CTRs can serve as a measure of action, eye tracking is a measure of attention.

The findings provide evidence of attention renewal propensities (Hypothesis 2), in which attention decay ceases and attention to remaining options renews when consumers are exposed to unexpected stimuli. To the best of our knowledge, no such renewal behaviors have been reported as a salient attention allocation mechanism. In the literature, the scrutiny of attention renewal has been largely neglected, although consumer attention-allocation behaviors reflect a dynamic, iterative psychological process that is highly contingent on sensory and perceptual environmental cues [21]. Experimental findings suggest that attention-decaying trajectories recur when consumers are exposed to stimuli that divert their attention. More strikingly, a post-hoc analysis reveals that consumers disregard ad stimuli presented in the middle of options lists and try to bypass them quickly. The amount of stimuli from these ads is similar to that induced by options listed at the bottom of a page (e.g., A10) in terms of average fixation count. Furthermore, the fixation duration analysis shows that consumers gaze at attention-diverting ads for roughly 0.50 seconds, far less than the time spent looking at product options presented right before the ads.

We offer plausible explanations for this attention renewal phenomenon. Although consumers often ignore attention-diverting stimuli, they unwittingly sense the presence of such inducements through peripheral sensory cues [56]. Cognition of these stimuli arrests on-going decay trajectories and compels a re-initiation of decay routines. Consequently, options positioned immediately after ad stimuli receive a level of attention similar to top-ranked choices, regardless of overall ranking. Although ad stimulus itself is an insignificant factor in attracting consumer attention, it produces positive outcomes for attention decay dynamics by triggering attention renewal. Ad stimuli structurally separate search choices into mental categories and eliminate ongoing attention decay patterns. Consumers may perceive options positioned immediately after an ad as belonging to a different product category, thereby, motivating them to revert to the attention decay process.

This finding is in line with the mere categorization effect observed by Mogilner, Rudnick, and Iyengar [58], who suggest that the mere presence of particular perceptual factors can be instrumental to consumers’ cognitive judgments and behaviors. They demonstrated that the presence of category labels in an option display increases consumer satisfaction. Building on this finding, we show that mere categorization may not only increase consumers’ satisfaction with a chosen product but also the level of attention that they pay to search options. Consistent with previous studies (e.g., Broniarczyk, Hoyer, and McAlister [9] and Kahn and Wansink [40]), the categorization effect may positively influence consumer perceptions on product variety.

This study contributes to extant scholarship through its application of a physiological methodology to IS and e-commerce research. An extensive body of literature has examined how consumers choose from an array of sequentially listed product options; however, the vast majority of relevant e-commerce studies have concentrated on perceptual and subjective approaches (e.g., survey questionnaires). Although such indirect mechanisms can enhance our understanding of consumers’ attention allocation behaviors, they fall short of accurately accounting for the phenomena because of inherent inaccuracies and cognitive biases. The intrinsic methodological shortcomings of these subjective approaches have precluded a broad generalization of findings. The eye-tracking mechanisms employed in the current research redress such oversights by capturing the spontaneous visual attention behaviors of users.

## Implications for Practice

The results of this study suggest several actionable strategies for managing exhaustive options lists. With exposure to vertically displayed choices, consumers’ attention span decreases exponentially and not linearly, as they progress from the top to the bottom of a page. When 10 options are available, consumers pay significant attention to only the top-ranked items and accord progressively less attention to lower-ranked choices. After the top two options, the rest receive a minimum level of attention. In fact, no difference was detected between the third and tenth options in terms of fixation frequency and duration. However, consumers adjust their attention decay patterns, either consciously or unconsciously, in accordance with the number of options on display. The results indicate that the rate of attention decay decelerates when 30 options are presented as opposed to when 10 choices are shown. Interestingly, the moderate decay pattern observed in the 30-option scenario persists only up to a certain position (e.g., A5 or A6), with no significant difference thereafter. These findings suggest that clutter caused by excessive options in a limited space is unfavorable. More than 60% of the choices in the 30-option case are disregarded. Furthermore, preferential treatment for highly ranked selections should be flexibly adjusted, commensurate to the total number of available options.

By manipulating the arrangement of product choices through grouping or categorizing, vendors may be able to elicit increased attention from consumers. A visual advertisement placed amid a search results list artificially creates option categories because it divides the list into two parts. Such ads also drive consumers to distribute attention more evenly, although they do not inspire increased attention. Consumers’ attention allocation behaviors are not rigid and are vulnerable to external factors. Assortment display structures affect the ease with which pertinent information is retrieved and processed, which in turn, determines the quality of consumers’ decision-making and motivation to choose [31]. In the context of offline shopping, consumer perceptions of variety are not necessarily determined by the number of options presented; instead, they are shaped by how these options are grouped and displayed. By manipulating display features, such as shelf space and disarray, marketers can appeal to consumers’ desire for variety without actually offering more options [9, 40].

This study puts forward additional insights into the paradox of choice [37]. Retailers appeal to consumers’ desire for variety by offering them an ever-increasing number of choices [39]. However, this strategy is negated by the fact that an extensive choice set decreases both consumer satisfaction and willingness to purchase, because it imposes upon consumers an overwhelming information-processing task that exceeds cognitive resources and capacities [7]. This study’s findings may shed light on this choice paradox as follows: simply grouping options into clusters may increase consumers’ cognitive resources and refresh their attention span for further product search and satisfaction.

## Limitations and Future Research

This study is subject to several limitations. First, the findings are based solely on a series of laboratory experiments, and cannot, therefore, be fully generalizable to selections among all product alternatives. Although we made substantial efforts to control for potential variations in our experimental settings (e.g., web designs, demographic factors) relative to real-world markets, the laboratory environment may have triggered unnatural participant behavior. Second, attention allocation dynamics can be more or less influenced by the choice of product categories. For example, product involvement, product characteristics, and product knowledge may all affect consumers’ attention allocation patterns [11]. We acknowledge that our exclusive use of clothing products may limit the generalizability of our findings. Thus, future studies should use other product categories. Third, we did not measure participants’ perception. If we measured the perception of stimuli as unwanted ads, we can better understand the impact of stimuli on attention renewal. If we used the self-reported measure on why visual attention dropped sharply after stimuli, we can further validate equilibrium-seeking behavior and analyze the coping strategies they are adopting [10].

Several areas warrant further research. As consumer behaviors are determined not by the assortment size of the product alternatives but by the consumer’s perceived assortment size [28]. Depending on the assortment layout and the amount of information provided to consumers, attention allocation patterns can be amended. This study examined only a single assortment structure and information format. An interesting natural extension of this study would be to investigate a variety of assortment structures and information-presentation formats. Cultural differences [69] and informationsearching type [72, Simon [75]] may serve as additional moderators of consumer attention allocation. For example, “preference matchers” familiar with a given domain will not be as easily influenced by mere categorization, as will “preference constructors” who have yet to identify their preferred attribute combinations. Consequently, future studies should reflect on the salient characteristics of both individuals and products to more comprehensively assess consumers’ attention adjustment, renewal, and equilibrium-seeking patterns.

Using a think-aloud protocol in the usability study will enable the further validation of equilibrium-seeking behaviors and the analysis of the coping strategies adopted by consumers [10]. Eye tracking reveals only what consumers’ see and not what they think. A think-aloud protocol is a means of understanding the cognitive processes of consumers through verbalization [26, 36]. Its presentation during or after a task may provide additional insight into eye-tracking, a direction that can be pursued in future research.

## Conclusion

Given the increasing prevalence of search technologies that generate sequential listings of numerous search outcomes and the current lack of research on consumer attention allocation behaviors, this study sought to explore the attention allocation dynamics of consumers in terms of attention adjustment, renewal, and equilibriumseeking. From a retailer’s perspective, the findings suggest that capturing consumers’ attention online is a highly competitive enterprise and poses oppressively burdensome managerial challenges. Approximately 20% of the options displayed in search outcomes receive a fair level of attention from consumers, whereas consumers almost completely ignore a greater number of choices, particularly those positioned at the bottom of a search results listing. Nevertheless, the total number of available options significantly regulates attention decay speeds and patterns. More importantly, in the tendency of attention to diminish, movement does not solely lean toward depletion; the presence of attention-diverting stimuli can refresh and renew attention. Often considered disruptive and worthless, ad stimuli can bring forth unexpected positive effects in rejuvenating consumers’ attention resources.

We also found that consumers tend to regulate their overall attention resources. Subsequent to significant attention renewal, consumers consciously or unconsciously reduce the attention that they devote to remaining options substantially. Consequently, overall attention allocation remains nearly unchanged, regardless of whether stimulating ads are present. Consumers’ attention allocation behaviors are complex and perplexing because of the dynamic interactions among attention adjustment, renewal, and equilibrium seeking. Our study still requires extension to include additional treatments and controls for greater generalizability, but its findings serve as a point of departure toward understanding the complicated nature of consumers’ attention-distributing and choice-making behaviors.
