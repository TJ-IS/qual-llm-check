# Illuminating the underground: the reality of unauthorised file sharing

Jenine Beekhuyzen, Liisa von Hellens & Sue Nielsen

Institute for Integrated and Intelligent Systems, Grif<sup>fi</sup>th University, Brisbane, Queensland 4111, Australia, email: j.beekhuyzen@grif<sup>fi</sup>th.edu.au

Abstract. This paper presents a new conceptualisation of online communities by exploring how an online community forms and is maintained. Many stakeholders in the music industry rightly point out that unauthorised <sup>fi</sup>le sharing is illegal, so why do so many people feel it is acceptable to download music without paying? Our study found highly cohesive, well-organised groups that were motivated by scarcity and the lack of high quality music <sup>fi</sup>les. Our ethnographic research provides insight into the values and beliefs of music <sup>fi</sup>le sharers: their demands are not currently being met. Using Actor-network theory, we are able to propose that the <sup>fi</sup>le sharers represent a growing potential market in the music industry and that music distribution systems should be developed accordingly to meet the demands of this user group. Therefore, this study can serve as a springboard for understanding unauthorised <sup>fi</sup>le sharing and perhaps other deviant behaviours using technology.

Keywords: underground music <sup>fi</sup>le sharing, actor-network theory, ethnography, online community

## INTRODUCTION

Underground music <sup>fi</sup>le sharing communities are online social spaces where individuals connect to share mostly unauthorised digital content. Unauthorised <sup>fi</sup>le sharing is an activity commonly referred to as piracy, with the term regularly used in public and academic discussions. Most academic authors do not attempt to de<sup>fi</sup>ne piracy because settling upon a speci<sup>fi</sup>c and internationally agreed de<sup>fi</sup>nition is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult; however, a de<sup>fi</sup>nition is necessary. Yar (2005) defines piracy' as an infringement of copyright manifested through the unauthorisec copying and distribution of copyrighted content for commercial gain. This de<sup>fi</sup>nition can be usefully applied in this study although the digital content shared within the community and examined for this study (Roswell) is not for commercial gain but instead downloaded and shared for personal use.

This paper presents a study of an underground music <sup>fi</sup>le sharing community. Roswell is a pseudonym for a research site that is a private and clandestine Australian online community of more than 1200 members who use BitTorrent peer-to-peer technology to share digital music and other media <sup>fi</sup>les. The community is conceptualised as a network in this paper using actor– network theory (ANT) to understand how a deviant community develops and stabilises the practices and motivations of its members. Conducted in a research site that few academics have access to the empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings in this study provide a new understanding of how online communities form and are shaped by members.

Many discussions of piracy in existing academic literature focus on determining and managing the costs of piracy (Sandararajan, 2004; Bagchi et al., 2006). More recent research examines perceptions of the legality and ethicality of downloading music <sup>fi</sup>les through <sup>fi</sup>le sharing and attempts to understand how ‘moral consideration’ works in this context (Hansen & Walden, 2013, p 521). Other research streams (Smith & Telang, 2009) studied the impact of free digita content (movies) on DVD sales and internet piracy and surprisingly found that ‘giving away content in one channel can stimulate sales in a paid channel if the free content is suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently differ entiated from its paid counterpart’ (p. 321). Smith and Telang argue that the presence of pirated content does not necessarily cannibalise sales for other digital content because free and paid products appeal to different customer segments (Smith & Telang, 2009).

Therefore, we ask the following research question:

How do information systems support unauthorised <sup>fi</sup>le sharing activities within an underground online community?

A signi<sup>fi</sup>cant ongoing dialogue on <sup>fi</sup>le sharing has been presented in the Communications of the Association for Computing Machinery over the past three decades, including the political motivation for the development of user-led systems (Franz & Robey, 1984); the emergence of electronic marketplaces on the Internet (Bakos, 1998); and the risks and conveniences of internet shopping in these electronic marketplaces (Bhatnagar et al., 2000). Then, Lam and Tan (2001) theorised about how the internet was changing the music industry.

Lee (2003) was one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst to empirically examine peer-to-peer <sup>fi</sup>le sharing from an end user perspective, arguing that ‘understanding what aspects of these systems really matter to the user is critical for designing better systems’ (p. 50). Premkumar (2003) presented a compre hensive analysis of alternative strategies for music distribution, whilst von Lohmann (2004) proposed another alternative that involved collective licencing schemes.

This paper builds on this literature to understand the <sup>fi</sup>le sharing activities and motivations o members of an underground <sup>fi</sup>le sharing community. We use ANT (Callon, 1986; Latour, 1987) as a lens for an ethnographic study to explain why and how a community was created. This lens emphasises the tracing of intricate networks and associations (Doolin & Lowe, 2002), revealing power relations. Unauthorised <sup>fi</sup>le sharing is complex and controversial; thus, ANT is a usefu theoretical perspective that helps to explain the intricate and <sup>fl</sup>uid relationships.

Central to ANT is the concept of translation that ‘advances an alternative framework for understanding the role of artifacts in everyday life’ (Shiga, 2006). This conceptualisation of translation helps explain why a centralised underground <sup>fi</sup>le sharing network such as Roswell was created and why the network forms the way it does. The translation process seeks to associate, combine and simplify elements of the network, transforming ‘weak, provisional, and generally de<sup>fi</sup>ned identities into durable and seemingly irreversible ties’ (Shiga, 2006). Callon (1986, p. 26) argues that if translation is successful, roles are well de<sup>fi</sup>ned, and the community is strengthened, as the principle actor ‘speaks for others but in its own language’ as a representative of the community.

This paper proceeds as follows. First, the literature on unauthorised <sup>fi</sup>le sharing is reviewed, piracy is de<sup>fi</sup>ned and the channels for music distribution are identi<sup>fi</sup>ed. Roswell is then situated within the theoretical understandings of unauthorised <sup>fi</sup>le sharing, and discussion of the methodology and the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of using an ethnographic approach are presented. Then, the translation within the actor-network is mapped through concepts of problematization, interessement, enrollment and mobilisation to reveal existing controversies and an understanding of how the network forms. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings, implications and limitations are discussed.

## LITERATURE REVIEW OF UNAUTHORISED FILE SHARING

Unauthorised <sup>fi</sup>le sharing may be viewed as the dark side of the entertainment world because of its associations with piracy and underground clandestine behaviour (Biddle et al., 2002). Yar (2005) presents a well-cited critical perspective by proposing that such activities represent socially constructed phenomena, resulting from evolving social, legal and political and economic pressures.

Such pressures can help account for motivations for unauthorised <sup>fi</sup>le sharing, including sampling (to ‘try before you buy’) and substitution (downloading instead of purchasing) (Andersen & Frenz, 2007; Quiring et al., 2008). In addition, unauthorised <sup>fi</sup>le sharing may be motivated by the desire to get access to content that is not copyrighted or that the copyright owner wants to give away for free (Lessig, 2004). Whilst <sup>fi</sup>le sharing in many instances is illegal, it is clear that we can no longer marginalise and criminalise these alternative ways of accessing digital music because, as von Lohmann puts it, ‘the genie is out of the bottle’ (2004, p. 21). Instead, the literature suggests that we should learn from <sup>fi</sup>le sharers (Hansen & Walden, 2013).

It is common to view all <sup>fi</sup>le sharing using BitTorrent technologies as unauthorised and illegal. However, in 2012, the BitTorrent file sharing company announced that it facilitated 124 191 862 legal music downloads in the <sup>fi</sup>rst 6 months of the year, composing 31% (around 405 million) of all BitTorrent downloads over that period (van Buskirk, 2012). Thus, many <sup>fi</sup>les shared through BitTorrent are not illegal; instead, they are authorised by the copyright owner highlighting that such technologies are becoming more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to ignore. These statistics also suggest that systems used for <sup>fi</sup>le sharing cannot all be condemned as only supporting illegal behaviour, creating complicated notions of <sup>fi</sup>le sharing and acceptable/unacceptable behaviour. Indeed, as this paper illustrates, <sup>fi</sup>le sharing is not only the practice of mere <sup>fi</sup>le sharers but also of musicians who authorise their music to be share and even representatives from the music recording industry. Garnering a deeper understanding of the motivations of such a diverse group of <sup>fi</sup>le sharers can provide much needed insight for those operating the legal systems of online music collections such as iTunes and Spotify. This paper argues that the conceptualisation of unauthorised <sup>fi</sup>le sharing as piracy according to Yar’s (2005) socially constructed perspective is helpful;

however, given the sheer number of people <sup>fi</sup>le sharing for personal use and not for commercial gain, the de<sup>fi</sup>nition needs modi<sup>fi</sup>cation or, at least, reconsideration.

Taking into account the various ways that digital music access and use are discussed in the research literature (Hansen & Walden, 2013; Kretschmer & Pratt, 2009), this paper identi<sup>fi</sup>es un derground unauthorised <sup>fi</sup>le sharing communities as possessing the following characteristics:

• The community is private with invitation-only membership as opposed to publicly accessible unauthorised <sup>fi</sup>le sharing systems, like Limewire, and public websites for unauthorised <sup>fi</sup>le sharing like thepiratebay.com.

• The community (website) and its access are also hidden from public view, and members are advised not to discuss the community in public places, such as forums.

• Members in the community trade in mostly unauthorised digital content that is considered illegal in most Western countries (Beekhuyzen et al., 2012).

We argue that unauthorised <sup>fi</sup>le sharing is a widespread response of a population of music users to the current mainstream accessibility of music similar to the growth in participation of open source development that democratises the creation of software (Bergquist & Ljungberg, 2001).

Underground <sup>fi</sup>le sharing communities, which can be considered as ‘exchange cultures (Raymond, 1999) or gift cultures, existed to share digital <sup>fi</sup>les long before the availability of com mercial systems such as iTunes (Thomas, 1993). What makes the underground communities worthy of investigation is that they were originally built for users by users, in contrast to iTunes and other systems designed with limited input by users (Mao & Zhang, 2008).

## THEORETICAL PERSPECTIVES ON UNAUTHORISED FILE SHARING

## An actor-network theory perspective

Actor-network theory emphasises the tracing of intricate networks and associations (Doolin & Lowe, 2002), and through organising these associations, power relations become visible. Through this framework, online communities like Roswell are given a voice and allowed to tell their own storey (Porsander, 2005). ANT investigations are situated within a particular time and place but without concern for historical context. The critical perspective is commensurable as it takes account of history and, in doing so, extends the ANT theoretical lens. The critica approach complements ANT to give an equal voice to all actors, empowering those (human and objects) that often do not get the opportunity to speak up. This is particularly important in the current political climate where those in power often marginalise and criminalise unauthorised <sup>fi</sup>le sharing behaviour.

Actor-network theory was adopted for this study, <sup>fi</sup>rstly, because it provides the tools to inves tigate the sensitive balance between the technical and social aspects of the community. For ex ample, Lee (2003) argues that peer-to-peer <sup>fi</sup>le sharing systems blur the distinction between the technologies that exist in the network and suggests that it is necessary to learn more about how individuals interact in this socio-technical network. Lee’s (2003) study gathers ‘an overall picture of how current users perceive existing <sup>fi</sup>le sharing systems, the importance of various features, and the concerns underlying these reactions’ (p 50), providing a foundation for this research.

Secondly, ANT has been useful to study a variety of situations where groups are marginalised or considered deviant, for example, in studies of drug users (Gomart, 2002) and ‘greening organisations’ (Newton, 2002). Contemporary online music access and use is a complex issue with many competing interests at stake. Social theories and their frameworks can help researchers to construct a variety of relevant worldviews enabling a focus on the everyday experience of music consumers (Shoib et al., 2006).

Finally, Alvesson & Deetz (2000) recommend the application of ANT with a critical approach to research because ANT offers a particularly effective way of increasing our insight and understanding of everyday life. This paper has adopted ANT with a critical approach to study the music <sup>fi</sup>le sharing phenomenon, since the actions of the <sup>fi</sup>le sharers are currently deemed to be deviant by many in society and the law.

Critics of ANT argue that it is not appropriate to use the same conceptual apparatus to ana lyse humans and nonhumans in the heterogeneous network; ANT treats technological and so cial elements as inseparable (Shoib et al., 2006). However, there is a strong information systems research stream using ANT to develop theoretical foundations (Walsham, 1997; Latour, 1999; Rose et al., 2005) and for the presentation of empirical data (Lanzara & Morner, 2005; Shoib et al., 2006). Additionally, ANT provides an interdisciplinary approach to studying issues of technology and society and is an established social theory for investigating information systems (Walsham, 1997). In this paper, ethnographic methods are used to identify instan tiations of relevant elements, which may assist in resolving the issues involved in applying ANT. In our study, ANT was useful for the analysis of ethnographic data as it provided tools for ob serving, describing and interpreting the formation of power within each of the groups of actors (Czarniawska & Hernes, 2005).

## The ethnographic perspective

Investigations of communities whose values and behaviours appear to contrast strongly with mainstream culture are well served by an ethnographic approach, helped by spending extended time in the <sup>fi</sup>eld. Through participant observations and interviews, we can begin to un derstand how the community formed and how the social arrangements have evolved as a result of what Thomas (1993) refers to as a ‘problem situation’ – in this case, it is the lack of ac cess to desirable digital music. These arrangements are based on the routines and rituals of ev eryday life of <sup>fi</sup>le sharers and are part of the daily practice of organising to stabilise the actor network (Callon, 1986; Latour, 1987).

Myers (1999) de<sup>fi</sup>nes ethnography by certain characteristics: spending a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant amount of time in the <sup>fi</sup>eld, immersion in the research context and placing the phenomena in the appropriate social and cultural context. The task as Wolcott (1990) phrases it is to ‘pull together nu merous observations of actual behaviour, coupled with insights and explanations to form some comprehensive blueprint, archetype code or set of implicit rules or standards for behaviour (1990, p. 55). Therefore, ethnography can provide rich insights into human behaviour by endeavouring to appropriately contextualise the behaviours (Walsham, 1997).

The ethnographic approach provides an opportunity for deep analysis resulting in thick descrip tions (Geertz, 1973) whilst purposefully choosing a particular setting or scenario for the study (Patton, 1990, p. 169). In this paper, the speci<sup>fi</sup>c community of Roswell can provide a good opportunity to investigate a problem in great detail. Because the purpose of this study is to understand the meanings shared by a social group (<sup>fi</sup>le sharers) and their culturally speci<sup>fi</sup>c model of experi ence (de Laine, 1997, p. 46), the ethnographic approach can help provide an actor-oriented (<sup>fi</sup>le sharer/insider) perspective of their participation, collaboration and engagement in the community.

## RESEARCH SETTING

The focus of this paper is the unauthorised <sup>fi</sup>le sharing community Roswell, a nontypical re search site rarely accessible to researchers because it operates underground on the internet.

In order to understand the community within the wider music downloading context, multiple methods were used. Participant observations of Roswell’s activities were supplemented by in dividual interviews and focus groups comprising stakeholders.<sup>1</sup> The discussion of illegal behaviour during much of the data collection meant that it was imperative to protect the research subjects at all times.

Interviews with key music recording industry <sup>fi</sup>gures were also conducted in order to understand the wider context and the mainstream music industry. Qualitative inquiry was undertaken because the community and concepts being investigated are emergent and contingent, and the study aimed to explore the taken for granted aspects of the music industry using ANT to identify signi<sup>fi</sup>cant controversies in music access and use. ## Participant observations of Roswell

The participant observations in the underground <sup>fi</sup>le sharing community were conducted over 120 days (including a 30-day pilot phase). There were approximately 1200 documented members in Roswell at the time of the study. Members joined this community via a personal invitation from a current member (the only option). Covert observations maintained the researcher’s anonymity (Hine, 2005) and were important in maintaining the integrity and con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality of the actions of those being observed. Members of Roswell therefore did not give informed consent to being part of this study; however, great care was taken not to harm the community or its members. The use of online observation data for research without direct permission in thi study met Marx’s (1998) the criteria of protecting individuals from harm.

The participant observations provided access to the dynamic data on traf<sup>fi</sup>c/sharing, new up loads, members and classes/roles. These were captured weekly in a table spreadsheet format that enabled the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of patterns of activity that gave evidence of the strength of the network. The observations were recorded in the researcher’s detailed observation notes. Screenshots were taken of all pages in the community at the beginning of the observation period and at key times throughout the observation period.

## Interviews of <sup>fi</sup>le sharers and other stakeholders

Because of the covert nature of the research, no members of Roswell could be contacted to be interviewed, and personal communications within the community such as forum posts were not permitted by the university’s ethics committee to be included in the data collection. Therefore, <sup>fi</sup>le sharers were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed for interviews from amongst those undergraduate university students who volunteered to participate in this research. A total of sixteen <sup>fi</sup>le sharers were interviewed; three research subjects indicated they were members of underground <sup>fi</sup>le sharing communities. Two of them (pseudonyms Rusty and Gates) have been quoted in this paper to provide furthe clari<sup>fi</sup>cation on underground <sup>fi</sup>le sharing. The interviews indicated that the unauthorised <sup>fi</sup>le sharing communities to which these subjects belonged were very similar with Roswell (they used the same open source software interface), although much larger (one example had 4500 plus members). Thus, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings discussed in this paper are likely to have wider appli cability than the one community (Roswell) observed.

For several reasons, most of the subjects interviewed were undergraduate students: they are uniquely immersed in the World Wide Web and in computer technologies in general as a part of their educational experiences; they regularly participate in newsgroups and use in stant messaging and email to communicate; the con<sup>fl</sup>ict over the effects of music downloading primarily involves the popular music that is marketed to, and consumed by, young people; legal actions and educational campaigns have been focused on changing stu dent behaviour (Kinnally et al., 2008).

The development of the interview questions was informed by the results of the observations. The interviews inquired about unauthorised <sup>fi</sup>le sharing practices, the technologies used and the motivations. The interviews were essential for understanding some of the jargon, physica arrangements and social rules related with unauthorised <sup>fi</sup>le sharing in underground communi ties. They were also helpful in understanding how new music is accessed in general and how research subjects felt about being labelled pirates. However, the focus groups were dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to conduct and yielded few insights as research subjects were reluctant to share their experiences of unauthorised (illegal) <sup>fi</sup>le sharing in a group situation.

Six musicians and eight music recording industry stakeholders were also interviewed to access the views of the mainstream music industry. All transcripts were analysed via <sup>2</sup>NVivo (QSR International Pty Ltd, Doncaster, Victoria, Australia) using the development of issues approach for ethnography, which includes description, analysis and interpretation of the culture-sharing group (Wolcott, 1994; Creswell, 2007). This systematic process helped to identify patterned regularity of behaviour (themes) in the data. Evidence of these patterns was then mapped to concepts in the actor-network (presented in the next section).

## ACTOR-NETWORK THEORY AND UNAUTHORISED FILE SHARING

To understand power relations using ANT, controversies need to be examined to see how particular struggles or problems become resolved and/or appear as black boxes – that is, taken for granted and arguably not needing any explanation. ANT predicates that it is only when particular controversies are examined that the black boxes begin to open and allow the complex chains of actor-networks to become clear, as these are normally concealed by the black box effect (Latour, 1993). An illustrative example is that to the casual observer, unauthorised <sup>fi</sup>le sharing communities are ad hoc and probably unorganised, yet this study suggests the exact opposite: underground communities are highly sophisticated and orderly.

## Identifying controversies in unauthorised <sup>fi</sup>le sharing communities

The ANT concept of controversies (Callon et al., 1986, p. 11) is a useful tool to identify problems and/or points of difference in the unauthorised <sup>fi</sup>le sharing actor-network. The emphasis on ex amining controversies is to see how particular struggles or controversies came to be and how they appear as black boxes, i.e. taken for granted. In other words, these are assumptions that appear not to need explanations.

The ANTapproach to analysis and interpretation used in the study is in line with the approach outlined in the collection of articles in the book Actor-network theory and Organising (Czarniawska & Hernes, 2005, p. 9; Hernes, 2005, p. 117). Firstly, actants<sup>3</sup> (those that act and are acted upon) are identi<sup>fi</sup>ed; then the actants are followed through a trajectory of programmes and anti-programmes until they become actors (acquiring a distinct and relatively stable character). Those with successful mainstream programmes (e.g. iTunes) or successful deviant programmes (e.g. Roswell, who has strength in opposition and resistance) become actors. The actor-network’s success is due to associations through the formation and stabilisation of networks of actants who can then present themselves as actor-networks (Czarniawska & Hernes, 2005, p. 9).

As a starting point for the analysis, actants in the network were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed. These include those identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in Figure 1: music consumers, musicians and those involved in distributing music. This was not an easy task due to the many new players continually entering the industry and the changing alliances between those in the network. However, the associations between each of the actants at a point in time were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed and documented. An example of an association between music consumers and musicians is that consumers may feel that musicians get a low monetary return from music sales as the recording company gets most of it (Faul & Karoub, 2012), so they choose not to pay.

The documented associations were then traced to identify the actants’ connections with the programmes (paid-authorised systems) and anti-programmes (free-unauthorised systems available; these associations then become actors in the music actor-network. This tracing reveals the details of how the groups/actors form and stabilise: for example, how Roswell came to exist. The concept of translation is then applied to gain knowledge of Roswell’s cultura practices and the technology artefact itself (Czarniawska & Hernes, 2005, p.9). Through this analysis, Roswell can be seen as what Czarniawksa and Hernes identify as a ‘super actor that seems to be much larger than any individuals that constitute it, and yet it is an association—a network—of these individuals, equipped with a voice’ (2005, p. 7). This super actor is akin to the Leviathan or ‘the sovereign’ identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by Callon and Latour (1981). Through tracing and organising these associations, power relations become visible, and the Roswell artefact (the anti-programme) is given a voice and allowed to tell its own storey (Porsander, 2005).

Once the actor-network was identi<sup>fi</sup>ed, ANTwas used to problematise unauthorised <sup>fi</sup>le sharing in order to uncover the main controversies. This was achieved through documenting all of the motivations raised by the research subjects and identifying and mapping patterns between them. In focusing on the controversies, the black boxes began to open to show the complex chains of actor- networks that the black box effect innately conceals (Latour, 1999). The issues fell into four main areas or controversies:

• Changes in music distribution channels have an in<sup>fl</sup>uence on how music is accessed and used – pirate vs. purchase ideologies, complex relationships between stakeholders, the move to digital music, legal cases/issues/interventions.

• Legal online music distribution does not appear to meet the needs of many users – quality, cost, choice, convenience, interoperability, scarcity and value.

• Technical competence in<sup>fl</sup>uences choices about which technology to use for music access and general acceptance/nonacceptance/awareness of restrictions such as digital rights management.

• Community life/culture including the everyday activities of <sup>fi</sup>le sharers is relatively unknown – roles, rules, rituals, norms, values, ideologies and language.

The ethnographic approach was useful to uncover important cultural symbols (Coates, 1997) such as the community’s changing logo based on calendar events (such as Christmas), member’s personalised avatars, their patterns of harmonisation (visible through the obvious effort by most to help others) and their rituals (regular predictable uploads of certain content). Members and ultimately the community’s philosophies, values and patterns of belief are manifested through such symbolic devices (Smircich, 1983). For example, there are myths and legends around keeping out those who could ultimately bring the community down (‘There’s like huge FBI stings’ Rusty interview). This is technically operationalised through the strict membership system.

There is also specialised language used in Roswell and in the wider underground <sup>fi</sup>le sharing community. Some commonly used terms seen in the community and used by <sup>fi</sup>le sharers in the interviews include the following: ‘nuked’, ‘the scene’, ‘top sites’ and ‘seed/leech’. Through such language, the meaning structures manifested in artefacts, beliefs, values, rituals, etc. are evidenced in this community subculture, and they are derived from the community’s history, the situations and rules imposed by dominant actors, and members’ cumulative interpretations and sense-making of ongoing actions and interactions (Allaire & Firsirotu, 1984).

## Group formation in unauthorised <sup>fi</sup>le sharing

Actor-network theory reveals group formation through the perspective of an actant, that is, any participant in an endeavour, whether human or nonhuman. An actant is de<sup>fi</sup>ned by what it does, in essence, and what function it performs. Actants emerge through trials. For example, a potential actant participates in an experiment, tradition or other typical group activity; if he or she is successful in the trial, he or she becomes a member of the group/institution who is then expected to contribute to regularise group activities (Jablonski, 2001). An example of such a trial can be seen within Roswell: seeding <sup>fi</sup>les to be shared and offering help. An actant in this instance, a highly active and privileged member of this unauthorised <sup>fi</sup>le sharing community, follows closely what is happening in the wider underground community called ‘the scene (Rusty), where the digital content in underground communities comes from after being leaked from the CD processing plant or by the music recording industry themselves. This then connects their community to the wider <sup>fi</sup>le sharing community. An example of a participant ac knowledging membership in the community and the existence of such actants can be seen in Rusty’s description: ‘Every day I wake up and I go to this FTP (File Transfer Protocol) <sup>fi</sup>rst thing in the morning … it’s like my lifeline … and I check what’s just been released through the scene…’. He goes on to explain that all digital content in the community is <sup>fi</sup>ltered down from other underground communities to public websites and systems. Thus, Roswell’s role in the wider <sup>fi</sup>le sharing community provides a clear illustration of an actant within an established network.

One criterion required to enable successful group formation is that members (peers) must be pro<sup>fi</sup>cient in the local language and participate according to set rules. The elite few who have privileges to contribute new content to the community need to follow these rules explicitly if they want to remain part of the community in their position of power, as Gates, a community member, explains in his interview:

It is really, really strict, so that is how they’ve been able to survive for a long time … and there is no centralised tracker like with public torrents, which also makes it very dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to destroy, especially when you have moderators based all around the world, you can’t really take down one moderator and the whole thing falls over, it doesn’t work like that.

Only 5% of the users in Roswell belong to the privileged few who have privileges to contribute new content to the community to be downloaded and shared. By limiting this number, the com munity is able to maintain strict quality control on the digital content shared to avoid spoofed content (which is an incorrectly named <sup>fi</sup>le or a <sup>fi</sup>le with white noise throughout) that is reportedly often found in public free-unauthorised <sup>fi</sup>le sharing systems like Limewire.

Once individuals are accepted as members of the underground community (by invitation only) through the community membership system, they pass over the obligatory passage point (OPP).<sup>4</sup> Once membership has been established by the passage of this stage, community activities are highly ritualised (evidenced by preceding quote), and members enact expected codes of behaviour. Their behaviour and values are deemed adequate if they are willing to <sup>fi</sup>le share the mostly unauthorised digital content that is available and if they are not part of law enforcement. Having a strong and enforceable OPP to gain entry to the community like Roswell does contribute to the establishment of a strongly formed group, as explained by Silva (2007) that ‘in an established power relation, A [the focal actor] will establish an OPP [Obligatory Passage Point] for B [a joining actor]. The OPP is a result of “translations” after which B has no other choice but to accept the OPP as created by A’ (p. 166).

The kind of community engagement and collaboration observed in Roswell is missing in many of the paid-authorised options for music (like iTunes). MySpace (free-authorised) was possibly the most successful attempt so far at online community engagement in music with

Last.fm, Pandora and more recently Spotify providing a more contemporary (also freeauthorised) collaborative listening experience. Although iTunes provides its customers with an account, there is little evidence of attempts to create any kind of collaborative community; it uses push rather than pull technology (Cannatella & Geoghegan, 2009). The strong collabora tive characteristics of unauthorised <sup>fi</sup>le sharing communities evidenced in the study of Roswell provide one reason why such groups need further investigation. Regardless of one’s view on the ethicality of their practices, it will be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to dismantle such groups whilst their require ments for access to music remain unmet by more traditional, authorised music distribution systems like iTunes and Spotify.

The collaborative aspect of Roswell is further demonstrated through the ANT concept of translation that helps explain the creation of a forum or a central network such as Roswell. Callon (1986) explains that there are four moments of translation in which actors agree that the network is worth building, worth participating in and worth defending. The four moments of translation are articulated individually as follows: Problematization, Interessement, Enrollment and Mobilisation, discussed in detail on the succeeding paragraphs.

According to ANT, stability and social order are continually negotiated as a social process of aligning interests during the process of translation, ‘which generates ordering effects such as devices, agents, institutions or organisations’ (Law, 1992, p. 366). Underground unauthorised <sup>fi</sup>le sharing communities are places to align such interests, and our study of Roswell demon strates that this is done through a surprisingly formal and organised group.

This aspect of interessement in the Roswell inscription de<sup>fi</sup>nes activities for actors to engage in, as well as the various roles and member classes in the community (Monteiro, 2000). In nego tiating their terms of involvement (enrollment), the actors must agree to the underlying unlawful ideology of the community, and their power is legitimised through speci<sup>fi</sup>c member classes/roles and associated privileges that are then delegated and disciplined (Star, 1991, p. 28).

Even though ANT has a strong focus on how actor-networks are formed, maintained and sustained, accounts of using ANToften do not explain why a network takes the form that it does; i.e. the translation. In discussing why Roswell takes the form that it does, it is useful to focus on a single actor (or micro actor) ‘from whose vantage point we wish to see the process of translation’ (Sidorova & Sarker, 2000). In this study, the exemplary micro actor is CaptTron (a pseudonym), who established Roswell on 25<sup>th</sup> of February 2005.

The power of ANT is that it allows the focus to move from the experience of building the Roswell community ‘to the fact that it might have been otherwise … [that] all constructions are historically contingent, no matter how stabilized’ (Star, 1991, p. 38). Roswell is entangled with the underlying ideologies of the diversity of stakeholder perspectives in the music recording industry, and Roswell’s very presence challenges the mainstream political, economic and social perspectives by embracing the alternative technologies and different practices fo sharing music.

## Problematization

Problematization is the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage in a series of actions by which an actor makes himself indispensable to others (Callon, 1986). The <sup>fi</sup>rst action in the establishment of Roswell was the creation of the artefact that provides a Web interface to the community and the underlying technology infrastructure to support the sharing of digital content. The problem that the new community addressed was the lack of available systems to share mostly unauthorised digita content and the need to create a private community in order to engage in such activities. Roswell was established as a private site (tracker) to share unauthorised digital content. Once established, the focal actor (CaptTron) identi<sup>fi</sup>ed and enrolled other actors to participate in the community. By delegating roles, actors could then identify other actors to enrol to build up the community membership base.

Through the establishment of the OPP, the focal actor becomes indispensable to the network, and membership provides a crossing over point into the uncharted territory. CaptTron created the OPP so that only individuals whose values and ideologies were consistent with his or her own interests were asked to join. This was enforced via the invitation-only closed membership system. Technical ability was also essential for new members. The <sup>fi</sup>rst member joined or was appointed (it is unclear) less than 2 h after Roswell was established. That member, Zer0 (a pseudonym), became a moderator of the community, ‘rendering himself indispensable’ (Callon, 1986).

Without the direct access link to the website and an invitation to join, the community is essen tially invisible to outsiders. To move across this OPP is to step into a new world, a frontier that is exclusively available only to members and one that is mostly operating outside of the law. Thus, CapTron initiated the problematization process, whereby he or she is made indispensable to the community. Next, CapTron must engage others to join and participate in the community.

## Interessement

Interessement refers to the focal actor using various strategies and devices to get actors interested in the community, negotiating the terms of their involvement and participation and accepting their new identities during problematization (Callon, 1986).

The notion of inscription is important to interessement and refers to the way technical artefacts embody patterns of use (Akrich, 1992); the inscription of certain characteristics (and community ideologies) into the system is designed to cater for actors’ speci<sup>fi</sup>c tastes, competencies, motives, aspirations and political prejudices (Akrich & Latour, 1992). For Roswell, this included tactics like the rules of participation. CaptTron also inscribed a multiplicity of identities, each o which had associated privileges, including the system operator, the administrator, the moderator, the power use, etc. (discussed on the succeeding paragraphs). As such, CaptTron inscribed what Monteiro (2000) refers to as a ‘vision of (or prediction about) the world in the technica content of the new object’.

To enrol actors, the community sets out expectations for behaviour and inscribes these into the rules for participation. For example, CaptTron, possibly with Zer0, de<sup>fi</sup>ned the roles and responsibilities for members of the community into seven member roles (see Table 2), including the rule that a member status changes depending on a member’s ratio of sharing (seeding) to downloading (leeching), called the <sup>fi</sup>le share ratio.

Interessement is also concerned with the process of translating the concerns of one world into that of another and then disciplining or maintaining that translation in order to stabilise the powerful network (Star, 1991, p. 32). The foremost concern in creating Roswell is the ability to share digital content in a new way, appealing to those who are unhappy or ambivalent abou access via the mainstream online music industry (e.g. the cost, convenience and choice of digital music) (Beekhuyzen et al., 2012).

To keep the participation in this community clandestine, membership to Roswell is highly controlled. New members must negotiate their terms of involvement and have aligned values. To illustrate these values and the expectations of members, the home page of the community hosts a disclaimer for the legality and responsibility of the content shared via the community to waive any responsibility for the digital content shared that is unauthorised. CapTron used interessement to enrol and maintain the tightly controlled membership of Roswell.

## Enrollment and mobilisation

Through activities in alignment and enrollment, the focal actor (CaptTron) and his/her allies (other members) seek to achieve irreversibility in network relations resulting in a stable network of relationships with actors inscribing regular patterns of use. This ‘black-boxing of the commit ments made by the diverse participants will give the arrangements a taken-for-granted charac ter’ (Ramiller, 2009, p. 38).

New members enrol through formally accepting the joining member class/role offered and accepting the associated privileges that have been assigned to them during interessement. The privileges associated with this role remain until the member is promoted (see Table 2), bar ring exceptional situations in which a new administrator is appointed, etc. Upon becoming a member of this underground community, the new member is greeted with a personalised welcome message.

## Generalised symmetry in unauthorised <sup>fi</sup>le sharing

Core to the use of ANT is the assumption of generalised symmetry in which all actors in the net work are described in the same terms and treated equally.

In regard to Roswell, all members must follow the same rules regardless of their class/role, except for the administrators who are able to subvert the rules to promote/demote members and add new content. For example, all members of the community have access to statistics, via the Home page, which summarise the network activity. These statistics include such data as the following: at one point in time, the number of digital (torrent) <sup>fi</sup>les available for download was 760; the number of peers, users and members of Roswell accessing the available content was 4213; and the number of seeds (those that share/upload) was 3943, whilst there were 270 leeches (those that do not share back downloaded content). Such collaborative statistics are representative of the participative nature of the community.

These summaries of activities refer to inscriptions within the community that are relatively <sup>fi</sup>xed with properties of irreversibility that are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to change. According to Monteiro (2000), the strength of inscriptions – that is, whether they must be followed or can be avoided – depends on the irreversibility of the actor-network into which they are inscribed. The previously mentioned activities are inscribed activities within the Roswell community. For example, the action of participation through seeding/leeching cannot be avoided; the technology infrastructure is irreversible: it is the underlying support of the community and must be enacted to enable participation.

## DISCUSSION

This paper seeks to understand how information systems support unauthorised <sup>fi</sup>le sharing communities, and through our study, we identify that motivations of <sup>fi</sup>le sharers and their willingness to engage in piracy are important for the future design of music distribution systems. Honesty (or lack of) is an obvious motivation, but Mazar et al. (2008) present an economic perspective that overrides honesty: they suggest that external rewards, a low probability of being caught and low expectations of punishment, lend themselves to dishonest behaviour. In short, the rewards of unauthorised <sup>fi</sup>le sharing are great (access to free, high quality content), and there is a low probability of getting caught and punished. Moreover, from a psychologica perspective, people internalise the norms and values of their society and their peer groups (others who <sup>fi</sup>le share) and compare these with their own and others’ behaviour (Mazar et al., 2008). In this age of the internet, it has become a societal norm to illegally download music, and it is likely that more people will continue to participate in such activities. In essence, com pliance leads to positive rewards (like collaboration with peers), and noncompliance leads to negative rewards (ostracisation, lack of connection to peers). We build upon Mazar et al. (2008) work to argue that social cooperation behaviours such as gift giving in unauthorised <sup>fi</sup>le sharing communities activate the same primary reward centres in the brain as external rewards such as preferred food or drink or monetary gains. We argue that this possibly balances out the potential negative rewards associated with joining an underground community.

This societal norm is evidenced by, for example, the long history of digital piracy in Australia, if piracy is taken to mean copying without authorisation for personal use. Many people interviewed for this study <sup>fi</sup>nd themselves in the highlighted quadrant in Table 2, including the music executive from Universal Music who fondly remembered taping (with cassettes) their favourite songs from the radio and making mixed tapes in childhood. Such behaviour is now evolving into adulthood with newer forms of technology. During the course of the study, employees at Universal Music even admitted to knowing of staff buying counterfeit DVDs and CDs in Asia whilst on holiday from Australia. This illustrates how complicated the notions of piracy and counterfeiting are because even those who argue that such practices are killing the music industry they work in are actively engaged in this practice. Ideally, this study thus high lights the misapplication of the term piracy to all who <sup>fi</sup>le share, when most do not engage in unauthorised <sup>fi</sup>le sharing for commercial gain.

Returning to the research question in Section 1, Rubin’s (1996) list of the machine actors that are essentially affordances of the Internet helps explain how information systems support unauthorised <sup>fi</sup>le sharing activities within an underground online <sup>fi</sup>le sharing community. He believes that the machine actors – the technology that supports unauthorised <sup>fi</sup>le sharing – present seven temptations that are unique to computer mediated communication:

1 Speed – the download occurs in the blink of an eye.

2 Privacy and anonymity – most acts occur in the privacy and perceived anonymity of home.

3 Nature of the medium – the copy is not actually removed from the original source.

4 Aesthetic attraction – the goal is to conquer, to be clever.

Rubin (1996) convincingly argues that these characteristics of technology promote a moral distancing between the act and the moral responsibility for the act; in other words, the individua can rationalise, remove himself from or ignore ethical considerations (Rubin, 1996). We agree with the work by Mazar et al. (2008) that the internal reward system exerts control over behav iour by in<sup>fl</sup>uencing people’s self-concept. Although <sup>fi</sup>le sharers are seen as deviants, even crim inals by some, we found that within the unauthorised <sup>fi</sup>le sharing community, they are also seen as peers, as comrades and even as fellow pirates. By using ANT, we have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the ways in which these feelings of belonging to the network/community are established, reinforced and rewarded. Moreover, <sup>fi</sup>le sharers are experiencing a more rewarding experience than they would if they were dealing with the complex and divided mainstream music industry. Overall, the extensiveness of such networks indicates that the contemporary mainstream music recording industry is not meeting the needs of a large group of its potential customers.

These <sup>fi</sup>ndings are demonstrated through the application of ANT as follows. Firstly, the conceptualisation of the actor (Roswell) reveals a ‘technological apparatus that bends space around itself, makes other elements dependent on itself, and translates other language into a will of its own’ (Slack & Wise, 2007, p. 118). Such dependence is evidenced by Rusty’s comment about Roswell being a ‘lifeline’ for him. The technological apparatuses (the website interface and the BitTorrent technology) interact with the actants (members) and emerge through trials of experimentation, tradition and practical application that are implicitly documented in the history of the community. Illustrated through the displayed statistics of collaboration in the community, once the fellows (human actants in the community) deem that the new member performs adequately through a regularised process, such as adherence to rules, he or she is ad mitted to an institution (Jablonski, 2001); membership is gained to the unauthorised <sup>fi</sup>le sharing underground.

## Practical implications

This study highlights the social relationships and the collective behaviour observed within a de viant online community that is situated amongst wider complex social, legal, political and economic pressures as Yar (2005) explains. We have learned that online communities can be vibrant online social spaces for interaction and collaboration. Understanding how a network forms and is shaped is useful in the practical implementation of systems aimed at supporting and encouraging online communities. Some guiding design principles for information systems may include the following.

In forming new communities, we must have a stated shared mission and values with rules for membership and for participation. It is necessary to create a sense of community and a feeling of belonging. This can be achieved through incentives for participation like the Roswell share ratio and promotions to roles with more privileges. Designing gift giving through rewarding members into the underlying philosophies of online communities through share ratios is a positive way to encourage user participation and engage members. High quality digital content is important to members of this community; in fact, this is what attracts most to the community. The digital content is also free, which is an appealing factor.

## Theoretical and research practice contribution

Unauthorised music <sup>fi</sup>le sharing is an area moving fast in terms of technology, but the basic con cepts identi<sup>fi</sup>ed and clari<sup>fi</sup>ed in this paper have long lasting applicability to studies of music distribution and to other digital content. We acknowledge that ANT is somewhat limited in explaining human behaviour, as it equates artefacts and human action. However, this study has shown how ANT can be complimented by ethnography to provide insights into the ways people organise themselves. Through covert ethnography, we study the social practices of a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant group of consumers.

This detailed study of user behaviour contributes to theory and research practice through a rich description of collaboration in an online community engaged in unauthorised <sup>fi</sup>le sharing. We have shown how this type of community on the dark side of information systems can be studied methodologically and ethically, following in the traditions of seminal studies of subcul tural groups such as those by Barley (1986, 1997). Here, we expand the boundaries of information systems research outside traditional forms of organisations to consider a new form of organisation, the virtual online community. Theoretically, we have shown that ethnography enables ANT to be grounded. This paper also informs research practitioners performing ethnography in ethically challenging situations.

This study contributes to our understanding of new organisational forms, and it puts into practice the theoretical advice on ANT given in Czarniawska and Hernes (2005). The detailed expla nation of methodology and the instantiations of the application of ANT in this paper are usefu contributions to the theory. In this paper, we aim to provide the same level of detail methodolog ically and theoretically as the groundbreaking studies by Suchman (2006) who theorised on humans, machines and their relations, and Thomas’s (1993) ethnography of digital <sup>fi</sup>le sharing.

The covert nature of this study has provided insights not easily accessible to researchers. Although this is a study of a now defunct community, we have shown ways to access clandestine communities like Roswell. We use analytical generalisation to further our knowledge of online collaboration.

## Future studies

In this landmark in-depth study of a new group of consumers, we have clari<sup>fi</sup>ed the concepts of group formation (problematization. interessement. enrollment. mobilisation and generalised symmetry) to identify the controversies in the black box context in ANT that relate to this subcultural group. To further examine the online collaboration within communities, there is now poten tial to measure these concepts in a large scale study using a mix of qualitative and quantitative methods. This work can be expanded to examine other deviant subcultural groups online.

To further this <sup>fi</sup>eld of research, it may be useful to examine other music distribution systems/communities such as the widely used Spotify. To do so, new business models related to 3-D printing economies (Lipson & Kurman, 2013) may be needed in addition to those related with social media networks (Oinas-Kukkonen et al., 2010) to understand these new forms of organisations. Other self-organising communities such as those found in the online gaming world and massively multiplayer online games may provide useful insight into supporting collaboration outside of traditional organisational boundaries (Crawford et al., 2011).

## CONCLUSION

This is the <sup>fi</sup>rst time that ANT has been used to explain how human actants participate in unauthorised <sup>fi</sup>le sharing activities in online communities. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that this participation is highly ritualised and is composed of strong, stable norms and strict rules of behaviour. The technology actors instil patterns of behaviour and enforce the rules for human actors. In Roswell, the interrelationships between the members across classes and their roles and associated privileges/abilities within the community are complicated to the uninformed. This study therefore contributes to a more detailed understanding of collaboration in online communities.

The strength and size of clandestine communities such as Roswell suggest that it is futile to ignore or use legal sanctions against them. The members’ desire for music is currently not met through mainstream and legal access channels; instead, their music access is manifested in a highly structured and disciplined approach to organisation that would be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to identify and dismantle. We believe that <sup>fi</sup>le sharers represent a growing segment in the music industry that cannot be ignored or successfully litigated against to any great extent. Music distribution sys tems should be developed accordingly to meet their demands for both high quality music <sup>fi</sup>les and scarce music that may not be accessible via current legitimate distribution systems.

In this paper, ANT is used as a lens to explore the controversies that surround unauthorised <sup>fi</sup>le sharing. This theory allows us to explore the associations and alliances that exist in the underground of music access and use. Such explorations are necessary to illuminate how the music recording industry might operate otherwise and/or how things could be. The ANT concept of controversies helped illuminate issues relevant to both industry stakeholders (creation and distribution) and consumers (access and use). This paper contributes to the literature by illustrat ing that ANT is useful in observing, describing and interpreting the formation of power (Czarniawska & Hernes, 2005, p. 13); the power relations in the music actor-network are framed within the discussion of controversies. This is important because changes in music distribution channels have an in<sup>fl</sup>uence on how music is accessed and used; if practices become widespread and entrenched regardless of their current legal status, other stakeholders need to consider how to accommodate the demand created by such practices.

We <sup>fi</sup>nd that legal online music distribution does not appear to meet the needs of many users in terms of quality, low cost, choice, convenience, interoperability, availability/range and value. These needs are inscribed to the members’ satisfaction in communities such as Roswell supported by readily available information systems. The mainstream music industry should investigate further how to satisfy these needs.
