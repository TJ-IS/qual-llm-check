# Information system development activities and inquiring systems: an integrating framework

## Abstract

This article presents a framework that maps information system development (ISD) activities on systems for the creation of knowledge. This work addresses the relevant and persisting problem of improving the chances of ISD success. The article builds upon previous research on knowledge aspects of ISD, abandoning the idea of a monolithic approach to knowledge and presenting a pluralistic approach based on the idea that different inquiring systems can support micro-level ISD activities. The article is divided into two parts. The first part presents the theoretical development of the framework based on ISD literature and on Churchman’s (1971) inquiring systems. The second part presents the use of the framework in an ISD project. The case is used to show the applicability of the framework and to highlight the advantages of this approach. The main theoretical implication is that the framework provides a new way to see the development of a system in terms of the knowledge created in the process. The main practical implication of the framework is that it improves the managers’ ability to guide ISD activities as knowledge activities embedded in a knowledge process, a crucial element in development projects. Keywords: information systems development; knowledge creation; inquiring systems

## Introduction

Information system development (ISD) can be conceived as the product of two interconnected activities: software producing activities and managerial functions (Abdel-Hamid and Madnick, 1991). While this dichotomy is highly intuitive, current research tends to pair these two dimensions with a third one concerning knowledge creation. This branch of research focuses on knowledge issues in ISD explicitly (Avison et $a l . ,$ 1998; Markus et al., 2002; Richardson and Courtney, 2004) or implicitly (Winter et al., 1995; Beck, 1999). This recent research presents a trend towards a social constructivist approach to ISD where information systems and their requirements emerge from the interaction of multiple stakeholders’ viewpoints.

The literature mentioned above points out that the use of alternative viewpoints can improve the chances for ISD success, but only addresses the pluralistic nature of knowledge creation to a limited extent. However, a good understanding of the knowledge dynamics of micro-level activities is relevant, as these activities are the building blocks of ISD methodologies (Hughes and Wood-Harper, 2000). Research in ISD methodologies typically focuses more on the level of the complete process rather than on the activity level. However, the actors involved in ISD find themselves continuously swinging between long-term planning (process) and dayto-day work (activity) and have to manage both. So, while a certain philosophy might be good ‘in general’ for an entire ISD process (Avison and Fitzgerald, 2002), it does not mean that it will be effective for every activity in the process.

This paper tries to fill this gap by presenting a theoretically grounded framework that focuses on the micro-level ISD activities and their knowledge creation aspects. There are three main purposes. First, focusing on micro-level ISD activities shows that multiple ways of creating knowledge should be used depending on the task and physical situation of the stakeholders. Second, the paper shows that it is necessary to understand the implications of the use of a particular way of creating knowledge in any specific ISD activity. The third purpose is to highlight the specific knowledge creation situations in ISD activities in order to provide managers with the appropriate means for their identification and management.

The paper presents a theoretical discussion that leads to mapping ISD activities on the inquiring systems proposed by Churchman (1971). The main contribution of this article is a framework – which will be called the Inquiring Systems ISD Framework or ISIF – to help managers view ISD activities as knowledge creation processes and to provide them with the principles for their management to improve the success of ISD projects.

This paper provides a brief introduction to Churchman’s inquiring systems, followed by a discussion of the framework’s creation. The framework will map the inquiring systems on basic ISD activities so that it will be robust enough to be used in conjunction with different methodologies. This section is followed by a case study of an ISD project. The case study is analyzed to identify the inquiring systems used by the developers during the project. The paper concludes with a comparison of the framework with the case study. Conclusions are drawn on the effectiveness of ISD methodologies, and management practices are presented to support the different knowledge creation situations.

## Inquiring systems and ISD

According to Churchman (1971), there are five basic modes of inquiry. These five inquiring systems find their basis in the philosophies of Leibniz, Locke, Kant, Hegel, and Singer (Churchman, 1971). In this section the main characteristics of the inquiring systems are presented.

## Rationalism or the Leibnizian inquiring system

In this mode the inquirer discovers through formal logic the truth governing the world. The system under study is considered as closed and governed by causal relationships. The inquirer creates networks of hypotheses, fact nets, and proceeds to build on these hypotheses until logic reveals a counterhypothesis that invalidates a part of the net. The Leibnizian inquirer is the isolated individual, the monad (Churchman, 1971), reasoning on the causal relationships that govern the system that he wants to improve. The individual’s logic becomes the guarantor of the truthfulness of the fact net. In ISD we may find that the Leibnizian inquiring system is used during coding: once the requirements are specified, the programmer proceeds on his own to fulfil the requirements based on his internal logical reasoning. The software produced will therefore reflect the fact nets developed by the inquirer (i.e. the programmer).

## Empiricism or Lockean inquiring system

This mode of inquiry gives more weight to data than theory. The inquirer is open to the environment and finds the truth in the external world. The inquirer is not isolated like the monad, but participates in creating knowledge with a community of inquirers, the Lockean community. The inquirer uses data and the opinion of his community to explain the world. The community acts as guarantor of the truth through agreement and consensus. The Lockean inquiring system searches for a single truth, but there is no guarantee that two communities will agree on one common truth. In ISD, the groups of users and developers can be regarded as two Lockean communities, where agreement can be found within a community but it is difficult to find it across communities.

## Idealism or Kantian inquiring system

This mode of inquiry gives equal weight to data and theory. It recognizes that there are multiple ways in which a problem may be analyzed using a variety of models that can be applied to the data. The Kantian inquirer proceeds in testing the fitness of a model to the data and creates knowledge by finding the model that best fits the data. In ISD it can be imagined that the Kantian inquiring system is used when developers search for a model to mirror the client system into the software system. Many possibilities are tried and recursively eliminated until only one, the one with the best fit, the truth, remains. One example of use of idealism in ISD is in the development of enterprise resource planning systems where the developers code parts of the systems to fit best practices.

## Dialectic or Hegelian inquiring system

This inquiring system sees the truth emerging from opposing views. Debate between different worldviews (Churchman, 1968; Linstone, 1984) is seen as the only way to develop theses and antitheses to arrive at a synthesis that accommodates both worldviews. Hegel introduces the idea of multiple interpretations of reality even though he believed in one final ‘grand synthesis’. Churchman (1968) envisioned two groups of people engaged in an ardent debate, respectively defending their thesis and trying to destroy the other group’s antithesis. The issue is then resolved by an impartial observer that combines the arguments to create a synthesis. The synthesis generated from the impartial observer acts as the guarantor of the truth (Courtney et al., 1998). Dialectic inquiry in ISD is normally sought in the discussions about requirements when using prototypes where developers and users can debate the appropriateness of the code produced.

## Pragmatism or the Singerian inquiring system

This inquiring system is based on the ardent debate of the Hegelian inquirer to create progress, but it accepts multiple sources of data and multiple interpretations of reality and consequently multiple truths. It is called pragmatic inquiring system because the truth/s produced is/are relative to the context and to the objectives of the inquiry. The multiple truths are found via an approach that continuously attacks currently held beliefs from multiple points of view. The world inquired is interpreted as an open system where all components might become part of the inquiry. The truth is temporary and contextually dependent, because as new elements are ‘swept in’ (Courtney et $a l . ,$ 1998) the truth is likely to change. Progress is the goal of the system and it is measured quantitatively whenever possible, otherwise the groups’ intuition that progress is being made becomes the guarantor for qualitative measures (Richardson et al., 2001). In ISD, the Singerian inquiring system can be used in the occasions when developers and users have the possibility to bring together and exchange their practices. This can typically happen in the testing phase of a software or during prototype testing. An attempt to formally use pragmatism can be seen in recent methodologies like the ones deriving from Soft Systems Methodology (Winter et al., 1995; Atkinson, 2000), Multiview2 (Avison et al., 1998) and in agile methodologies (Beck, 1999; Fowler, 2002). Richardson and Courtney (2004) propose a design theory for knowledge management systems based specifically on the Singerian inquiry. These methodologies let the information system emerge from the debate between developers and users, letting multiple truths be created pragmatically by the participants and not imposed top-down.

The traditional assumption is that the Singerian inquirer is more useful for ill-structured problems where objectives and methods to reach the objectives are unclear (Richardson and Courtney, 2004). However, this view considers the characteristic of the situation as existing outside the individual. Taking an interpretive approach (Orlikowski and Baroudi, 1991), ill-structured and well-structured are not characteristic features of the problem but of the observer: it is the result of individual and social construction. This is reflected in the fact that the way in which a problem is perceived by the individual changes according to the context in which the individual is. From the individual perspective it is therefore acceptable – in principle – to use different inquiring systems according to the context rather than according to an externally defined characteristic of the problem. An individual working in isolation might tend to rely mostly on rationalism and idealism to move forward. Members of the same group will rely mostly on empiricism, since members of one group possess a shared worldview (Boland and Tenkasi, 1995) and the group can guarantee the goodness of their truth (Courtney et al., 1998). Dialectic and pragmatism are instead inquiring modes used when two groups with different worldviews meet with the intent of learning from each other. Harrison and Bramson (1982) suggest however that individuals have a favorite inquiry system and that they will tend to use it if not told – or taught – to do otherwise. This means that individuals will tend to shape their situation so that they can apply (and justify) their favorite inquiring systems despite the need for alternative ones. The corollary of this is that if a manager is given a framework to see the connection between the inquiring systems and the development activities, then he or she can lead the team towards the use of the appropriate inquiring system in a consistent way.

## Development of the ISIF

The ISD process can vary radically depending on the development methodology chosen. However, there are five basic activities that are shared – albeit with different names – by most methodologies.

(1) Identification and Concept; (2) Requirement Definition; (3) System Design; (4) Implementation; (5) Testing and Operation.

These activities can be performed in various sequences, recursions, and revisions, focusing more or less on the customer organization (Avison et al., 1998). According to the specific sequence adopted, different ISD methodologies can be recognized: waterfall (Boehm, 1988) if the activities are performed in sequence; spiral (Boehm, 1988) if the activities are performed cyclically; rapid prototyping when activity 2 is in fact a fast cycle through activities 2–5 (Martin, 1991; Beath and Orlikowski, 1994). A methodology like Multiview includes activities 1 and 2, but with a major techno-social scope (Avison and Wood-Harper, 1986), whereas Multiview2 (Avison et al., 1998) includes all activities specifically including organizational and socio-technical analysis in activity 2. This article bypasses the methodological specificities, considering the five ISD activities as building blocks of ISD processes. Figures 1a and b depict the idea of activities as building blocks. The ISD activities are shown on the left with the time dimension on the horizontal axis. Figure 1a shows the activities unfolding following a waterfall pattern, and Figure 1b shows the activities unfolding according to the agile paradigm. As Figure 1 shows, the activities remain the same, but their sequence, specific content, and duration change.

Interpreting these five activities in terms of systems for creating knowledge, it is possible to link them to the use of one or more inquiring systems. The following section explains how the ISD activities can be mapped on the inquiring systems, taking into consideration the goal of the activity, the actors involved, but also the context – that is, the physical situation – in which the activity is carried out.

## Identification and concept

This activity can be carried out by the future users or by the designers of a system. The users discuss the situation and how they expect an IS to help them improve it. Users are not usually expert developers, and therefore – although they can conceive some functionalities for the IS – they cannot conceive the details nor the additional features that developers can create for them. Similarly the developers – internal or external IT specialists – discuss how a certain technology will help a group of users to better accomplish their job. Developers are not experts in the domain of the users, and therefore they are likely to underestimate the complexity and variety of the users’ work environment.

During this activity users will consider the world as an open system and will in the environment try to find the data to indicate the solutions (Avison and Fitzgerald, 2002). Consensus within the group acts as a guarantor for the created knowledge (Avison and Fitzgerald, 2002). The observed inquiring mode through which any of these two groups create knowledge is therefore empiricism.

## Requirements definition

This activity is carried out in cooperation between users and systems developers with the scope of specifying what the IS has to do (Sommerville and Sawyer, 1997). Functional requirements emerge from a discussion between users and developers with the purpose of reaching an agreement on the goals of the project. In this phase the groups have an opportunity to behave as Singerian inquirers. The two groups debate their ideas about problems and possibilities. Considering elements from all interconnected systems, they will come up with an idea of what the information system has to do. When using this inquiring system, ideas are not necessarily shared by every individual. This is known and therefore contingency plans for rework can be made. The phase is over when adequate progress is made. Progress can be measured by changes in the requirement document (Sommerville and Sawyer, 1997), in the use cases (Martin and Odell, 1992), or in the user stories (Fowler, 2002) depending on the ISD methodology adopted.

## Design

During the design phase the developers work as a group to plan how to bring the requirements to life. A variety of technical decisions are made, for example, about the programming language and style, modularization, interfaces, etc. These decisions usually allow developers to work independently during the coding activities. During this phase the developers add to the knowledge ‘obtained’ from the users creating their own hypotheses about the users’ system and the solution requested. Knowledge is created through interpretation of the information gathered from the users filtered through previous programming experiences.

In this situation the development group can use the Lockean inquiry with elements of the Kantian inquiry. The group is open to the environment but creates the truth, the knowledge of how the new system has to operate, within the group. At times the group enters the Kantian inquiry when they engage in discussions about models and their fit to solve the users’ problem. As before, the members seek consensus within the group, which acts as a guarantor for the created knowledge.

## Implementation

In this activity the developer, now isolated, begins to elaborate on what he learned from the users and from his colleagues. During the coding process the developer interprets the information in his possession and creates, consciously or unconsciously, a network of hypotheses (fact nets) that take him from ideas to actual code.

In the basic case the system developer acts as a monad in a Leibnizian inquiring system, creating new knowledge through logical deduction. Fact nets are created based on hypotheses about the users’ system. The fact nets grow as more and more elements are coded into the software. The reason for behaving as a rational enquirer resides in the need for the developers to have brief periods of ‘peace’, where requirements can be turned into code without changes. This view has been challenged by the proponents of agile methodologies (Beck, 1999; Lindstrom and Jeffries, 2004) who propose to have the client on location during coding and paired programming (two programmers working together on the same piece of code). If these practices are carried out, then there is the possibility to use the Lockean and the Singerian enquirers. These practices are known to bring quantifiable advantages (Williams et al., 2000; Abrahamsson, 2003).

Occasionally the developer faces uncertainty regarding how well a particular algorithm fits the users’ system. If multiple algorithms have to be compared, the developer enters a Kantian mode of inquiry trying to find which algorithm best fits the users’ system. In this inquiry the developer might follow his own judgment or search for answers outside. Even though the answers come from the outside, the goal of the search is to justify the use of a certain model, not to question the work previously done. The search ends when a model is found that fits the particular situation. The result of the knowledge work during design and implementation is a continuous evolution in the developers’ knowledge. These activities are not simply an operationalization of the requirements agreed upon with the users, but are the arena for criticisms, suggestions, logical developments, and experimentation. All these processes influence the creation of software artifacts that can be different from what the users expect.

## Testing and operation

In this phase the finished product, prototype or release is shown to the users for testing. Both users and developers should be present during the operation phase. The users operate the software and compare the experience with their expectations. Expectations are debated to achieve new agreements: the Singerian inquiring system is used again. If the software is implemented, the users have the opportunity to use it for regular work and can request additional features or modifications at the subsequent meetings. New knowledge created by the users during operation is outside the modus operandi of the debate and is therefore created using the Lockean inquiring mode.

The resulting framework that will be called the inquiring systems ISD frame work (ISIF) is presented in Table 1. ISD activities are placed on the vertical axis and inquiring systems on the horizontal axis. We therefore obtain a grid where, at each intersection, an activity can be mapped on multiple inquiring systems. Table 1 shows one instance of ISIF with the inquiring systems that should be used in the different activities according to the discussion presented above. While between one case and another the grid might change, the discussion above follows established and theoretically recognized relations.

ISIF shows that for each activity several inquiring systems can be active and that throughout an ISD project, all inquiring systems should be used. This variety requires careful management.

Given the ISIF structure, the second part of the paper presents the results of an ethnographical investigation of an ISD project. The aim is to show the applicability of ISIF to understand micro-level knowledge creation in ISD. The case will show some differences between the theoretical framework and the framework in practice and will be used to propose ways for conciliation.

## Research methodology and data collection

This research aims at developing a framework, validated in practice, which maps ISD activities on inquiring systems. According to Markus et al. (2002), the most appropriate methodological choice for studying systems development in innovative knowledge intensive contexts is the longitudinal case approach. Therefore the research design of this study draws together multiple streams of qualitative research methodology (Baskerville et al., 2006) with longitudinal data collection (Yin, 2002).

The case examined involves the implementation of a planning and control system for the inventory department of a shipyard (SteelCo). This case is particularly relevant because the software had to be developed specifically for the shipyard, and the client required a particular solution that could only be achieved using a mathematical technique never applied to this kind of problem before. Hence, the ISD project required a very high level of knowledge exchange for innovation.

The case study encompassed multiple levels of analysis including the development team, the user group, and the individuals. Multiple research techniques were used, addressing the pluralistic nature of the phenomenon under study. The data gathered included field notes, transcripts and minutes of meetings, e-mails, interviews, and software versions. Multiple sources of data were used for triangulation. Observation was carried out by the author who participated in the project as a member of the development team. During the collaboration, field notes were taken of the various activities in which users and developers took part. Being a participant and a researcher at the same time could lead to some selection biases, a common problem in ethnographic studies. For example, Van Maanen (1990), studying culture at Disney, took field notes while being an employee at Disney World. In the present study, selective biases were avoided by triangulating field notes with other data sources collected after the project (e.g. interviews and e-mails).

Subsequent to the observation studies, six semistructured interviews of 60–80 min in length were conducted at the end of the project. All interviews were recorded and transcribed. Interviews were carried out with all active participants in the project (four developers and two users). The interviewees were asked to talk about their view of the history of the project, their roles and activities within it. The choice for semi-structured interviews reflected the need to let the interviewees express in their own terms how they experienced and viewed the sequence and content of the ISD activities. The interviews were carried out at the end of the project, because the aim was to uncover the individual reasons for certain actions – a type of sense-making that is inherently backward looking (Weick, 1979). This choice, as well as the number of interviews, is in line with Baskerville et al. (2006) for research in knowledge aspects of IS implementation.

After the project was concluded the most active participants were convinced to provide the author with all their e-mail exchanges during the development period. It was important to ask for the e-mails after the project ended to avoid biases in the content and opportunistic selection. E-mails were an interesting data source, because they recorded direct communication between team members and therefore helped to support field notes and interviews. As the team was distributed, most of the communication was electronic (the total number of e-mails exceeds 3000). These e-mails gave a very precise log of the project because a time and date stamp was included in the heading, as well as information about the sender and the recipient and attached files. From this information it was possible to infer, who was active at a particular time and for what purpose.

In this study qualitative techniques were used to analyze the data (Eisenhardt, 1989; Yin, 2002). The analysis was guided by my focus on the ISD activities and on the use of the inquiring systems. The data were coded in two iterations. First reviewing the empirical material to identify the activities carried out by the developers. This process was carried out three times: once for my field notes, once for the interviews, and once for the e-mails. Then the data were re-coded to look for the inquiring systems which were used. Again the process was carried out three times. The intention was to find overlapping or disproving evidence across the data sources and ultimately present an accurate case story. The main reason for repeating the two coding cycles on the three data sources was to grant internal validity.

## An overview of the Lager project

The case study concerns the Lager project (Lager is a Danish word and means warehouse), an innovative ISD effort at SteelCo, a Danish shipyard. SteelCo is known on the market for producing very sophisticated vessels. While advanced design is their highest concern, they cannot neglect the other areas of operational improvement to remain competitive on the market. SteelCo had, over the years, experimented with information technology to increase efficiency. The Lager project, aimed at the creation of software for the planning and control of the inventory of SteelCo, was not the first of this kind in the warehouse; other projects had been carried out before, but the technical characteristics of the problem had so far defied all attempts. For this reason the managers at SteelCo had decided to try a software solution based on an advanced technique called Combinatorial Optimization (CO). Combinatorial optimization is a rather new technique for production planning, but it promises more sophistication than other heuristic solutions available on the market. Given the novelty of the technique the project involved university researchers to carry out the development.

The project was structured in a classic way with an external development team led by a project manager. The project manager, a professional IT consultant, had developed software for the shipyard before and held a Ph.D. in computer science. The other team members were the developer of the planning part of the software (Joshua), the developer of the control part of the software (Walter), the person in charge with system modeling (Drew), the person in charge with organizational change (Tom), henceforth we will refer to these five using the expression ‘developers’. From SteelCo the main players were the inventory manager (Alan), the area manager (Ian) and the project sponsors (Rob and Jim). The developers had not met before the project started, and they kept working from different locations throughout the development. Table 2 provides an overview of the tasks of the people in the development team (from the project charter).

The Lager project was planned to last one year and included activities of modeling, development (planning and control modules) and change management. The development methodology chosen was very close to the spiral model of Boehm (1988) with a focus on users’ involvement and multiple prototype demonstrations.

However, the actual development followed a waterfall structure (Boehm, 1988). Both the setting and the CO technique proved more difficult to handle than originally planned. The modeling and requirement definition phase lasted almost 6 months and disengaged the users instead of involving them. This resulted in a request from SteelCo to interact with the users only when the prototypes were ready. The developers worked on the prototypes without contact with the users and finally presented the prototypes in March, August, and October the following year. The reason for the late demonstrations was that the development of the solution algorithm was very complex, and its coding into the software even more complex. The developers – in particular Joshua and Walter – had many interactions both via e-mail and in person to solve technical problems. Ultimately, this resulted in a continuously drifting schedule with late demonstrations. On the occasions of these demonstrations the users did not have many requests for changes, and the developers pursued their own plan for development.

The project was formally concluded at the end of 2002 – 2 years late – and was not implemented at SteelCo. At a final request to extend the project, the project sponsor Jim replied that he saw no reason ‘to prolong their frustrations’, and the project was closed.

## Inquiring systems in the Lager project

As suggested by previous ISD research, the development of the Lager software was highly dependent on the knowledge exchanged throughout the case. Yet, while previous ISD research would look for an overreaching philosophy to explain the entire Lager case (Avison and Fitzgerald, 2002), examining the activities enacted during the case, it can be concluded that the richness of the exchanges, and their implications, could not be appreciated with a unified view, but had to be analyzed using multiple perspectives. Thus, the ISIF was developed and used to explain the use of the inquiring systems in ISD practice. In this section the ISIF will be used to highlight the variety of knowledge exchange activities that unfolded during the Lager project to provide a knowledgebased metric to evaluate and manage ISD projects.

## Identification and concept

This activity was carried out by the management of SteelCo and the project manager. SteelCo had some positive experience with the use of combinatorial optimization to solve complex planning problems, and they decided to start the Larger program aimed at developing a skill base in this technique. The identification and concept phase was carried out before the development team was constituted. The results were included in the Lager project charter that was later used as the starting point of the requirement definition phase. The existence of the project charter and the initial – consensual and optimistic – discussions about the project allow us to judge that the decision to start the project was achieved through a Lockean inquiry.

## Requirements definition

This activity was mostly done by the developers. The developers were taken around the factory one afternoon and were then stationed for 2 weeks at the users’ workstations to observe their work. Based on these two observations, the developers were required to create a requirement document. Only two of the three developers worked on the requirement document and they did so individually. In this phase the approach was very rational and based on the idea of division of labor:

The primary goal for me is to have the control software functioning in a real system and to get an idea of the problems with data collection and processing y and I took the decision to work on this other task, because it was well defined and ready to be started’. (Walter, e-mail, March 2000)

Rationalistic inquiry in this phase, and in agreement with Table 1, is dysfunctional. In hindsight the other developer – Joshua – recognized this situation was not satisfactory, though his comment reflects a firm anchoring in the rational way of acting:

I think that the [problem definition] could have been done better, if someone that knew about the problem could have interacted with us from the very beginning. Instead we went around without really knowing what we were looking for. The problem was that they [SteelCo] did not know what we needed, and we did not know either. (Joshua, interview)

The creation of the document was based on rationalism rather than based on the Singerian inquirer where multiple perspectives collaborate to make the requirements emerge. This situation was also made possible by the attitude of the project sponsors at SteelCo that did not participate in the initial stages. For example, when invited to one of the initial meetings, Rob wrote:

I will not come to the meeting on Tuesday. After all the idea with the meeting is that the research partners (the development group) will prepare an action plan. (Rob, e-mail, February, 2000)

## Design

The design activity was exclusively carried out by the development team. In this phase the developers decided that the software had to be divided into modules; they decided about processes for material handling; they decided about the use of overtime. All these decisions were taken because they were either logical or possible. Commenting on the lack of involvement of SteelCo’s users in the design phase, Joshua stated:

I do not think that we could have got any constructive inputs from the crane operators or from the inventory manager. I think that we could have gained, if SteelCo had appointed an internal project manager that would have played this role. The official guy was a queer fish that we almost never saw, that never said anything at the meetings, and that never contributed with anything. (Joshua, interview)

These developers’ design decisions became the hypotheses at the base of the fact nets, the networks of causal relationships that are embedded and shape the software. The inquiring system used by the developers during this phase was again the rational one.

It is worth noting that the same principle applied, also within the development group. The creation of the system architecture by the project manager, again based on the principles of division of labor, did not take into account the locally embedded knowledge and needs of the developers. While the architecture should have been an aid to unify the work, the developers were unable to use it and decided to abandon it. The following e-mail presents Walter’s reaction to the architecture:

I am not advancing at all. All of my code must be re-written. I think we must drop the common data model and instead define our own interfaces. Now I have lost another day. (Walter, e-mail, 12-10-2000)

In conclusion, the design phase was characterized by individual decision making, individual development of fact nets, and within group disagreements: a rationalistic approach both for the developers and the project manager.

## Implementation

The implementation activity, the coding of the software, was very problematic. The complexity of the mathematical technique made the code very difficult to implement. During this phase the developers worked mostly alone, like Leibnizian inquirers developing their own modules on the hypotheses obtained in the previous phases and further developing these hypotheses on an individual basis. For example, Walter decided that for testing purposes it was not necessary to consider machine breakdowns. Challenged to include the disturbances in the software by Drew, he wrote the following e-mail:

Hi, to make a point clear, we are NOT going to change the priorities for the disturbances in the software. Obviously I cannot decide, but it will not be done with my acceptance. I disagree with Drew y but this should not surprise anyone. I think we should finally move on with the concrete tasks we have in front of us instead of talking (Walter, e-mail)

This decision was logical at the time: details were less important than speed. The developers decided that the validity of this simplification had to be discussed during the testing phase. However, simplifications are not easy to discuss and get radically embedded in the Leibnizian fact nets and these nets are not easy to dismantle as logical exercises (Churchman, 1971), but are extremely complex and evolving. The developers worked as rational inquirers in the implementation phase, developing fact nets that grew and reflected the developer’s momentary state of mind.

I do not know how many simplifications are still in the system. The problem is that when you make a code over such a long time, the code becomes incomprehensible. What was done a long time ago is not clear today and to understand where all simplification are and their ramification is totally hopeless y nowadays we can say that it is quite a mess y he he he. (Joshua, interview)

During the implementation, the developers also used idealism as a mode of inquiry. For example, the modeling of the arrival of material or the modeling of the distribution of delays emerged from a process of data collection and research for the best fitting model (usually a statistical distribution). As with the other simplifications, the developers thought that the users would validate the use of these models during testing.

During coding, the developers therefore used rationalism and idealism as expected in the framework (Table 1).

## Testing and operation

During the development project the development team held three software demonstrations. The aim of testing was that the users could control the accuracy of the software and specify new requirements. The testing was organized like a presentation where the developers used the system and showed it to the users. Not knowing exactly what they were observing, the users were not able to sustain the ardent debate required to create knowledge in a Singerian inquiry. The developers led the discussion firmly, depriving the users of the chance to criticize the validity of the simplifications in the software. These testing phases were not done according to the Singerian inquiring system seeking ardent debate. There was very limited knowledge creation that could have brought the project closer to success. The presentations were more monologues than debates; they were about telling rather than listening, self-affirmation instead of debates.

From the comments of the project sponsor at SteelCo, it can be evidenced that the problematic nature of this behavior was obvious even within the company:

We felt that some people withdrew to their ivory tower, and cultivated their own interests there, where it perhaps could have been more advantageous for them and us, if we had more dialogue in that period. (Rob, interview)

The similarity between retiring to the ivory tower and the modus operandi of the rational inquirer is very well pointed out. The comment is however more pervasive, since it hints that the rational inquiring system was not only used and apparent in the single activities, but was a practice from which the whole system suffered.

It hasn’t worked as a project, it has worked as individual persons, each with their goals, and who did their little thing, and then we have tried our best to lead it to something that made sense yand this ended up causing us a lot of problems. (Joshua, interview)

As displayed in Table 3, the ISIF framework allows us to see the modes of inquiry happening in practice during the Lager case. In this project the development team showed a predilection for the rational inquiry in all activities. Remarkably there is no use of dialectic or pragmatism, which one would predict, given the level of innovation in the project. It is only in the implementation activity that the displayed behavior and the desired behavior overlap. This overlap seems, however, to be the result of the predilection of the developers for the rational inquiring system, rather than the result of a purposeful decision. The ISIF for the Lager project and its monolithic approach to knowledge creation is in striking contrast with the pluralism indicated in the ISIF from theory. This contrast is indicative of the dangerous course taken by the team during this project.

It is not possible to link the lack of overlap to the failure of the project, since failure could derive from a combination of multiple factors including the new mathematical technique, inexperienced programmers, distributed team members, etc.; however, the case shows that choosing a specific path of knowledge creation influenced the way in which the project unfolded. The lengthy time used to create the software resulted in very complex fact nets that were very difficult to change even for the programmers that created them. This influenced the programmers’ acceptance of users’ comments – or lack of acceptance – and ultimately it shaped the content and capability of the finished product. Both the developers and the clients remained dissatisfied with the way in which knowledge was exchanged during the project. Monitoring how knowledge was exchanged during the project could have allowed the project managers to take specific measures, not least to kill the project and save money.

As reported in the theory section, the analysis can be continued at two levels: activity level and process level.

At the activity level the analysis shows that the developers followed mainly a single mode of inquiry even when other modes would seem more adequate. This is in line with the results of Kienholz (1999) who says that people have a predilection for a particular mode of inquiry. Nonetheless, managers can influence the way people interact to create knowledge. Mitroff et al. (1977) and Linstone (1984) proved that given the right preparation and context, people who are used to rational inquiry are able to shift their style and work as Singerian inquirers. The laissez faire style of the project manager did not guide the LAGER developers through the delicate process of adapting their inquiring style.

At the process level this study shows that despite the rational reasons for promoting the Singerian-type of inquirers in all ISD activities (Richardson et al., 2001), there is a gap between what is theoretically desirable and what is practically doable. This gap is part of normal and traditional ISD practices and therefore very difficult to close. One way of proceeding towards a wider adoption of the Singerian inquirer is through continuous utilization of this practice until it becomes a habit. The Singerian inquirer requires cooperation and no locus of authority within the systems: authority should be diffused in every participant (Richardson et al., 2001). Therefore, project managers have to find the right equilibrium between managing and coaching if they want both the right inquiring system used and the right channels open between users and developers.

## Implications of ISIF for ISD theory

A first reading of the case study confirms previous results in ISD research. An overreaching use of the rational inquiry by the developers caused the breakdown of communicative and collaborative structures that the exposed theory would have upheld instead. Remaining at the level of practice would have allowed us only to propose counter-practices. Rather than reiterating previous findings, this study proposes a new analytical framework, ISIF, to illuminate knowledge aspects instead of code creation aspects in the development process. The ISIF framework allows us to go a step further and understand the reasons behind practice at an epistemological level. The main implication of ISIF is to have brought the discourse on inquiring systems to the level of the ISD activity and to have done so in a feasible way.

Evidencing in the development case an almost monistic approach to knowledge creation, this study shows why previous research so often tends to be monistic and to rely mainly on one philosophy. To counterbalance this overpowering rationalism, we advocate the alternative, namely, the balanced use of all inquiring systems.

The discussion above shows however that there are different levels at which this advocacy can be carried out: the ISD process level, and the ISD activity level. At the level of the entire ISD process, the use of a single philosophy based on pragmatism can be advocated (Bennetts et al., 2000; Elgarah et al., 2001; Richardson and Courtney, 2004) because of its openness towards multiple perspective. ISIF shows us however that at the activity level, we need pluralism in order to carry out the different activities. This pluralism is needed to make sense of the intentions and actions of the developers. In the Lager case, the developers wanted to use prototypes for knowledge exchange but then they locked themselves in the ivory tower to reason their way through the problem. The project manager wanted to create the architecture to enhance common understanding, but then he left the developers to themselves. The developers wanted to present the software to get feedback from the users, but they used their knowledge to oppose the users’ requests. In all these occasions the ISIF allows us to see the knowledge process and thus act accordingly.

The ease of grasping these complex knowledge processes at the activity level, without losing sight of the entire process, is the main feature of ISIF. Since ISD activities are the building blocks of methodologies we can draw a connection between the activity level and the methodological level. For waterfall-type developments (Boehm, 1988), a method based chiefly on rational inquiry, we can find that in the process very large fact nets are created. It is therefore advisable to speed up the development and to identify and develop working modules that can be put into operation quickly. In this way the fact nets are controlled (kept small) and knowledge can be collected from actual use. For spiral-types developments (Boehm, 1988), a method based on the Lockean inquiry, the takeaway would be to go through the cycle rapidly before the fact nets become too big. For rapid prototyping the attention is on creating the right condition during testing so that the Singerian inquiring system can operate effectively. Finally, ISIF provides theoretical support for the practices advocated in agile methodologies (Lindstrom and Jeffries, 2004).

## Implications of ISIF for ISD practice

ISIF allows us to see ISD activities not merely from the point of view of what is done, but from the point of view of how knowledge is created. It brings the ISD discussion to a higher level with respect to practice, showing why actions follow a certain path. ISIF indicates the consequences of the inquiring systems adopted and is therefore useful as a management tool. The framework guides the manager in the selection of shortterm strategies for the exchange of information and knowledge.

Viewing ISD as a process of knowledge creation provides a different agenda for what should be done to create relevant systems. As expressed by Wenger (2000), when knowledge creation is first on the agenda, informal processes like conversations and brainstorming become the primary source of value creation, while formal designs contribute to value creation to the extent that they are serving knowledge processes. This section provides normative implications on process structuring and managerial activities derived from this way of interpreting ISD.

## Process implications

Speed and size The main implication of the analysis presented above is to show the connection between the developers working in isolation and the content of the fact nets. Developers will tend to develop the software partially from discussions with the users and partially from their own reasoning. If this integration results in large fact nets, then the discovery of all the causal relationships that have been embedded in the nets is almost impossible. Furthermore, the complexity of the fact nets negatively influences the users’ understanding of the code and therefore decreases the ability of the users to argue for changes. Finally, the investment in the work done is a natural source of resistance to users’ feedback. Inversely, if the knowledge integration results in small fact nets then they can easily be analyzed and invalidated during testing. The time intervals between testing activities should therefore be kept short to limit the uncontrolled growth of fact nets. Consequently, the size of the development task should be controlled so that small prototypes or modules can be developed in the short time allocated.

Cyclicality In the identification and in the design activity, consensus is achieved in different groups. Therefore, there is no a priori guarantee that the system will serve the users correctly. However, the more often the Singerian inquiring system is used, the more likely it is that the resulting information system would serve all interests involved. The direct consequence of the identification of the fact nets in the code is in showing the importance of going through multiple cycles of designing, implementing, and testing. When structuring an ISD process, this means going through the testing activity many times, putting developers and users in the situation of debating the work done according to the Singerian inquirer. As advocated earlier, short time windows and small tasks are the key to making this iterative process possible.

## Managerial implications

Support the Singerian inquirer Entering into the Singerian inquiring mode is not easy. A presentation, like in the example case, is not a debate. The Singerian inquirer requires cooperation and the sharing of authority by every participant (Richardson et al., 2001). Project managers on both sides should agree to mediate the discussion instead of leading it, and should structure it so that all necessary elements are swept in. Managers are also the guarantors that ethics are upheld, that is, the client is served.

Live testing In ISD projects most of the work on the code is done by developers. They know the code inside out and their assumptions easily become black boxed. In other words, developers cannot critically attack their own fact nets, because these have emerged from the developers’ own logic. Therefore, users should be able to test the information system personally in order to use the knowledge gained during testing as a basis for debate (Carugati, 2004). Project managers should agree on a formal requirement that prototypes or modules should be usable by the users during testing in an operational context. Anything less than that spells failure in the long run.

It has to be noticed, in conclusion, that ISIF has still to be tested proactively in new ISD projects. The mapping between the LAGER case and the ISIF framework was relatively simple to carry out because the ISD project was already over by the time of the analysis. Other studies have to be carried out – for example using action research – to effectively test the framework as a practical tool and increase its external validity. The use of this framework is not straightforward because it requires that the inquiring systems are well known and their interactions well understood to the user. In many cases the solution to a problem cannot be found at the same level of complexity as the problem itself, ISIF is one way to move to a higher level with respect to traditional ISD practices. ## Conclusions

This paper addresses the perennial and relevant problem of low probability of ISD success, presenting a view of ISD where the software creating activities and managerial activities are paired with knowledge producing activities. Building on previous research, the article shows that it is possible and advantageous to add an epistemological view of micro-level ISD activities, because this allows for a better understanding of the situation, better prediction, and ultimately better management. Despite the recent focus of researchers on the Singerian and Hegelian inquiring systems as superior epistemological underpinnings for the ISD process, human nature and physical constraints prevent the application of these inquiring modes in every ISD activity. The ISIF framework – presented and tested as a conceptual/analytical framework – highlights the complexity of knowledge creation activities and their impact on software creation and managerial activities. The variety of knowledge creation practices highlighted by the framework goes beyond what traditional lenses would allow us to see. ISIF shows that different inquiring systems can and should be active resulting in diversified ways of creating knowledge, which in turn have to be supported by multiple and adequate practices.

A case study is presented showing that – while it is not possible to quantify the goodness of fit between ISD activities and inquiring systems – the ISIF framework can be used to predict the consequences of a chosen path. In the LAGER case, the developers chose a path that followed mainly the rational inquirer with only brief use of idealism: the ISIF framework would predict that the consequences of this path would be a development failure, which is indeed how the case unfolded. Consciously focusing on the knowledge dimension of ISD activities using IFIS will provide the key elements of ISD success. The most important considerations with this focus are speed and cyclicality, size of modules, and the type of testing situation.
