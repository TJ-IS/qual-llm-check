# Anger or fear? Efects of discrete emotions on employee’s computer-related deviant behavior

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Anger Fear Deterrence theory Computer-related deviant behavio

## A B S T R A C T

Security research on the employees’ expressive motives in committing computer-related deviant behavior is limited. In this research, drawing on the appraisal tendency framework (ATF), we examine how employees emotions (anger vs. fear) and perceived sanctions influence their computer-related deviant behaviors in the workplace. Our empirical results show that employees who experienced anger were more likely to commit computer-related deviant behavior mediated by perceived informal sanctions. In contrast, employees who experienced fear were less likely to commit computer-related deviant behavior mediated by perceived formal and informal sanctions. The results provide important guidance for organizations to improve the efectiveness of deterrent controls.

## 1. Introduction

Deviant workplace behavior, such as ethical wrongdoings or violence, has long been regarded as disruptive and harmful to organiza tional performance [1–3]. With an increasing reliance on information technology (IT) in everyday jobs, this also accompanies the rise of computer-related deviant behavior by insiders. Such acts include computer abuse [4,5] or information systems (IS) misuse [6,7], ranging from stealing a co-worker’s password, gaining unauthorized access, or applying a modification to organizational data, and sabotage [8], which can result in devastating financial or operational loss to organizations. In 2016, insider employees caused 40% of security incidents [4,9], making it one of the most serious threats to organizations [6,10,11]. Another 2016 global security report also indicated that the malicious behaviors of insiders incurred an average annual cost of \$167,890 per company and that attacks from malicious insiders could take 51 days or longer to have the problem resolved [12]. As such, preventing and mitigating insider threats is an important academic and practical agenda for organizational information security management.

In the extant IS security literature, deterrence research, based on rational choice assumptions, indicates that most individuals’ detrimental security behavior intentions are afected by their judgment of the perceived severity and certainty of formal and informal sanctions [11,13]. The former refers to the perceived certainty of and the perceived severity of sanctions (e.g., company disciplinary actions) if caught, while the latter indicates individuals’ certainty and severity perception of the loss of respect and disapproval from significant others (e.g., peers, friends). However, empirical deterrence research has shown contradictory results. For instance, Lowry et al. [14] found that enhanced information security policy (ISP) may lead to reactive computer abuse. Other studies also argue that deterrence may not be effective to mitigate expressive crimes motivated by certain emotions, such as venting anger [15,16]. These inconsistent results suggest the need to go beyond the deterrence approach and investigate the efect of employees’ motivational factors on computer-related deviant behavior.

Prior work on organizational behavior research has shown that emotions employees experience in the workplace critically influence their cognition and workplace behaviors [17,18]. Appraisal theorists suggest that the cognitive appraisal of events determines “whether an emotion will be felt and which emotion it will be” [19] (p. 899). The emotion, which can be distinguished by an individual’s facial expression and biological processes, is described as a discrete emotion such as anger, fear, happiness, and sadness [20]. For example, Judge et al. [21] found that state hostility, as an afective experience to interpersonal injustice, reduces the level of job satisfaction and increases workplace deviance. In the IS security context, Willison and Warkentin [11] highlight the importance of exploring employees’ expressive motives to understand the intention to misuse technology and encourage more studies on “examining the relationship between emotions and deter rence” (p. 10). Building on this line of thinking, in this paper, we strive to provide a deeper understanding of employees’ computer-related deviant behavior in the workplace by investigating the relationship between emotions at the workplace, formal/informal sanctions, and deviant behaviors. In particular, we draw on the appraisal tendency framework (ATF) and focus on the role of discrete emotions in influ encing organizational sanctions and subsequent computer-related deviant behavior.

Regarding types of emotions, the organizational behavior literature suggests that negative emotions are an important factor in the causation of workplace deviance behavior [22]. In IS research, Beaudry and Pinsonneault [23] also categorized negative emotions into loss emotions, such as anger, and deterrence emotions, such as fear. Fear and anger are both negative-valence and high-arousal emotions that moti vate individuals to act. Fear is a response to threats [24], uncertainty, and danger to the self [25]. Anger is a response to threats that harm one’s goal [24]. In this paper, we investigate the fear or anger induced in employees by an organizational security policy change. Lowry and Moody [26] and Lowry et al. [14] found that enhanced ISPs increase employees’ psychological reactance, which is associated with emotional responses, such as anger. Johnston et al. [27] and Boss et al. [28] have argued that fear is an important factor that motivates employees to engage in compliance behaviors. Similarly, we argue that the introduction of new organizational security policies and/or countermeasures is regarded as a form of organizational change that could serve as a trigger event inducing diferent emotions and could potentially prompt changes in employee attitudes and behaviors [29]. Moreover, researchers suggest that fear and anger, although both are negative, difer in terms of certainty and control dimensions and have opposite influences on individual cognition and behavior [45]. These diferences highlight the importance of using discrete emotions in understanding the functions of negative emotions [39]. However, research on the actual efect of fear and anger on the likelihood of computerrelated deviant behavior is limited.

Thus, our research objective is to examine how anger and fear are associated with computer-related deviant behaviors directly and in directly through altering perceived formal and informal organizational sanctions. For example, fearful and angry employees have diferent perceptions of formal and informal sanctions with regard to committing detrimental security behaviors, such as accessing the corporate com puter system without permission. Fearful individuals are more likely to perceive a higher probability of getting caught and a severe punishment. In contrast, angry individuals might perceive a low apprehension risk along with minimal severity with regard to sanctions.

This study endeavors to provide two primary theoretical contribu tions to the IS security literature. First, we respond to Willison and Warkentin [11] recent call by empirically investigating the relationship between emotions and computer-related deviant behavior. While existing studies have yet to investigate the role of expressive motives in committing computer-related deviant behavior, we further extend the understanding of employee security behaviors in the workplace with a focus on two types of emotions: anger and fear. Second, we distinguish between the diferent efects of anger and fear through the ATF theoretical lens. This research sheds light on the paramount role of discrete negative emotions in predicting employee security behaviors. Our results provide important insights that will help organizations in considering the implications of employees’ emotions when designing and implementing security management programs.

The structure of the paper is as follows. First, we conduct a comprehensive literature review on the IS security research thus far and discuss the existing research gaps. Second, based on the ATF, we introduce our research model and develop the hypotheses. Third, we describe our methodology, which examines and analyzes data from 327 respondents in China. We conclude with a discussion of the findings along with theoretical and practical implications.

## 2. Prior research and theoretical foundation

## 2.1. Prior research on computer-related deviant behavior

Prior research defines workplace deviant behavior as the “voluntary behavior of organizational members that violates significant organiza tional norms, and in so doing, threatens the well-being of the organization and/or its members” [30] (p. 556). This study focuses on computer-related deviant behavior, which is defined as the employees behavior when using their work computer in a manner that violates organizational norms and security policies. To reduce the likelihood of employees’ computer-related deviant behavior, research has largely emphasized the implementation of deterrent controls or sanctions in minimizing employees’ computer misuse and increasing security policy compliance [7,31,32]. However, deterrence-based studies to date are non-conclusive or even contradictory (see Table 1). For instance, Straub [31] showed that organizations can take actions to efectively reduce the likelihood of computer abuse through investing in security countermeasures, such as the deployment of security software. D'Arcy et al. [7] also suggested that user awareness of security countermeasures significantly increases the perceived severity of sanctions. This, in turn, reduces employees’ computer misuse. However, Posey et al. [33] argued that organizational computer monitoring activities increased internal computer abuse because the monitoring activities resulted in perceptions of procedural injustice. Lowry et al. [14] concluded from their empirical study that organizational enhanced ISPs increased reactive computer abuse.

In light of the mixed empirical results, Willison and Warkentin [11] have proposed the need to study computer-related deviant behavior beyond the dominant deterrence approach. They suggested three research areas that warrant further empirical studies: 1) criminal justifications and deterrence, 2) instrumental and expressive motivation and deterrence, and 3) workplace disgruntlement and employee com puter crime. To date, progressive eforts are shown in studies pertaining to the neutralization efect and organizational injustice. Techniques of neutralization are deployed by violators to rationalize away their selfblame and guilt imposed by others and motivate them to commit deviant acts [34]. An example of this could be an ofender justifying their deviant behavior by claiming that they did not really hurt others. Other recent studies have shown that the neutralization technique plays a critical role in influencing computer-related deviant behavior [35,36], particularly in regard to the efect of sanctions on security policy violation intention, which may be insignificant in the presence of neutralization [37–39]. For example, Ingram and Hinduja [35] found that neutralization techniques were statistically significant in predicting college students’ illegal downloading of MP3 files. Lim [36] also concluded that one neutralization technique, the metaphor of the ledger, significantly increased cyberloafing behaviors by rationalizing these deviant behaviors as minor and justified. Besides research on neutralization techniques, other security researchers have also looked into workplace injustice or disgruntlement as motives for committing computer misuse [14,33,40,41]. For instance, Li et al. [40] revealed that organizational justice positively influenced employees’ compliance with the Internet use policy. Lowry et al. [14] drew on the fairness theory and showed that the explanation adequacy of security policies increased trust, which significantly reduced reactive computer abuse. Posey et al. [33] suggested that computer monitoring activities that invade employees’ privacy may cause a perception of injustice, thus increasing internal computer abuse.

By comparison, we find limited studies on instrumental and expressive motivations [4,5]. Baskerville et al. [5] developed an emote opportunity (EO) model, which integrated organizational “crime-specific opportunity structure” (p. 156) (CSOS) with a psychological process model of emotions. The EO model explained how computer abuse was influenced by the mutual efect of organizational factors and abusers’ emotions. Kim et al. [4] investigated employees’ abuse intent, based on the emotion process, and suggested that the abuse-positive afect, defined as “the pleasant-activated feelings that potential abusers experience when they appraise a situation as supporting the achievement of their goal to abuse information systems” (p. 93), positively influenced abuse intent. These two studies highlight the need to consider the emotion process in general, and our goal is to extend this stream of research by focusing on two forms of negative emotions (anger and fear). Drawing on the ATF, we are able to theorize the carry over efects of emotions on judgments and behaviors. Before introdu cing our research model in the following section, we summarize the IS literature on negative emotion–related studies.

## 2.2. Role of emotion in IS studies

A number of IS researchers have attempted to investigate the role of discrete negative emotions in IT usage behaviors, such as the relationship between anxiety, anger, and IT usage [23,46,47]; anxiety and e-trust [48]; fear; and protective motivation behaviors [28]. For instance, Hackbarth et al. [46] showed that computer anxiety negatively influenced the ease-of-use perception and fully mediated the effect of system experience on ease of use. Hwang and Kim [48] found that anxiety negatively influenced e-trust and also partially mediated the efect of perceived Web quality on e-trust. Beaudry and Pinsonneault [23] concluded that anxiety was positively related to IT usage through seeking social support and was negatively related to IT usage through psychological distancing. Within the IS security literature, most of the research has focused on the role of fear from a theoretical perspective of protection motivation, which emphasized the efect of fear in response to threats [27,28,49–52]. This stream of research contends that individuals engaged in protective motivation behaviors (e.g., security policy compliance) as a mechanism to mitigate security threats. While valuable, most IS security research had not measured actual fear [28]. Boss et al. [28] argued that “omitting fear from the full PMT [protection motivation theory] nomology is unnecessary and could undermine ISec research” (p. 841).

On the role of anger in afecting computer-related behaviors, Beaudry and Pinsonneault [23] concluded that anger had an indirect and positive efect on IT use through seeking social support. Yin et al. [53] compared diferent efects of anger-embedded and anxiety-em bedded reviews on perceived helpfulness and found that anger-embedded reviews were perceived to be less helpful than anxiety-embedded reviews. Lowry and Moody [26] and Lowry et al. [14] drew on the reactance theory and discovered that a high level of control resulted in individual reactance that negatively influenced the intention of security policy compliance. Although they did not investigate or gauge anger, reactance in the workplace can create a negative emotional response in employees, such as anger or frustration [54,55], both of which are strongly linked to workplace deviance behaviors.

In short, while both IS behavioral and security research have increasingly provided evidence that the role of negative emotion (anxiety, anger, fear) was highly relevant to users’ IT and security behaviors, research into the efect of actual fear and anger on the likelihood of computer-related deviant behavior is limited. To develop a theoretical link between emotion and computer-related deviant behavior, we draw on the ATF to empirically estimate the direct and indirect efects (by altering perceived sanctions) of anger and fear on computer-related deviant behavior.

## 3.1. Appraisal tendency framework

The ATF was originally developed by Lerner and Keltner [59] to distinguish the efects of distinct emotions on judgment and choices. Lerner and Keltner [59] posited that “each emotion activates a cognitive predisposition to appraise future events in line with the centralappraisal dimensions that triggered the emotion” (p. 477). They propose that emotions trigger behavioral and cognitive changes in response to future events. Furthermore, they argue that specific appraisal themes determine the efect of emotions on subsequent judgment and choices in a new situation. Each emotion causes a tendency to assess future situations consistently with the appraisal that induces the emotion.

## 3.1.1. Specific emotion

The ATF thus distinguishes two kinds of emotions that influence judgment and choice: integral emotion and incidental emotion. Integral emotion is related to present judgment and choice. For instance, experiencing fear and anticipated regret when individuals evaluate a gamble influences their intention to gamble [60]. Incidental emotion is associated with irrelevant judgment and choice. The ATF has primarily concentrated on incidental emotion, predicting that specific emotion arises in appraisal of the situation and that diferent appraisal dimensions determine specific emotions. For example, Smith and Ellsworth [61] identified six appraisal dimensions of emotions, including “certainty, pleasantness, attentional activity, control, anticipated efort, and responsibility” (p. 813). Certainty and control are two core appraisal themes that distinguish anger from fear [59]. For instance, anger can be related to the appraisal dimension of certainty and individual control. In contrast, fear is related to the appraisal dimension of uncertainty and situational control.

## 3.1.2. Appraisal tendencies

The ATF suggests that each emotion has carry-over efects on subsequent judgment and decisions that are in line with appraisal themes that induce emotion, which is called appraisal tendency. Emotions that are diferentiated in an appraisal theme will have a diferent cognitive predisposition in order to appraise new situations. In this research model, we aim to integrate the ATF and deterrence theory and test the appraisal tendency hypothesis. Based on the ATF, emotions that individuals experience will have a carry-over efect on the appraisal of other situations. The appraisal dimensions of certainty and control that induce anger and fear will determine the judgment of risk and the perception of formal and informal sanctions. Therefore, we hypothesize that emotions influence employees’ perceived sanctions and that the influence of emotion on cognitive dimensions matches with the appraisal themes of emotion. For example, fear is created by the appraisal of uncertainty and situational control. The carry-over efect of fear indicates that fearful individuals will treat a new situation as uncertain and a loss of individual control. In contrast, anger arises in the appraisa of certainty and individual control. Angry individuals will treat new situations as certain and under their control [59]. Therefore, fearful and angry individuals have diferent assessments of risks [62]. In the security context, the risks of computer-related deviant behavior include the certainty and severity of formal and informal sanctions. We consider that fear would be more likely to increase the perception of formal and informal sanctions and that anger would be more likely to decrease the perception of formal and informal sanctions.

## 3.1.3. Judgment or decision outcome

The ATF emphasizes that appraisal tendencies appear to influence decision outcomes [59] and that the carry-over efect is limited to the appraisal themes of emotion [59]. It predicts that discrete emotions, which cause diferent cognitive predispositions, will have diferent effects on future judgment and decision outcomes. For example, Lerner and Keltner [62] concluded that angry individuals perceive low risks and thus favor risk-seeking behaviors and that fearful individuals per ceive a high risk and thus favor risk-averse behaviors. In the security context, we consider that fearful individuals are more likely to reduce their computer-related deviant behavioral intention due to the appraisal of uncertainty and situational control. In contrast, angry individuals are more likely to increase their computer-related deviant behavioral intention due to the appraisal of certainty and individual control.

## 3.2. Impact of perceived formal/informal sanctions on computer-related deviant behavior

There is an abundance of security research based on deterrence theory that estimates the efect of formal sanctions. The central argument of deterrence theory is that formal sanctions are essential in combating criminal activities [63]. Specifically, if individuals believe that the chance of being caught and the level of punishment (i.e., certainty and severity of sanction) are high, deterrence theory posits that they would not commit criminal behaviors. The ATF also afirmed that the risk of formal sanctions played an important role in deterring criminal activities [62,64]. For example, Boufard [64] predicted that individuals who had a high perception of legal costs tended to reduce drunk-driving behaviors.

In our research context, the perceived certainty of formal sanctions is used to indicate the perception of probability in which employees may be caught when conducting computer-related deviant behaviors in the workplace. For example, Peace et al. [65] predicted that a low level of certainty of sanction may lead to high software piracy. Individuals were less likely to change or print information from others’ computer files without permission when they had a high perception of being caught for a security violation. Perceived severity of formal sanctions presented cost perception, meaning that employees may be punished for their computer-related deviant behavior. For example, D'Arcy et al. [7] held that the perceived severity of formal sanctions significantly and negatively influenced IS resource misuse intention. A high level of perceived severity of formal sanctions may reduce employees’ computer-related deviant behavior. In this study, we follow Siponen and Vance's [37] work, and multiply the severity measure by the certainty measure to reflect both the risk and cost of the perceived formal and informal sanctions. This implies that the deterrent controls would be an efective mechanism to increase the perceived cost of security violation and lead to reduced computer-related deviant behavior in organizations [7,31,32].

Therefore, we predict that perceived formal sanctions will efec tively reduce computer-related deviant behavioral intention. As such, we hypothesize the following:

H1. Perceived formal sanctions are negatively related to employees computer-related deviant behavioral intention.

Informal sanctions are diferent from formal sanctions in that they refer to the perceived certainty and severity of disapproval from peers or friends [66]. In prior research, informal sanctions have been regarded as an important addition to deterrence theory, reflecting the “non-legal costs” [67]. Hollinger and Clark [68] found that informal sanctions have a positive efect on employee compliance behaviors. Siponen and Vance [37] also concluded that informal sanctions efectively deterred policy violation behaviors in the absence of neutralization.

In our context, the perceived certainty and severity of informal sanctions refers to the probability and severity of losing respect or inducing disapproval from significant others (e.g., co-workers, leaders) for committing computer-related deviant behavior. Johnston et al. [27] held that the perceived certainty and severity of informal sanctions positively influenced security policy compliance intention. If individuals considered that important others may disapprove of their computer-related deviant behavior and could thus despise them for violating security policies, they are more likely to reduce these deviant behaviors, such as guessing a supervisor’s password to get into the corporate computer account. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

H2. Perceived informal sanctions are negatively related to employees computer-related deviant behavioral intention.

## 3.3. Impact of fear on formal/informal sanctions and computer-related deviant behavior

The ATF posits that discrete emotions have a unique efect on judgment and choices [59]. Fear is an emotional response to the appraisal theme of situational control and uncertainty, and this determines the subsequent cognitive and behavioral reactions to a new situation. Individuals experiencing fear will have carry-over efects to the appraisal of a new situation. They will perceive high uncertainty and a loss of control in the new situation, leading to higher risk perception. For example, Lerner and Keltner [62] and Rydell et al. [69] found that individuals experiencing fear may have higher risk perceptions, which leads to favoring risk-averse behaviors.

In the IS security context, employees experiencing fear will appraise the computer-related deviant behavior as uncertain and a loss of control. For example, they will perceive a higher risk of being caught and a severe punishment for accessing their supervisor’s computer account without permission. Boufard [64] suggested that fear leads to a perception of the high legal cost of crime. In addition, fearful individuals will perceive a higher risk of disapproval and loss of respect from individuals who are important to them. We hypothesize that employees experiencing fear have a higher perception of formal and informal sanctions.

In addition, the ATF proposes that fear has an influence on risk perception and judgment outcomes or decision-making. Loewenstein and Lerner [70] summarized that there exist two distinct ways of emotional state influencing decision-making—direct impact and indirect impact. For example, Boufard [64] suggested that fear that individuals experienced indirectly reduced their drunk-driving behaviors by increasing perceived cost perceptions. Their results show that fear seems to influence ofending through cost perceptions. Prior IS research suggested that fear may directly influence decision-making, such as decreasing anti-malware software use intentions [28] and promoting protective security behaviors [27,28,49–52]. Therefore, we consider that the employees’ experience of fear has both direct and indirect effects on computer-related deviant behavioral intention. We hypothesize that perceived sanctions partially mediate the efect of experienced fear on computer-related deviant behavioral intention.

H3. Employees’ experienced fear in the workplace is positively related to perceived formal sanction.

H4. Employees’ experienced fear in the workplace is positively related to perceived informal sanction.

H5. Employees’ experienced fear in the workplace is negatively related to their computer-related deviant behavioral intention.

H6. Perceived sanctions partially mediate the efect of experienced fear in the workplace on computer-related deviant behavioral intention.

## 3.4. Impact of anger on formal/informal sanctions and computer-related deviant behavior

Anger is an emotional reaction based on the appraisal of certainty and individual control. Anger may lead to confrontational behaviors and has a dysfunctional efect [53]. Lerner and Keltner [62] and Rydell et al. [69] suggested that individuals with a high level of anger have a lower perception of risk and thus adopt risk-seeking behavior. Anger leads to perceptions of low costs regarding crime and thus more criminal behaviors, such as drunk-driving [64] and assaultive violence [16].

In the IS security context, individuals experiencing anger will appraise computer-related deviant behavior as certainty and individual control. For example, Boufard [64] suggested that anger decreases the perception of the legal cost of crime. Such individuals believe that the risk of being caught and severely penalized is low and perceive less risk in receiving disapproval from others that they deem important. We thus hypothesize that employees’ experienced anger at work may reduce the perception of formal and informal sanctions.

In addition, the ATF proposes that anger afects individuals’ perception of risk and their decision-making. Prior research has found that anger has both direct and indirect efects on judgment outcomes [64]. For example, individuals experiencing anger would have a higher likelihood of knowingly giving the corporate account password to others without the permission of managers. Rodell and Judge [71] found that the hostility individuals experienced increased the likelihood of workplace deviance. Boufard [64] suggested that anger increased drunk-driving behaviors through decreasing perceived legal cost perceptions. Therefore, we consider that employees’ experienced anger in the workplace has both direct and indirect efects on computer-related deviant behavioral intention. We hypothesize that perceived sanctions partially mediate the efect of experienced anger on computer-related deviant behavioral intention.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Study design and procedure

This research aims to investigate employee’s computer-related deviant behavioral intention in the workplace. Computer-related deviant behavior is dificult to measure directly because employees are reluctant to report their deviant behaviors in the workplace if their answers are not completely anonymous. To improve the reliability and accuracy of respondents, we collected data from an online survey and a market research company in China. Participants were contacted through a third party and remained completely anonymous to the researchers.

We asked the market research company to contact participants who were working full time and were computer-using professionals. The research company sent an email invitation to 2123 of its members to create a diverse sample population. Respondents were given a pointsbased incentive for their participation. Prior to their participation in the questionnaire, participants were notified of the purpose of this research.

We designed an organizational security policy announcement to induce employees’ fear or anger according to Lowry and Moody [26] (see Appendix A). Participants were asked to assume that their organization was implementing a new security policy that may be restrictive and threatening to them. Prior research suggested that organizational change events lead to individuals’ specific emotions, such as anger or fear [29]. Lowry and Moody [26] investigated employees’ reactance to new security policies, which is a negative emotional response. Reactance leads individuals to experience anger or frustration toward the change events. In addition, the security change events might threaten employees’ working status, which leads to their experience of fear over the potential loss of power, status, or influence [72]. We considered that the new security policy implementation might evoke employees emotional reactions, such as anger or fear.

We adopted the emotion induction procedures from Lerner and Keltner [62]. They conducted a study to induce participants’ emotions by instructing participants to answer two open-ended questions. This two-part writing task has been widely used in prior research [73–76]. In each condition, we instructed participants to answer the following two questions: 1) What aspects of this ISP make you the most fearful (angry)? 2) Why does it make you so fearful (angry)? Immediately after the induction, participants were asked to finish the remaining questionnaire. A total of 412 participants were randomly assigned to either the fear or anger condition. After checking the results, there were 327 valid responses. The results showed that 160 employees participated in the fear condition and that 167 employees participated in the anger condition.

To check for non-response bias, we compared the diferences between early and late responses and the diferences between usable responses and eliminated responses based on Ref. [77]. The results indicted no significant diferences, so we concluded that non-response bias was not a serious issue for our study (Table 3).

## 4.2. Measurement

To maintain measurement reliability, the construct items were adapted from prior literature and slightly revised according to our research context. Anger and fear are measured using items from the po sitive and negative afect schedule (PANAS) [78]. Fear was assessed with two adjectives from PANAS, afraid and scared, and anger was also assessed with two adjectives from PANAS, hostile and irritable. According to Lerner et al. [79], participants were asked to rate the extent to which they felt the emotions while writing the descriptions. Response scales ranged from 1 to 5.

Perceived formal and informal sanctions comprised six items from Siponen and Vance [37]. Composite measures for sanctions were calculated by multiplying the measure of each severity and associated certainty. Composite measures “create a sanction measure that reflected both the risk and cost of perceived punishment” [37] (p. 493). Computer-related deviant behavioral intention was measured using scales developed by Rogers et al. [58]. Participants were asked to indicate the intention for their engagement in these behaviors at work. All items were reflective and used a 5-point Likert scale. Details are listed in Appendix B.

We controlled for several variables that may influence computerrelated deviant behavior: age, gender, working experience (years), computer use (hours at work), educational level, position, income, and organization size (number of employees). Lowry et al. [14] estimated that age, gender, and computer use significantly influenced reactive computer abuse. After controlling for these variables, we could better determine the contributions made by fear, anger, and perceived sanctions on computer-related deviant behavior.

## 5. Data analysis

We conducted an ANOVA analysis to test the diference between the target emotions under the fear and anger conditions. The results show that respondents reported more anger in the anger condition than in the fear condition (M = 3.525 vs. 3.209, $\underline { { \mathsf { p } } } \ : < \ : 0 . 0 1 )$ . They also reported more fear in the fear condition than in the anger condition $( \mathbf { M } = 3 . 2 4 7$ vs. $2 . 5 7 2 , \mathrm { p } \mathrm { ~ < ~ } 0 . 0 1 )$

We used the SmartPLS v.3.2 software to estimate and test our research model. As a component-based structural equation modeling (SEM) analysis approach, PLS was more suitable for theory develop ment than the covariance-based analysis approach [80] because it has minimal requirements for sample size and residual distribution [81]; was more suitable for the research model, which required an estimation of the measurement model first; enabled the testing of the magnitude of hypothesized paths and the relationships between constructs [82]; and could maximize the explained variance of endogenous variables along with estimating how much variance was explained through anger, fear, and computer-related deviant behavior [83].

## 5.2. Measurement model

We first tested the quality of our measurement model for convergent validity, reliability, and discriminant validity. Appendix C shows that all factor loadings, except ComDevBeh1 and InforSan1, were more than 0.7 and thus significant in the latent construct. This suggested that the measurement model had sound convergent validity [84]. To increase reliability and construct validity, we dropped the items ComDevBeh1 and ForSan1; we found composite reliability to be above 0.7 and the average variance extracted to be above 0.6, suggesting a high reliability for the constructs [85]. We examined the discriminant validity based on loadings, cross loadings, and correlations between constructs. Appendix C shows that item loadings on respective constructs were higher than the loadings on other constructs. Appendix D indicates that the correlation between the construct and other constructs was lower than the square root of AVE for each construct. Based on the results, our measurement model showed sound validity and reliability.

Schwarz et al. [86] compared the influence of common method bias sources and the efectiveness of common method bias techniques, including Harman’s one factor, with the confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) marker and unmeasured latent method construct (ULMC). To identify common method variance (CMV) in this paper, we followed the guidelines from Podsakof et al. [87]. We used a marker variable—outside activity—to test the CMV. Outside activity was theoretically unrelated to the research constructs. The results showed that the marker variable had no significant relationship with computer-related deviant behavioral intention (dependent variable) (β = -0.041, $\mathrm { ~ p ~ } > \ 0 . 1 )$ . Based on the above-mentioned results, we concluded that CMV was not a serious problem for this study.

## 5.3. Hypotheses testing

Before testing our research model, we checked the collinearity issues by examining the variance inflation factor (VIF) values of all predictors in the structural model. The results show that all VIF values of the endogenous constructs and predictor constructs were clearly below the threshold of 5 (see Appendix E). Hair et al. [88] and Hair et al. [89] suggest that the value for VIF should be less than 5. Therefore, collinearity was not a critical issue in our structural model.

The path coeficient results are shown in Fig. 2. The results de monstrate that 21.3% of the variances of computer-related deviant behavioral intention were explained by this model. We found that formal and informal sanctions were negatively associated with computer-related deviant behavioral intention, and this supports H1 and H2. In addition, the results show that fear significantly and positively influenced formal and informal sanctions, which meant that fear in: creases the perception of sanctions of computer-related deviant behavioral intention. Anger significantly and negatively influenced the perception of informal sanctions. The efect of anger on formal sanctions was not significant. Therefore, H3, H4, and H8 are supported, and H7 was not supported. For the control variables, our results show that position (β = 0.183, p < 0.05) and income $( \beta \ = \ - 0 . 1 3 5 , \ \mathrm { p } \ < \ 0 . 0 5 )$ significantly influenced computer-related deviant behavioral intention.

We used bootstrapping to test the mediation efect of sanction on the relationship between fear, anger, and computer-related deviant behavioral intention. We selected a bias-corrected and accelerated bootstrap, which had higher statistical power and more accurate confidence intervals. We chose 5000 bootstrap samples and completed bootstrapping bias and two-tailed testing at a significance level of 0.05. The bootstrapping results presented the significance of the indirect effect of fear and anger on computer-related deviant behavioral intention. The significance assessment of each indirect efect required the calculation of each efect’s standard error. Following the procedures proposed by Nitzl et al. [90] and Hair et al. [88], we computed the bootstrapping results of a specific indirect efect by multiplying the results of certain direct efects. For example, the bootstrapping results of the indirect efect of anger on computer-related deviant behavioral inten tion via formal sanction were calculated by multiplying the direct efect of anger on formal sanctions and the direct efect of formal sanction on computer-related deviant behavioral intention. Thereafter, we calculated the standard error, t value, p value, and confidence interval (Table 5).

## 6. Discussion and implications

## 6.1. Key findings

We draw on the appraisal tendency theory of emotion and deterrence theory to investigate the efects of anger and fear on perceived sanctions and computer-related deviant behavioral intention in organizations. Our results showed that emotions significantly influenced employee security behaviors. In addition, we found that discrete negative emotions, namely anger and fear, had an opposite efect on computer-related deviant behavioral intention. The results are consistent with the literature in psychology and organizational behavior, confirming that discrete emotions have a unique efect on individual behaviors [59].

Furthermore, we found that both perceived formal and informal sanctions had a significant and negative efect on computer-related deviant behavioral intention. The results are consistent with the underlying assumptions of deterrence theory and are also supported by the ATF in that individuals who had a high perception of a risk of sanctions would be more likely to partake in risk-averse behaviors. In addition, we found that experienced fear and anger had an opposite relationship to computer-related deviant behavioral intention, including their contradictory efects on perceived sanctions. In particular, fear increases the perception of formal and informal sanctions, while anger decreases the perception of informal sanctions. Lerner and Keltner [62] suggested that anger and fear led to diferent risks and perception of the costs. Therefore, the expressive motives to commit security behavior may be influenced by the perception of possible sanctions. Individuals experiencing fear may decrease computer-related deviant behavioral intention due to an increased perception of the effects of sanctions. In contrast, individuals experiencing anger may increase computer-related deviant behavioral intention due to their decreased perception of the efects of sanctions.

## 6.2. Contributions

This paper makes significant contributions to the IS security literature in three essential ways. First, it identifies two negative emotions (anger and fear) as factors that influence employees’ computer-related deviant behavioral intention. This responds to Willison and Warkentin's [11] recent call regarding the important role of emotions in the security literature. The proposed model, based on the ATF, enhanced our understanding of employees’ security behaviors in the workplace. Second, our model provides valuable insights into the thought processes of malicious insiders. As there are numerous IS security studies that estimate the efectiveness of deterrents on preventing computer-related deviant behavior [7,31,32], explaining employees’ security behaviors without considering their expressive motives may lead to inconsistent results, a conclusion confirmed in the comprehensive literature review we undertook prior to the commencement of our model. Our study found that the negative emotions of fear and anger may influence individual computer-related deviant behavioral intention. The results are consistent with PMT [27,28] and fairness theory [14]. Furthermore, the study deeply enhances the understanding of the role of emotions in computer-related deviant behavior. Based on the ATF, we compared the diferent roles of anger and fear in predicting the perception of sanctions with computer-related deviant behavioral intention. Although anger and fear have the same negative valence, they resulted in a contradictory efect on computer-related deviant behavioral intention. Our results thus confirmed the contradictory efect of fear and anger on risk perception and preference [62].

## 6.3. Implications for research

The results provide several theoretical implications. First, our results showed that experienced negative emotions, such as anger and fear, significantly influence computer-related deviant behavioral in tention. This result implies that emotion may have an efect on employee security behavior, as reported in prior studies [4,5]. Most prior security research has studied security behavior based on deterrence theory, with inconsistent results in regard to the efect of deterrent controls [7,31,32,42]. We thus expand the security behavior research by exploring the emotional factors suggested by Willison and Warkentin [11]. For example, our results show that the efect of deterrent controls (e.g., security policy, monitoring, and training) may be ofset by the anger that employees may have experienced in the workplace. Future research can further this research stream by examining the efect of emotions on security behaviors rather than on only cognitive judgment.

Second, we found that discrete emotions had diferent efects on security behaviors. Based on the ATF of emotion, we concluded that anger has a positive efect and fear has a negative efect on computerrelated deviant behavioral intention. Our findings confirm the role of fear and anger in influencing security behaviors, as reported in PMT [27,28] and in fairness theory [14]. We added theoretical contributions to the literature by considering fear and anger from an ATF perspective. Our findings argue that fear and anger not only are important in terms of IS security threats but could also be pivotal in influencing deviant behaviors. In addition, previous behavioral literature has estimated the efect of emotion on other workplace behaviors, such as organizational citizenship behavior [18,71]. Although we investigated computer-related deviant behavioral intention, the empirical support for expressive motives provides an important understanding in regard to how em ployee security behaviors regulate other activities, such as policy compliance and extra-role security behaviors. For example, with respect to security policy compliance behavior, employees’ expressive motives as well as distinguishing the efects of discrete emotions on their in tentions toward security policy compliance must be considered.

Third, we estimated the mediating efect of perceived sanctions on the relationship between experienced anger, fear, and computer-related deviant behavioral intention. Besides the direct efect of emotion on behaviors, we found that emotions also had an indirect efect on behaviors mediated by cognitive factors (perception of sanctions). The results were also supported by the afective events theory proposed by Weiss and Cropanzano [17]. Although the mediation efect is small, the estimates of the indirect efects of emotional states have important implications in terms of the ATF, which holds that fear and anger have opposite efects on risk perception and judgment outcomes [62]. However, the direct or indirect efects of diferent emotional states on decision-making need to be further investigated [62,64].

The results provide an integrated view of employee’s computer-related deviant behavioral intention by considering both sanctions and negative emotions along with suggesting the need to develop a framework that includes both cognitive and emotional factors to better predict security behaviors [4,37]. For example, other cognitive factors, such as attitudes toward security countermeasures, and other emotions, such as guilt, should be integrated for a deeper understanding of security behaviors.

Based on the above discussion, we conclude that emotion theory should be incorporated into the IS security literature to better understand employee security behaviors in the workplace. The ATF provides a useful theoretical foundation for understanding computer-related deviant behavior. The theory predicts that discrete emotions have unique efects on security behaviors. Further, the ATF considers the importance of integrating both cognitive and emotional factors in predicting behaviors.

## 6.4. Implications for practice

The findings also have several practical implications. First, our results suggest that emotions influence employees’ security behaviors. Experienced anger positively influences and fear negatively influences computer-related deviant behavioral intention. The deterrent controls may likely be more efective by considering expressive motives. For instance, fear leads to more deliberate considerations and increases the perception of the efects of sanctions. Organizations could extend more efort toward instilling states of fear in potential malicious insiders to increase cost perceptions. For example, notifying employees that the company uses electronic surveillance may increase insiders’ fear, which then decreases subsequent computer-related deviant behavior.

Second, our results conclude that anger and fear have opposite effects on computer-related deviant behavioral intention. Therefore, beyond the improvement of the fear factor for deterrence programs, organizations should pay more attention to the way in which anger afects computer-related deviant behavioral intention. Organizations should make eforts to reduce employees’ anger or make them aware of the negative impact of anger. For example, the perceived injustice of a new security policy implementation may lead to employees’ anger or frustration, which could then cause negative reactions to the security policy [26].

## 6.5. Limitations and future research

This study has several limitations. First, we have investigated only two discrete emotions and found that experienced anger and fear had opposite efects on computer-related deviant behavioral intention. Future research is needed to estimate the relationships between other discrete emotions, such as guilt, anxiety, and happiness, and other security behaviors, such as intra- or extra-role security behaviors. Further research on the efect of the expressive motives of security behaviors is also required. In addition, diferent discrete emotions might be acti vated in the same person simultaneously. Further studies should also consider the interaction efect of diferent emotions, such as mixed emotions on security behaviors. Second, although we conclude that cognitive factors should be considered along with emotional factors in the prediction of employees’ security behaviors, we have focused only on the perception of sanctions. Further research is thus needed to investigate other cognitive factors. In this paper, we consider emotions that employees experience in reaction to an organizational security policy change. We investigate the efect of anger and fear that employees experience on cognitive factors. Prior literature has also considered the specific emotion context. For example, the perception of injustice is likely to induce employees’ anger [21]. Further research should consider the specific security context. For example, it is possible to examine how particular sanctions influence employees’ emotions. Third, we used a self-reported method to collect data through an online survey and market research company. Although the self-reported method may result in high-quality answers under the condition of anonymity, this method has been criticized for underscoring deviant behaviors by employees themselves. The method of utilizing co-worker or supervisor ratings should be implemented to enhance the efectiveness of results. Lastly, we collected data from an online survey company in China. Culture may influence employees’ security behaviors [91], so there should be further investigations into how cultural factors influ ence emotional processes to explain why individuals behave diferently in diferent cultural contexts.
