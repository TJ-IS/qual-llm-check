RESEARCH ARTICLE

# Towards integrating acceptance and resistance research: evidence from a telecare case study

Marjolein van Offenbeek<sup>1</sup> Albert Boonstra<sup>1</sup> and DongBack Seo<sup>1</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Faculty of Economics and Business, University of Groningen, Groningen, The Netherlands

## Abstract

There is wide agreement that acceptance and resistance are crucial factors in information system (IS) adoption. Research has yielded many theories that have focused on either acceptance or resistance, often implicitly assuming that these are opposites. This paper proposes a two-factor view on acceptance and resistance, and shows how this idea may advance our knowledge of IS adoption. In developing a user reactions framework, we take a first step towards integrating the IS literature on acceptance and on resistance. This framework distinguishes between two behavioural dimensions, namely, acceptance, ranging from high use to non-use, and a dimension that ranges from enthusiastic support to aggressive resistance. Combining the two dimensions leads to four categories of user reactions. We show the framework’s usefulness by analysing data from a telecare implementation project. The findings identify ambivalent reactions. Many clients are identified as supporting but non-using, while we also find telenurses and care coordinators that show themselves to be resisting but using. These findings support the view that non-acceptance and resistance are conceptually non-equivalent. Our data suggest voluntariness as one determinant of the variation in behavioural reactions encountered. We argue that the concepts are also functionally different: IS implementers will have to adapt their strategies to the different reactions described. ## Introduction

Most information system (IS) adoption theories focus either on acceptance (e.g., Davis, 1989; Venkatesh & Davis, 2000) or on resistance (e.g., Markus, 1983; Joshi, 1991; Kim & Kankanhalli, 2009). Meanwhile, the relationship between acceptance and resistance has received conspicuously little attention. Acceptance and resistance are simply assumed to be opposite ends of a single dimension (e.g., Martinko et al, 1996; Jiang et al, 2000; Lapointe & Rivard, 2005). For instance, Martinko et al (1996, p. 313) propose an attributional explanation for individual resistance (or acceptance of) information technology.

If acceptance and resistance are positions along a common conceptual dimension, one would expect IS resistance and acceptance studies to apply similar theories, attributes and methods. However, while directed at related phenomena, these two research streams tend to draw on distinctly different assumptions. The most prominent theories underlying user acceptance research are the Theory of Reasoned Action (Ajzen & Fishbein, 1980) and the extended Theory on Planned Behaviour (Ajzen, 1985). Both stress intrapersonal factors in explaining behaviour and are widely applied within the IS field, especially in the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) (Davis, 1989) and the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT)

(Venkatesh et al, 2003). In contrast, Lewin (1947) originally conceptualized resistance within a systems view of change. Within the IS field, the most cited user resistance models are Interaction Theory (Markus, 1983), Equity Theory (Joshi, 1991) and the more recent Multilevel Theory of Resistance to IT (Lapointe & Rivard, 2005).

Alongside this divergence in theoretical underpinnings, ambivalent user behaviours can be observed in daily life that also lead us to question the bipolarity view. People may use a technology while also resisting its consequences. Sales people may rely heavily on their mobile phones and at the same time openly revolt against being connected 24/7 and express dislike of the noise mobiles create in public spaces. The opposite occurs when people support a technology without feeling obliged to use it themselves. Certain teachers strongly advocate the use of the electronic learning environment implemented in their schools, but for various reasons such as lacking support or high switching costs, they do not use it themselves.

These observations are consonant with Knowles & Linn’s (2004, p. 141) approach-avoidance model, which posits that requests for adoption will meet multiple and usually conflicting motives. This raises the question as to whether acceptance and resistance can best be conceptualized as opposite ends of one dimension (bipolarity), or as two dimensions that may be largely independent. Are non-acceptance and resistance conceptually equivalent (Cronbach, 1984), or not? To the extent that they are not, the two concepts can also be functionally nonequivalent. That is, user acceptance and resistance may have different antecedents and may influence different IS implementation outcomes, or influence the same outcome differently. The idea of conceptualizing apparently polar opposites into two separate constructs is not new, for example Herzberg (1964) proposed the two-factor motivation-hygiene theory, suggesting that job satisfaction and dissatisfaction have different antecedents.

This paper aims to advance our understanding of the relationship between, and the co-occurrence of, acceptance and resistance in IS implementation. The paper’s objective is to propose and then empirically evaluate a framework that offers an initial conceptual connection between acceptance and resistance perspectives. Moreover, our exploration of theoretical connections between these perspectives contributes to bridging the gap between two related but distinct research streams (Smith & Graetz, 2011, pp. 36–37). Further, the framework’s behavioural focus is expected to be relevant for IS implementers, who will be stimulated to develop more differentiated IS adoption management strategies.

The paper is structured as follows. In the next section, we discuss the most prominent theories on acceptance and on resistance, and analyse their differences. We agree with Bhattacherjee & Hikmet (2007, p. 728) that resistance is not just an antecedent of acceptance, and take their reasoning a step further by conceptualizing resistance as a separate reaction involving different behaviours and underlying mechanisms. This leads to a reflection on the opposites of not only acceptance- but also resistance-related behaviours. On the basis of these two behavioural dimensions we propose an adoption framework for mapping user reactions, one that acknowledges ambivalent user behaviours.

We then evaluate the framework’s applicability through a longitudinal telecare case study. After explaining the method, we analyse, on the individual level, the adoption behaviours of direct and indirect users in this Dutch telecare project. This answers the questions as to (1a) whether user reactions occur that can be positioned in each of the framework’s four behavioural categories, and (1b) what changes in user reactions develop over time. Any occurrence of ambivalent reactions would provide support for the conceptual non-equivalence of acceptance and support. On the project level, we ask (2) whether the differences in user reactions, both between groups and over time, can be understood by combining acceptance and resistance lenses. In analysing the resulting adoption, we compare the insights generated by both acceptance and resistance lenses to explore their theoretical connections (Smith & Graetz, 2011, pp. 36–37) in terms of offering corresponding, complementary and/or contrasting views.

We finish with a discussion on the contribution made by distinguishing between acceptance and resistance behaviours in understanding and managing problematic adoptions such as those found in telecare – the field chosen for our case study. In so doing, we explore whether acceptance and support are also functionally non-equivalent. We conclude that the proposed adoption framework, through the combination of concepts from different theoretical perspectives (Okhuysen & Bonardi, 2011), offers a more complete view of complex user reactions to technology and their consequences for adoption.

## Comparing acceptance and resistance research

In this section, a reflection on the most prominent IS acceptance and resistance models leads to the conclusion that building connections may add value. The rationale for choosing these models for our review is their pervasive application (e.g., the TAM – Davis, 1989; the Interaction Model – Markus, 1983; the Equity-Implementation Model – Joshi, 1991) and their comprehensiveness (e.g., the UTAUT – Venkatesh et al, 2003; the model of resistance – Lapointe & Rivard, 2005). An initial observation during the selection was that IS research focusing on resistance is much scarcer than that on acceptance: Business Source Premier offered only 17 titles relevant to resistance and 135 to acceptance between 1990 and 2011.

## Acceptance vs non-acceptance

Since Davis (1989) first introduced the TAM that has its roots in cognitive psychology, many researchers have applied and modified this model (e.g., Bhattacherjee & Hikmet. 2007: Pramatari & Theotokis. 2009).

Davis identified two variables, perceived usefulness and perceived ease-of-use, as determinants of IS use. These have become the core variables in technology acceptance research, although later studies have extended the range of variables and refined their measurement to better explain use behaviour. For example, Venkatesh & Davis (2000) extended the original TAM by incorporating subjective norms, image, job relevance, output quality and result demonstrability as determinants of perceived usefulness. Venkatesh et al (2003) reviewed user acceptance models and proposed an integrated model. In this Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology, three variables – performance expectancy, effort expectancy and social influence – determine the intention to use. In addition, they incorporated facilitating conditions that moderate use behaviour. Their inclusion of environment-based voluntariness, as a moderator in shaping an individual’s intention to use, was later supported by Wu & Lederer’s (2009) meta-analysis. However, Brown et al (2002) and Chae & Poole (2005) warn that voluntariness vs mandatoriness is a complex and dynamic continuum, and its effect on use behaviour requires careful analysis.

IS acceptance researchers tend to view acceptance as consisting of cognitive (what users think), affective (what users feel) and behavioural (their actual or intended use) components. Acceptance is defined as a user’s employment of a system to perform a task (Burton-Jones & Straub, 2006). Meta-analyses (King & He, 2006; Schepers & Wetzels, 2007) show support, although mixed, for the enhanced TAM theories. Studies have been successful in demonstrating use to be highly correlated with positive affect and awareness (cognition) of the IS. Whereas most acceptance studies stress the cognitive components, Beaudry & Pinsonneault (2010) recently demonstrated early emotions’ influence on IT use.

For all its success, acceptance research has three characteristics that limit its scope. First, it explains the intentions of individual users to use a system themselves. As such, acceptance researchers implicitly restrict acceptance behaviours to ‘system usage’, thereby implying that the behavioural component of acceptance is equivalent to use. Within this view, acceptance is conceptualized at the individual level (Burton-Jones & Gallivan, 2007; Lapointe & Rivard, 2007) and is restricted to those actors that are actual or potential, direct or indirect, users.

A second characteristic of mainstream acceptance research, and a consequence of the first, is that the antecedents of acceptance’s behavioural component stem from (Appendix A):

(1) a largely cognitive evaluation of system features, such as ‘ease-of-use’ and ‘usefulness’;

(2) individual users’ characteristics, such as self-efficacy and intrinsic motivation; and

(3) the immediate task environment as perceived by the individual user, such as visibility, image, social influence and direct facilitating conditions.

The impact of non-cognitive concepts, and those on higher levels of analysis, and the wider change context tend to fall outside the scope of such research.

A third feature of acceptance research is that acceptance’s behavioural component is often positioned on a unipolar continuum from non-use to high use (Marakas & Hornik, 1996). This illustrates that acceptance studies have not set out to explain resisting behaviours (Liang & Xue, 2009). Use equates to an actor employing the system to perform tasks, and is measured through indicators such as frequency, duration and intensity (Venkatesh et al, 2008).

Summarizing, we agree with Seeman & Gibson (2009) who, after having applied the TAM and the related Theory of Planned Behaviour, suggested that a broader view on adoption behaviours may be needed. One option would be to break away from research strategies based solely on surveys, as these tend to reinforce a one-sided focus on individual cognition (Silva, 2007). Strategies that are more differentiated can be seen in resistance research.

## Support vs resistance

Resistance is defined as behavioural reactions expressing reservation in the face of pressure exerted by change supporters seeking to alter the status quo (Waddell & Sohal, 1998; Coetsee, 1999; Lapointe & Rivard, 2005; Meissonier & Houze´, 2010). Nowadays, authors do not regard resistance as a necessarily good or bad reaction: resistance can involve either dysfunctional or functional conflict. While resource-consuming in the short term, managing resistance may prevent long-term adverse effects in system adoption (Markus, 1983; Joshi, 1991). Influential contributions to resistance research have been made by Markus (1983), Joshi (1991) and Lapointe & Rivard (2005). Google scholar yielded 1363, 157 and 207 citations for these three papers, respectively, up to 2011.

As with mainstream acceptance models, Joshi’s (1991) Equity-Implementation Model has its roots in psycho logy, and specifically in Adam’s (1963) Equity Theory. It aims to explain user resistance when implementing ISs. Joshi argues that individuals strive for equity by constantly comparing the ratio of their own inputs and outcomes with the ratios of others and of the organization. IS implementation may affect the ratios between these inputs and outcomes. Changes in outcomes can include gains, such as better working conditions and less tension, or pains, such as reduced power and reduced job security. Examples of input changes are the need to spend more time and effort, and the need for a higher skills level. If individuals perceive a lower personal net gain, or inequity, relative to others or to the organization, when implementing an IS, they tend to resist the change. As with acceptance research, the theory stresses cognitive evaluation. However, it emphasizes that evaluation of the contextual consequences, rather than of the system itself, determines users’ reactions to IS implementation. Two recent contributions also approach resistance from a psychological perspective. According to Kim & Kankanhalli (2009), users evaluate changes related to IS implementation. Their model explains the subsequent decision, whether or not to resist, using a status quo bias perspective (Samuelson & Zeckhauser, 1988). Status quo bias is the preference demonstrated by many people for the current situation. They found that switching costs increase user resistance, and that these costs mediate the relationships among the other antecedents. Organizational support and self-efficacy may temper switching costs. Klaus & Blanton (2010), in turn, interpret resistance as a violation of the psychological contract between employees and employer. With its emphasis on resistance’s relational nature within a political reality, this model offers a connection to the prominent resistance models discussed below that build on social-psychological and sociological work.

Markus’s (1983) seminal paper distinguished three resistance theories and demonstrated that two of them (system-determined and people-determined) have limited explanatory power. The third, interaction theory, more satisfactorily explained resistance in organizational settings. The system-determined view assumes that overcoming resistance requires modification of the technical features of a system. The people-determined view argues for HR-oriented interventions, such as informing and training people. The interaction theory, however, argues that y people or groups resist systems because of an interaction between characteristics related to the people and characteristics related to the system y (Markus, 1983, p. 431). Further, interaction theory has two variants: first, a sociotechnical variant that focuses on the distribution of organizational tasks across various roles and on the workrelated communication and coordination around this division of labour. The second, political variant of interaction theory raises the interesting view that wider contextual issues, such as the redistribution of power among stakeholders. may affect IS adoption.

Lapointe & Rivard (2005) identified five interacting resistance components in their Multilevel Model of Resistance to ISs. These are: (1) initial conditions (the context before implementation); (2) subject of resistance (the user or actor); (3) object of resistance (the IS); (4) perceived threats (the possible negative consequences of the object for the subject); and (5) resistance behaviours. Resistance behaviour may, according to the authors, vary from apathy to aggressive resistance. They also stress that resistance behaviours can vary over time and, not dissimilarly to Markus (1983) and Joshi (1991), argue that resistance results from perceived threats rather than from the system itself. Their system model, however, seems more in keeping with Lewin’s original idea (Dent & Goldberg, 1999) of resistance as a social system and less of a psychological phenomenon. The same is true for Meissonier & Houze´ (2010), who illustrate, with their ‘IT conflict-resistance theory’, that resistance and conflict are embedded within IT choices and IS design. They argue that support and resistance behaviours are inherent to any IT project that interferes with established organizational settings.

A common characteristic of the resistance research reported is its aim of explaining the resistance behaviours of all the actors involved, not just the users. Resistance research often starts with a stakeholder analysis and shows how IS may redistribute resources, such as power, control and finance, and may be welcomed and suppor ted by certain parties but resisted by others. Moreover, resistance research tends to pay attention to the interaction between these parties’ reactions over time. Finally, antecedents of resistance to technological change are often related to wider contextual issues than the new system’s technical and functional features (Appendix B). Markus (1983) provides an example where accountants working at headquarters gained power through a centralized financial IS at the cost of organizational members at the divisional level. Joshi (1991) also sees resistance as originating from actors’ perceptions of the consequences of IS implementation for themselves relative to those for other actors and for the organization. Lapointe & Rivard (2005) demonstrate how power and status loss, reorganization of work and economic loss can all lead to resistance whereas, in another situation, the withdrawal of a module, and a relaxed implementation scheme, eventually led to supportive use of basically the same system.

## Comparing acceptance and resistance

These three characteristics of resistance research, covering the reactions of ‘all actors’ ‘over time’ ‘in their wider context’, reflect how resistance theories have a broader scope than acceptance theories. However, the latter offer more detailed explanations. Further, whereas the acceptance lens stresses individual cognition, the resistance lens emphasizes the social and power relationships between groups. A limitation of IS resistance research is, however, that it tends to focus on explaining resistive behaviours, and largely ignores the opposite end of the dimension, which we see as supportive behaviour. Coetsee (1999) is among the few who does explicitly address the opposite end of the resistance continuum as support or even enthusiastic cooperation. Such supportive reactions, beyond system use following IS implementation, warrant greater attention. Judson (1991, p. 23) states: any management’s ability to achieve maximum benefits from change depends in part on how effectively they create and maintain a climate that minimizes resistant behaviour and encourages y support. ## A framework to link acceptance and resistance research

The behaviour-oriented framework we propose in this section is directed towards identifying and categorizing user reactions to IS implementation. Figure 1 depicts the continuums of acceptance/non-acceptance and support/ resistance that are viewed as separate behavioural dimensions. The framework uses the bipolar spectrum of behaviours suggested by Coetsee (1999), ranging from enthusiastic support through constructive cooperation, neutrality, passive resistance, to active resistance (see also Lapointe & Rivard, 2005), and then adds the use dimension that is so characteristic of IS change. Thus, acceptance is defined exclusively in terms of use behaviour. In so doing, our framework avoids the problem of Coetsee’s model, which cannot accommodate resisting users or supporting non-users.

Examples of support include active participation in an implementation team, promoting system use to a colleague and adapting one’s work routines to facilitate others’ system use. Examples of resistance are apathy (Lee & Clark, 1997), complaining (Markus, 1983), absenteeism, voicing opposing views (Kossek et al, 1994), whistleblowing, committing errors (Marakas & Hornik, 1996) and sabotage (Day, 2000).

The framework’s feasibility is evaluated in the ‘Results’ section. Alongside offering a more complete taxonomy of user reactions, its expected added value is its careful positioning of mandatory users who may or may not resist the system. It also recognizes that, in a voluntary setting, non-users are not necessarily resistant. Theories drawn from both research streams can be used to explain these reactions, and they may connect in different ways (Smith & Graetz, 2011). For instance, our case features both voluntary and mandatory users within a single setting. Acceptance theory, through the factor of ‘social influence’, recognizes that their respective reactions may interact, but does not give cues on how this might happen and how this will affect adoption. It implies that contagion and other social influence processes are seen as the primary cause of convergence (Leonardi & Barley, 2010, p. 6). Resistance research addresses this by offering insights into possible interaction mechanisms, between individuals and groups, and how these will determine the resulting adoption.

A two-factor view stresses that addressing resistance will not guarantee system use, and that promoting or forcing acceptance will not always increase support. This is reflected in appropriation studies that describe how deviating IS-use patterns (Leonardi & Barley, 2010) may emerge that ultimately affect overall adoption. Such deviating appropriation may result from and signal resisting usage in a setting experienced as mandatory. For practitioners it is similarly relevant to recognize that potential users who are supportive can still demonstrate low or nonuse behaviours. This can be due to time constraints or because the system is viewed as beneficial for the organization or unit, but not so useful or easy-to-use for the individual concerned. Beaudry & Pinsonneault (2005) suggest that individuals who perceive an IT as an opportunity, but feel they do not have control over the system, will tend to be more passive in their interaction with it. According to their Coping Model of User Adaptation, only users that perceive IT as an opportunity and feel they have control over it will actively try to maximize the IT benefits. Within our taxonomy, they would be expected to show actively supporting use. This short elaboration illustrates how the taxonomy can help to pinpoint and distinguish more problematic and sometimes apparently contradictory user responses that fit well with descriptions of adoption in process-oriented IS research. In line with our daily experiences, the literature features all four categories of user reactions (see the examples in Table 2). Whereas, variance-oriented IS research has tended to focus on a narrower set of less ambiguous behaviours, our model offers the opportunity to extend the scope.

To conclude, the framework distinguishes four groups: (1) supporting users; (2) resisting users; (3) resisting nonusers; and (4) supporting non-users. Although this taxonomy is based on discrete categories, the underlying dimensions can be conceived of as continuous variables. In this study, acceptance and resistance are conceptualized as unipolar and bipolar constructs, respectively, and need not necessarily correlate (Burke et al, 2003). We further argue that the constructs of acceptance/ non-acceptance and support/resistance are both expected to contribute to explaining adoption at the level of the organization. We therefore consider these two distinct but related dimensions collectively as a set, and label this as adoption (Edwards, 2011). Following Edwards (2011), the literature-based indicators introduced in the ‘Method’ section are assumed to reflect either the level of acceptance or the level of resistance.

## Method

## Context

For not only describing but also understanding people’s complex and sometimes ambivalent and changing intentions and behaviours in IS adoption, a rich and longitudinal data set is required. Here, such data were drawn from a telecare implementation project in the Netherlands. The term telecare relates to the use of information and communication technology to facilitate health and social care delivery to individuals in their own homes (Barlow et al, 2006). While the expectations of telecare are high, large-scale adoption of telecare has proven difficult (Hebert et al, 2006). For example, eight projects running in the Netherlands had only managed to connect 468 clients by the end of 2007 (Peeters et al, 2008). Such apparent adoption problems make home telecare an excellent field for studying acceptance/nonacceptance and support/resistance. The site selected for the study was being confronted with similar adoption problems: 28% of connected clients unsubscribed within the first 6 months, and few became active users. This telecare system was implemented in 2007 and included a video connection and camera in the clients’ homes that enabled them to contact their homecare provider’s service centre. At the centre, registered nurses provide telecare on a 24/7 basis.

Before this telecare system was implemented, clients would receive homecare visits from a carer. As shown on the right-hand side of Figure 2, the conventional procedure was as follows: (1) a client requests a regular homecare service; (2) a care coordinator discusses the situation with the client to establish a care plan; (3) the care coordinator schedules and plans for a carer to visit the client; (4) the carer regularly visits and delivers care to the client; and (5) the carer updates the coordinator about the client’s status.

With the telecare system (see left-hand side of Figure 2), in addition to visits by the carer, a client can initiate video calls: (i) the client makes a video call to the service centre whenever a need is felt; (ii) a telenurse answers the call and counsels the client; (iii) during the call, the software displays the client’s personal and medical data and allows the telenurse to access and update the client’s health record; (iv) as the coordinators cannot access this record, the telenurse may fax or mail updates about the client’s condition. The telecare system aims to efficiently utilize resources, for instance by providing clients with unlimited access to care services while decreasing the number of unnecessary home visits (4).

The telecare project involved stakeholder groups made up of individuals, such as carers, coordinators, telenurses, clients and project managers, and, on the project level, project sponsors (e.g., insurers, government) and implementers (e.g., telecommunications company, government, homecare agency). To an extent, the behaviours of all these stakeholders influenced the project’s fate. Nevertheless, this paper focuses on the users’ intentions and behaviours: clients and telenurses as direct users, and care coordinators as indirect ones. The behaviours that users display are crucial to the eventual adoption. Moreover, the user categories differed in voluntariness of system use, allowing us to explore the role of this important contextual dimension (see above). Clients subscribed and subsequently had video consultations on a voluntary basis since their regular care continued. Care coordinators were not only responsible for the clients’ care delivery, whether face-to face or through telecare, they had also been ordered to recruit clients for the telecare system. During implementation, coordinators were asked to recruit suitable clients for telecare from those currently cared for by their team. Thus, it was the coordinators who selected and approached clients to facilitate their subsequent use of the system and to integrate telecare into the homecare services provided to the client. Consequently, they can be viewed as indirect system users. The mandatory role assigned to the telenurses was to operate the system at the service centre.

## Research design

Our design combined variance and process strategies (Sabherwal & Robey, 1995; Eisenhardt & Bourgeois, 1988), both of which used qualitative and quantitative data (Ibid., p. 322) as will be explained below. Our epistemological position in terms of social theory is one of inter-subjectivity: some accounts are better than others (Miles & Huberman, 1994, p. 277) because this fits more closely with the details of what happened according to observers and those involved. In the variance strategy, we stretch this position in the direction of a positivist approach to reach a confirmable, shared standard as to whether ambivalent intentions and behaviours have surfaced or not. This standard is in part contextually bounded (Lewis & Grimes, 1999), as it necessarily depends on the researchers’ appreciation of the situation (Mingers, 2001, p. 245). As such, we checked our coding and grounded our positioning not only in observed behaviour, but also against our holistic understanding of interview reports and of in-depth process data.

In the process strategy, we lean towards interpretivism to arrive at an evaluation of the two theoretical streams’ contributions to understanding the adoption process in this telecare project. In turn, this interpretative reading of the text aimed at ‘thickness’, fitting the multiple data sources available, and explicitly comparing the contributions of the concepts in Appendices A and B to reach an understanding of the data patterns. Therefore, our process strategy, unlike our analysis of the variance data, was not purely subjective, but neither was it purely objective.

To summarize, our research sits in the transition zone (Mingers, 2001, p. 247) between positivism and interpretivism, the boundaries of which we see as fuzzy and permeable (Lewis & Grimes, 1999). In using methods originating from different paradigms, we follow the recommendations of Mingers (2001) for carrying out multi-method IS research to best grasp the inherent complexity and multi-dimensionality of the situation investigated.

## Data collection

Data collection, for answering the research questions: (1a) whether user reactions occur that can be positioned in each of the framework’s four behavioural categories, and (1b) what changes in user reactions develop over time; and (2) whether the differences in user reactions, both between groups and over time, can be understood by combining acceptance and resistance lenses, proceeded as follows. In May 2007, we conducted semistructured telephone interviews with 30 clients, both men and women, from both urban and rural areas to capture their intentions regarding telecare adoption. In February 2008, well after the telecare system had been installed in their homes, we repeated the interviews to capture changes in the clients’ reactions. The interviewer noted down any strong non-verbal impressions such as the interviewee ‘sounding very angry’ or ‘too ill to say much’. Six in-depth face-to-face client interviews put the telephone interviews into context.

During the Summer of 2007, we interviewed six care coordinators and three telenurses for 60–90 min each, using open-ended questions to determine their reactions to the telecare system. and see which concepts of the acceptance and resistance literatures they reflected (Appendices A and B). Open-ended questions encouraged the respondents to tell us about their experiences from the initial decision to introduce telecare, through implementation, to using the system. To capture the antecedents of their reactions we also asked questions about whether the system was appropriate for their jobs, and whether and how it affected their work and lives. Interviews were taped and then transcribed, and the interviewee was asked to review the interview report and provide feedback on any inaccuracies. Answering the second question was based on the above data, as well as on other methods and sources. In addition to interviewing direct and indirect users, we held workshops;

collected written reports, policy plans, minutes, usage records and call forms; and observed a sample of calls. These sources informed our analysis of the adoption process and further ensured internal validity or ‘credibility’ (Miles & Huberman, 1994, p. 279). It took about 18 months (October 2006–March 2008) to collect the data. This was a sufficient period, from a process view, to capture policy changes and effects, as well as from a variance view to capture changes in the actors’ reactions.

## Describing the variance at actor level

For the purpose of answering the first and second research questions, the data coding proceeded deductively. We first split the relevant interview summaries into 379 separate quotes or ‘utterances’ (unitization). We then classified these based on a start-list of codes (Miles & Huberman, 1994, p. 58) that consisted of concepts derived from the acceptance and resistance models (see Appendices A and B). Three researchers independently reviewed and classified the quotes. This check-coding (Miles & Huberman, 1994) enabled us to assess the interrater reliability (Fleenor et al, 1996). After a few revisions to the code list, the three researchers’ initial classifications matched at just above the 70% level. Where they failed to agree, the three researchers discussed their codings until a consensus was achieved: sometimes a quote had to be further divided, at other times we had to look at the context (such as the entire paragraph in the interview report) to reach a consensus on categorization. Abstraction then followed (Spiggle, 1994): that is, the coded quotes were used to position people’s acceptance and resistance towards the telecare system in terms of the proposed framework. To further increase reliability or ‘auditability’ (Miles & Huberman, 1994, p. 278), we checked this initial quote-based actor positioning against the quantitative data on direct and indirect system use, as well as against the context in terms of the interview report. Tables were constructed by putting the individuals in rows and then, in the columns, noting for each source what other data we had on that person.

## The sources for these data matrices were as follows:

Clients – The frequency of use was automatically registered. The kinds of use were determined through the client interviews, and also by forms the telenurses were asked to complete for a sample of video calls (n ¼ 1139). A list with reasons that clients had offered for subscribing could be compared with the reasons for rejection given by 288 non-subscribers.

Care coordinators – In addition to the interviews, we received a list of the care coordinators that had recruited clients and a file with the number of subscribed clients per team, and this allowed us to triangulate the care coordinators’ indirect use.

Telenurses – The three interviews were complemented with weekly 2-hour observations, informal discussions, and a meeting with telenurses and their supervisors in

March 2008 during which adoption data were fed back. The system also showed which nurses had taken calls. This variance-oriented strategy resulted in a filling of the quadrants with user reactions.

## Analysing the adoption process

To also gain insight into the ‘fittingness’ (Miles & Huberman, 1994, p. 279) or ‘theoretical validity’ (Maxwell, 1992) of the proposed framework, we used a process strategy alongside the descriptive analysis of variance. This step in the data analysis was aimed at answering our second research question. The process strategy consisted of a careful interpretative step to determine the extent to which the descriptive quadrant filling could be understood from the antecedents of the two theoretical streams (see Appendices A and B). Contextualized process details were used to build an understanding of the reasons for the developments in adoption reactions, which we were able to trace since data had been collected over an extended period. In interpreting the adoption process in its context, we applied the insights from the two theoretical streams and evaluated how the insights overlapped, complemented or contrasted one another. This reading of the adoption process was based on data gained from interviews and meetings held with managers and project members who shared their experiences and impressions about the adoption. For example, in July 2007 and March 2008, workshops were held to discuss the findings and interpretations with co-researchers and stakeholder representatives. The trends emerging from the individual-level data fitted with their project-level observations.

## Results

Following the two research questions that guide our framework evaluation, this section is divided into two parts. The first part asks (1) whether user reactions occur in each of the framework’s four behavioural categories and (2) what changes in user reactions develop over time. As explained earlier, an a priori distinction was made between user groups, that is, clients and employees, because of differences in voluntariness (Karahanna et al, 1999; Chae & Poole, 2005). To answer this question, we first categorize clients, and then employees, into the quadrants to determine whether all quadrants empirically exist. For both actor groups, we start here by focusing on one individual’s acceptance and resistance to illustrate how the quotes were placed on the axes (see also Appendices A–D). From a variance perspective (see ‘Method’ section), we present the resulting pictures for the respective actor groups. Within a variance perspective, a change of (intended) behaviours over time can be analysed through repeated measurements. We will illustrate this with regard to clients. That is, we show how some clients moved from one quadrant to another between the first (May 2007) and the second (February 2008) interviews. The employee subsection focuses on acceptance and resistance at the time of the interviews in Summer 2007. Next, we address the second question whether differences in user reactions, both between groups and over time, can be understood by combining acceptance and resistance lenses? Here, we use the process details available to develop an understanding of how and why user reactions come about. Our variance data is understood within the context of our qualitative data on the evolving implementation process. Here, by providing separate analyses from acceptance and from resistance perspectives, we explore whether the acceptance and resistance literatures offer corresponding, complementary or contrasting insights.

## Results question 2

In this subsection, we first use sensitizing concepts from the acceptance literature (Appendix A), and then from the resistance literature (Appendix B). This answers the second research question of how the differences in user reactions both between groups and over time can be understood by combining acceptance and a resistance lenses.

Analysis from the acceptance perspective The clients who agreed to have a connection generally expected the system to be useful. During the interviews, the researchers tried to elicit their use expectations. Interestingly, before implementation, most had no specific uses for the system in mind. This fits with the finding that care coordinators thought the usefulness limited, because the client had to initiate the video call. Initially, the system did not allow the telenurses to establish a video call and so proactively check on clients and, as one observed, for that reason, I see the telecare service as a separate service. The limited usefulness perceived may explain why care coordinators put little effort into encouraging clients to subscribe. For C1 and C4, this also resulted in a very low indirect use.

Having been connected for 8 months, the interviewed clients’ reactions had evolved in different directions: to the system being very useful, for example they [telenurses] watch when I inject insulin, so now it’s no longer necessary to wait for a nurse to come to my home, to it being impractical, such as the telecare system did not provide opportunities to connect with family, friends or other carers. While the clients agreed that the system itself was easy to operate, some criticized the equipment: I don’t like all these cables in my house, or the television and the equipment are in a fixed place and thus not in easy reach in the event of an accident in another room. Another client used telecare for a specific service (the daily medication monitoring), thus replacing a large number of homecare visits. This very satisfied client used the system in line with the project team’s substitution intention. Five interviewed clients experienced telecare as a ‘free’ additional and flexible service. They used it from time to time for a social chat, to check whether the technology still worked as they had been requested to do or for urgent medical questions; such as when a client’s spouse fell, or when a client had a question about using a certain drug. Of the entire group of connected clients (n ¼ 335), 72% did not use, or hardly used, the system. Some of these clients were among the interviewees. They mentioned that the scheme was still beneficial as they experienced an increased feeling of safety. The forms completed by the telenurses confirmed that few of the 335 connected clients made substantial use of the scheme: 84% of the 39.3% of video calls that substituted for a home visit were made by just 14 clients.

The care coordinators still felt little inclination to stimulate the carers and connected clients to appropriate the system: since we don’t have access to the electronic client record, I don’t know how often clients use telecare and what care the telenurses deliver. This can be interpreted as a low ease-of-use for these indirect users. The telenurses also felt that ease-of-use could be enhanced by integrating the telecare system with other ISs. However, they stressed that the system was useful and came up with many different ways of using it, such as using video calls to ‘check on clients during the night’ and ‘managing client information’. They reported that they were well trained on the system and felt that our nursing skills enable us to exploit the social exchanges in depth. Their use behaviour ranged from moderate to high, with telecare delivery being one of their formal tasks.

The low recruitment and client-use percentages were disappointing for the project managers who expected the system to have a broader applicability. Therefore, during one discussion the care coordinators were invited to forget about the current telecare system for a moment and the way it had been organized. They were instead asked to focus just on the technology and think about whether you can come up with any useful homecare applications.

The project manager noted that the coordinators suddenly opened up and, surprisingly, pictured themselves as direct users of the technology instead of as indirect users. In such a situation, the coordinators portrayed the implemented telecare as useful and easy-to-use.

To summarize, in line with an acceptance perspective, the few clients who actually used the system perceived it as useful and easy-to-use. The same seems to be true for the telenurses: they recognize the system’s merits and use it. However, acceptance theory does less well in explaining why most clients that subscribed did not regularly use the telecare even though many of them acknowledged its usefulness and ease-of-use after their initial experiences. Further, this perspective does not fully explain why some care coordinators tried to get clients to participate in the initiative, whereas others refused to offer telecare to any of their clients. Below, the data are reinterpreted from a support/resistance perspective (Appendix B).

Analysis from the resistance perspective During implementation, some clients voiced concerns that they might have to start paying for telecare once the pilot period ended. The conditions under which the system would be provided were unclear to them. This relates to expected changes in power and resources. Further, five clients indicated their fears over it replacing their regular home visits and the loss of face-to-face contact with carers. Consequently, a lack of active support by some became apparent: 28% of clients cancelled their telecare subscriptions during the free trial period. Although this percentage included clients who had died or moved away, it still points towards a considerable proportion who no longer wanted to support the scheme. Carers even noted that a few clients showed resistance by advising others against participation in the pilot. On the other hand, at the start of 2008, a newly hired telenurse called 50 clients to ask their experiences, which led her to conclude that 75% were satisfied with the situation. This shows that many clients stayed supportive, even though system registrations tell they were not actually using the system.

This particular telenurse, with whom we only had informal talks, demonstrated far greater support, for example by pro-actively starting to call clients, than the three telenurses interviewed. The resistance perspective reveals that, even though the latter accepted the system, they were not happy with its integration into their work. For them, the system increased their workload: y it is additional, and we just have to do it on top of our usual work and y you feel you are being awfully interrupted by the system. They also thought it was not as challenging or significant as their other tasks in the service centre: data entry is not a task for a qualified nurse and the calls are very often technical or social in nature y of the care-oriented calls only 50% require a registered nurse.

At the same time, care coordinators resisted delegating responsibility related to the telecare provided by the service centre: y with telecare, I am not only responsible for my own team of carers but also for the care provided by this centre. At the same time, I have to delegate such responsibility to a manager at the service centre whom I hardly know. They anticipated an unwelcome shift in their power and resources, and wanted to remain fully accountable for their clients. Thus, some coordinators (including C4 and C6) thought that ‘telecare should be decentralized to the local teams’. Others (C2 and C5) did not necessarily reject the centralized nature, but insisted that it would simply not be useful for coordinators if they could not access the service centre’s client information (see above). Care coordinator C2 showed no overt resistance as such but still a very limited acceptance.

Care coordinators further indicated that they did not like the top-down implementation, and this seemed to have pushed them towards resistance, for example, I couldn’t exert any influence. Moreover, they did not see the service centre as a part of their homecare organization. One coordinator commented: I don’t know who they are and I don’t care. My job is to organize and deliver homecare. The telecare operation is the responsibility of headquarters. Coordinators were also aware that their carers feared losing their jobs: Many carers have part-time, low income, jobs. Many of them experience telecare as a technological threat. Because the telecare initiative has not been properly discussed with them, they may be unaware, indifferent, or opposed to it. Only a very few have a positive attitude. Consequently, many coordinators maintained a passive role and refrained from stimulating carers to promote, or clients to adopt, telecare despite this task having been assigned to them.

In the second round of interviews, clients were asked who had talked to them about the way telecare could be used to their advantage. The above explains why most answered no one. Three interviewees commented that carers had explicitly advised them not to accept a connection. The managers attributed this finding to the care coordinators’ resistance: those opposed to it transfer their views [to their team] y we should deal with this problem. When coordinators neglect assigned tasks, or even work against changes introduced by their management, this can be interpreted as resistance. In fact, it was difficult to find care coordinators who agreed to an interview. Their resisting behaviour may influence their teams’ and clients’ reactions. This phenomenon would indicate an interaction effect between resistance and acceptance through the mechanism of social influence. On the positive side, one of the interviewed care coordinators eventually became openly supportive of the system and was actively telling others about her experiences. She had overcome the ‘bad start’.

Connections between acceptance and resistance perspectives As is shown above, the resistance perspective draws out aspects that are not directly related to the system and its features, but rather to its consequences. Consequences that clients worried about involved the fear of losing human contact, lack of clarity in the telecare delivery conditions and a fear (unfounded) of having to spend financial resources. The employees’ resistances were related to changing jobs, working conditions and the fear of carers being laid off. Although the telenurses were critical about their working conditions, the acceptance perspective shows they also saw the system’s benefits. Resistance theory complements acceptance theory by explaining why people might complain about or work against adoption, even when the system in itself is regarded as ‘useful’ and ‘easy-to-use’. In our case, acceptance and resistance co-existed. Another insight was that while the acceptance perspective suggested that the problems with integrating telecare with the existing information and operational processes decreased its easeof-use and usefulness, and that this seemed to explain why not all coordinators enlisted clients; the resistance perspective complemented this insight by showing that, for all the interviewed coordinators but one, more fundamental issues were at play. Some translated their resistance into non-use, others did not.

In its turn, the resistance perspective fails to address ‘system use’ factors, and so also offers only a partial understanding of the variance in user reactions encountered. From the resistance perspective, an observer might conclude that potential users simply do not want the system, whereas the acceptance perspective showed that this is not necessarily the case.

## Discussion

Most literature implicitly takes a bipolar view of acceptance and resistance and then focuses either on acceptance or on its assumed opposite resistance. Taking a different view, this paper proposes a two-factor framework in which acceptance and support/resistance are conceptualized as two distinct behavioural dimensions. We see these as a set of adoption behaviours because together they contribute to IS adoption in a unit, company or market. By distinguishing these two dimensions in individual user reactions, our framework combines two rich research traditions, and adds value to the field by proposing conceptualizing acceptance and resistance as complimentary rather than as opposites.

From the case findings reported, it follows that it is indeed an oversimplification to see acceptance and resistance as opposites. Conceptually, the analysis of employees’ voiced and displayed reactions shows that the two dimensions refer to distinct behaviours that are to some extent, although not entirely, independent. Theoretically, both literature streams contribute to our understanding of user reactions in the situation studied. Only by combining the two perspectives, we were able to explain the direct and indirect users’ reactions.

The antecedents of acceptance stem from the relatively narrow context of the system-in-use, whereas the antecedents of support/resistance relate to the anticipated and experienced changes in a wider context. As different explanations seem to apply to the two behavioural dimensions, we propose viewing adoption as a label, and not as a multidimensional construct (Edwards, 2001, p. 148). Moreover, a methodological advantage of the two-factor view is that we can more specifically and accurately measure positive and negative user reactions.

The in-depth case analysis offers support for this conceptual distinction in terms of construct validity. The results demonstrate that acceptance and support/ resistance behaviours can co-exist and that the variance in these behaviours may differ by user group. The variance encountered in our data shows that each user’s behavioural reaction towards IS adoption can be categorized into one of four quadrants and that this is relevant. While the category ‘supporting users’ has been well identified in the IS acceptance literature, the other user reaction categories have either received less attention, not been distinguished between, or may have been wrongly categorized in the conventional IS acceptance and resistance literature. Our study especially highlights the difference between resisting and supporting nonusers. The former included a few clients who were initially connected but later made the effort to unsubscribe from the system and also to actively spread their resistance to others. The IS acceptance literature would simply identify them as non-users. Next, the somewhat ambivalent category of ‘supporting non-users’ contained clients who were positive about the initiative, and thought the option of being able to contact telenurses a good one, but did not feel the immediate need themselves, or did not quite know how to embed it in their routines. These clients would again be simply identified as non-users according to the IS acceptance literature. However, the role of these supporting non-using clients in the adoption process differs considerably from the resisting non-using clients mentioned above.

The final category, that of resisting users, brings us to a closer understanding of the significance of voluntariness in the intra-person interaction between acceptance and support/resistance reactions (Brown et al, 2002; Chae & Poole, 2005). Voluntary use occurs when users perceive technology adoption to be a wilful choice whereas, in a mandated environment, users perceive use to be organizationally compulsory (Venkatesh & Davis, 2000). Research suggests that there is a continuum from voluntary to mandatory use (Karahanna et al, 1999; Brown et al, 2002). Our three user groups (clients, telenurses and coordinators) seemed to differ in their reactions to the telecare implementation, partly because of differences in voluntariness. For clients, usage was fully voluntary: they would therefore only use the system if they perceived this additional service to be valuable and, at the same time, did not fear losing power or human contact. In this group, we barely encountered any ‘resisting users’. At the mandatory end of the continuum, telenurses, given their roles, simply had to use the system whether they supported or resisted its implementation. Here, use did not co-vary with resistance behaviours. Our data suggest that, in a fully mandatory environment, workers will end up being either supporting or resisting users, as non-use will lead to inadequate job performance or will require resignation. This supports the coping model (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005). The coordinators, as indirect users, were told to integrate this communication channel within their care delivery system, but it did not directly affect their job or their performance if they did not. Coordinators displayed some resisting behaviours and our study shows that these were combined with different degrees of use.

## Theoretical contribution

The theoretical contributions of this research are: (1) disentangling the two perspectives (acceptance/ non-acceptance and support/resistance) that have ambiguously been combined in most IS adoption literature; (2) conceptually combining these two perspectives for describing and analysing complex behaviour during IS implementation; and (3) illustrating the applicability of the proposed framework. If, instead, only an acceptance model had been used for the case analysis, then clients and care coordinators would have been categorized based only on their behaviours related to their own system use, something determined by variables in the immediate system context (Venkatesh et al, 2003). Alternatively, if only a resistance model had been applied (e.g., Lapointe & Rivard, 2005), the categorization’s scope would have been limited to the range from ‘enthusiastic support’ to ‘aggressive resistance’, and mainly determined by variables referring to the wider context of telecare-related changes. The adoption framework proposed in this study reveals additional behavioural categories. The model would have been rejected if ambivalent behaviours had not occurred.

The importance of recognizing these less obvious reactions is supported by insights from appropriation and enactment studies (e.g., Leonardi & Barley, 2010, p.7) that show adoption to be a negotiation process in which users demonstrate agency in how they start to use a system to accomplish tasks. The resulting system-in-use may conform to or deviate from implementers’ intentions. Our framework suggests that deviation will most often result from resistive user reactions and conformance from supportive user reactions. Likewise, our distinction between resisting and supporting non-users reflects reactions to IS that have been described elsewhere, but not mentioned as such in social-constructivist leaning studies. For example, the case reported by Dey et al (2011) featured Bangladeshi farmers who saw social, psychological and occupational benefits in using mobile phones, but faced major usability problems. In the end, they developed creative ways to overcome these problems and became supportive users. So not only can people’s adoption behaviours change over time, in these adoption behaviours people can show agency leading to changes in the context, in the way in which a system is used, or even in the system itself. For implementers, it is important to differentiate between clear-cut and more ambivalent user reactions.

## Practical contribution

A first contribution of this research to practitioners is that it unveils the unobserved ambivalent categories (resisting users and supporting non-users) that have been overlooked in the conventional IS acceptance and resistance literature. Implementers can use the framework as a tool to assess and monitor people’s behaviours during IS implementation and to develop differentiated interventions. This is especially important given the finding that, initially relatively independent, individual users’ reactions later converge to group behaviour (Lapointe & Rivard, 2005). As such, this emphasizes the managerial relevance of continuing to monitor both acceptance and resistance from the start.

A second practical contribution is that this research enables a better understanding of users’ complex reactions and the way they interact. Especially in a situation of mandated use (Brown et al, 2002; Chae & Poole, 2005), resistance can be manifested in many ways from mild through to destructive behaviours. Resistant use may lead to decreasing job satisfaction, negative feelings towards supervisors and decreasing loyalty to the organization (Markus, 1983; Zuboff, 1988). Support and acceptance should be viewed differently by implementers. Supportive behaviour is a resource that they can use, whereas acceptance is first and foremost a performance indicator of system implementation. Also, this framework can warn implementers that active users can be either suppor ting or resisting. The first reaction can be built upon; the latter may be detrimental over time and thus needs to be acted upon.

Finally, this research opens up a novel perspective for practitioners in developing more focused implementation strategies that, hopefully, will lead to better outcomes (Markus, 1983, p. 430). Understanding the antecedents of user reactions (Appendices A and B) offers cues on how to manage them. When confronted with resistant use behaviours, making system use more attractive may not be effective. A proposition following from our framework is that resistance needs managerial interventions directed at the context in which the system is being used. Knowles & Linn’s (2004) strategies for reducing resistance, by decreasing what they call avoidance motivation, may be particularly effective. This is illustrated by the telenurses’ resistance, which was rooted in their perception of the telecare system as a disturbance to their ‘regular’ highly skilled telecure tasks, and as a threat to their job status. To resolve this problem, one nurse became primarily responsible for telecare during the daytime shifts. This distinct role gave this telenurse responsibility and status, and the other telenurses suffered fewer disturbances to their scheduled telecure tasks during the working day. This example shows how a managerial intervention, by clarifying and reassigning responsibility, is able to mitigate an experienced threat. However, in the event of non-acceptance, interventions do need to address the system’s functionality and design, or at least users’ perceptions of it. Here, approach motivating interventions (Knowles & Linn, 2004) that make system use more attractive may be effective. For example, the supportive clients who, after their initial telecare experiences, stopped using it did so because of its limited functionality, or because they disliked hardware aspects such as the systems’ fixed location.

## Strengths and limitations

A strength of our study is that, rather than focusing on intrapersonal factors, we also measured actual behaviour. In psychology, contrasting bipolar and two-factor views, such as Herzberg’s (1964) two-factor theory, has encountered methodological problems, as different data measurement techniques vary in their ability to separate positive and negative effects (Russell & Carroll, 1999). Such measurement problems will not occur with the two-factor approach proposed here as our dependent variables reflect distinct behaviours that can be observed separately. Therefore, answering the two empirical questions as to whether these behaviours can and do cooccur, and whether they have different antecedents raises fewer methodological problems. This study also has limitations. One is that we could only formally interview clients two times and employees one time, which limited the formal tracking of behaviour changes over time. Informal observations and sessions were more continuous. Furthermore, we did not systematically measure all the antecedents of acceptance/nonacceptance and of support/resistance proposed in the literature (Appendices A and B). Rather, we used these as sensitizing concepts in sorting relevant quotes that emerged from the interviews and other sources, in terms of the intended behaviour to which they theoretically contribute. This was a consequence of our methodological focus on identifying, categorizing and analysing user reactions. A last limitation pertains to our study’s specific telecare context, which may constrain the generalizability of more particular findings. However, we propose that the adoption framework (Figure 1) is applicable to other situations. Dependent on the context, different combinations and frequencies of user reactions may occur.

## Future research

It follows that the framework’s generalizability should be tested by applying it in other contexts. In the telecare project studied, the voluntariness of system use seemed to draw a number of clients towards the quadrant of supportive non-users. In other contexts, such as authoritarian or entrepreneurial work climates, other patterns of actor clustering seem likely to emerge. This brings us to the important role of social influence (e.g., Venkatesh & Davis, 2000), as our analytical results point towards users’ reactions interacting (notably among clients, and between clients and employees). These imply social influence mechanisms, which we did not investigate in depth, that deserve greater attention in future research.

Moreover, actors outside the user groups could be considered, although only on their impact on the support/ resistance dimension. Resistance or support exhibited by ‘others’ might impact on users’ reactions. The psycho logical literature has argued that individual behaviour, for example displayed emotions (Kelly & Barsade, 2001), may result in, and evolve from, group level phenomena. Lapointe & Rivard (2005) suggest that while individual behaviours are independent early in an implementation process, they later converge into group behaviours. This may also explain why Tyre & Orlikowski (1994) find that the narrow context of a system attracts the most attention in the early implementation stages, whereas the wider context become more significant in later stages such that it politicizes the implementation.

A cross-sectional test of the taxonomy, including the antecedents, within a quantitative design would be one option for further validation. Our study points to the developmental nature of reactions towards ISs. A more extended longitudinal design would be needed to explain the expected interactions between acceptance and resistance over time both within and between actor groups, and to analyse the contributions of the existing theoretical perspectives to this. This could yield descriptions of the paths that users follow in switching between and moving within quadrants, and the conditions under which they do so.

A final area for future study would be how to choose adoption strategies that fit with the specific antecedents. A multilevel study would be appropriate for such research. The proposed framework could easily be adapted for understanding acceptance and support on individual, group, as well as organizational levels (Burton-Jones & Gallivan, 2007).

## Conclusions

This research is one of the few studies (see also Jiang et al, 2000; Bhattacherjee & Hikmet, 2007; Kim & Kankanhalli, 2009) that draws on both acceptance and resistance models in attempting to explain adoption in IS implementation projects. This study adds to the earlier contributions by proposing conceptualizing acceptance and resistance as two distinct dimensions rather than as opposite ends of a bipolar continuum. We have achieved this by developing a framework, empirically exploring the applicability of its dimensions and then evaluating its added value in understanding user adoption. At the actor level, the data show that while an individual’s acceptance and support levels tend to be similar, ambivalent behaviours such as ‘use and resistance’ and ‘support and nonuse’ do occur and may change over time. At the project level, our case study shows how an adequate interpretation of user adoption requires the application of both acceptance and resistance theories as they provide complementary insights. It can be concluded that IS adoption research will benefit from linking the two research streams. Our framework contributes to this crossover by providing a richer. more accurate. description of behavioural reactions to new technology. We would encourage further theorizing, and adopting a broader scope when seeking explanations. Finally, our findings have practical relevance, especially in organizational contexts where individuals interact and influence one another. The framework leads to suggestions on how to more accurately monitor and more effectively manage adoption.
