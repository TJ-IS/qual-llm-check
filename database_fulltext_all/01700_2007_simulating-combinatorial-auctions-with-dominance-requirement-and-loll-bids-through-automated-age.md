# Simulating combinatorial auctions with dominance requirement and loll bids through automated agents ☆

Alessandro Avenali <sup>⁎</sup>, Anna Bassanini

Dipartimento di Informatica e Sistemistica, Università degli Studi di Roma “La Sapienza”, via Buonarroti 12, 00185 Roma, Italy

Received 6 November 2003; received in revised form 13 June 2006; accepted 10 September 2006 Available online 17 November 2006

## Abstract

When complementarity or substitutability relations exist among the items for auction, the use of combinatorial bidding (i.e. permitting players to bid on bundles of items) enhances the seller's revenue and ex-post market efficiency. We present a new firstprice multi-round combinatorial format, where bids are subject to a stronger requirement than validity, and a new tool is applied, which in a sense generalizes the concept of waiver. The auction format is simulated through automated agents, assuming a model of bidders' beliefs about their opponents. We perform statistical analyses on simulation results, obtaining useful hints for the design of ascending combinatorial formats.

Keywords: Combinatorial auctions; Auction rules; Multi-agent systems; Simulation

## 1. Introduction

Thanks to their transparency and objectivity, auctions are increasingly used worldwide to allocate valuable economic resources and items of any kind among multiple competing bidders. Airport slots [31], railway track capacity [5] and the telecommunications spectrum (e.g., see [9]) are the most renowned examples of scarce resources assigned through auction-based mechanisms. Moreover, an enormous number of commodities are eauctioned on the Internet.

In many auctions (especially electronic ones), complementarity or substitutability relations exist between two or more of the items for sale. In the first case, a particular item is valued more when it is obtained in combination with another specific item. In the second, a bidder is, to a certain extent, indifferent to getting either one item or another. In such cases, if simultaneous or sequential separate auctions are performed for each item, the bidders could anticipate the risk of incurring the exposure problem (for example, they could overpay to win only one of two complementary items [27]) and thus be discouraged from participating in the auctions. This affects both the seller's revenue and ex-post market efficiency [6,19].

One way to avoid this problem is to allow combinatorial bidding, namely to allow players to place bids on sets of items as well as on single ones. This enables the taking into account of complementary or substitute preferences, decreasing the implicit cost of entering the auction and thus minimizing barriers to participation. For this reason, studies on combinatorial auctions have lately attracted considerable attention [13,20,22,23,25,33,34]. Moreover, combinatorial auctions are characterized by an extremely wide field of applications, from e-commerce [8] to multi-agent problems [16], to the formulation of market-based mechanisms for resource allocation problems in production and transportation [21]. Recently, Trade Extensions, Accenture and Volvo have performed an electronic combinatorial auction over the Internet for the procurement of wooden packaging material and reported obtaining substantial savings by inducing strong competition between potential suppliers [36]. Another relevant example is the case of the procurement of meals for 1,300,000 students in the Chilean public school system, which was awarded through a single round sealedbid combinatorial auction. This improved the price– quality ratio of the meals and obtained estimated yearly savings of around US\$40 million [12].

However, with respect to more simple and common formats, combinatorial auctions can entail a higher level of complexity. For instance, from a theoretical point of view, the number of packages each player can bid on increases exponentially with the number of items for auction (while, in real contexts, it is more likely that every player will only bid on a few bundles). The added complexity might also hamper bidders in formulating optimal strategies. Moreover, the seller's problem (i.e. the choice of a set of bids that maximizes the revenue) is equivalent to the maximum weighted stable set problem and is thus NP-hard [33]. Both exact and approximate methods have been developed to deal with this problem [10,17,34]. The AUSM procedure [3], instead, does not solve the optimization problem directly, but requires new winning bids to outweigh the sum of all the displaced ones.

In addition, other problems exist. Firstly, combinatorial environments are prone to the threshold problem, that is, a group of players who jointly desire a specific set of items may have difficulty in coordinating their bids to displace a single bid on a bundle containing those items. In order to deal with this, AUSM is often used with a standby queue (i.e. a public bulletin board on which potentially combinable bids can be displayed). Secondly, even if players could coordinate their bids (via, for instance, this bulletin board), each player from the group would have an incentive to wait for the others to improve their offers, in order to save on his own (freerider problem). The PAUSE format [18] tries to solve this problem, while retaining computational tractability, by dividing the auction into two stages; combinatorial bidding takes place only in the second stage and the number of items permitted in any valid combination increases as the rounds proceed.

The strategy-proof Vickrey–Clarke–Groves (VCG) mechanism induces maximum efficiency as it possesses the desirable game-theoretic property of making truthful bidding optimal for each player [37], hence the threshold and free-rider problems are not an issue. However, in practice, VCG schemes are not easily implemented as they are usually prone to misunderstanding by the players and are regularly opposed on the grounds that a transparent, politically implementable auction should be a “pay-your-bid” mechanism. Alternatively, specific ascending formats have been proposed in order to induce the VCG outcome. The scheme in Ref. [1] yields the same outcome as the VCG auction in the case of homogenous items. Parkes [27] provides a first-price ascending combinatorial auction (iBundle) which gives optimal allocations under the assumption of myopic bidding, that is, the players take current bids as given and make no conjectures on opponents' valuations or future bids (this bidding strategy is not optimal in a game-theoretic sense). This scheme is characterized by a high number of rounds, since the minimum increment has to be sufficiently low to guarantee optimal allocations. Parkes and Ungar extend iBundle [28,29] in order to generate a mechanism which terminates with VCG prices, and is thus incentive compatible in special cases. In Ref. [30] Parkes and Ungar provide an ascending auction which converges to the VCG payments for players with general valuations. Unfortunately, the VCG payments are highly affected by revenue failures [2,7].

The difficulty in dealing with combinatorial bidding from a theoretical point of view has encouraged the development of a large variety of software packages, simulating specific combinatorial auction formats. However, in many cases, only the seller's revenue maximization problem is solved. Optimization of the bidding has to be performed by external human agents (e.g., see the RAD mechanism in [11]). An exception is iBundle [27], where simulation is restricted to the case of myopic bidders.

In this paper we propose a new, combinatorial, firstprice, multi-round format and we simulate its crucial features by applying automated agents with independent and private valuations. In particular, we wish to experiment with a set of auction rules, which facilitate good auction performance without unduly increasing the number of rounds. To simulate this, we introduce players who take beliefs about their opponents into account. However, like in Ref. [27], our bidding strategy is not optimal in a game-theoretic sense.

The auction we propose is characterized by memory, in the sense that, at each round, all highest offers are stored (both winning and non-winning) and can subsequently be used by players to “complete” their offers and form a winning configuration.

The offers for each bundle must be (at least) dominant, i.e. they must increase the maximum revenue that the auctioneer is currently obtaining from the items in that bundle; note that this revenue is calculated considering offers for any subset of the given bundle. The revenue increment must be at least equal to an exogenous minimum bid increment. The requirement that an offer be dominant is obviously stronger than that commonly used in the auction literature, i.e. validity. The validity would in fact amount to ensuring that the current bid offsets the highest past bid on the bundle as a whole by at least the minimum increment.

To avoid an excessive number of rounds, we require that the new bids of every player also be currently winning, i.e. they should be winning if all the other submitted offers were left unchanged. This rule could favor phenomena such as the threshold problem. In order to address this, we endow each player with a given number of loll bids, i.e. with the possibility to submit a given number of dominant but not currently winning bids. A bidder can use loll bids both as a means to implicitly signal his intention to coordinate with other players in order to displace previously winning offers or simply to remain in the auction. Players are also entitled to a certain number of bidding exemptions (waivers).

Players' behavior derives from the assumption that each bidder maximizes the surplus he expects at the next round according to the information available about the opponents.

We evaluate auction results in terms of different performance indexes and we study the sensitivity of such measures to design parameters such as, for example, the availability of waivers and loll bids.

## 2. The auction model

The auction literature comprises a limited number of contributions concerning models for bidders' beliefs in combinatorial auctions. Parkes [27] proposes a setting with an arbitrary number of items and players, and provides a first-price ascending auction (iBundle), which gives optimal allocations under the assumption of myopic bidding. Parkes and Ungar [28,29] extend iBundle in such a way that in special cases the auction is designed to converge to the VCG payments. Since the latter is an ex-post Nash equilibrium strategy, the assumption of myopic bidding does not impair the final auction results. Moreover, Parkes and Ungar [30] provide an ascending auction which converges to the VCG payments for general valuations. However, as noted in [2], the VCG payments are in general affected by revenue failure. A common problem among these ascending auction formats is that they are characterized by a high number of rounds, as the minimum bid increment has to be sufficiently low to guarantee maximum efficiency.

Note that, for general players' valuations, iBundle is not granted to converge to the VCG outcome. Therefore, the final outcome depends on the assumed players' belief structures. In this case, the threshold and free-rider problems can hamper the auction's performance and needs to be explicitly dealt with.

We provide some further insight into first-price combinatorial auctions, in a setting where new rules to accelerate convergence (e.g., dominant bids) and a new tool (loll bids) for tackling the threshold problem are given. To analyze the effectiveness of the proposed format, we perform simulations by applying automated agents with independent and private valuations. Providing an analytical formulation of the optimal bidding strategy for general valuations in a combinatorial auction is an arduous task, and indeed, no such formulation has been provided to date. We therefore employ a simple bidder's model, where players use their knowledge of the (common) valuations' distribution for bundles and bids at previous rounds to predict the opponents' moves at the next stage. In particular, taking Parkes myopic bidding model as a starting point, we partly generalize it as follows. Although there is still no strategic interaction, in that each player ignores the effects of his bids on his opponents' future strategies and on the future minimum prices required by the auctioneer, our bidders' model is different in that players are no longer bid takers, but compute expected highest bids on bundles.

Wellman and colleagues [24,32] propose a bidding model for auctions exhibiting complementarities between items. This extends beyond the myopic bidding assumption and endows players with a (limited) predictive capability, based, for example, on environmental factors. We share their view that «…environmental information is often plausibly available, from knowledge of the structure of the market, or empirical observation of previous market outcomes (e.g., historical prices)» [24]. However, our bidding model necessarily differs from theirs, since the latter is formulated within a simultaneous ascending auction and thus implies inherently different considerations on the factors driving players bidding behavior.

## 2.1. The auction format

We consider a first-price multi-round auction, where the auctioneer sells a given number of items simultaneously, allowing combinatorial offers. The auctioneer sets reserve prices for single items. Although some of the items for sale could be identical, each item is univocally determined (each copy of an item is identified separately). A bundle is a set of items and may contain multiple copies of a certain item. Any player can bid on any bundle.

Two bundles are incompatible when they cannot be won jointly. Incompatibilities can arise for different reasons; for example, two bundles could have an item in common, or there may exist substitutability relations (or other economic reasons) that make a player unwilling to obtain them both. In our notation, two bundles $B _ { i }$ and $B _ { j }$ are incompatible iff the pair $( B _ { i } , \ B _ { j } )$ belongs to set I. We will indicate by $I _ { j }$ the set of bundles incompatible with $B _ { j } ,$ plus $B _ { j }$ itself. Incompatibilities can be used to represent different types of relations between bundles, such as complementarity or substitutability. For example, assume that a player's valuations are: 10 for item A, 10 for item $B ,$ , 11 for bundle $\{ A , B \}$ The substitutability relation between A and B can be represented by creating incompatibility constraints between bundles $\{ A \}$ and $\left\{ B \right\} , \left\{ B \right\}$ and $\{ A , B \} , \{ A \}$ and $\{ A , B \}$ . In the following, in order to reduce notational complexity and simplify the description of the auction, we assume that two bundles are incompatible iff they share an item; hence substitutability relations cannot be modelled. This assumption does not imply any loss of generality, in fact, as shown, incompatibility relations can actually represent both substitutability and complementarity.

The auction we propose is characterized by memory. At each round only the highest bids (both winning and nonwinning), among the ones submitted so far, are stored and can subsequently be used by players to “complete” their offers and form a winning configuration. Note that, if ties occur, more than one highest bid is stored for a single bundle. The auctioneer then determines, among the highest bids, the ones which maximize his revenue.

In the following, we will indicate by $V _ { p }$ the set of bundles which are valued by player $p ,$ while $O _ { p } \subseteq V _ { p }$ will represent, at any time, the set of bundles whose current highest bid (if any) is offered by player p at that time. Moreover, let us define $O { = } O _ { 1 } \cup . . . \cup O _ { n } , V { = } V _ { 1 } \cup . . . \cup V _ { n }$ and $O _ { - p } { = } O \setminus O _ { p } , V _ { - p } { = } V \setminus V _ { p } ;$ ; let us also set, without loss of generality, $\begin{array} { r } { V _ { 1 } = \{ B _ { 1 } , . . . , B _ { n _ { 1 } } \} , V _ { 2 } = \{ B _ { n _ { 1 } + 1 } , . . . , B _ { n _ { 1 } + n 2 } \} } \end{array}$ , etc. Finally, $\hat { \cal I } = \{ ( B _ { i } , B _ { j } ) \dot { \in } V \times V ; i \neq j , B _ { i } \cap B _ { j } \neq \emptyset \}$ . To simplify the notation, given a set of bundles, say O, we will write $i \in O$ to indicate that index i is such that bundle $B _ { i } \in O$

We define a set of auction rules aimed at accelerating auction convergence, which differ in some respects from the ones commonly found in the literature. These rules are based on the following definitions. A bid for a bundle is valid iff it increases the standing highest offer on that bundle by at least the minimum increment. The value of a bundle is defined as the maximum revenue the auctioneer obtains from the items included in that bundle. A bid for a bundle is dominant iff it increases the current value of that bundle by at least the minimum increment. New bids submitted by the same player are currently winning iff, at the next round, they would become winning if all other bids in memory were left unchanged. Observe that, in the single-item English auction, all these definitions overlap, in the sense that a bid is valid iff it is dominant iff it is currently winning.

A common rule in combinatorial auctions is the requirement for bids to be valid. Note that any dominant bid will be valid, while a valid offer will not necessarily be dominant. For example, assume that the auctioneer has received a bid of 7 for bundle $\{ A \}$ , a bid of 5 for bundle $\{ B \}$ and a bid of 10 for bundle $\{ A , B \}$ . The current revenue on items A and B is 12; this is the value of bundle $\{ A , B \}$ . Suppose the minimum increment required is equal to 10%. Then, an offer of 11 for $\{ A , B \}$ is valid (it increases by 10% the highest bid on $\{ A , B \} \colon 1 1 \geq 1 0 { \cdot } 1 . 1 = 1 1 )$ but not dominant (it does no increase by 10% the value of $\{ A , B \} : 1 1 < 1 2 \cdot 1 . 1 = 1 3 . 2 )$ . In order to speed up the auction convergence without making use of complex eligibility rules, we require bids to be at least dominant. Note that when only complementarity relations exist, allowing bids less than dominant is as ingenuous as allowing less than valid bids in the single-item English auction (see Example 1 available at http://www.dis.uniroma1.it/\~avenali/).

However, in a combinatorial environment, dominant offers do not necessarily succeed in displacing previously winning bids, even when all other offers remain unchanged (i.e. they may not be currently winning). For example, assume that three offers have been submitted: player $p _ { 1 }$ has bid 7 for $\{ A \}$ , player $p _ { 2 }$ has bid 5 for $\{ B \}$ and player $p _ { 3 }$ has bid 20 for $\{ A , B , C \}$ . The auctioneer's current revenue from items in bundle $\{ A , B \}$ (which is therefore the value of that bundle) is equal to 12. Suppose the minimum increment required is equal to 10%. A bid of 15 for $\{ A , B \}$ by player $p _ { 4 }$ would be dominant (since it sufficiently increases the current value of $\{ A , B \} )$ but is not winning (since the winning bid still remains 20 for $\{ A , B , C \} )$ . Therefore, in order to further increase the auction convergence, we require that every bid is also currently winning. Preventing players from submitting bids which are not currently winning could, however, compromise efficiency and final revenue, as new bids which are dominant but not currently winning could become winning at some point. For instance, if in the last example player $p _ { 1 }$ had bid an adequate amount for C alone (e.g., equal to 6), the currently winning offer could be displaced. It then becomes necessary to look for ways to solve this problem that may be easily implemented, ensuring at the same time that the number of rounds for auction convergence remains reasonable. The main auction design tool that can be exploited to deal with this problem consists of assigning a certain number of waivers to each player. However, waivers allow players only to wait for opponents' moves, generating signals with a limited informational content. In order to enhance players ability to transmit the relevant information, we propose to endow bidders with a certain number of loll bids, i.e. offers that are dominant but not currently winning. Note that loll bids can also be seen as a generalization of waivers, in that they allow one to “stand $\boldsymbol { \mathrm { b y } } ^ { \mathsf { * } }$ in the bidding process by “paying the price” required to ensure dominance and thereby generating a higher degree of information. Loll bids could be used by players both to implicitly signal the intention to coordinate with some other agents, and simply to remain in the auction. Allowing the use of loll bids can, therefore, increase efficiency and yield a higher revenue (see Example 2 available at http://www.dis.uniroma1.it/ \~avenali/). Moreover, since loll bids are always dominant, they favor convergence. Observe that in the single-item English auction, a dominant bid is doomed to be currently winning, which means that waivers are the sole device available.

Since loll bids increase the possibilities for players to coordinate their bids, they could prove to be a useful tool in overcoming the threshold problem. However, even when the threshold problem is solved, the free-rider problem could persist. We endeavor to verify whether loll bids are a useful means to deal with these issues, and analyze the effectiveness of waivers and loll bids in terms of the performance measures which we will define.

We now describe the auction dynamics. At the beginning of each round, the auctioneer announces the minimum dominant offer for all bundles considered in the auction so far. This includes new bundles which have not been previously bid on, if any player requires it (these requests are kept secret). At the first round the auctioneer simply declares the reserve price of every item for sale and establishes that the reserve price for any bundle is the sum of the reserve prices of the component items. Then, based on their beliefs, players simultaneously calculate their optimal bids, taking into account (i) the auctioneer's requirements of submitting only dominant and currently winning bids with the exception (if any) of a limited number of loll bids, and (ii) the possibility of playing a waiver. Each bidder accordingly either submits new offers or plays a waiver or quits the auction.

The auctioneer collects the new bids, compares them with the previously stored ones and keeps in memory only all highest bids (if ties occur they are all stored). Considering these stored bids, the auctioneer then solves the revenue maximization problem (as described in Section 2.2). All bids submitted at the current round and all stored bids are disclosed to players; the winning bids are also indicated. The identity of the players who have placed such bids are not disclosed, hence each player neither knows how many other bidders are in the auction nor who submitted what; this should prevent players from colluding or in any case mitigate the collusion phenomenon. In particular, each player only sees a list of bids announced by the auctioneer and is privately informed by the auctioneer about his own offers. Based on this information, bidders update their beliefs (as specified in Section 2.3). A bidder quits the auction when he does not submit new offers and does not play any waiver. The auction ends when all players have quit.

## 2.2. The auctioneer's model

The auctioneer is neutral to risk and maximizes his revenue, subject to the compatibility constraints concerning winning offers. The related optimization problem, called Combinatorial Auction Problem (CAP) or Winner Determination Problem, can be formulated as follows:

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \max \sum_ {i \in O} u _ {i} x _ {i} \\ \text { subject   to } \\ x _ {i} + x _ {j} \leq 1 & (i, j) \in I: i, j \in O \\ x _ {i} \in \{0, 1 \} & i \in O \end{array} \right.
$$

where variable $x _ { i }$ equals 1 iff highest bid $u _ { i }$ for bundle $B _ { i }$ is selected winning. Determining the set of winning bids is an extremely complicated optimization problem. In particular, it has been shown that it is an NP-hard problem [33]. To solve CAP, both exact and approximate methods have been developed [10]. In this paper, all CAP instances are solved to optimality through a branch-and-bound scheme as implemented by CPLEX 7.1.

In general, alternative sets of winning offers that ensure the same (maximum) revenue exist. In the software we use, the order in which these sets are found depends on an integer value label assigned to each offer. In order to solve ties, these labels are randomly assigned.

The value of a bundle B for the auctioneer (i.e. the maximum revenue which the auctioneer obtains from all and only the items included in bundle B) is the optimal value of the following optimization problem:

$$
\text { value } (B) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \max \sum_ {i \in O: B _ {i} \subseteq B} u _ {i} x _ {i} \\ \text { subject   to } \\ x _ {i} + x _ {j} \leq 1 & (i, j) \in I: i, j \in O, B _ {i}, B _ {j} \subseteq B \\ x _ {i} \in \{0, 1 \} & i \in O, B _ {i} \subseteq B \end{array} \right.
$$

## 2.3. The bidder's model

We assume that bidders are neutral to risk. At each stage, they submit dominant bids that maximize their “expected” surplus, in the sense we will specify below. However, the resulting bidding strategy is not optimal in a game-theoretic sense. In particular, we represent each player's decision process at any stage as follows. First, the player anticipates an expected scenario at the next round. This scenario is built by assuming a probability function for the valuation of each bundle $B _ { i }$ which has been bid on by the opponents in previous rounds, and by calculating the expected values of all such probability functions. These will be assumed as the expected offers at the next round. Each player computes his surplus-maximizing bids, ensuring that such bids are dominant and expected winning (i.e. winning in the expected scenario), possibly using one or more loll bids (if any surplus-maximizing offer is not currently winning). The bidder's resulting surplus (SU) in the expected scenario is then calculated.

The valuations' probability functions are updated at each round according to the highest past bids, which results in the following. We assume a non-uniform distribution to represent the players' beliefs. In particular, we select a probability function which allows us to model the main feature of our bidders (i.e. that they are not bid takers, but compute expected highest bids on bundles). This represents an ad-hoc and reasonable way of taking into account players with adaptive behavioral rules. These expectations are obtained by determining the expected value (EV) of the chosen probability function. A similar approach is presented in Ref. [15]. In particular, the probability density function adopted for a bundle $B _ { i } { } ^ { \circ } \mathrm { s }$ valuation is given by:

$$
f (v, u, t, r) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \gamma \cdot \left(\frac {r v - \alpha}{(r v - \alpha) ^ {2} + 1} + \beta\right) & \text { in } [ u, t ] \\ 0 & \text { else } \end{array} \right.
$$

Quantities u and t are the minimum and maximum possible valuations across players for bundle $B _ { i } ,$ respectively. Parameters α and $\beta$ are such that the function is zero in u and $t ,$ while γ ensures that the total probability equals 1 (see Appendix B); r is used to move the curve's maximum (which moves left as r grows). Parameter u is set equal to the highest offer on the bundle in consideration. The assumption underlying this probability function is that the expected offer will be nearer to u than to t. We are, in a sense, modelling “conservative” beliefs; in fact, the expected bids' values at the next round will be nearer to the highest previous offer u (myopic bidding) than to the maximum possible value t. This is increasingly true as r grows (as r approaches infinity, we end up with myopic players). Moreover, if r and u are fixed and t approaches infinity, then $\textstyle { \frac { \operatorname { E V } - u } { t - u } }$ approaches zero. This means that the more the uncertainty for players (i.e. the distance between u and t), the closer EV is to u than to t (i.e. the more conservative the players).

Player $p ^ { \star }$ decision process goes as follows. Bidder p maximizes his expected surplus in the sense explained above (i.e. his surplus in the expected scenario). In order to do this, the selected new offers (i) have to be expected winning, and (ii) have to satisfy the auctioneer's requirements of being dominant and currently winning. Moreover, the bidder has to take into account that a maximum number (NL) of new bids can be dominant and not currently winning (i.e. loll bids). When the player has multiple optimal strategies, he will choose the one which minimizes the number of loll bids employed. If the optimal strategy consists of submitting no new bids, the player will verify whether he may stay in the auction or not. To do this, he evaluates whether he can make a dominant offer by calculating, for each bundle he values, the ratio between the price required to ensure a bid for this bundle is dominant and his valuation. If the minimum value for this ratio across bundles is greater than 1, he will not be able to place any further dominant offers and thus he will quit (even if he has remaining waivers). Alternatively, the player will play a waiver to stay in the auction, whenever he has any left. If he does not, he will stay by submitting a suitable dominant bid (e.g., a loll bid). In particular, he will select, among all the dominant bids which could be placed (discarding the loll ones if ${ \mathrm { N L } } { = } 0 )$ , the dominant bid containing only items that the player is not yet winning in the expected scenario and with the minimum above ratio. If such a bid does not exist, he will quit.

Examine now the problem of determining each player's optimal strategy. The surplus maximization problem at each round is analogous to the leader's decision problem in a two-stage Stackelberg game. At the first stage, each player chooses his new bids (given the expectations about his opponents' moves), and at the second, the auctioneer maximizes his revenue. Thus, for each player, a bi-level optimization problem has to be solved. In Appendix A, however, we show that it can be equivalently formulated as an NP-hard integer linear problem. In order to provide the NP-hard integer linear formulation for the surplus maximization problem of bidder $p ,$ we introduce the following notation:

• Variable $b _ { i }$ will represent the increment to be added to the current highest offer $u _ { i }$ in the optimal bidding strategy $( \operatorname { i f } i \in O _ { p } ;$ if instead $i \in V _ { p } \backslash O _ { p } , b _ { i }$ will represent the total offer). Since any bid is required to be a multiple of a given basic unit, $b _ { i }$ is an integer.

• Variable $x _ { i }$ equals 1 iff bid $u _ { i } + b _ { i } ( \mathrm { i f } i \in O _ { p } )$ or bid $b _ { i } ( \mathrm { i f } i \in V _ { p } \backslash O _ { p } )$ is expected winning; it is otherwise zero.

• Variable $y _ { i }$ is equal to 1 iff in the optimal strategy the bidder submits a new bid for bundle $B _ { i }$ (i.e. when $b _ { i } { > } 0 )$ ; it is otherwise zero.

• Variable $z _ { i }$ is equal to 1 iff bid $u _ { i } + b _ { i } ( \mathrm { i f } i \in O _ { p } )$ or bid $b _ { i } ( \mathrm { i f } i \in V _ { p } \backslash O _ { p } )$ is currently winning; it is otherwise zero.

• Variable $w _ { i }$ is equal to 1 if the new bid for $B _ { i }$ is a loll bid. Note that the opposite is not true and the number of loll bids to be played at the optimal solution is equal to $\textstyle \sum _ { i \in V _ { r } }$ max $\{ y _ { i } - z _ { i } , 0 \}$

<sup>f g</sup>• Parameter NL represents the maximum number of loll bids currently available to the player.

• Parameter $b _ { i } ^ { \mathrm { { d o m } } }$ is the current value of bundle $B _ { i }$ plus the minimum bid increment, i.e. the minimum value required to ensure a bid for $B _ { i }$ is dominant.

• H is the set of all possible maximal sets of pairwise compatible bundles (where maximal means that such sets cannot be further extended to comprise other bundles). By S we denote any maximal set of pairwise compatible bundles.

Player $p ^ { \star } { } ^  $ model can thus be formulated as follows.

$$

The objective function represents the (net) surplus of the player in the expected scenario. Constraints of type (1) ensure that new offers are dominant. Constraints of type (2) impose that bids are expected winning, given the player's highest bids $( u _ { i }$ for $i \in O _ { p } )$ and his opponents' expected offers $( u _ { i } ^ { E } \mathrm { f o r } i { \in } O _ { - p } )$ . Constraints of type (3) concern loll bids, in the sense that they are used to calculate the number of loll bids in the optimal solution; more specifically, constraints (3.1) impose that bids are currently winning. In Appendix A we provide a formal proof that constraints (2) and (3.1) work as desired. The approach used to find an optimal solution of the bidder's problem is described at the end of Appendix A.

Bidder $p ^ { \star }$ optimal strategy consists of maximizing his surplus; in addition, the player wants to minimize the number of loll bids used. This goal is achieved as follows. First, bidder $p ^ { \star } { } ^  $ problem is solved, considering all available loll bids, obtaining an optimal surplus $\mathrm { S U ^ { * } }$ . Then, p verifies whether another optimal solution exists (i.e. such that the surplus is still $\mathrm { S U ^ { * } } )$ , which makes use of a lesser number of loll bids. Therefore a sequence of bidder $p ^ { \star } { } ^  $ surplus maximization problems are solved, where the value of NL progressively decreases by 1. When the obtained surplus is lower than $\mathrm { S U ^ { * } }$ , the process stops, as this means that the last value of NL is so small that it is no longer possible to obtain $\mathrm { S U ^ { * } }$ . An equivalent way of proceeding consists of solving a single, different, additional problem where the number of loll bids is minimized, subject to requiring the same expected surplus SU\*. However, we use the first approach described, as it allows us to consider all optimization problems with the same structure.

Obviously, although our players are not bid takers, they remain limited in terms of cognitive abilities, and the bidding strategy is not optimal in a game-theoretic sense. More general bidding models are made impracticable because of the high complexity characterizing the assumed combinatorial format. For instance, players with foresight might strategically use loll bids to their advantage (e.g., to free-ride or to signal some kind of message to the opponents).

However, due to the complexity of planning fully rational strategies in the considered format, the proposed bidding strategy may be a reasonable approximation to the bidders' behavior in practice. In simpler environments, more forward-looking strategies can be used. In Ref. [35], for example, where simultaneous auctions are considered, the bidding strategy is based on the predicted prices at the end of the auction, rather than the expected bids at the next round. An alternative approach, where no assumption on the bidders' behaviors is required, consists of developing parameterized mechanisms which self-adapt on the observed outcomes of past auctions in order to obtain a given goal [26].

The complexity due to the assumed format also discourages us from applying more sophisticated ways of computing the players' surplus-maximizing bids than the presented approach (e.g., procedures based on randomly sampling a multitude of next-round bids scenarios).

## 3. The simulator

Our simulations are completely automated and do not require human intervention, since both the auctioneer's and the bidders' behaviors are mathematically modelled as described in previous sections (Example 3, available at http:// www.dis.uniroma1.it/\~avenali/, shows both the auction dynamics and how the loll bids can help improve the auction performance). In particular, to solve the optimality of all integer linear problems due to CAPs and to bidders optimization problems, we use CPLEX 7.1.

## 3.1. Auction performance measures

In evaluating simulation results, we will use various auction performance measures. First, we will obviously analyze optimality and efficiency. The former is defined [27] by the ratio OP of the revenue obtained by the auctioneer to the maximum revenue $R _ { \mathrm { m a x } }$ he could obtain if he knew the players' valuations. On the other hand, efficiency is defined [27] as the ratio EF of bidders' gross surplus to the maximum surplus $\mathrm { S U } _ { \mathrm { m a x } }$ they could obtain (which equals $R _ { \mathrm { m a x } } )$ Moreover, both these indexes provide an estimate of the effectiveness of the auction format in solving instances of the threshold problem.

The third index we use provides an estimate for the degree of information extraction from the players in the auction. Indicating by v<sub>i</sub> a player's valuation for bundle $B _ { i }$ and by $u _ { i }$ the last offer announced by the player for that bundle (zero if that offer does not exist), we define as a measure of extracted information the index $\mathrm { E I } = \textstyle \sum _ { i \in V } w _ { i } { \frac { u _ { i } } { \nu _ { i } } }$ where $\sum _ { \mathbf { r } } w _ { i } = 1 ;$ in particular, setting $\begin{array} { r } { w _ { i } = \frac { \nu _ { i } } { \sum _ { j \in V } \nu _ { j } } } \end{array}$ for any $i \in V ,$ we obtain the index proposed by Parkes in [27]: $\begin{array} { r } { \mathrm { E I } = \stackrel {  } { \sum } _ { i \in V } w _ { i } \frac { i \in \nu } { \nu _ { i } } = \frac { \sum _ { i \in V } u _ { i } } { \sum _ { i \in V } \nu _ { i } } . } \end{array}$ In order to ascertain the impact of the free-rider problem on our auction format, we propose a specific index $\begin{array} { r } { \operatorname { F R } = \sqrt { \frac { \sum _ { i \in \operatorname { W S } } ( g _ { i } - \overline { { g } } ) ^ { 2 } } { | \mathbf { W S } | } } } \end{array}$ , where WS is the set of bundles that are sold at the end of the auction, $g _ { i } = \frac { u _ { i } } { \nu _ { i } }$ for any $i \in \mathrm { W S }$ and $\begin{array} { r } { \overline { { g } } = \frac { \sum _ { i \in \mathbf { W } \mathrm { S } } { g _ { i } } } { | \mathbf { W } \mathbf { S } | } } \end{array}$ (FR is the deviation of $\{ g _ { i } \colon i \in W S \} ,$ ). Note that a higher value of FR means a higher incidence of the free-rider problem. Finally, we observe the average percentage of played waivers AW, the average percentage of played loll bids AL, and the number of rounds NR before the auction terminates.

## 3.2. The testbed environment

In our simulations, we consider nine different contexts by varying the level of scarcity (i.e. the number of items with respect to the number of players) and the competitive scenario.

Concerning the levels of scarcity, we select three cases: (i) high scarcity, when the number of items NI for sale is less than the number of players NP; (ii) average scarcity, when the number of items for sale are roughly equal to the number of players; (iii) low scarcity, when the number of items for sale exceeds the number of players. In particular, in case (i), NI is chosen randomly in the set of integers from 2 (the minimum to ensure the auction is combinatorial) to round(1/2·NP); in case (ii), from round(9/10·NP) to round(11/10·NP); in case (iii), from round (3/2·NP) to 2·NP.

In order to define the competitive scenarios, we first define three types of players, namely strong, average and weak. We then deal with three cases: (i) heterogeneous scenario, when bidders are uniformly spread across the types (1/3 strong, 1/3 average, 1/3 weak bidders); (ii) homogeneous scenario, when all players belong to the average type, (iii) outlier scenario, when most players are weak and there are a few strong outliers (1/5 strong, 4/5 weak bidders). In order to specify the characteristics of strong, average and weak players, it is necessary to describe the way players' valuations are generated. First, we randomly generate a set M of different bundles from all the single items for sale (all items are different to each other, in the sense that every item is present as a single copy). The cardinality of M is equal to round(3/2·NI) and every bundle composed by only one item is contained in M. The number of items in any bundle of M is at most round(1/4·NI). Given any player p and for every bundle in M, an interval of integers of possible valuations for the bundle is associated to p. Each bundle in M is then “assigned” to a strong (average or weak) player p with a probability equal to $1 - 0 . 7 5 ^ { 3 } \ ( 1 \mathrm { - } 0 . 7 5 ^ { 2 }$ or 1–0.75, respectively). The corresponding valuation of a strong (average or weak) player $\cdot _ { p }$ is obtained by choosing the highest (second highest or lowest, respectively) of three random draws in the interval associated to p for that bundle. Player p's valuations for all bundles he is not assigned to are set to zero. Given any player $p ,$ the interval of integers of possible valuations for each bundle in M is defined as follows. For every single item, the interval is the same for all players and is randomly chosen in the set $\{ 1 0 0 0 , 1 0 0 1 , . . . , 2 0 0 0 \}$ . As explained in Section 2.1, we only consider complementarity relations among items. Then, given any bundle in M with at least two items and any player p, we set the lower bound of the interval for that bundle equal to the sum of the player $p ^ { \star }$ valuations of the single items composing the bundle. While the upper bound of the interval for this bundle is the same across all players and it is randomly chosen in the set $\{ k , k + 1 , . . . , 2 k \}$ , where k is the sum of the upper bounds of the intervals for the single items composing the bundle.

Every combination between competitive scenario (heterogeneous/homogeneous/outlier) and scarcity level (high average/low) defines exactly one context. By varying the number of players (NP), the number of waivers (NW) and the number of loll bids (NL) which every bidder can play in the auction, 1728 instances have been generated (a set of 192 instances for every considered context). In particular, NP varies from 5 to 20 and is chosen randomly; while NW and NL can take four values: 0, round(1/2·max{NP, NI}), max{NP, NI}, or 2 max{NP, NI}). The reserve price for each item is set equal to 10% of the upper bound of the interval of integers of possible valuations for the item. The reserve price for any bundle is the sum of the reserve prices of the component items. In all simulations a minimum percentage bid increment is adopted in order to promote convergence as the rounds proceed. In fact, the larger the number of rounds, the larger the required increment for dominance (as it is a percentage of the value, which necessarily increases round by round). We set the required minimum increment equal to 5% in all simulations.

We have characterized bidders by considering two possible values for r (the belief update parameter), specifically 0.5 or 20. This is aimed at assessing the sensitivity of auction performance to different belief structures. The case r = 20 is nearer to the myopic scenario (characterized by players with lower expectations), while if r = 0.5, then players' expectations are farther from opponents' previous bids (players with higher expectations). Hence, each group of 192 instances has been analyzed twice, varying the value of r. We have thus carried out eighteen different analyses, by totally solving 3456 instances.

## 3.3. Simulation results

The results obtained from the simulations have been studied by means of a multivariate analysis (computational results are available at http://www.dis.uniroma1.it/\~avenali/). In order to evaluate the dependence between design parameters (predictors in the analysis) and auction performance measures, we have searched for forecasting rules on auction outcomes given the design parameters. Since all aforementioned auction performance measures but NR are continuous, we will also treat NR as if continuous, in order to use a unified procedure.

In our analysis we use four predictors. The first is the number of players NP; note that the number of items NI is implicitly represented by NP as we analyze separately contexts with different levels of scarcity. The second is the number of waivers per player NW, while the third is the number of loll bids per player NL. As explained in the previous section, the valuations characterizing the generated instances are chosen randomly in given ranges, therefore, we need a quantitative measure which approximately represents the random valuations scenario of each generated instance. We propose the ratio of the revenue obtained when players bid truthfully and the second-price rule is applied (i.e. the revenue induced by the VCG rule) to the revenue obtained when players bid truthfully and the first-price rule is applied $( \mathrm { i . e . ~ } R _ { \mathrm { m a x } } )$ . Such a quantity is the fourth predictor used in the analysis and it is called Vickrey ratio (SF); it ranges between 0 and 1. In a sense, this represents the minimum effort (in terms of prices offered) that players with the highest valuations have to accomplish in order to win (the larger SF, the higher the effort). Observe that, for every level of scarcity, the Vickrey ratio is on average highest in the heterogeneous scenario. In fact, the simultaneous presence of weak, average and strong bidders increases the number of ways of subdividing any bundle of items among distinct players according to their valuations. Moreover, SF is lowest in the outlier scenario for all levels of scarcity. This is due to the large gap, in terms of valuations, between strong and weak players. On the other hand, given any kind of scenario, SF is largest for high level of scarcity. In fact, the fewer the items with respect to the players, the larger the probability that an item is contended by many more players.

As already observed, there exists a trade-off between the auction performance and the number of rounds to auction completion. Thus, the choice of the minimum increment required to ensure that bids are dominant is a crucial factor in determining the final outcome and, in analyzing auction results, one should take into account its potential distortionary effects. In particular, consider a very simple case where two players compete for one item. Assume that their valuations for the item are “sufficiently similar” (i.e. differ by no more than the minimum increment); then, if the low-value player's (winning) bid at the previous round was near his valuation, the high-value player would obviously lose the auction. Of course, the consequences of this distortionary effect (which we will call step effect) get worse as the minimum bid increment grows. Moreover, combinatorial frameworks tend to amplify this problem due to the complexity of the bidding environment. The probability of incurring the step effect depends on the distribution of bidders' valuations. As an example, in the case of homogeneity, the step effect is more likely to occur than in cases of heterogeneity and of outliers, since all bidders have similar valuations. On the other hand, in the case of outliers we expect that this problem will have the least consequences on auction performance. In addition, the possible magnitude of the step effect will be likely to increase with the number of players (ceteris paribus), with the number of items (ceteris paribus) and with SF (ceteris paribus). In the presented auction format we do not propose measures to reduce or eliminate the step effect. This problem could be efficiently mitigated by allowing each player to submit some non-dominant but currently winning bids (in a sense, a dual of loll bids). Moreover, in a more sophisticated analysis, a further predictor, based on the minimum increment and on the distribution of bidders' valuations, could be defined to study the sensitivity of the auction outcomes (e.g., the number of rounds and the step effect) to variations of the minimum increment.

Efficiency (EF) is high and keeps the same order of magnitude throughout all scarcity level and competitive scenarios. This depends mainly on our multi-round ascending format and on the presence of high scarcity or, alternatively, on the use of tools which soften the threshold problem in cases with a low/medium scarcity. Extracted information (EI) is highest when scarcity is high. In the heterogeneous and outlier scenarios, information extraction is much lower for low/average scarcity, while in the homogeneous scenario there is a smaller difference between the scarcity levels. In particular, for every level of scarcity, the maximum value for EI is always obtained in the homogeneous scenario. Moreover, note that FR is almost double in the outlier scenario as compared to the homogeneous one. ## 3.3.2. Relations between design parameters and auction performance indexes

In each of the nine contexts we have verified the existence of significant rules indicating relations between the predictors and the performance indexes by means of Classification and Regression Trees (C&RT [4]), as implemented by SPSS AnswerTree 3.0. Consequently, eighteen regression trees (two for each context) have been computed for every parameter.

The C&RT method makes use of segmentation techniques. It is based on binary decision trees and consists of partitioning a sample into two sub-samples (with respect to the value of a predictor), in such a way as to maximize the explained variance of the chosen target variable, i.e. the difference between the variance in the root sample and the sum of the variances in the two sub-samples (weighted by the percentage of instances in the sub-samples). This procedure is repeated on every new sub-sample until specified stop criteria are verified. In our case, the adopted stop criteria are as follows. Any sample can be partitioned iff (i) the explained variance is at least 1% of the variance in the root sample and every sub-sample contains at least 1/8 of the root instances (i.e. at least 24), or (ii) the explained variance is at least 10% of the variance in the root sample and every sub-sample contains at least 1/16 of the root instances (i.e. at least 12). Note that these criteria are applied in order to prevent the trees from over-learning from the samples; in other words, in order to perform only splits representing significant relations between predictors and target variables. The accuracy of a tre is the ratio between the overall explained variance and the root sample variance. The overall explained variance is equal to the root sample variance minus the sum of the variances in every leaf sample (terminal node of the tree) weighted by the percentage of cases in the leaf samples. Once each decision tree has been generated, a cross-validation method is used to estimate how well the resulting tree generalizes new data. In particular, all of the data are partitioned into j different randomly drawn samples (we set j at 10). Then, j trees are generated, each one considering all samples except one (the considered samples are called learning samples while the one excluded is called test sample), in such a way that every sample has been excluded exactly once. After a tree has been generated, only the test sample is used to compute the accuracy of the tree so as to measure the generalizing power of the tree. Finally, the selected tree is the one showing the best accuracy. The cross-validated accuracy is the sum of the accuracies of these j trees, weighted by the percentage of cases in the corresponding test samples.

In Fig. 1 we present, as an example, the tree related to optimality (OP) in the homogeneous scenario-low scarcity context for the framework r = 0.5 (all trees are available at http://www.dis.uniroma1.it/\~avenali/). In this context, where players are similar and items are more numerous than players, optimality primarily depends on the number of players. In fact, for high values of NP, any item is likely to be desired by more bidders and therefore competition increases (each item is present in a higher number of bundles). Moreover, for high values of NP, loll bids positively effect optimality as they favor information disclosure (the threshold problem is softened)

The analysis of the generated trees for every performance parameter suggests the following conclusions. Loll bids increase the information extracted from players in many of the considered contexts, but this is not always sufficient to increase optimality or efficiency. In particular, loll bids play a very significant role in the homogeneous and outlier scenarios, where, in fact, similarity among players who try to displace a currently winning offer tends to induce freeriding. Conversely, in the heterogeneous scenario they are of little help. Moreover, loll bids are more effective in the homogeneous scenario than in the outlier one, in the sense that in the former case loll bids have a greater effect on optimality and efficiency (i.e. they allow the extraction of a larger amount of information). This depends on the fact that in the outlier scenario the threshold problem tends to be more dramatic due to the gap between the valuations of weak players as compared to those of strong players. In these cases, loll bids can soften but not solve the threshold problem. Loll bids prove to be an effective tool in the case of low/medium scarcity, while they are of little use in high scarcity cases, where the Vickrey ratio plays the dominant role. Indeed, in cases with a high scarcity the threshold problem is automatically curbed by competition. In some rare situations, where scarcity alone can guarantee a high performance, loll bids have a negative effect since they allow players with the highest valuations to minimize the bidding effort.

Waivers are always less effective than loll bids in terms of their influence on auction performance. Both waivers and loll bids tend to increase the number of rounds, but waivers generally have a bigger impact in this respect (with the only exception of the outlier scenario-low scarcity context, where roles are reversed). Moreover, in scenarios with a high similarity among players and occurrence of the threshold problem, waivers have a very counterproductive effect on the number of rounds without adding any information, since they are actually used by players in an attempt to free-ride.

The step effect shows in the decrease of efficiency (1–2%) in two of the considered scenarios, namely heterogeneous with high scarcity and homogeneous with high scarcity. Note that these scenarios have the highest and the second highest mean values of the Vickrey ratio (91.64% and 88.70%), respectively

Finally, observe that in the two frameworks characterized by players with lower and higher expectations, the predictors which significantly affect auction performance are mostly the same. In rare cases the level of the sensitivity of performance indexes to predictors is different (i.e. the magnitude of the explained variance). For example, in the homogeneous case loll bids are more significant as a tool when players have higher expectations, while in the outlier scenario they are more effective when players have lower expectations.

## 4. Conclusions

In this paper we have dealt with the problem of defining implementable mechanisms for combinatorial auctions. In particular, we have proposed a multi-round combinatorial format and we have suggested devices, such as, for example, dominant bidding and loll bids, in order to enhance both optimality and efficiency, while ensuring a tractable number of rounds. We have also proposed a model for players' expectations and we have assumed for players a bidding behavior in some degree affected by these expectations. This model can describe different types of bidders by varying an exogenous parameter.

Under our assumptions about bidders' bidding behavior and expectations, we have analyzed the effects of entitling players with a limited number of waivers and loll bids on auction performance. This was done by carrying out simulations in nine different contexts, each one characterized by a different level of scarcity and competitive scenarios. The outcomes of the simulations give some indication as to the most efficient use of waivers and loll bids. For instance, it is shown that in the context of high scarcity and heterogeneous bidders, waivers and loll bids have no significant effect on the auctioneer's revenue or efficiency, while they increase the number of rounds (in very few instances loll bids even cause a reduction of the seller's revenue). Our results would suggest that the best approach is to entitle players with a small number of both waivers and loll bids in this context. Instead, in the case where players are homogeneous, loll bids positively affect the auction performance, while waivers favor instances of the threshold problem. Our results would suggest to entitle players to a small number of waivers and many loll bids (the number of rounds remains controlled as loll bids are, in any case, dominant bids).

In general, loll bids prove to be helpful in extracting information in many contexts but this is not always sufficient to significantly increase revenue and efficiency. Moreover, in the case where players are homogeneous, loll bids are much more crucial than in the case of heterogeneous players. If the scarcity is high, they play a subordinate role with respect to the Vickrey ratio. Note that our simulations suggest that loll bids are, in most cases, significantly more effective than waivers. In fact, loll bids allow players to “stand by” in the auction at the cost of revealing information in relation to their valuations (dominance requirement).

Our results could be used to infer suggestions for combinatorial auction design. In general, the auctioneer will be in a position to define the level of scarcity and identify, to a certain extent, the pattern of players distribution across strong, average and weak types; he will, however, be unable to determine the bidders features (such as their way of expecting opponents' bids). It then becomes crucial to endow auctioneers with knowledge about mechanisms which are robust with regard to bidders' characteristics, yet effective in terms of improving optimality and efficiency, without excessively increasing the number of rounds. In any case, we strongly suggest at least a requirement for dominant bids in combinatorial auctions when there are only complementarity relations. In fact, in this case, to allow bids less than dominant is as ingenuous as to allow bids less than valid in the single-item English auctions (see Example 1 available at http://www.dis. uniroma1.it/\~avenali/).

We will now show how such a problem can be formulated as an integer linear problem. First we need a few definitions. Let us consider an undirected weighted graph $G { = } ( V , E , d )$ , where V is the vertex set, E the edge set, and $d \in Z _ { + + } ^ { | V | }$ (where $Z _ { + + }$ is the set of positive integers) the vector of weights of the vertices. A combinatorial auction can be modelled as an undirected weighted graph $G { = } ( V , E , d )$ . In fact, every vertex of G is associated with a distinct bid, and the weight of the vertex equals the corresponding monetary offer. There is an edge between two vertices iff the corresponding bids cannot be selected simultaneously (for example, since they share an item). Clearly, any stable set of G corresponds to a set of bids which can simultaneously win (and vice-versa); therefore, the set of winning bids which maximizes the auctioneer's revenue corresponds to a stable set of G of maximum weight.

In order to show that constraints of type (2) and (3.1) work as described, we analyze a simplified choice environment, where the player determines the minimum increments to be added to his own bids so that at least a certain number of them become winning with respect to his opponents' offers (from now on this problem will be referred to as Π). It is straightforward to show that this implies no loss of generality with regard to the bidder's surplus maximization problem.

This problem is a two-stage game, where at the first stage the player chooses bid increments and at the second the auctioneer maximizes his revenue. This type of choice environment is known as a Stackelberg (or leader-follower) game. It can be modelled by graph theory as follows.

Alessandro Avenali was born in 1973 in Rome (Italy). In 1998 he obtained a degree in Computer Engineering from the University of Rome “La Sapienza". In 2002 he received his PhD in Operations Research from the University of Rome “La Sapienza". He is currently a Research Associate at the Department of Computer and Systems Science of the Faculty of Engineering at the University of Rome “La Sapienza". His research interests include network design, with particular focus on the efficient managing of the radio spectrum, combinatorial mechanisms for allocating valuable economic resources, and economic issues underlying the access networks in the telecommunications industry. He has been member of several working groups supporting the Italian National Administration in the price revision and in the definition of the economic offer of information services.

Anna Bassanini was born in 1972 in Rome (Italy). In 1997 she obtained a degree in Management and Production Engineering from the University of Rome “Tor Vergata". In 2001 she received her PhD in Management and Production Engineering from the University of San Marino. She has been a Research Associate at the Department of Computer and Systems Science of the Faculty of Engineering at the University of Rome “La Sapienza". She is currently working at the European Patent Office (EPO) in The Hague. Her research interests include the issues related to the allocation of scarce resources through auctions and the deregulation of network industries, especially transport and telecommunications. She has participated in the working group supporting the Italian government in the allocation of the WLL telecommunication licenses.
