# Doxing on Social Networking Sites: An Extension of the Social Cognitive Theory of Moral Thought and Action

<sup>1</sup>School of Management, Beijing Institute of Technology, China, liyangjun@bit.edu.cn <sup>2</sup>School of Business, Hong Kong Baptist University, Hong Kong SAR, ccheung@hkbu.edu.hk <sup>3</sup>School of Information Management, Wuhan University, China, xlshen@whu.edu.cn <sup>4</sup>College of Business, City University of Hong Kong, Hong Kong SAR, ismatlee@cityu.edu.hk

## Abstract

Doxing on social networking sites (SNS doxing) has attracted scholarly and public attention due to the devastating consequences that this behavior can have on individuals and society. SNS doxing occurs when netizens disclose wrongdoers’ personal information on SNSs to punish them and hold them accountable for their unethical and improper acts. To offer a compelling theoretical explanation for this behavior beyond descriptive analyses of SNS doxing, we extend the social cognitive theory of moral thought and action by using retributive proportionality and social approval to articulate doxers’ moral judgments and decision-making. We conducted two independent studies using scenario-based and field surveys to validate the research model. The results of polynomial regression analysis suggest that users’ moral approval of SNS doxing is the result of the combined analyses between perceived blameworthiness of the doxee, perceived severity of SNS doxing, and perceived social approval of SNS doxing. We also found that users’ moral approval of SNS doxing determines their engagement in SNS doxing. Through this study, we offer rich theoretical and practical insights into the prevention of SNS doxing.

Keywords: Doxing, Morality, Social Networking Sites, Social Cognitive Theory of Moral Thought and Action, Retributive Proportionality, Social Approval, Polynomial Regression Analysis

## 1 Introduction

In July 2015, the Sun Sentinel reported that Cecil the lion, a favorite among visiting photographers and safari guests, was “lured from a protected national park in Zimbabwe and killed in an illegal hunt.” The hunter was identified as Walter Palmer, a dentist from Minneapolis, Minnesota. Palmer was ultimately not legally charged with any crime in the United States or Zimbabwe, as he had purchased a permit to hunt in unprotected areas. However,

Palmer soon became subjected to international outrage on social media. Several Facebook pages were created, such as “Shame Lion Killer Dr. Walter Palmer and River Bluff Dental,” and thousands of Facebook users expressed their disapproval. His address, phone number, and social media home pages were plastered everywhere, and his Facebook page quickly filled with death threats and hate messages calling him a “scumbag” and a “disgrace to humankind.” As a result, his professional website and Facebook page were taken down. “It’s been especially hard on my wife and daughter,” Palmer told news organizations. “They’ve been threatened. In the media, as well, and [on] social media … I don’t understand that level of humanity, to come after people not involved at all,” he continued. In addition to online harassment, he experienced protests outside his Minneapolis office, and his Florida vacation home was vandalized.

As the opening vignette illustrates, social technologies such as social networking sites (SNSs) have empowered people to not only express their outrage against moral violations but also to directly engage in the identification and punishment of alleged wrongdoers to enforce social norms (Miranda et al., 2016; Selander & Jarvenpaa, 2016; Crockett, 2017). Considering the increasingly digital society (Turel et al., 2020), the availability of personal information about individuals on the internet has proliferated (Leidner & Tona, 2021). These data are also being weaponized to punish wrongdoers, as shown by the phenomenon of doxing (Cheung, 2021). Doxing on social networking sites (SNS doxing) refers to the act of disclosing a target person’s personal information and alleged moral violations on SNSs with the purpose of identifying them and opening them up to public shaming, harassment, and vilification (Douglas, 2016). In a typical SNS doxing incident, the exposed information can include names, ages, photos, emails, contact addresses, phone numbers, occupations, SNS profiles and data, moral violations, and any other information that one might be reluctant to disclose to the public (Douglas, 2016). For example, as shown in the opening vignette, Walter Palmer was doxed for killing a lion. His personal information was disclosed and exploited to enable people to harass him both online and offline. Thus, SNS doxing can be understood as a means of publicly holding wrongdoers accountable by weaponizing their personal information and digital visibility on SNSs.

SNS doxing is a global challenge, and researchers have explored its prevalence and negative consequences. Similar to the case of Walter Palmer, more than 80 doxing cases against animal abuse were identified in China between 2006 and 2018 (Huang, 2021). Snyder et al. (2017) also detected more than 5,500 doxing posts on Pastebin and 4chan over two periods of about 13 weeks from 2016 to 2017. Doxing has also become rampant in Hong Kong in recent years, with more than 5,800 doxing complaints made to the privacy watchdog between June 2019 and June 2021 (Office of the Privacy Commissioner for Personal Data, Hong Kong, 2022).

While some personal information used in doxing behavior can often be legally obtained from available public sources in an easily accessible form, SNSs can potentially be used to distribute personal information on a large scale (Kane et al., 2014; Douglas, 2016). The exposure of doxees’ personal information ultimately brings unwanted and intense visibility leading to the loss of privacy, the potential for identity theft, public shaming, social rejection, and physical harm (Ransbotham et al., 2016; Trottier, 2017). SNS doxing may also influence the doxee’s family members, particularly those who cannot defend themselves against doxers, such as the elderly and children. Once the doxed information has been circulated online via SNSs, it is extremely difficult (if not impossible) to expunge from the internet (Kane et al., 2014). As a result, SNS doxing may put doxees and their loved ones at risk of repeated harassment by online and offline crowds for extended periods, which makes it difficult for them to defend themselves and may possibly lead to continuous feelings of fear, anxiety, and despair (MacAllister, 2017; Li et al., 2022). Overall, SNS doxing tends to have disproportionately harmful consequences for the doxee and ruin their personal, social, and professional lives.

Due to the irreparable and devastating impact of SNS doxing on doxees, researchers, industry experts, and policymakers have expressed serious concerns about this societal problem (Douglas, 2016; CBS Chicago, 2020; Wong, 2020). SNS platform owners have also been criticized for allowing doxing behavior on their platforms and are thus vulnerable to harm, such as reputational damage, loss of users, strict regulations, and legislative backlash (Ransbotham et al., 2016; Wong, 2020). In mainland China, for example, SNS platforms may bear administrative liabilities for not prohibiting the posting and circulation of another person’s private or identifying information without permission on their platforms, including fines, suspension, and website closure (Weng et al., 2021). Unsurprisingly, SNS doxing has received increasing cross-disciplinary attention from researchers. However, our literature review reveals that studies of doxing-related behaviors have primarily focused on descriptive analyses of the SNS doxing phenomenon and its related moral and legal issues in the absence of compelling theories (see Appendix A). This is a critical omission, as the design of principles, technologies, and policies to combat SNS doxing inevitably requires a theoretical explanation of why users engage in SNS doxing behavior (Majchrzak et al., 2016; Lowry et al., 2019; Chan et al., 2021).

Given this research opportunity, we seek to advance the scientific understanding of SNS doxing behavior by focusing on the role of morality in developing a theoretical model that explains users’ engagement in SNS doxing behavior. Although doxing is mostly undertaken to expose wrongdoers and thus enforce social norms, it constitutes a deviant act in most cultures, as it conflicts with norms that prevent privacy violations, harassment, and humiliation (Lever, 2021; Weng et al., 2021). As with other deviant information technology (IT) behaviors in the information systems (IS) field, such as cyberbullying (Chan et al., 2023), digital piracy (Tam et al., 2019), and IS security policy violations (Li et al., 2021), users’ engagement in doxing is largely determined by their moral judgment of doxing. Guided by the social cognitive theory of moral thought and action (SCTMTA) (Bandura, 1991), we further integrate retributive proportionality with social approval to explain how personal moral reasoning and situational moral norms collectively determine users’ moral approval of SNS doxing. In doing so, we endeavor to answer the following research question:

RQ: How do components of retributive proportionality and social approval of SNS doxing interactively influence users’ moral evaluations of SNS doxing that determine their intention to engage in SNS doxing?

As one of the first theory-driven empirical studies of SNS doxing behavior in the IS field, our study moves beyond prior descriptive analyses of this deviant behavior by introducing a much-needed rich theorization based on the SCTMTA into the doxing literature. We investigate how users’ moral approval of SNS doxing translates the interactions between their personal moral reasoning and situational moral norms to the intention to engage in SNS doxing. We also contribute to the line of IS research on deviant IT behaviors by delving into users’ moral reasoning about a unique and much-debated deviant IT behavior—SNS doxing. Finally, improving the understanding of the underlying nature and mechanisms of SNS doxing, better positions SNS platforms and policymakers to tackle this deviant behavior.

## 2 Theoretical Development

## 2.1 Conceptualization of SNS Doxing

Doxing (also known as doxxing, or human flesh search) derives from the IS slang, “dropping documents” or “dropping docs,” which involves the online release of someone’s (i.e., the doxee’s) personal information to encourage public shaming, typically as punishment for socially unacceptable actions (Douglas, 2016). People may have a variety of motives to dox others, including political ends (e.g., anticorporate activism, antipower, and antiprogressivism), digital vigilantism, personal revenge, and curiosity (Schneier, 2015; Douglas, 2016; Anderson & Wood, 2021). Doxing is not a new phenomenon but has increasingly emerged as a public concern with the prevalence of SNSs, which provide ideal venues for crowds to collect, pool, spread, and exploit doxees’ personal information.

In this study, we define SNS doxing as the act of disclosing an alleged wrongdoer’s personal information and moral violations on SNSs with the purpose of inciting crowds to collectively hunt down and harass them (Douglas, 2016; Chang & Zhu, 2020). Accordingly, SNS doxing often appears in the form of doxing campaigns involving multiple stakeholders (Wang et al., 2014; Zhu & Hu, 2017), including (1) the initiator, who publishes the original SNS doxing post that releases the doxee’s personal details and alleged moral violations to the mob; (2) the core contributors, who voluntarily investigate the doxee using various techniques (e.g., online searches, deep profiling, hacking, and phishing) to collect and disclose more details about the doxee; and (3) the discussants, who may leverage the disclosed personal information to harass and shame the doxee. To address the issue of SNS doxing at the source, we explain users’ intention to publish SNS doxing posts targeting alleged wrongdoers.

The justifiability of SNS doxing behavior has long been the focus of public debate (Bu, 2013; Lever, 2021; Weng et al., 2021). Our review of doxing-related studies also indicates that conceptual, moral, and legal issues have dominated the doxing literature (see Appendix A). Some believe that SNS doxing is analogous to whistleblowing to expose moral violations and can be morally justified by attributing blame to the doxee or by portraying doxing behavior as virtue signaling in the service of valuable moral purposes (Li et al., 2014; Douglas, 2016). That is, SNS doxing is a form of crowdsourced vigilante justice (Chia, 2019). Online crowds engage in SNS doxing to take the law and justice into their own hands (Chang & Zhu, 2020). Others have argued that regardless of the underlying motives, SNS doxing is a perilous and morally unacceptable form of vigilante justice, as it violates the doxee’s privacy, and the end results of SNS doxing are often disproportionately harmful (Ellis, 2017). We argue that doxing is a deviant act that inherently violates social norms designed to prevent invasions of privacy, harassment, and insults, where personal morality has been well-established as the central predictor variable (Wikström, 2010; Craig, 2019).

As detailed in Appendix A, in addition to clarifying the conceptual, moral, and legal issues of doxing behavior, scholars have examined doxing processes and mechanisms (Zhu & Hu, 2017; Heng et al., 2019; Nian & Diao, 2020), the motivational and behavioral features of doxers (Chang & Poon, 2017; Neubaum et al., 2018), and the impacts of doxing (Chen et al., 2018; Hansen & Lim, 2019). Several scholars have also offered theoretical explanations for doxing using the theory of planned behavior, conformity theories, and social influence theory (Neubaum et al., 2018; Chang & Zhu, 2020; Chia, 2020). However, most studies have been descriptive and exploratory in nature, seeking to reveal the prevalence, nature, and characteristics of the doxing phenomenon via conceptual analysis (Douglas, 2016), surveys (Chen et al., 2018), case studies (Heng et al., 2019), and numerical simulations (Nian & Diao, 2020). Although personal morality is relevant to explaining SNS doxing behavior, a nuanced theoretical understanding of users’ moral thoughts about and decisions related to this deviant behavior remains lacking in the doxing literature. We advance the literature by offering a theoretical explanation based on the SCTMTA for users’ moral judgments of SNS doxing behavior.

## 2.2 The Social Cognitive Theory of Moral Thought and Action

The SCTMTA is a powerful theory that attempts to explain how individuals form and shape their moral thoughts during social interactions to govern their moral actions (Bandura, 1991). Individuals generally tend to exercise their own morality and make moral judgments before engaging in deviant behaviors (Myyry et al., 2009). Personal morality refers to individuals’ moral judgment about whether a particular action is morally acceptable in a particular context (Lowry et al., 2016) and has been widely examined as one of the key determinants of user engagement in deviant IT behaviors (Lowry et al., 2016; Tam et al., 2019; Li et al., 2021; Chan et al., 2023). Personal morality regulates human behavior through a self-regulatory mechanism that relies on internal overseers, such as self-sanctions (e.g., shame and guilt) and intrinsic motivation. Once people believe that their actions are moral, they will act, despite any external sanctions (Bandura, 1991). Thus, leveraging personal morality to explain SNS doxing behavior is likely to inform effective prevention strategies for combating this deviant IT behavior.

The SCTMTA adopts the interactionist view of personal morality as arising from diverse sources of influence (Bandura, 1991). According to the social cognitive view (Bandura, 1989), we categorize multifaceted sources of influence into two major types of morality: personal moral reasoning and situational moral norms. Personal moral reasoning involves the use of personal moral principles to interpret the moral aspects of deviant behavior, including the possible consequences of the behavior and the victims’ characteristics (e.g., their blameworthiness), which are relevant to assessing whether deviant behavior can be considered to be moral action (Jones, 1991; Billingham & Parr, 2020). Situational moral norms refer to socially shared morals and prevailing values, which are important in cultivating and changing existing moral judgments (Kohlberg, 1981; Bandura, 1991). Other researchers have approached personal moral evaluation as a process of information integration using various factors and multidimensional standards (Kohlberg, 1981; Kaplan, 2016; Venkatesh et al., 2023).

## 2.2.1 Personal Moral Reasoning: Retributive Proportionality

We draw on the concept of retributive proportionality to explore users’ moral reasoning about SNS doxing because it has been widely used to determine the morality of punishment measures (von Hirsch, 1992; Fish, 2008). A punishment measure can be justified to the extent that it is proportionate in its severity to the blameworthiness of the target. As such, retributive proportionality involves the combined analysis of the severity of the punishment measure and the blameworthiness of the punished target when determining whether the punishment is moral. Perceived severity is defined as the magnitude of the negative consequences for the deviant behavior (Jones, 1991), while the perceived blameworthiness of the target refers to the extent of the target’s liability and responsibility (Billingham & Parr, 2020). Although perceived severity and blameworthiness have been widely considered in personal moral reasoning about doxing (Li et al., 2014; Douglas, 2016) and other deviant behaviors (Siponen & Vance, 2010; Valentine & Godkin, 2019), they are treated independently in the literature. Given that personal moral reasoning depends on a constellation of judged moral factors rather than a single factor (Bandura, 1991), retributive proportionality offers an integrated framework to understand how users exercise personal moral reasoning about SNS doxing.

We further apply the congruence perspective (Venkatraman, 1989; Klein et al., 2009) to theorize retributive proportionality involving different combinations of the perceived severity of SNS doxing and the perceived blameworthiness of the doxee. There are two forms of perceived retributive proportionality and disproportionality (Table 1). Specifically, perceived retributive proportionality refers to the extent to which individuals perceive retribution in the severity of SNS doxing as proportionate to the doxee’s blameworthiness. According to the congruence literature (Shanock et al., 2010; Benlian, 2013), the effect of perceived retributive proportionality describes how the outcome variable varies as perceived blameworthiness and severity increase in the same direction. Perceived retributive disproportionality describes the extent to which individuals’ perceived severity of SNS doxing differs from the perceived blameworthiness of the doxee, including: (1) perceived severe retributive disproportionality, which refers to the extent to which individuals perceive SNS doxing as imposing a more severe punishment on the doxee than deserved, and (2) perceived lenient retributive disproportionality, which refers to the extent to which individuals perceive SNS doxing as imposing a more lenient punishment on the doxee than deserved.

Following the congruence literature (Venkatesh & Goyal, 2010; Daniel et al., 2018), we consider the two forms of perceived retributive disproportionality to be two different continua, instead of the two extremes of a continuum, and further examine their individual effects. In line with Venkatesh and Goyal (2022), individuals can perceive the severity of SNS doxing as retribution that is proportionate to the doxee’s blameworthiness, in which case the two forms of perceived retributive disproportionality are both low. Similar cases in the IS field that have all been treated as separate components with related continua include expectation disconfirmation, such as positive and negative disconfirmation (Venkatesh & Goyal, 2010); ideology misfit, such as over- and underfit (Daniel et al., 2018); and business-IT strategic misalignment, such as positive and negative misalignment (Chau et al., 2020).

## 2.2.2 Situational Moral Norms: Social Approval

To understand situational moral norms, we consider social approval, which is defined as the degree to which social practices are regarded as acceptable by group members (Chia, 2020) and is one of the social norms specifying action-related societal expectations (Cialdini & Trost, 1998; Huang et al., 2022; Venkatesh et al., 2023). Social approval refers to social beliefs about what is socially acceptable and therefore establishes the moral rules of society. Accordingly, when people perceive high social approval for a specific moral action in a particular situation, they will recognize the moral norms and standards of the situation; hence, social approval signals situational moral norms. IS scholars have established that peers’ social acceptance or approval determines users’ moral judgments about moral actions (Myyry et al., 2009; Xu et al., 2016; Lowry et al., 2019). Therefore, the perceived social approval of SNS doxing can be used to examine the role of situational moral norms in users’ moral judgments about SNS doxing.

According to the interactionist view of the SCTMTA, the perceived social approval of SNS doxing will interact with components of retributive proportionality to determine users’ moral approval of SNS doxing. In line with the application of the congruence perspective in examining retributive proportionality, we apply the congruence perspective to understand how the perceived social approval of SNS doxing interacts with these components (i.e., perceived blameworthiness of the doxee and perceived severity of SNS doxing). This analysis responds to calls to use the congruence effect as a more informative alternative to moderation analysis (Shanock et al., 2010; Sedera & Atapattu, 2019).

## 3 Model and Hypothesis Development

Guided by the SCTMTA, we integrate retributive proportionality with social approval to explain users’ moral approval of SNS doxing and their engagement in SNS doxing behavior. To uncover the underlying mechanisms of users’ moral evaluations of SNS doxing behavior, we follow the congruence literature (Venkatraman, 1989; Shanock et al., 2010; Venkatesh & Goyal, 2010) and hypothesize how users’ moral approval of SNS doxing is determined by different combinations of the perceived blameworthiness of the doxee, the perceived severity of SNS doxing, and the perceived social approval of SNS doxing. We also hypothesize the positive effect of users’ moral approval of SNS doxing on their intention to publish doxing posts on SNSs. Given that users’ intentions to engage in SNS doxing behavior may vary across demographic characteristics (Wang et al., 2014; Chen et al., 2019; Chang & Zhu, 2020), we control for several characteristics (i.e., age, gender, education, and SNS experience) to ensure the robustness of our model. We also include the perceived effectiveness of SNS guardianship in our model. Prior IS studies have extensively examined the effects of deterrence measures on users’ engagement in deviant IT behaviors (D’Arcy et al., 2009; Johnston et al., 2015; Chan et al., 2019). Thus, controlling for the perceived effectiveness of guardianship in our model can rule out the effect of SNS efforts to deter doxing when examining the effects of moral approval of SNS doxing, which enhances the validity of our results. ## 3.1 Interaction between Perceived Blameworthiness of the Doxee and Perceived Severity of SNS Doxing

The retributive proportionality perspective posits that whether a punishment measure is moral or not depends on different combinations of the blameworthiness of the punished target and the severity of the punishment measure (von Hirsch, 1992; Fish, 2008). Following the congruence literature (Shanock et al., 2010; Benlian, 2013), we hypothesize the relationship between perceived retributive proportionality and disproportionality and users’ moral approval of SNS doxing.

Perceived retributive proportionality describes the extent to which individuals believe that the severity of SNS doxing is proportionate to the doxee’s blameworthiness. The principle of retributive proportionality is one of the basic requirements of morality and fairness in the legal literature, indicating that the punishment measure is moral when its severity is proportionate to the target’s deservedness (von Hirsch, 1992; Fish, 2008). In this study, there are two cases of perceived retributive proportionality: (1) the doxee is blameworthy and SNS doxing is severe (Quadrant I) and (2) the doxee should not be blamed and the consequences of SNS doxing are not serious (Quadrant III). In both cases, people may believe that SNS doxing is morally acceptable because its severity is proportionate to the doxee’s blameworthiness. As such, we posit that there will be no difference between users’ moral approval of SNS doxing in the two cases of perceived retributive proportionality.

H1a: Perceived retributive proportionality between different levels of perceived blameworthiness of the doxee and perceived severity of SNS doxing has no effect on users’ moral approval of SNS doxing.

Perceived lenient retributive disproportionality describes the extent to which individuals believe that SNS doxing imposes a more lenient punishment on the doxee than is deserved. Doxers largely engage in SNS doxing behavior to expose alleged moral violations and enforce social norms while recognizing the potential impact of their doxing behavior on the doxee (Chia, 2019; Chang & Zhu, 2020). High perceived lenient retributive disproportionality indicates that SNS doxing behavior causes less serious consequences for the doxee relative to the doxee’s blameworthiness. In this case, the doxer may believe their SNS doxing behavior to be morally acceptable, as it enforces social norms and protects public values. Meanwhile, they may not feel guilty or be condemned socially, as the potential consequences of doxing are justifiable due to the doxee’s blameworthiness (Douglas, 2016). Thus, we posit that doxers will perceive SNS doxing as morally acceptable if the doxee is highly blameworthy and the consequences of SNS doxing are less severe. IS studies have also suggested that denial of injury to punished doxees may increase users’ moral acceptance of deviant IT behaviors (Lowry et al., 2016; Chan et al., 2023).

H1b: Perceived lenient retributive disproportionality is positively related to users’ moral approval of SNS doxing.

Perceived severe retributive disproportionality describes the extent to which individuals believe that SNS doxing imposes a more severe punishment on the doxee than is deserved. Considering that SNS doxing is a deviant activity that violates social norms related to invasions of privacy and harassment, the doxer is likely to rationalize their doxing behavior to avoid social censure and dissipate any feelings of guilt and shame (Bandura et al., 1996; Harrison, 2018). IS studies have suggested that people tend to attribute blame and deny injury to the target to rationalize their own deviant IT behaviors (Lowry et al., 2016; Chan et al., 2023). In our study, if people perceive that their SNS doxing punishes the doxee more severely than is deserved, they may find it difficult to morally justify their doxing behavior. In this case, they may accept that their SNS doxing behavior is morally wrong.

H1c: Perceived severe retributive disproportionality is negatively related to users’ moral approval of SNS doxing.

## 3.2 Interaction between Perceived Blameworthiness of the Doxee and Perceived Social Approval of SNS Doxing

Following the interactionist view of the SCTMTA, we further apply the congruence perspective to examine how the congruence and incongruence between the perceived blameworthiness of the doxee and the perceived social approval of SNS doxing relate to users’ moral approval of SNS doxing. The effect of the congruence between two predictors describes how the outcome variable varies as the two predictors increase in the same direction (Shanock et al., 2010). In our study, doxers tend to attribute wrongdoing to the doxee and justify their doxing behavior as a means of holding the doxee accountable to the public (Li et al., 2014). On the one hand, when users believe the doxee is blameworthy, they are likely to accept that SNS doxing is moral. On the other hand, as an explicit signal of situational moral norms, perceived social approval of SNS doxing will inform users of the collective acceptance of doxing behavior on SNS platforms. This situation involves direct social pressure that makes users morally accept SNS doxing behavior to avoid dissonance (Cialdini & Trost, 1998). Taken together, as the perceived blameworthiness of the doxee and the perceived social approval of SNS doxing increase in the same direction, we posit that users’ moral approval of SNS doxing will increase. Thus, users’ moral approval of SNS doxing will be higher when the perceived blameworthiness of the doxee and the perceived social approval of SNS doxing are both high than when they are both low.

H2a: The congruence between different levels of perceived blameworthiness of the doxee and perceived social approval of SNS doxing is positively related to users’ moral approval of SNS doxing.

The effect of incongruence between two predictors describes how the outcome variable varies as the degree of discrepancy between the two predictors increases; that is, the two predictors increase in opposite directions (Shanock et al., 2010). There are two cases of incongruence between the perceived blameworthiness of the doxee and the perceived social approval of SNS doxing: (1) the doxee is highly blameworthy when social approval of SNS doxing is low and (2) the doxee is not blameworthy when SNS doxing is socially approvable. As suggested in H2a, the perceived blameworthiness of the doxee and the perceived social approval of SNS doxing are both positively related to users’ moral approval of SNS doxing. Hence, in either case of incongruence, users will be highly likely to hold conflicting moral principles and experience dissonance in judging whether SNS doxing behavior is moral, which will make them feel less confident and comfortable in accepting this behavior. Thus, users’ moral approval of SNS doxing will decrease as the incongruence between the perceived blameworthiness of the doxee and the perceived social approval of SNS doxing increases.

H2b: The incongruence between the perceived blameworthiness of the doxee and the perceived social approval of SNS doxing is negatively related to users’ moral approval of SNS doxing.

Following previous congruence studies (Shanock et al., 2010; Benlian, 2013), we hypothesize that the two forms of incongruence between the perceived blameworthiness of the doxee and the perceived social approval of SNS doxing have different negative impacts on users’ moral approval of SNS doxing. While both positively relate to users’ moral approval of SNS doxing, the perceived blameworthiness of the doxee may play a stronger role than the perceived social approval of SNS doxing in users’ moral evaluations of SNS doxing because most people engage in doxing behavior to hold doxees accountable for their moral violations and enforce social norms (Chang & Zhu, 2020). As a result, attributing wrongdoing to the doxee is likely to be one of the most common tactics for doxers to morally justify their doxing behavior. Moreover, the perceived blameworthiness of the doxee involves the exercise of personal moral reasoning on doxing behavior (i.e., internal beliefs) with a deep-seated effect on users’ moral evaluation. In contrast, the perceived social approval of SNS doxing involves social pressure (i.e., external beliefs) and its effect on users’ moral evaluation is likely to be mitigated and thus flimsy. As such, incongruence associated with low perceived blameworthiness of the doxee may have a stronger negative impact on users’ moral approval of SNS doxing than incongruence associated with low perceived social approval of SNS doxing.

H2c: Incongruence involving low perceived blameworthiness of the doxee and high perceived social approval of SNS doxing has a stronger negative impact on users’ moral approval of SNS doxing than incongruence involving high perceived blameworthiness of the doxee and low perceived social approval of SNS doxing.

## 3.3 Interaction between Perceived Severity of SNS Doxing and Perceived Social Approval of SNS Doxing

Consistent with the interactionist view of the SCTMTA, we further apply the congruence perspective to understand how congruence and incongruence between the perceived severity of SNS doxing and the perceived social approval of SNS doxing relate to users’ moral approval of SNS doxing. Scholars have suggested that the severity of deviant behavior negatively impacts users’ moral evaluations, while social approval is positively related to users’ moral evaluations (Jones, 1991; Lowry et al., 2019; Valentine & Godkin, 2019). Therefore, in either case of congruence, we posit that when the perceived severity of SNS doxing and the perceived social approval of SNS doxing are both high or low, users will find it difficult to make moral evaluations about SNS doxing. That is, when the perceived severity of SNS doxing and the perceived social approval of SNS doxing are both low, users’ moral approval of SNS doxing will not significantly differ from that when they are both high.

H3a: The congruence between different levels of perceived severity of SNS doxing and the perceived social approval of SNS doxing has no effect on users’ moral approval of SNS doxing.

In cases of incongruence involving high perceived severity of SNS doxing behavior coupled with low perceived social approval, we posit that users are likely to consider SNS doxing to be immoral. That is, users will consider SNS doxing to be immoral regardless of any moral evaluations of SNS doxing based on personal moral reasoning (i.e., high perceived severity of SNS doxing) or situational moral norms (i.e., low perceived social approval of SNS doxing). As the incongruence increases, i.e., when the perceived severity of SNS doxing increases while the perceived social approval of SNS doxing decreases, we posit that users’ moral approval of SNS doxing will also decrease.

H3b: Incongruence involving high perceived severity of SNS doxing and low perceived social approval of SNS doxing is negatively related to users’ moral approval of SNS doxing.

In cases of incongruence involving low perceived severity of SNS doxing coupled with high perceived social approval, users are likely to believe that SNS doxing is morally acceptable because they perceive the negative impact of the SNS doxing to be under control and approved by most users. That is, based on users’ moral evaluations of both personal moral reasoning (i.e., low perceived severity of SNS doxing) and situational moral norms (i.e., high perceived social approval of SNS doxing), the SNS doxing behavior is believed to be moral. As the incongruence increases, i.e., the perceived severity of the SNS doxing decreases while the perceived social approval of the SNS doxing increases, we posit that users’ moral approval of the SNS doxing will also increase.

H3c: Incongruence involving low perceived severity of SNS doxing and high perceived social approval of SNS doxing is positively related to users’ moral approval of SNS doxing.

## 3.4 Role of Moral Approval of SNS Doxing

As suggested by the SCTMTA, users’ morality is based on their moral reasoning and situational moral norms and determines their intention to engage in deviant behavior (Bandura, 1991). It is not difficult to understand that people will be more likely to engage in SNS doxing behavior when they consider such behavior to be moral than when they consider it to be immoral. Prior IS studies have also broadly established that users’ moral evaluations significantly determine their engagement in deviant IT behaviors (Lowry et al., 2016; Tam et al., 2019; Li et al., 2021; Chan et al., 2023). As such, we posit that users’ moral approval of SNS doxing positively will influence their intention to engage in SNS doxing:

H4: Users’ moral approval of SNS doxing is positively related to their intention to publish SNS doxing posts.

## 4 Method

## 4.1 Research Design

We designed two studies to test our research model and hypotheses. In Study 1, we used a scenario-based survey to collect data, in which the participants intention to publish doxing posts was measured indirectly by asking them to answer survey questions and indicate what they would do in certain hypothetical scenarios (Jasso, 2006). Reported behavioral intentions in hypothetical scenarios have been shown to offer a satisfactory approximation of true behavioral intentions (Jasso, 2006). Indirect measures of behavioral intentions also remove feelings of incrimination toward deviant behaviors, thereby reducing social desirability bias (SDB) while still capturing true intentions (Trevino, 1992). Therefore, this technique has been widely used to examine deviant IT behaviors (Barlow et al., 2018; Chan et al., 2023).

Despite its advantages, the scenario-based survey method may be criticized for its reliance on unrealistic scenarios and poor generalizability (Jasso, 2006; Aguinis & Bradley, 2014). IS studies using the scenario-based survey method have primarily adopted statistical measures (e.g., adding validation questions) to evaluate the realism of scenarios (Barlow et al., 2018). To better address these issues of generalizability, we followed recent IS studies (Liang et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2021) and used a field survey in Study 2 in which we asked the participants to recall a real-life doxing incident they had witnessed on SNS platforms and then answer questions based on the provided SNS doxing incident. In doing so, we ensured the realism of the scenarios.

## 4.2 Data Collection

Data collection for the two studies was conducted in China for the following reasons. First, although doxing is a global challenge, SNS doxing in the form of weaponizing personal information to expose alleged moral violations is most prevalent in China (Wang et al., 2014; Chang & Zhu, 2020). Second, doxing behavior is much debated in China. A considerable number of Chinese people believe that doxing may be morally justifiable, as doxing cases in China are generally undertaken to expose alleged moral violations (e.g., animal abuse, bullying, adultery, and fraud), enforce social norms, and protect social justice (Wang et al., 2014; Chang & Zhu, 2020). A survey conducted in China indicated that more than 70% of doxers reported engaging in doxing behavior based on a sense of social justice or community responsibility (Wang et al., 2014). Accordingly, China offers an appropriate cultural context to delve into how SNS users apply multiple moral principles to determine the morality of SNS doxing.

## 4.2.1 Study 1: The Scenario-Based Survey

We developed SNS doxing scenarios by following the suggested guidelines from the literature (Siponen & Vance, 2010; Aguinis & Bradley, 2014; Barlow et al., 2018). Doxing triggers vary according to the situation, such as doxing against animal abuse or disclosing antisocial behavior (Heng et al., 2019). Using an exploratory open-ended survey, we identified three common doxing scenarios by asking respondents to describe a real-life doxing incident they had recently witnessed on SNSs, including doxing against animal abuse (e.g., cat abuse), inappropriate public speech (e.g., sexism), and antisocial behavior (e.g., seat taking). We chose Weibo as the research context to construct these scenarios because it is the leading Chinese SNS platform on which doxing is rampant (Brancart, 2018). Appendix B details the developed scenarios.

We recruited the participants from Credamo, a Chinese online crowdsourcing platform that allows registered users across multiple demographics and backgrounds to participate in tasks for rewards. The use of a thirdparty crowdsourcing platform to recruit participants to take surveys concerning deviant behavior is recommended in the IS literature (Steelman et al., 2014; Wong et al., 2021; Shen & Wu, 2024). Each participant was randomly assigned a scenario. Two validation-check questions appearing immediately after the scenario was presented asked the participants to rate how realistic the given scenario was and how likely they would be to encounter similar doxing behavior in their real-world life on a 10-point Likert scale. The realism scores ranged from 8.22 to 8.72, while the likelihood scores ranged from 7.37 to 8.20, suggesting that the SNS scenarios were generally perceived as realistic. We designed several attentioncheck questions to ensure the quality of responses. In the end, we obtained 496 valid responses.

## 4.2.2 Study 2: The Field Survey

We also recruited the participants for Study 2 from Credamo. Rather than relying on our hypothetical scenarios, the participants were asked to recall a specific incident of real-life SNS doxing they had recently witnessed on an SNS platform and answered survey questions based on their perception of that incident. Hence, the realism of the scenarios was guaranteed. To ensure that the participants had a clear and consistent understanding of SNS doxing, we provided a working definition of SNS doxing before asking the survey questions. Similar to Study 1, we asked the participants to indicate the likelihood that they would publish the same SNS doxing posts if they were in the same situation, thereby reducing SDB (Jasso, 2006). We also included the attention-check questions used in Study 1 to ensure the same response quality in Study 2. Furthermore, we asked the participants to indicate the SNS platform on which they saw the SNS doxing incident and to list the doxee’s personal information as disclosed in the SNS doxing post. The SNS platforms provided by the participants included Weibo, WeChat, TikTok, Douban, and Hupu, among others, while the general personal information listed in the SNS doxing incidents included names, gender, phone numbers, photos, and details of moral violations, among other information. Moreover, the participants reported a variety of moral violations exposed by doxing behavior (e.g., animal abuse, antisocial behavior, sexual harassment, and official corruption, among other forms of misconduct). We also reviewed each reported SNS doxing incident to check whether it was consistent with the provided working definition of SNS doxing. In the end, we obtained 384 valid responses.

## 4.4 Measures

We adapted items from prior studies to measure the major constructs in our model. We measured perceived blameworthiness of the doxee using items adapted from Roopnarine et al. (2014). We adapted items from Valentine and Godkin (2019) to measure perceived severity of SNS doxing. We used items from Lowry et al. (2019) to measure perceived social approval of SNS doxing. Similarly, moral approval of SNS doxing was measured using items adapted from Lowry et al. (2016). We measured intention to publish SNS doxing posts using items adapted from Siponen and Vance (2010) and Lowry et al. (2017a). The control variable, i.e., perceived effectiveness of guardianship, was measured using items from Chan et al. (2019) in Study 1. We adapted the items of perceived sanction certainty and perceived sanction severity from D’Arcy et al. (2009) to measure perceived effectiveness of guardianship in Study 2, where we followed Polites et al. (2012) in conceptualizing perceived effectiveness of guardianship as a second-order reflective construct. We slightly modified the prompts and items to fit the contexts of Studies 1 and 2 where necessary. We used a 7-point Likert scale to frame all items. Appendix C presents the measurement items.

## 5 Data Analysis and Results

## 5.1 Response Bias Evaluation

We assessed the risk of common method bias (CMB) in several ways. First, we performed Harman’s single-factor test. The results showed that the extracted primary component accounted for less than half of the variance in the data (Study 1: 46.85%; Study 2: 43.16%), indicating that CMB did not present a threat to our study (Podsakoff et al., 2003). Second, to reduce potential CMB concerns, we placed items of constructs in different parts of the survey and thus constructed a psychological separation in the survey (Lowry et al., 2016; Shi et al., 2023). Third, we adopted the marker variable technique to control for potential CMB. We chose the second smallest positive correlation among the manifest variables as our estimate of CMB (Lindell & Whitney, 2001; Li et al., 2023). We followed the procedure detailed by Lindell and Whitney (2001) to partial out the CMB estimate from the previously observed correlations for which contamination by CMB was suspected. The results indicated that the significance levels of the correlations among the variables were nearly unchanged. Thus, we concluded that CMB did not present a serious threat in either study.

Another concern that may arise when collecting data from an online survey is SDB, particularly when collecting data on deviant behavior (Kwak et al., 2019). We applied several measures to minimize this threat. First, doxing intention was measured indirectly in both studies by asking the participants what they would do if they were in a certain scenario. Thus, their responses should be less susceptible to SDB (Jasso, 2006). Second, the survey was entirely anonymous to encourage the participants to respond honestly. Third, we included the SDB scale proposed by Reynolds (1982) in our questionnaire to detect potential SDB. The Spearman’s rho value between the SDB score and doxing intention was -0.002 $( p > 0 . 0 5 )$ in Study 1, which was not significant. Thus, SDB did not present a concern in Study 1 (Chan et al., 2019). In Study 2, however, the Spearman’s rho value between the SDB score and doxing intention was -0.202 $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ Although this negative correlation was significant, the correlation was comparable with those between SDB and other socially undesirable behaviors, such as compulsive buying (rho = -0.21, $p < 0 . 0 1$ ; Ridgway et al., 2008), technology addiction (rho = -0.12, p < 0.05; Turel et al., 2011), and cyberbullying (rho = -0.176, p < 0.05; Chan et al., 2019). Overall, we concluded that SDB was not a major issue in either study.

The CFA results showed that the model fit to the data was acceptable: in Study $1 \colon \chi ^ { 2 }$ [137] = 226.195, p < 0.001, RMSEA = 0.036, CFI = 0.989, TLI = 0.987, SRMR = 0.024, and CD = 1.000; and in Study 2: ??<sup>2</sup> [136] = 198,430, p < 0.001, RMSEA = 0.035, CFI = 0.990, TLI= 0.988, SRMR = 0.028, and CD = 1.000. ## 5.3 Polynomial Regression Analysis

To test the three types of proposed congruence and incongruence effects, we followed previous congruence studies (Edwards, 2002; Klein et al., 2009; Venkatesh & Goyal, 2010) and used polynomial regression and response surface analysis (PRRSA) for data analysis. Other data analysis strategies in congruence research (e.g., difference scores and direct measurement) have been criticized for issues of conceptual ambiguity, discarded information, and problematic psychometric properties (Klein et al., 2009; Venkatesh & Goyal, 2010). Alternatively, PRRSA uses the correspondence between two predictor variables to measure congruence and incongruence and can capture each direction of incongruence, which remedies the methodological and analytical limitations of difference scores and direct measurement (Edwards, 2002; Venkatesh & Goyal, 2010). PRRSA is also a more informative alternative to moderation analysis when analyzing the interaction between two predictors (Shanock et al., 2010). The following equations were used for PRRSA (Edwards, 2002):

where Z is the dependent variable, and X and Y are the two predictors. In our study, Z represented the moral approval of SNS doxing, and X and Y represented the different combinations of perceived blameworthiness of the doxee, the perceived severity of SNS doxing, and the perceived social approval of SNS doxing. We scale-centered all data before computing the interaction and quadratic terms to reduce the potential impact of multicollinearity and facilitate the interpretation of the response surface plots (Venkatesh & Goyal, 2010).

In PRRSA, the effects of congruence and incongruence are tested by examining several features of the response surface, which was constructed using the coefficients estimated in Equation (2). According to the congruence literature (Shanock et al., 2010; Benlian, 2013; Yao & Ma, 2023), there are three key response surface features. (1) The line of congruence (LOC), that is, X = Y, along which the outcome variable varies as two predictors increase in the same direction. The LOC is important to examine to determine how the congruence between different levels of two predictors relates to the outcome variable. (2) The line of incongruence (LOIC), that is, X = -Y, along which the outcome variable varies as two predictors increase in opposite directions. The LOIC is important to examine to determine how the degree of discrepancy between two predictors relates to the outcome variable. (3) The first principal axis (FPA), along which the downward curvature of a concave response surface is minimized, while the upward curvature of a convex response surface is maximized. The FPA is important to examine to determine whether the incongruence effects are asymmetric. Guided by the SCTMTA, we developed a research model by integrating retributive proportionality with social approval to understand the role of personal morality in explaining SNS doxing. We further applied the congruence perspective and used PRRSA to test how different combinations of perceived blameworthiness of the doxee, perceived severity of SNS doxing, and perceived social approval of SNS doxing influence users’ moral approval of SNS doxing. We designed two studies to validate the model and the results supported all hypotheses (Table 5).

First, we found that perceived retributive proportionality has no effect on users’ moral approval of SNS doxing. This result suggests that when the perceived blameworthiness of the doxee and the perceived severity of SNS doxing are high, users’ moral approval of SNS doxing does not differ from that when they are low. That is, as the perceived blameworthiness of the doxee and the perceived severity of SNS doxing increase in the same direction, users’ moral approval of SNS doxing remains unchanged. Moreover, as expected, users’ moral approval of SNS doxing increases as the perceived lenient retributive disproportionality increases (i.e., perceived blameworthiness of the doxee increases and perceived severity of SNS doxing decreases), while it decreases as the perceived severe retributive disproportionality increases (i.e., perceived blameworthiness of the doxee decreases and perceived severity of SNS doxing increases). These findings offer strong empirical evidence for the key role of retributive proportionality in users’ moral evaluations of SNS doxing.

Second, our results show that perceived social approval of SNS doxing interacts with components of retributive proportionality (i.e., perceived blameworthiness of the doxee and perceived severity of SNS doxing) to determine users’ moral approval of SNS doxing. Specifically, when perceived blameworthiness of the doxee and perceived social approval of SNS doxing are high, users’ moral approval of SNS doxing is higher than it is when they are low, and the absence of either (even if the other is high) results in lower moral approval of SNS doxing. That is, in either case of incongruence between perceived blameworthiness of the doxee and perceived social approval of SNS doxing, users’ moral approval of SNS doxing decreases as the incongruence increases. Contrary to our expectations, we demonstrated that the two forms of incongruence have a similar negative impact on users’ moral approval of SNS doxing. That is, perceived blameworthiness of the doxee and perceived social approval of SNS doxing are equally important in users’ moral approval of SNS doxing.

Moreover, when the perceived severity of SNS doxing and the perceived social approval of SNS doxing are high, users’ moral approval of SNS doxing is not significantly different from that when they are low. Thus, as expected, the congruence between different levels of perceived severity of SNS doxing and perceived social approval of SNS doxing has no effect on users’ moral approval of SNS doxing. We also found that users’ moral approval of SNS doxing decreases when perceived incongruence involving high perceived severity of SNS doxing and low perceived social approval of SNS doxing increases, whereas it increases when perceived incongruence involving low perceived severity of SNS doxing and high perceived social approval of SNS doxing increases. Overall, the detailed interaction analyses between perceived social approval of SNS doxing and components of retributive proportionality confirm that users’ moral evaluations involve a process of information integration, supporting the interactionist view of the SCTMTA. These findings also suggest that PRRSA can yield more informative details than prior moderation analysis (Shanock et al., 2010; Sedera & Atapattu, 2019).

Finally, our results show that users’ moral approval of SNS doxing positively relates to their intention to publish SNS doxing, confirming the key role of moral evaluations in determining users’ engagement in deviant IT behaviors. Our results regarding the control variables also revealed that the perceived effectiveness of guardianship has no effect on users’ intention to publish SNS doxing posts when users’ moral approval of SNS doxing is considered. That is, once users morally accept SNS doxing, the effect of the perceived effectiveness of guardianship becomes nonsignificant. This further confirms the salient role of users’ moral evaluations in explaining SNS doxing.

## 6.2 Implications for Research

First, the IS discipline has a long tradition of studying deviant IT behaviors (Venkatraman et al., 2018; Wong et al., 2021; Shi et al., 2023). We contribute to this stream of IS research by offering a rich theoretical understanding of a unique deviant IT behavior, namely, SNS doxing. Unlike other deviant IT behaviors in SNSs (e.g., cyberbullying and online gambling), most of which are an extension of offline deviant behaviors, doxing largely relies on SNSs where large online crowds can easily pool and distribute the doxee’s personal details. Therefore, SNS doxing is particularly relevant to IS research (Lowry et al., 2017b). However, except for several limited theoretical investigations of doxing (e.g., Li et al., 2014; Neubaum et al., 2018; Heng et al., 2019), most doxing studies have been descriptive and exploratory in nature, seeking to reveal the prevalence, nature, and characteristics of the doxing phenomenon (see Appendix A). Guided by the SCTMTA, we propose a theoretical explanation for SNS doxing from the perspective of personal morality to inform theorydriven prevention strategies for SNS doxing. Thus, by offering a rich theoretical understanding of SNS doxing, our findings can inform IS researchers’ SNS doxing studies.

Second, we contribute to the retributive proportionality literature. Similar to IS studies borrowing theories from reference disciplines (Baskerville & Myers, 2002), we introduced retributive proportionality from the legal discipline into our model to theorize personal moral reasoning in our model. In its combined analysis of the perceived blameworthiness of the doxee and the severity of SNS doxing, retributive proportionality offers an integrated understanding of how SNS users exercise their personal moral reasoning regarding SNS doxing. However, although retributive proportionality has been widely used to determine whether punishment measures are moral (von Hirsch, 1992; Fish, 2008), empirical research evaluating its effects continues to be lacking. As a type of theoretical contribution, it is critical to test theories when developing them (Colquitt & Zapata-Phelan, 2007). Drawing on the congruence literature (Venkatraman, 1989; Edwards, 2002; Shanock et al., 2010), we leveraged PRRSA and conducted rigorous empirical testing for the effects of perceived retributive proportionality and disproportionality on users’ moral approval of SNS doxing. In doing so, we are the first to rigorously test retributive proportionality empirically in an effort to contribute methodologically and theoretically to reference disciplines (Baskerville & Myers, 2002).

Third, we also contribute to the SCTMTA literature. Based on the unique nature of SNS doxing, we applied retributive proportionality to understand personal moral reasoning and applied social approval to understand situational moral norms, thereby contextualizing the SCTMTA. Moreover, in elaborating on the interactionist view of the SCTMTA, we drew on the congruence perspective and leveraged PRRSA to examine how the perceived social approval of SNS doxing interacts with components of retributive proportionality (i.e., the perceived blameworthiness of the doxee and the perceived severity of SNS doxing) in users’ moral evaluations of SNS doxing. In doing so, we uncovered several novel and detailed mechanisms (e.g., effects of congruence types, incongruence, and incongruence types) that cannot be detected by moderation analysis, thereby enriching the SCTMTA literature. These findings also enhance our understanding of how people make moral evaluations of SNS doxing when holding multiple moral principles. Therefore, through the novel application of the congruence perspective, our study not only advances the SCTMTA literature but also provides a good starting point for IS researchers interested in using the congruence perspective to better explain the complex interactions between two predictors.

## 6.3 Implications for Practice

Our research findings demonstrate the importance of users’ moral approval of SNS doxing in explaining SNS doxing behavior, especially when controlling for the perceived effectiveness of SNS guardianship. This provides practitioners with an alternative approach to prevent SNS doxing behavior beyond the direct application of deterrence strategies, i.e., reducing users’ moral approval of SNS doxing. Specifically, our PRRSA results suggest that SNS platforms should seek to enhance users’ perceived severity of SNS doxing and reduce their perceived blameworthiness of the doxee and perceived social approval of SNS doxing.

To increase users’ perceived severity of SNS doxing, SNS platforms could educate users to help them understand that SNS doxing can easily become uncontrollable and cause long-lasting harm to the doxee and their innocent family members. This could encourage users to think twice before publishing SNS doxing posts. Built-in features on SNS platforms (e.g., detection and reminders) could facilitate this education. For example, given that personal information in doxing (e.g., name, address, and phone number) usually appears in a fixed format and style, which can be detected automatically, SNS platforms could categorize personal information in terms of its potential harm to the doxee and politely remind users of the consequences of SNS doxing if SNS doxing posts containing sensitive personal information are detected.

To reduce perceived blameworthiness of the doxee and perceived social approval of SNS doxing, SNS platforms should intervene when users attribute wrongdoing to doxees. Moral education campaigns could be conducted to educate users on how to take responsibility for their own behavior on SNS platforms. SNS platforms should also issue doxingspecific rules and policies in addition to clarifying the unique nature of SNS doxing to reduce the collective acceptance of doxing on SNS platforms. SNS platforms should publish as many posts and articles as possible to define the nature of SNS doxing and the platforms’ boundaries in the form of pictures, videos, documents, comics, and animated images.

## 6.4 Limitations and Future Research

Our study has several limitations, which provide opportunities for future research. First, despite successfully demonstrating the key roles of retributive proportionality and social approval in determining users moral approval of doxing, we excluded other determinants of personal morality from the model (e.g., moral identity). Moreover, given that the question of whether doxing is legal is much debated and varies from case to case, the roles of laws and regulations are important in shaping users’ moral judgments about SNS doxing. Morality is an enduring research topic in the IS field (Lowry et al., 2016; Tam et al., 2019). Thus, future research could continue this line of IS research to delve into moral principles that individuals could use to evaluate SNS doxing. Specifically, considering the nature of SNS doxing, it is necessary to examine users’ moral decision-making regarding SNS doxing when they hold different moral principles that conflict with each other.

Second, our research model sought to explain users’ intention to publish SNS doxing posts. Although the theoretical understanding of why users publish SNS doxing posts could inform strategies to address the issues of SNS doxing at the source, we did not include other key participants (e.g., contributors and discussants) of SNS doxing campaigns in this study. The influence of SNS doxing largely depends on the massive collaboration of online crowds (Heng et al., 2019). Thus, future research could shift from focusing on the initiators of doxing to examining bystanders in SNS doxing campaigns. Researchers could clarify the nature and forms of bystanders’ responses to doxing, identify the motivations and consequences of bystanders’ responses, and develop intervention strategies to mitigate the potential negative impact of bystanders’ responses.

Finally, data collection for our two studies was conducted in China, as SNS doxing is highly prevalent and much debated in the Greater China region (Chang & Poon, 2017; Chang & Zhu, 2020). Thus, China offers an ideal cultural and moral context to investigate users’ moral judgments about doxing. We adopted several measures to ensure the generalizability of our findings (e.g., designing two studies and adding validation questions). However, moral norms and laws regarding doxing are likely to vary across countries and cultures. Future studies could replicate our research model in other cultural contexts until a scientific consensus on our findings is reached.

## 7 Conclusion

Through its weaponization of personal information and digital visibility, SNS doxing behavior poses an increasing threat to SNS users, platforms, and society. We propose a theoretical explanation for SNS doxing to inform prevention strategies. Guided by the SCTMTA, we applied retributive proportionality and social approval to understand users’ moral evaluations of SNS doxing that determine their engagement in this deviant IT behavior. The PRRSA results confirm that users’ moral evaluation of SNS doxing is the result of the combined analyses of retributive proportionality components and social approval of SNS doxing. We expect our study to not only stimulate more IS discussions focused on SNS doxing but also to serve as a theoretical framework for actionable measures to prevent SNS doxing behavior.

## Appendix B: Scenario Details

Please note that the following scenarios contain potentially disturbing content. Sensitive readers should proceed with caution)

## Scenario #1 Cat abuse

Zhang Hong likes cats. One day, Zhang Hong saw someone (hereafter referred to as “Wang Wei”) and several accomplices torturing a kitten at the entrance of a gated community. Zhang Hong became very angry. Considering that she was outnumbered, Zhang Hong secretly video-recorded Wang Wei’s abusive behavior and facial features. Wang Wei and his accomplices then talked and laughed as they entered the gated community. Zhang Hong stepped forward to check the kitten’s injuries and found that it was lifeless. After returning home, Zhang Hong posted the video of Wang Wei abusing the cat along with other information (such as the kitten’s wounds and basic information about the gated community) on Weibo and “@” tagged some influential Weibo users (such as pet bloggers and local media organizations) with a call for netizens to provide more information about Wang Wei and shame him (i.e., her post doxed Wang Wei).

## Scenario #2 Sexism

As a college student who is about to graduate, Zhang Hong has recently been busy attending job-seeking seminars. In one seminar, the recruitment manager, Wang Wei, repeatedly discriminated against women during his introduction of a company’s personnel policies. For example, Wang Wei emphasized the stereotype of “men’s work is centered around the office and women’s work is centered around the home” with additional requirements (e.g., body shape and appearance) for certain positions. In addition, Wang Wei required female applicants to not take maternity leave. Zhang Hong was very angry and video-recorded Wang Wei’s facial features and sexist remarks. After the seminar, she published a doxing post on Weibo, including the video and Wang Wei’s personal information (e.g., name, gender, company name, position, phone number, and email address), and also “@” tagged several influential Weibo users (e.g., women’s rights organizations and local media agencies) in her Weibo post, calling on these users to hunt down and shame Wang Wei (i.e., her post doxed Wang Wei).

## Scenario #3 Seat-taking

Zhang Hong likes to travel by train. One day, Zhang Hong heard someone arguing in the railway carriage. It turned out that a male passenger (hereinafter referred to as “Wang Wei”) had not taken his assigned seat but insisted on sitting by the window. After receiving complaints from the passenger whose seat Wang Wei had taken by the window, the train attendant repeatedly attempted to communicate with Wang Wei but failed to move him. Wang Wei shouted, “I have a ticket. This seat is 10D, you are all wrong, and I am not sitting in the wrong seat! Who stipulated that 10D is the aisle seat? There is no seat number on the seat!” Although Zhang Hong and other nearby passengers all became angry, Wang Wei still refused to give up the seat. Zhang Hong then recorded Wang Wei’s facial features and his arrogant words. After arriving at the destination, Zhang Hong posted the video on Weibo and “@” tagged several influential Weibo users (e.g., official agencies and local media agencies) in her post, calling on these users to hunt down and shame Wang Wei (i.e., her post doxed Wang Wei).

## Appendix E: Details of Polynomial Regression Analysis

The basic equations used in the polynomial regression analysis are as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & Z = b _ {0} + b _ {1} X + b _ {2} Y + e \\ & Z = b _ {0} + b _ {1} X + b _ {2} Y + b _ {3} X ^ {2} + b _ {4} X Y + b _ {5} Y ^ {2} + e, \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

(2)

where Z is the dependent variable and X and Y are the two predictor variables in a theoretical congruence (Edwards, 2002). The slope $( a _ { 1 } )$ and the curvature $( a _ { 2 } )$ of the surface along the line of congruence (LOC), the slope $( a _ { 3 } )$ and the curvature $( a _ { 4 } )$ of the surface along the line of incongruence (LOIC), and their significance test values $( t _ { 1 } \sim t _ { 4 } )$ can be given by the following:

$$
a _ {1} = b _ {1} + b _ {2}\tag{3}
$$

$$
t _ {1} = a _ {1} \big / \sqrt {(S E _ {b 1} ^ {2} + S E _ {b 2} ^ {2}) + 2 C O V _ {b 1 b 2}}\tag{4}
$$

$$
a _ {2} = b _ {3} + b _ {4} + b _ {5}\tag{5}
$$

$$
t _ {2} = a _ {2} \big / \sqrt {(S E _ {b 3} ^ {2} + S E _ {b 4} ^ {2} + S E _ {b 5} ^ {2}) + 2 C O V _ {b 3 b 4} + 2 C O V _ {b 3 b 5} + 2 C O V _ {b 4 b 5}}\tag{6}
$$

$$
a _ {3} = b _ {1} - b _ {2}\tag{7}
$$

$$
t _ {3} = a _ {3} \big / \sqrt {(S E _ {b 1} ^ {2} + S E _ {b 2} ^ {2}) - 2 C O V _ {b 1 b 2}}\tag{8}
$$

$$
a _ {4} = b _ {3} - b _ {4} + b _ {5}\tag{9}
$$

$$
t _ {4} = a _ {4} \big / \sqrt {(S E _ {b 3} ^ {2} + S E _ {b 4} ^ {2} + S E _ {b 5} ^ {2}) - 2 C O V _ {b 3 b 4} + 2 C O V _ {b 3 b 5} - 2 C O V _ {b 4 b 5}},\tag{10}
$$

where $b _ { 1 } { \sim } b _ { 5 }$ are unstandardized coefficients estimated by Equation (2), $S E _ { b 1 } { \sim } S E _ { b 5 }$ are standard errors of $b _ { 1 } { \sim } b _ { 5 } .$ , and COV is the covariance between the corresponding coefficients.

Moreover, the intercept $( p _ { 1 0 } )$ and the slope $( p _ { 1 1 } )$ of the first principal axis (FPA) can be given by

$$
\begin{array}{l} {p _ {1 0} = Y _ {0} - p _ {1 1} X _ {0}} \\ {p _ {1 1} = \frac {b _ {5} - b _ {3} + \sqrt {(b _ {3} - b _ {5}) ^ {2} + b _ {4} ^ {2}}}{b _ {4}},} \end{array}\tag{11}
$$

(12)

$( X _ { 0 } , Y _ { 0 } )$ is the stationary point of the response surface, at which the response surface is flat; therefore, the slope of the surface is 0 in all directions at this point (Edwards, 2002). The values of $X _ { 0 }$ and $Y _ { 0 }$ can be given by the following:

$$
\begin{array}{r} X _ {0} = \frac {b _ {2} b _ {4} - 2 b _ {1} b _ {5}}{4 b _ {3} b _ {5} - b _ {4} ^ {2}} \\ Y _ {0} = \frac {b _ {1} b _ {4} - 2 b _ {2} b _ {3}}{4 b _ {3} b _ {5} - b _ {4} ^ {2}} \end{array}\tag{13}
$$

(14)

where $b _ { 1 } { \sim } b _ { 5 }$ are unstandardized coefficients estimated by Equation (2).

The FPA is particularly important for testing the effects of incongruence types (Benlian, 2013; Yao & Ma, 2023; Tsai et al., 2022). If the effect of incongruence types is the research focus, the degree of incongruence between the two predictors has a negative or positive impact on the outcome, indicating an inverted-U or U-shape of the LOIC (i.e., $a _ { 4 } > 0 \mathrm { o r } a _ { 4 } < 0 \}$ . Specifically, if researchers hypothesize that the incongruence effects are asymmetrical, the FPA should shift laterally away from the LOC, requiring $p _ { 1 0 } \neq 0$ . To empirically test whether $p _ { 1 0 }$ significantly differs from 0, studies have suggested using the bootstrapping approach to check whether the 95% confidence interval of $p _ { 1 0 }$ includes 0 (Edwards, 2002).

If the asymmetric incongruence effects between the two predictors are demonstrated to exist, the magnitude and direction of the lateral shift determine which type of incongruence $( { \mathrm { i . e . , } } Y > X { \mathrm { o r } } Y < X )$ has a greater or smaller impact on the outcome variable (Benlian, 2013; Yao & Ma, 2023). Specifically, the lateral shift quantity (LSQ) is given by the following equation (Benlian, 2013):

$$
L S Q = - \frac {b _ {2} - b _ {1}}{2 (b _ {3} - b _ {4} + b _ {5})}\tag{15}
$$

Substituting $b _ { 1 } { \sim } b _ { 5 }$ estimated by Equation (2) to Equation (15), a positive LSQ value indicates a shift toward the region where $Y > X ,$ , whereas a negative value indicates a shift toward the region where $Y < X .$
