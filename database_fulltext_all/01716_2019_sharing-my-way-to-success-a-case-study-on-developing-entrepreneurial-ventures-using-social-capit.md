# Sharing my way to success: A case study on developing entrepreneurial ventures using social capital in an OSS community

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Entrepreneurship OSS Online communities Social capital Virtual worlds Mixed methods case study

## A B S T R A C T

While Open Source Software (OSS) communities provide opportunities for knowledge creation, we have a limited understanding of how entrepreneurs leverage OSS communities for their entrepreneurial ventures. Using social capital theory in a mixed methods case study, we compare entrepreneur and non-entrepreneur behaviors to investigate how entrepreneurs build social ca pital within an OSS community. This study shows that entrepreneurs di<sup>f</sup>erentiate themselves from non-entrepreneurs by focusing on cognitive and relational capital building activities, which in return makes it possible for them to leverage their social capital to in<sup>fl</sup>uence and shape the environment in which they are operating. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that entrepreneurs strategically select which activities within the community to expend their limited resources on (e.g., developing code over participating in email conversations) and build their social capital more through their actions than through their words (e.g., showing their commitment to the community through code commits, bug <sup>fi</sup>xes, and documentation). Given the liabilities of newness and smallness as well as other challenges faced by entrepreneurs, applying an open innovation strategy in OSS communities could be one approach where entrepreneurs, by developing and freely revealing their intellectual property to the community, share their way to success via OSS infused entrepreneurial business ventures.

## 1. Introduction

Organizations have begun to realize the immense opportunity open innovation a<sup>f</sup>ords them to capture value by leveraging external resources that complement internal research and development capabilities (Bogers & West. 2012: Chesbrough. 2003: Chesbrough, 2006a; Chesbrough, 2006b). An organization can capture new knowledge, absorb resources, or share resources (e.g., intellectual property) to reshape its competitive market to its advantage by strategically managing the in<sup>fl</sup>ows and out<sup>fl</sup>ows of its resources (Chesbrough, 2003; Chesbrough, 2006b; Huizingh, 2011; West & Bogers, 2014). A primary example of this strategy is the leveraging by <sup>fi</sup>rms of open source software (OSS) communities to develop software that can complement their internal capabilities and product/service portfolio (Bogers & West, 2012; Dahlander & Wallin, 2006; Teigland, Di Gangi, Flåten, Giovacchini, & Pastorino, 2014; West & Gallagher, 2006). For instance, IBM shared its intellectual property and developer capacity with the Apache web server OSS community and now maintains its position as the premier consulting services provider for the Apache software, an OSS software application that is at the heart of today's digital marketplace (Capek, Frank, Gerdt, & Shields, 2005).

From a conceptual perspective, open innovation is an organizational approach to understanding and leveraging network resources, such as OSS communities, to improve <sup>fi</sup>rm performance and resource utilization (Chesbrough, 2003; Chesbrough, 2006b; Ghapanchi, 2013; Schadler, 2004). Beyond the study of open innovation for large enterprises (e.g., IBM and the Apache OSS community), only a few notable exceptions have explored small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) within OSS communities (e.g., Macredie & Mijinyawa, 2011; Teigland et al., 2014). Noticeably absent from the literature is an examination of how the open innovation model can be applied at the individual level by entrepreneurs seeking to support their new ventures. In this study, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne entrepreneurs as individuals who found or establish an organization for the purpose of obtaining economic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts through the sale and/or use of a product and/or service (Shane & Venkatraman, 2000)

The absence of research into how entrepreneurs leverage OSS communities can be contrasted with the prevalence of research into what they stand to gain from participation in OSS communities. OSS communities have been found to provide entrepreneurs with access to a wide range of resources such as developer capacity (Gruber & Henkel, 2006), access to sales and marketing channels (Thistoll, 2011), and opportunities to solicit information about the marketplace and the competitive landscape (Waguespack & Fleming, 2009), leading to a potential increase in the survivability of their new venture and increased innovation performance (Piva, Rentocchini, & Rossi-Lamastra, 2012). What remains underexplored are the mechanisms and approaches through which entrepreneurs realize such bene<sup>fi</sup>ts.

For entrepreneurs and researchers, this area of inquiry is of signi<sup>fi</sup>cant interest since entrepreneurs leveraging OSS communities face not only liabilities of smallness and newness but also a considerable number of challenges, such as a contradiction to the OSS community's philosophy of freely revealing intellectual property (Lerner & Tirole, 2002; von Hippel & von Krogh, 2003), reliance on a volunteer developer workforce (Fitzgerald, 2006), low survivability rates of OSS communities (Gruber & Henkel, 2006), and potentially divergent development interests that may not align with the needs of the entrepreneur. One might expect that entrepreneurs, due to their commercial motivations, would choose to either not participate in OSS communities or engage in free-riding within the community while expending personal development e<sup>f</sup>orts to extend the software in ways commercially attractive for them (Krishnamurthy, Ou, & Tripathi, 2014). While we understand what entrepreneurs stand to gain through access to valuable resources, research has yet to fully examine how entrepreneurs manage this balance of opportunity and challenge to successfully leverage OSS communities for their strategic interests.

Previous research in the entrepreneurship <sup>fi</sup>eld has noted the importance of social capital to an entrepreneur's success, where social capital entails the resources embedded in a social structure that are accessed and/or mobilized in purposive action (Lin, 2001). Prior research has explored the dynamics of online communities with a social capital lens (Smith, Brock Smith, & Shaw, 2017; Stam & Elfring, 2008; Wasko & Faraj, 2005) as well as the performance of entrepreneurs (Piva et al., 2012). Based on the existing literature in online communities and entrepreneurship, we develop the following research question: How do entrepreneurs diferentiate themselves from non-entrepreneurs when building social capital within an OSS community?

To address this question, we conducted a mixed method case study that compares entrepreneurs with non-entrepreneurs regarding how they build social capital within a large, successful OSS community, the OpenSimulator project. We provide additional insights into our <sup>fi</sup>ndings by using semi-structured and unstructured interviews to understand why entrepreneurs exhibit di<sup>f</sup>erences with non-entrepreneurs by examining how leverage their social capital to develop their ventures.

This paper contributes to two literature streams - OSS communities and entrepreneurship. First, we contribute to the OSS community literature by providing a deeper understanding of the di<sup>f</sup>erences between entrepreneurs and non-entrepreneurs in terms of their relationship with OSS communities. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that entrepreneurs actively develop their social capital within an OSS community by focusing on building their cognitive and relational capital within the community. This in turn enables them to shape how the OSS community facilitates their entrepreneurial venture, which can take forms ranging from providing consulting and support services to selling add-on features on the open source software. Second, our study extends the open innovation concept to the individual entrepreneur level and reveals that the social capital building strategies of entrepreneurs enable them to leverage the resources of an OSS community to overcome liabilities of newness and smallness as well as the inherent challenges of these communities.

This paper proceeds as follows. First, we review the literature on OSS communities, entrepreneurs in OSS communities, and socia capital. Building on this review, we develop a set of hypotheses on how entrepreneurs in OSS communities di<sup>f</sup>er in terms of their structural, relational and cognitive social capital compared to non-entrepreneurs. We test and further develop this model using a mixed methods case study approach using quantitative (e.g., archival data) and qualitative data (e.g., interview transcripts) collected from the virtual world OpenSimulator project. We conclude with a discussion of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings and their implications for theory and practice.

## 2. Theoretical background

We integrate the literature on OSS communities, entrepreneurship in OSS communities, and social capital to develop a theoretical framework that guided our investigation of entrepreneurs in OSS communities

## 2.1. OSS communities

OSS communities have been of interest among IS scholars for over two decades. Research has explored the structure of OSS communities (Daniel & Stewart, 2016; Grewal, Lilien, & Mallapragada, 2006), motivations for participation (Choi & Pruett, 2015; Ho and Rai, in press; Phang, Kankanhalli, & Tan, 2015; Roberts, Hann, & Slaughter, 2006; Spaeth, von Krogh, & He, 2014; Zhang,

Anzalone, Faria, & Pearce, 2013), community performance (Daniel & Stewart, 2016; Grewal et al., 2006; Peng, Wan, & Woodlock, 2013), and adoption of OSS (Gallego, Bueno, Racero, & Noyes, 2015; Gwebu & Wang, 2011; Marsan, Pare, & Beaudry, 2012; Rossi, Russo, & Succi, 2012). To date, this research has found that OSS communities rival proprietary software in terms of quality (Benkler, Shaw, & Hill, 2013), leading researchers to focus on value creation in such communities as an alternative to <sup>fi</sup>rm-based value creation (Lee & Cole, 2003; Raymond, 1999).

Research on OSS communities intersect with a growing literature on the loosening up of <sup>fi</sup>rm boundaries to understand how organizations utilize knowledge through collaboration within a loosely coupled network of distinct actors (Chesbrough, 2003; Dodgson, Gann, & Salter, 2006; Kogut, 2000). For example, large <sup>fi</sup>rms have been found to leverage OSS projects as external resources to complement in-house R&D capabilities (Dahlander & Wallin, 2006). Moreover, Dahlander (2007) explored how small <sup>fi</sup>rms were established with the speci<sup>fi</sup>c intent to utilize expertise in OSS communities. Leveraging such external, complementary resources falls under the broader concept of open innovation (Chesbrough, 2003, 2006a, 2006b) as it involves managing the in<sup>fl</sup>ows and out<sup>fl</sup>ows of resources across permeable organizational boundaries to capture new knowledge and co-create value with outside actors (Chesbrough, 2003, 2006a, 2006b; Huizingh, 2011; West & Bogers, 2014).

From a structural perspective, OSS communities have followed a bazaar governance model, which relies on decentralized and <sup>fl</sup>uid social structures (Raymond, 1999; Stam & Elfring, 2008; Wasko & Faraj, 2005), while each open source community having a unique governance model (Shaikh & Henfridsson, 2017). Unlike traditional <sup>fi</sup>rms, OSS development relies on non-monetary, bottomup mechanisms of organization, coordination, task distribution, and enforcement (Brabham, 2012; Gulati, Puranam, & Tushman, 2012). Thus, <sup>fi</sup>rms enacting open innovation through leveraging OSS communities cannot manage the communities through traditional hierarchical mechanisms. To in<sup>fl</sup>uence the community, a <sup>fi</sup>rm may appoint a “man on the inside” to participate in an OSS community and support community development to influence and extract knowledge from the community that will provide insight into how the <sup>fi</sup>rm can leverage the OSS community's e<sup>f</sup>orts for its product/service portfolio (Dahlander & Wallin, 2006; Randhawa, Wilden, & Hohberger, 2016; West & Bogers, 2014; West & Lakhani, 2008).

## 2.2. Entrepreneurs in OSS communities

Research that focuses on entrepreneurs in OSS communities is limited. Initial research either fails to detect the participation of entrepreneurs or classi<sup>fi</sup>es entrepreneurs as members of SMEs or large <sup>fi</sup>rms. For example, studies have divided community participants into two sets of actors: 1) hobbyists who volunteer on their own time and 2) <sup>fi</sup>rm employees (e.g., Dahlander & Wallin, 2006; Henkel, 2006). The <sup>fi</sup>rm category represented a mixed collection of individuals including entrepreneurs who create and manage their own ventures (Dahlander & Magnusson, 2005) and employees of <sup>fi</sup>rms that pay for the employees to participate in the OSS community (Lakhani & Wolf, 2005; Mehra, Black, & Lee, 2010). However, entrepreneurs do not fall into either of these categories - they are neither a hobbyist nor an employee. Entrepreneurs are individuals who volunteer their own time and e<sup>f</sup>ort but who also are interested in obtaining economic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts through the sale and/or use of a product and/or service through an organization or <sup>fi</sup>rm established by themselves (Shane & Venkatraman, 2000).

A second set of studies has focused on entrepreneurial ventures leveraging an OSS community with the level of analysis at the <sup>fi</sup>rm level. For example, in a study of innovative performance, Piva et al., 2012 divided entrepreneurial ventures into two categories: 1) <sup>fi</sup>rms with ten or fewer employees and 2) <sup>fi</sup>rms with greater than ten employees. Gruber and Henkel (2006) conducted a more <sup>fi</sup>negrained approach and divided <sup>fi</sup>rms into the following: 1 person, 2 10 people, 11 50 people, 51 200 people, and > 200, and investigated how the three key challenges of venture management – the liabilities of newness and smallness of start-ups and market entry barriers – a<sup>f</sup>ect new ventures in OSS. Collectively, this research suggests that entrepreneurs actively participating in OSS communities experience increased innovative performance due to their ability to access an alternative and, in many respects, valuable pool of external competences.

Based on our review, only Yetis-Larsson, Teigland, and Dovbysh (2015) examines entrepreneurs who base their entire venture on the underlying software of an OSS community. Yetis-Larsson et al. (2015) found that these entrepreneurs actively work and collaborate with other community members and utilize a range of social strategies that enable them to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll the pro<sup>fi</sup>t motives of their entrepreneurial ventures. Aside from this exception, the lack of explicit focus on entrepreneurs is surprising given the increasing focus by government, industry, and academia on entrepreneurs as the drivers of innovation, economic growth, and labor employment in both developed and developing nations during recent decades (Busenitz et al., 2003; Drucker, 1985; Wenneker & Thurik, 1999). A lack of consistent categorization limits the ability of researchers and practitioners alike to understand entrepreneurs in OSS communities and what actions they take to ensure the success of their ventures. Unlike large <sup>fi</sup>rms and even SMEs (e.g., Dahlander, 2007), entrepreneurs are limited in their abilities to govern or in<sup>fl</sup>uence the development of an OSS community due to their liabilities of newness and smallness. They neither have the reputation nor the resources that large <sup>fi</sup>rms or established SMEs possess.

As a result, an entrepreneur developing their venture based on an OSS community faces considerable challenges in achieving sustainable success. On the one hand, OSS communities have been found to be signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly di<sup>f</sup>erent in their ability to create attractiveness, leading to a highly-skewed distribution of resources among them and high failure rates (Gruber & Henkel. 2006: Santos, Kuk, Kon, & Pearson, 2013). Even if an entrepreneur has located a seemingly attractive community, there is no guarantee for long-term survival as new OSS projects are continuously created (Santos et al., 2013) and may prove to be more attractive to the volunteer developer workforce (Fitzgerald, 2006). This leaves entrepreneurs with the risk of relying on an unsustainable OSS community to support their entrepreneurial venture.

On the other hand, even if the entrepreneur associates with a sustainable OSS community, other issues arise related to the use and further development of the software. Entrepreneurs may run the risk of intellectual property infringement (e.g., a large <sup>fi</sup>rm chooses to assert patent rights against the OSS community) (e.g., Wen, Ceccagnoli, & Forman, 2015). Additionally, conditions related to the entrepreneur's use of the software can arise due to changes in the software license regulating its commercial use (Santos et al., 2013). The entrepreneur also runs the risk that the community's interests diverge from those of the entrepreneurs leaving the entrepreneur with software that eventually becomes outdated.

The entrepreneur must also continually balance their private interests with the OSS community's collective interests characterized by a philosophy of freely revealing intellectual property (Lerner & Tirole, 2002; von Hippel & von Krogh, 2003). Thus, while OSS communities appear to provide a viable open innovation strategy for entrepreneurs, research to date has focused primarily on <sup>fi</sup>rm performance and strategy concerns with the level of analysis at the <sup>fi</sup>rm level and has yet to explicitly examine the behaviors of how entrepreneurs developing their ventures based on an OSS community capture these performance and resource advantages while overcoming the inherent challenges.

## 2.3. Social capital

Social Capital Theory (e.g., Burt, 1992; Lin, 2001; Putnam, 1995a) is a widely used theoretical perspective that suggests socia capital is the “sum of actual and potential resources embedded within, available through, and derived from the network of relationships possessed by an individual or social unit” (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998: 243). Social capital encourages cooperative behavior and value creation through two means – knowledge diversity and exchange e<sup>fi</sup>ciency (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998). Social capital increases the e<sup>fi</sup>ciency of action, such as information di<sup>f</sup>usion, in networks characterized by weak ties or structural holes. In addition, socia capital increases trust and decreases opportunism and the need for costly monitoring processes, thus reducing the cost of transactions (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998; Putnam, 1993).

Nahapiet and Ghoshal (1998) developed three dimensions of social capital: structural, cognitive, and relational capital. The <sup>fi</sup>rst dimension, structural capital, concerns the properties of the social system and its network and describes the con<sup>fi</sup>guration of linkages among actors in a network (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998). Structural capital e<sup>f</sup>ects knowledge exchange within a community through three factors: 1) the absence or presence of network ties (Scott, 1991; Wasserman & Faust, 1994), 2) the morphology of those ties such as density, connectivity, and hierarchy (Tichy, Tushman, & Fombrun, 1979), and 3) the appropriability, that is the transferability of the goodwill of the ties established for one purpose to another context (Coleman, 1988; Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998). Secondly, cognitive capital refers to the shared representations and interpretations among parties within a social network (Cicourel, 1973; Giddens, 1974). Cognitive capital taps into the social artifact that is embedded in a social context and it is sustained through ongoing relationships. Components may include shared language, codes, and narratives as well as boundary spanning objects that enable knowledge sharing and collaboration (Liao & Welsch, 2005; Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998). Lastly, relational capital is associated with trust, common norms, expectations and obligations, and identi<sup>fi</sup>cation within a network (Coleman, 1988; Fukuyama, 1997; Putnam, 1993), all of which have been associated with the willingness to share knowledge (Gulati & Gargiulo, 1999; Powell, 1998; Uzzi & Gillespie, 2002). Social bonding occurs as relational capital is created and leveraged (Hakansson & Snehota, 1995; Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998).

The subdued role of formal hierarchical structures and monetary incentives in OSS and other online communities has led researchers to adopt social capital theory as a useful framework for studying the dynamics in such communities (Stam & Elfring, 2008; Wasko & Faraj, 2005). Unlike traditional physical settings such as neighborhood communities, online communities may have more di<sup>fi</sup>culty developing social capital due to their open structure and low bandwidth communication (e.g., mailing list, code notation). However, Wasko and Faraj (2005) and Wasko, Teigland, and Faraj (2009) examined knowledge exchange and found that “socia capital develops and plays an important role underlying knowledge exchange, despite the media richness limitations inherent in online communication” (Wasko & Faraj, 2005: 50). In the context of OSS communities, Zhang and Watts (2008) suggest that an OSS community can overcome these online limitations while Méndez-Durón and García (2009) found that investment in social capital building activities can positively impact OSS project success.

Within the entrepreneurship literature, social capital has continuously been found to be influential to an entrepreneur's ability to achieve success (e.g., Kim & Aldrich, 2005). Social capital provides an entrepreneur with social networks that facilitate the recognition and creation of entrepreneurial opportunities as well as the identification, collection, and allocation of scarce resources (Birley, 1985; Greene & Brown, 1997; Stam & Elfring, 2008; Uzzi, 1999). For example, entrepreneurs can gain access to information (Birley, 1985), <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources (Batjargal, 2003), potential customers (Florin, Lubatkin, & Schulze, 2003), emotional support (Brüderl & Preisendörfer, 1998), legitimacy (Stuart, Hoang, & Hybels, 1999), and competitive capabilities (McEvily & Zaheer, 1999) through their embeddedness in social networks

While research has explored social capital at the community level of online and OSS communities as well as the role of social capital in an individual's entrepreneurial success, OSS community research has yet to explicitly focus on social capital at the individual level, and even much less on how entrepreneurs active within OSS communities build and leverage their social capital for entrepreneurial success.

## 3. Building and using social capital in OSS

Using Social Capital Theory as a theoretical base, we develop hypotheses examining the di<sup>f</sup>erences between entrepreneurs and non-entrepreneurs in OSS communities in how they build their structural, relational, and cognitive capital to address our research question: How do entrepreneurs diferentiate themselves from non-entrepreneurs when building social capital within an OSS community?

## 3.1. Structural capital

Structural capital refers to the con<sup>fi</sup>guration of connections among individuals within a network (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998). At the individual level, individuals with a higher degree of centrality in a network have greater information and control bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (Burt, 1992). Entrepreneurship research suggests that an entrepreneur's structural position within a social network in<sup>fl</sup>uences their startup's success (Gloor, Dorsaz, & Fuehres, 2011).

While there are several types of centrality measures used by social scientists (Wasserman & Faust, 1994), out-degree centrality is more commonly associated with potential in<sup>fl</sup>uence (Russo & Koesten, 2005) as well as information and advice-seeking behavior (Vardaman, Taylor, Allen, Gondo, & Amis, 2015). Entrepreneurs who signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly seek out other individuals in their networks have a high level of out-degree centrality, which has been found to provide greater <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance than their peers (Ho & Pollack, 2014). Entrepreneurs in OSS communities are interested in seeking diverse information such as market intelligence and technica know-how and to gather information on customer needs and interests to competitively develop and position their product/service o<sup>f</sup>erings for customers. Therefore we hypothesize:

H1a. Entrepreneurs have a higher out-degree centrality than that of non-entrepreneurs in an OSS community.

Betweenness centrality, or the extent to which an actor is connected to otherwise disconnected parts of a network, is associated with how often individuals are on the information path between other people in a network (Witt, 2004). This provides access to nonredundant sources of information (Cross, Baker, & Parker, 2003) and control over information <sup>fl</sup>ows (Freeman, 1979). Individuals with high betweenness can be said to assert control over the <sup>fl</sup>ow in the network since going around them would require a longer path (Opsahl, Agneessens, & Skvoretz, 2010). Entrepreneurs develop betweenness centrality by establishing a pattern of interaction with others in their network that maximizes access to diverse sources of information and that positions themselves as brokers of information between di<sup>f</sup>erent groups (Bodin, Crona, & Ernstson, 2006; Freeman, 1979; Gloor et al., 2011; Kim & Aldrich, 2005). As entrepreneurs have a high level of interest in accessing information and controlling bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to support their entrepreneurial ventures while ensuring the sustainability of the OSS community's underlying software, we hypothesize that entrepreneurs have higher betweenness centrality than non-entrepreneurs. Thus,

H1b. Entrepreneurs have a higher betweenness centrality than that of non-entrepreneurs in an OSS community.

## 3.2. Relational capital

While structural capital focuses on how people are positioned within the social structure of the OSS community, relational capital refers to the a<sup>f</sup>ective nature of relationships, i.e., identi<sup>fi</sup>cation (Lewicki & Bunker, 1996; Wasko & Faraj, 2005), trust (Putnam, 1995b), obligation (Coleman, 1990), and recognition and abiding by cooperative norms of the relationship (Putnam, 1995a). Relationships with a high degree of relational capital lead to a willingness to cooperate and exchange intellectual capital among parties (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998).

OSS communities rely on collective problem solving and consensus building for community decisions (Risse, 2000), and many OSS community members may view the for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t motives of entrepreneurs as counter to the broader culture of OSS philosophy (Lerner & Tirole, 2002; von Hippel & von Krogh, 2003) since the traditional view of entrepreneurs is that they are individualistic and self-interested (e.g., Cable & Shane, 1997; McGrath, MacMillan, & Scheinberg, 1992). Entrepreneurs therefore face a challenge since at times they may need critical support from the community's developers when further developing and implementing their product/ service for their commercial purposes. To overcome this challenge, entrepreneurs in OSS communities may try to build their relational capital with the community to a higher degree than non-entrepreneurs, such that they can access help when they need it.

Relational capital can be built by demonstrating commitment and reciprocity (Wasko & Faraj, 2005). Commitment conveys a sense of responsibility to help others in the community (Wasko & Faraj, 2005), and entrepreneurs who want to signal their commitment to an OSS community may perform a welcoming role to newcomers to the community. Welcoming newcomers demonstrates a willingness to help, which represents an overt expression of caring for another person. Welcoming also re<sup>fl</sup>ects empathy towards incumbent members in the community who may be uninterested in helping new community members, thereby signaling the entrepreneur is seeking to understand the needs of others (Chua, 2002). Thus,

H2a. Entrepreneurs are more welcoming to newcomers to the community than non-entrepreneurs are in an OSS community.

In addition to commitment, relational capital has been found to be consistent with reciprocity or the view that “there is no such thing as a free lunch” as exchange brings with it expectations about future obligations (Mauss. 1954: Bourdieu. 1977: Cheal. 1998: Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998). In crowdfunding settings, entrepreneurs have been found to develop obligations from other entrepreneurs to fund their projects by themselves funding the projects of others (Staber, 2006; Zheng, Li, Wu, & Xu, 2014). Similarly, we would expect entrepreneurs in an OSS community to develop their relational capital by showing a high willingness to contribute to the community as a way for them to give back in light of their exploitation of the community's collective resources for their ventures (Yetis-Larsson et al., 2015). One signi<sup>fi</sup>cant means to contribute to an OSS community is to make code commits to the underlying software. Thus, we would expect entrepreneurs to contribute more code commits than non-entrepreneurs in an OSS community.

H2b. Entrepreneurs contribute more code commits than non-entrepreneurs in an OSS community.

Entrepreneurs also try to establish shared norms in the OSS community that are favorable to entrepreneurial pursuits (Yetis-Larsson et al., 2015). Norms are regular behavioral patterns that are relatively stable within a particular group (Bettenhausen & Murnighan, 1986). Research has found that those group members who are more active are those who establish and legitimize certain behaviors that may eventually become behavioral norms. These members have certain expectations about what ought to happen in the group and therefore actively implement their expectations through their own actions and assertive behavior (Bettenhausen & Murnighan, 1986). Therefore, we suggest that entrepreneurs are more active than non-entrepreneurs in stating their opinion and using their tone (either positive or negative) in their communications with other members of the community. Thus,

H2c. Entrepreneurs express their opinion to a higher degree than non-entrepreneurs in an OSS community.

## 3.3. Cognitive capital

Cognitive capital relates to a shared language and system of meaning (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998), and at the individual level, cognitive capital concerns a community member's expertise and value to the community (Wasko & Faraj, 2005). Wasko & Fara (2005:41) suggest that an individual's cognitive capital “develops as he or she interacts over time with others sharing the same practice and learns the skills, knowledge, specialized discourse, and norms of the practice”.

In OSS communities, expertise translates into the development and recombination of knowledge in the form of developing the codebase, which is a key intellectual asset within OSS communities (Gousios, Kalliamvakou, & Spinellis, 2008). Thus, to develop their cognitive capital, entrepreneurs need to partake in activities related to developing the codebase that serves as the shared knowledge base for the community and its members. This also enables them to gain an appreciation for the shared practice of software development within the community and to understand how the software code develops over time along with its strengths and limitations, thereby providing them with a more fundamental understanding of the code.

In an OSS community, two or more developers may work on the same component of the larger code (e.g., software module) (Faust, 1997) by modifying the code over time. Regardless of whether the developers engage in the coding at the same point, their work on the same code <sup>fi</sup>le within a module represents a shared practice. This interaction of all developers on a software's code then results in a network within which central developers are those who work on <sup>fi</sup>les many other developers also work on while peripheral developers are those who work on code with little connection to the knowledge embedded in the software's kernel that only a small group of other developers are interested in.

H3a. Entrepreneurs have higher degree centrality in the shared modules (codebase) network than non-entrepreneurs in an OSS community.

Entrepreneurs in OSS communities may develop their cognitive capital by participating in the shared modules network to a high degree as well as by seeking to understand the diverse sets of knowledge in the community by bridging cliques of developers working on di<sup>f</sup>erent clusters of code. Through a deeper understanding of the software code and what is required to integrate diverse knowledge, the entrepreneurs may not only gain early access to unique information that may be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to their entrepreneurial ventures, but they may also increase their ability to assert control over the development and future trajectory of the software. Such an advantage corresponds to a high level of degree centrality and betweenness centrality (Freeman, 1979), capturing the presence of structural holes (Gargiulo & Benassi, 2000; Kim & Aldrich, 2005; Naan et al., 2010).

H3b. Entrepreneurs have higher betweenness centrality in the shared modules (codebase) network than other developers in an OSS community.

In addition to expertise, cognitive capital also relates to one's value within a community. In many OSS communities, a developer's contribution to the software project is evaluated by the community through a “kudo rank”, which is based upon the number of kudos bestowed on a developer by community members as a token of their appreciation for work well done (Hemetsberger, 2002). For entrepreneurs, the commercial motivation for participating in the OSS community encourages the use of signals to the market of their expertise for prospective clients (Connelly, Certo, Ireland, & Reutzel, 2011). As such, the kudo rank can serve as a signal of quality based on peer assessment within the community. As entrepreneurs in OSS communities rely on the underlying software for their commercial enterprise, they are driven by an interest in developing a product that is stable and rich in features in order to satisfy demanding customers as well as ensure that the trajectory of software development is in line with their commercial interests (Teigland et al., 2014), we would expect them to have a higher level of degree and betweenness centrality in the shared module network as well as a higher average kudo rank than non-entrepreneurs. Thus,

H3c. Entrepreneurs have a higher kudo rank than non-entrepreneurs in an OSS community.

## 4. Research design and methods

For our research design, we utilized a mixed methods case study approach (Mingers, 2001; Morgan, 1998; Teddlie & Tashakkori, 2009; Venkatesh, Brown, & Bala, 2013) in the study of one OSS community, the OpenSimulator project. We <sup>fi</sup>rst used archival data from the developer mailing lists and code contributions from the community's code repository to test our hypotheses to address our research question - how do entrepreneurs diferentiate themselves from non-entrepreneurs when building social capital within an OSS community? We then conducted interviews with members of the OpenSimulator community, which helped us develop insight into the context and actions entrepreneurs take when developing their entrepreneurial ventures. Table 1 presents an overview of the data sources used across the two phases of our study.

## 4.1. The OpenSimulator OSS community

In our study we chose to research the OpenSimulator project over an eight year period - from August 2007 to July 2015. OpenSimulator (www.http://opensimulator.org) is an open source software platform for hosting virtual worlds that enables its users to customize their virtual worlds based on their speci<sup>fi</sup>c preferences. OpenSimulator project started shortly after Second Life, an online virtual world developed and owned by Linden Lab, released part of its software as open source in early 2007. OpenSimulator has enjoyed an active and long period of sustained participation and growth from a global community of developers. OpenSimulator is an OSS community with a diverse set of members that exists within a highly uncertain external environment due to the relative immaturity of the virtual world industry. Fig. 1 provides a depiction of the OpenSimulator virtual world environment.

The community involves entrepreneurial ventures due to its business-friendly intellectual property license (BSD) that allows for the creation of unique, proprietary software modules and use of the software application for both consulting and commercial ventures based on the OpenSimulator software. The OpenHub.net characterizes OpenSimulator as a “well established, mature codebase maintained by a large development team”.<sup>1</sup> Given the longevity of the OpenSimulator community, its large and diverse developer base, and the relative maturity of the application, OpenSimulator is an ideal OSS community to explore how entrepreneurs engage in OSS communities.

## 4.2. Phase 1: identifying entrepreneur and non-entrepreneur diferences

Our <sup>fi</sup>rst step was to collect data from the secondary sources to develop an understanding of the OpenSimulator community, the resources used to coordinate development activity, and the individuals participating in the community. We also scraped (i.e., extracted information from websites) the OpenSimulator project repository for code commits as well as the developer mailing list for messages and information on the individual developers who were active in the community and only used openly available online information. We then used the developer mailing list and code commit network data to identify developers who actively participated and/or contributed to the OpenSimulator project during the study period. We selected developers who contributed at least <sup>fi</sup>ve messages during this period. We determined that <sup>fi</sup>ve messages was a su<sup>fi</sup>cient cuto<sup>f</sup> based on the mean number of messages sent b all OpenSimulator community members during the study period. Any individual posting fewer than <sup>fi</sup>ve messages to the developer mailing list was classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as inactive .

We then classi<sup>fi</sup>ed each active developer as either an entrepreneur or a non-entrepreneur. To be an entrepreneur, the developer must have indicated they were self-employed or the founder of a <sup>fi</sup>rm based on the OpenSimulator software. The OpenSimulator wiki was the primary source of information for classi<sup>fi</sup>cation because OpenSimulator maintains a page for developers to post descriptive information about themselves and self-identify their organizational a<sup>fi</sup>liation, expertise, timezone, and personal interests in OpenSimulator. In addition, we used publicly available information from social networking sites such as LinkedIn, Facebook, Google +, and personal blogs to con<sup>fi</sup>rm the organizational a<sup>fi</sup>liation of each active developer. Throughout the period, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed only three individuals who started as a hobbyist and then became an entrepreneur. We accounted for this change by classifying the members in the category they had been part of for the longest time. To ensure the reliability of our developer classification, two researchers classi<sup>fi</sup>ed each developer independently and cross-checked each other's work. Any discrepancies were discussed between the two researchers to categorize each developer.

Once developers were classi<sup>fi</sup>ed, we used the developer mailing list and code repository of the OpenSimulator community to gain an appreciation of the structural network of the community and intellectual contributions made by each developer. In total, there were $^ { 1 4 , 6 2 6 }$ messages posted to the mailing list and 31,008 code commits posted to the code repository by 534 participants of whom 287 were active between August 2007 and July 2015. As 28 of these 287 were unidentifiable, we removed them from the analysis. Thus, after removing the inactive and unidenti<sup>fi</sup>able individuals, 259 developers remained, with 14,145 messages in the mailing list, or 96.7% of the total messages, and 30,899 remained in the code commits or 99.6% of the total commits.

4.2.1.2. Relational capital. We operationalized relational capital through the welcomingness, code commits to the repository, and opinionatedness variables. Welcomingness refers to the extent to which a focal developer replies to <sup>fi</sup>rst messages sent to the community by new members entering the community, controlling for the focal developer's general tendency to answer other people's emails. The welcomingness measure is calculated as the focal developer's share of the total number of responses to <sup>fi</sup>rst emails, weighed by the focal developer's total number of responses. Number of code commits was measured by the number of code <sup>fi</sup>les committed to the code repository by a developer during the period.

Opinionatedness is a measure of the degree to which a focal developer uses words that represent signs of positivity or negativity (Duhan, Kaur, & Sangeeta, 2015) in his or her emails. We conducted a sentiment analysis using the SentiStrength algorithm (Thelwall, Buckley, Paltoglou, Cai, & Kappas, 2010) for each developer in the OpenSimulator community. The SentiStrength algorithm has been found to perform better than other sentiment analysis techniques (Nielsen, 2011) by estimating the strength of positive and negative sentiment in text based upon strength scales of −5 (strongly negative) to 5 (strongly positive), 1 and − 1 indicating no positivity and negativity respectively (Thelwall et al., 2010). Since opinionatedness is captured by both strong positive and negative words, for our calculations we used the absolute value for words indicating negative sentiment. The opinionatedness measure is calculated as the focal developer's share of the total opinionatedness score of the community, weighed by the total number of words used by the focal developer.

4.2.1.3. Cognitive capital. Cognitive capital was operationalized through the degree and betweenness centrality measures and the kudo rank. We used the shared modules network to determine degree centrality and betweenness centrality for each developer. When two developers work on the same code module, a connection is formed between the two developers (Lopez-Fernandez, Robles, & Gonzalez-Barahona, 2004), and the resulting network describes the overall social pattern of code contribution. Degree centrality measures the number of developers that a focal developer is connected to while betweenness centrality assesses the extent to which a focal developer lies on the shortest path between two other developers in the network formed through collaboration on the same module in the OpenSimulator code repository. Both measures were calculated using UCINET version 6.181 (Borgatti et al., 2002).

## 4.2.2. Statistical analysis

To select the most appropriate statistical test to assess whether entrepreneurs were distinct from non-entrepreneurs across dif ferent dimensions of social capital, we <sup>fi</sup>rst considered the distribution of our data. In all measurements, our data had excessive positive skew with several extremely large observations that obscured measures of central tendency. Due to the large skew, we decided against using an independent t-test to compare the two groups as this test assumes a normally distributed population and a log-normal transformation did not su<sup>fi</sup>ciently correct skewness.

We conducted a Wilcoxon's rank-sum test (also known as Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney test), which is a non-parametric analog to the independent t-test. Non-parametric statistical procedures are useful in cases where “serious doubt exists about the assumptions that underlie standard methodology” (Wackerly, Mendenhall, & Schea<sup>f</sup>er, 2008: 742). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test has greater power compared to the t-test for moderate to large samples with heavy-tailed and skewed distributions (Higgins, 2004). We used the R programming language (R Development Core Team, 2016) to perform the Wilcoxon rank-sum test using the wilcox.test function in the stats package (version 3.4.0).

“[W]e contribute a lot less than what we develop because it's not – most often than not, it's not worth the time. With the amount of time required to make things accepted or it's usually not worth the time. We only do that if we – oh, not ‘only’ – but mostly we spend time in big features because we need things to run in other grids.”

No support was found for H2c concerning opinionatedness $( \mathbf { z } = 1 . 4 6 , p = . 0 7 3 )$ . However, opinionatedness emerged as a strong theme within the interview data. In particular, actions to protect intellectual property through patenting evoke strong opinions across the entire community. Some entrepreneurs oppose patenting and a protection strategy while others defend it. Our analysis suggests that entrepreneurs may play an instrumental role in shaping the discussion topics but, once introduced, the community in general engage in a dialogue that evokes strong opinions. As one entrepreneur mentions,

“I should confess my position is very anti-software patents ... a couple of us hold this view ... [name of a non-entrepreneur] in particular holds this view – of really disliking this kinds of patents and stu<sup>f</sup> kind of happening ... not just developers, community members as well.”

Ultimately, the concern focuses on the contradiction between entrepreneurs' commercial goals and OSS philosophy about sharing and openness. However, the more widespread opinion is not about the presence of commercially-minded members; rather the concern is that such motivation will encourage free riding and result in opportunism and a perception of non-entrepreneur exploitation. “[B]ased on the fact that OpenSim is a community ... a lot of people get upset that people take and don't give back.” When combined with the quantitative analysis, it appears entrepreneurs within OpenSimulator note this concern and engage in more code contributions than non-entrepreneurs to mitigate the above.

For cognitive capital, all three hypotheses were supported. Entrepreneurs had signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly higher degree centrality $( \mathbf { z } = 1 . 7 1$ $\begin{array} { r } { p = . 0 4 4 ) } \end{array}$ and betweenness centrality $( \mathbf { z } = 1 . 7 1 , p = . 0 4 4 )$ , providing evidence that entrepreneurs build their cognitive capital to a higher degree than non-entrepreneurs. We also found qualitative support that entrepreneurs get involved in the infrastructure and core framework, working on a wide variety of code modules more than non-entrepreneurs.

Due to their reliance on the software as a strategic asset within their entrepreneurial venture. entrepreneurs use their cognitive capital to make infrastructure and core framework contributions to ensure the viability of the software as a commercial product. As one entrepreneur explains. “I mgintain all servers. backups – just about all of the technical stuff for the grid – vou also mentioned like. just recently been inducted into core developer team”. Both entrepreneurs and non-entrepreneurs mention the dependence that the community has on core developers, where entrepreneurs are over-represented, and that entrepreneurs take a key development role in the OpenSimulator community.

Lastly. the quantitative analysis shows that entrepreneurs had a significantly higher kudo rank $( \mathbf { z } = 1 . 7 6 , p = . 0 3 9 )$ than nonentrepreneurs. Our qualitative analysis also supports that entrepreneurs build their reputation within the community to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the development of the software's trajectory. Contributions give the OSS community legitimacy and credibility to attract further talent and establish the software as a legitimate competitor in the virtual world software industry. As one non-entrepreneur expresses the appreciation towards contributions made to the community by other entrepreneurs:

[Name of a commercial platform], they have contributed a lot a company like that, it kind of puts a professional kind of face on the whole thing – it kind of validates the software.”

Contributions from entrepreneurs were also perceived as helpful in ensuring the maturity of the codebase and its progression towards a stable operating stance. The perception is that entrepreneurs “enforce[s] a discipline of actually having to have things work and actually have some bugs fixed which you don't have if you're all just hobbyists.”

Thus, cognitive capital is used by entrepreneurs to safeguard their strategic use of the OSS community through in<sup>fl</sup>uencing both the development and the trajectory of the software. This enables entrepreneurs to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll both their own commercial interests and the OSS community's philosophy for sharing intellectual property.

## 6. Discussion

The purpose of this study was to investigate how entrepreneurs di<sup>f</sup>erentiate themselves from non-entrepreneurs in how they build their social capital within an OSS community. The results of the present study contribute to two main areas of literature: OSS communities and entrepreneurship. In terms of OSS communities, while previous research has found that entrepreneurs and small <sup>fi</sup>rms through their employees can bene<sup>fi</sup>t from participation in OSS communities (e.g., Gruber & Henkel, 2006; Piva et al., 2012), our study moves beyond these studies by clearly delineating between entrepreneurs and non-entrepreneurs and illustrating how entrepreneurs di<sup>f</sup>er from non-entrepreneurs in their building of social capital within an OSS community. Contrary to our expectations, we did not <sup>fi</sup>nd quantitative evidence that entrepreneurs are more likely than non-entrepreneurs to build structural capital and leverage a central position within the discussion network nor bridge different parts of the network. However. our qualitative analysis provides a more nuanced understanding, suggesting that entrepreneurs are selective in their structural capital by strategicall communicating to maximize the advantages from engaging in the mailing lists.

From a relational capital perspective, our quantitative results show that entrepreneurs display a high degree of welcomingness to community newcomers. This ambassadorial role creates opportunities to develop relationships with new developers, thereby gaining awareness of their expertise as well as in<sup>fl</sup>uencing norms and trust in the community. By cultivating relationships, entrepreneurs can receive help with code testing and debugging and in<sup>fl</sup>uence the development agenda of the community.

Contrary to expectations, our results suggest entrepreneurs are not more opinionated in the mailing list than non-entrepreneurs. However, our qualitative analysis found both entrepreneurs and non-entrepreneurs both display opinionatedness but on certain topics. For example, through the mailing list, entrepreneurs are made aware that other members hold strong views about their commercial activities, especially when they are not seen as su<sup>fi</sup>ciently giving back to the community. It thus appears that the topics that trigger these strong opinions are di<sup>f</sup>erent for the two groups. Furthermore, our quantitative and qualitative results reveal that entrepreneurs are more likely to foster their relational capital than non-entrepreneurs by assuming responsibilities in relation to direct code development (e.g., code contribution, bug <sup>fi</sup>xes). We believe this helps entrepreneurs achieve good relations with other community members, which can mobilize resources and get the community to commit to their strategic needs. Thus, while such e<sup>f</sup>orts may not always be worth the e<sup>f</sup>ort, they can bring goodwill and concrete business bene<sup>fi</sup>ts in the future.

Lastly, our cognitive capital results show that entrepreneurs play a more central role within the structure of the code, working to connect more disconnected or loosely connected parts of the code base, and make more highly valued code contributions to the community than non-entrepreneurs. Our qualitative analysis reveals several means for entrepreneurs to leverage their cognitive capital. Collaborating with others on the central part of the code ensures that the software core remains robust and stable, while helping others with their di<sup>f</sup>erent projects is a way for entrepreneurs to gain customers as well as in<sup>fl</sup>uence the software's trajectory. Additionally, helping the formation of shared language, particularly through documentation writing and translating diverse forms of bug reporting into requirements that developers easily can understand makes the software development move faster.

Our findings also contribute to the entrepreneurship literature by extending the open innovation literature to the individual level of the entrepreneur. While previous research has found that small <sup>fi</sup>rms leverage OSS communities as external, complementary resources in an open innovation approach (Dahlander, 2007), our research illustrates how entrepreneurs can also capture new knowledge and co-create value with outside actors (Chesbrough. 2003. 2006a. 2006b: Huizingh. 2011: West & Bogers. 2014). To do so, our research suggests that entrepreneurs leverage their social capital within an OSS community in order to mitigate their liabilities of smallness and newness. They overcome their liability of smallness by accessing and leveraging the community's intellectual capital, or the knowledge and knowing of the community (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998), as the key resource upon which to build and support their entrepreneurial venture. Furthermore, through their strategic actions developing the community's software, they are able to overcome their liability of newness since their commitment to the community and its software enables them to build their reputation and gain business leads and recommendations through a central position in the network of active developers.

## 7. Practitioner implications

Entrepreneurs are faced with the liabilities of newness and smallness that may hamper their ability to successfully develop their venture. While OSS projects provide additional challenges to their success, our results con<sup>fi</sup>rm that entrepreneurs may leverage an OSS community's resources to overcome these challenges and their liabilities. Entrepreneurs need <sup>fi</sup>rst to understand that building their social capital within the community is a critical step in this process. However, they should do so in a strategic manner. Building and maintaining relationships within a community, as in any social situation, is a time-consuming process. Thus, entrepreneurs should spend their valuable time building the right relationships, not just more relationships within the community.

Our results indicate that entrepreneurs should be strategic in their sharing of intellectual property with the community. Through their structural position between the market and core developers as well as between the bug reporters and developers, entrepreneurs can gain access to valuable industry knowledge. This enables them to better understand how they can potentially add the most value in the market and thereby carve out their own niche. Selectively revealing intellectual property then that is not at the core of their product or service serves to assure the community that they are aligned with the community's OSS open and sharing philosophy. Finally, it should be noted that OSS communities are continuously evolving social networks, with individuals coming and going, thus entrepreneurs should continuously reevaluate and potentially refocus their social capital building strategies, keeping in mind that al actions conducted within the OSS community are digitally traceable and can have a lasting in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the entrepreneur's socia capital within the community.

It should also be noted that our <sup>fi</sup>ndings have relevance for entrepreneurs in settings beyond that of OSS communities, especially as open source as a philosophy spreads to other industries such as open hardware and open data. Entrepreneurs should take note that they can leverage these openly available resources to build their ventures, but that they should take care in understanding the dynamics of the community <sup>fi</sup>rst such that they may best develop their social capital strategy to successfully leverage the resources available. This approach may be especially successful in commercial areas involving fast-paced technologies, $\mathrm { e . g . , }$ arti<sup>fi</sup>cial intelligence, blockchain, as it enables entrepreneurs to not only successfully position themselves in an emerging industry but also to potentially in<sup>fl</sup>uence areas of the industry's development especially as many emerging areas require a high level of technical skills and the underlying software or products may be subject to development bugs and errors.

Finally, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings have implications for organizations interested in building <sup>fi</sup>rm-sponsored OSS communities, a trend that has been growing in recent years, e.g., Teigland et al. (2014). Managers of these <sup>fi</sup>rms should understand that entrepreneurs are a critical component in the development and sustainability of OSS communities and work to attract such individuals. As such, managers of these <sup>fi</sup>rms should enable entrepreneurs to build their social capital within the community, such as providing relevant forums for communication between entrepreneurs and users and developers, software development kits or training to enable entrepreneurs to make valuable developments to the software, and ranking systems to enable them to build their legitimacy and overcome situations of resource scarcity.

## 8. Conclusion

The results of this study are limited in that we investigated a single OSS community, OpenSimulator. Thus, the generalizability of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings should be taken with some caution. Future research should replicate this study in other OSS communities. For instance, OpenSimulator community utilizes a BSD license, a type of intellectual property license that is business friendly and more conducive to entrepreneurial opportunities compared to strong-copyleft licenses that restrict derivative works building on the source code. Since derivative works can never be re-licensed under a GPL/Copyleft license, individuals involved in communities with such licenses might shy away from investing resources in the community, leading to di<sup>f</sup>erent behavior then what found in this study. Furthermore, as noted above, some OSS communities are <sup>fi</sup>rm-sponsored and as a result may have di<sup>f</sup>erent characteristics and dynamics than purely emergent OSS communities. Future research exploring how entrepreneurs embed themselves within strong-copyleft licensed or <sup>fi</sup>rm-sponsored OSS communities may produce contradictory <sup>fi</sup>ndings due to the potential restrictiveness of such communities.

Furthermore, we have made certain choices in our operationalization of the social capital dimensions. For example, we chose to only look at out-degree centrality and betweenness centrality due to the structure of the community's mailing list for structural capital. Other centrality measures such as in-degree or eigenvector might be more relevant in other settings. Additionally, there may be other means to operationalize relational capital (e.g., welcomingness), and cognitive capital (e.g., kudo rank), that may be more relevant in other communities. Future research could further explore not only additional measures of the dimensions of social capital but also the relationships among them.

In conclusion, our study provides one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst in depth accounts of entrepreneur behavior in an OSS community by investigating how entrepreneurs build and leverage their social capital within the community. We show that entrepreneurs di<sup>f</sup>erentiate themselves from non-entrepreneurs by focusing on cognitive and relational capital building activities, which in return makes it possible for them to leverage their social capital to in<sup>fl</sup>uence and shape the environment in which they are operating. Given the liabilities of newness and smallness as well as other challenges faced by entrepreneurs, applying an open innovation strategy in OSS communities could be one approach where entrepreneurs, by developing and freely revealing their intellectual property to the community, share their way to success via OSS-infused entrepreneurial business ventures
