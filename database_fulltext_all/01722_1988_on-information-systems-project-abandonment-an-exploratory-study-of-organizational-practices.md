# On Information Systems Project Abandonment: An Exploratory Study of Organizational Practices

By: Kweku Ewusi-Mensah Zbigniew H. Przasnyski

College of Business Administration
Loyola Marymount University
Loyola Blvd. at West 80th Street
Los Angeles, California 90045

## Abstract

Information systems failure is a widely recognized problem in the IS community. However, abandonment of IS projects is an aspect of IS failure that has not gained much attention in either IS practice or research. This article examines the organizational practices resulting in the underlying characteristics of IS project abandonment. The results of a survey show IS project abandonment to be a complex multidimensional issue defying easy explanations. IS projects may be abandoned for any combination of factors including cost overruns and/or schedule slippages, technological inadequacies, and behavioral, political, or organizational issues. The last set of factors emerged as being the most dominant in most companies' decisions.

Keywords: Information systems failure or abandonment, information systems development, technology and organizational behavioral/political issues

ACM Categories: D.2.9, H.0, H.1.m, K.6.0, K.6.1, K.6.3

## Introduction

Evaluating information systems projects is increasingly becoming a major concern with senior management in most organizations. This concern is a consequence of the escalating development costs of information systems projects and their great potential for affecting the strategic, as well as the competitive posture of the firm. Another increasing concern of management is the growing backlog of IS projects waiting to be tackled (Carroll, 1988); and still another is the significant amount of all project development undertaken that is never completed or if completed, never used (Gladden, 1982). The conceptual nature of IS project work, the inherent risks associated with their successful completion, their varied requirements, and the need for proper effective management or oversight may be contributing factors to project abandonment.

McFarlan (1981) presents examples of attempts to develop information systems that resulted in failure in three different large companies. He claims to have data indicating such failures are quite widespread in organizations of all sizes worldwide including Fortune 500 companies. He partly attributes these IS project failures to lack of assessment of individual project risks and the ripple effects such risks can have on a portfolio of projects. Glaser (1984) reports, “In the computer industry . . . [project] objectives are missed and schedule and cost targets overrun with distressing regularity and, at times with equally distressing results” (p.45). Lyytinen and Hirschheim (1987) also confirm that IS failures continue in organizations despite the enormous progress and strides made in the development, implementation, and usage of IS.

There are many similarities between IS project failure and IS project abandonment. The former may be the consequence of failing or dwindling expectations of the implemented system, and the latter is concerned with the anticipated or perceived failure of the project prior to its full implementation. IS project abandonment may therefore be characterized as an endemic part of the larger organizational issue of IS failure. While IS failure has been the focus of many articles in the IS discipline (Forman and Hess, 1988; Keider, 1984; Lucas, 1975; Lyytinen, 1988; Lyytinen and Hirschheim, 1987), the IS abandonment phenomenon has been singularly neglected.

The purpose of this study is to help shed some light on the practice of IS project abandonment within organizations. What factors contribute to decisions of project abandonment within organizations? How are such decisions undertaken? What characteristics do abandoned projects have in common? What benefits, if any, do organizations derive from past decisions to abandon projects? What guidelines can be offered to management to aid in project selection and project management to minimize this practice and to help make the IS budget allocations more productive?

## IS Abandonment

IS abandonment occurs when management decides, for whatever reasons, to discontinue temporarily or retire permanently a project under development or a system currently in operation. There are, in essence, two categories of IS abandonment:

\- Abandonment of projects under development, i.e., project abandonment, and

\- Abandonment of existing systems in operation, i.e., system abandonment.

The former type of abandonment is the focus of the work reported in this article, IS project abandonment is therefore a microcosm of the larger IS failure problems reported in the literature (Lyytinen and Hirschheim, 1987).

Meredith (1988) observes an increasing number of articles on how to manage projects, but very few concerning the identification of failing projects with a view to their termination or abandonment. He notes, however, that most of what has been written (e.g., Balanchadra and Raelin, 1980) has been limited to research and development projects.

Several authors have identified a number of project management issues that lead to project failure: staffing, managerial, and communicational aspects of project management are consistently cited as critical reasons (e.g., Might and Fischer, 1985; Pinto and Slevin, 1987; Slevin and Pinto, 1986; Viskovich, 1988). Some of these issues are investigated in White (1984), who suggests that project failures are rooted in the way participants interact with each other and perceive work-related issues. Dos Santos and Hawk (1988)

demonstrate that analysts' attitudes toward IS development vary and therefore hypothesize that the assignment of analysts to projects could affect project success or failure. Some preliminary evidence was obtained by Keider (1984), who questioned 100 MIS professionals for the single reason that caused most project failures. He concluded that "although some projects fail because of technology or design problems, [the main reasons] for project failure indicate a lack of understanding of project management" (p.38).

The concept of “expectation failure” advanced by Lyytinen and Hirschheim (1987) can be used to characterize IS project abandonment as the perceived inability of the IS project to meet the requirements or expectations of various groups or combinations of organizational stakeholders. The stakeholders, as far as IS project development is concerned, are those claimants within the organization who have a vested interest in the project and its successful completion or termination, to paraphrase Mason and Mitroff (1981). These stakeholders are (a) management with financial control of the project, who make long term decisions affecting the future of the organization, (b) the IS personnel who are in charge of the technical aspects of the project, and (c) the user community who may or may not be favorably predisposed to the project. IS project abandonment is therefore likely to occur whenever any of the above groups of stakeholders perceive major potential problems in the project’s development that may lead to its subsequent failure.

Thus, IS project abandonment can occur when problems arise in perceiving, analyzing, designing, or configuring the system objectives; when the technological basis for the system and its behavioral, political, or organizational issues directly or indirectly affect ways to bring the project to a successful completion within the estimated budget and schedule constraints; and/or when organization environment factors combine to reduce the project's expected benefits or increase its expected costs (e.g., Keider, 1984; Lyytinen, 1988; Lyytinen and Hirshheim, 1987).

In general, project abandonment in itself may be a good and acceptable management practice because it may prevent further investment of scarce organizational resources in a non-productive venture (Staw and Ross, 1987). Good

IS project management will dictate cancellation of projects deemed infeasible irrespective of the means used to arrive at that decision. However, the frequency and the widespread nature at which projects are abandoned in organizations (see McFarlan, 1981) may be an indication of a systemic industry-wide problem of failure to deal swiftly and appropriately with the underlying causes—in project selection and/or management—which lead to the fateful decision. This is the problem we are concerned with in this work. We recognize, however, that project abandonment may be a continuing practice or experience organizations may go through from time to time, partly as a consequence of human shortcomings and/or technological inadequacies. However, our hope is that the underlying causes may be less an issue of failure in selection, approval, and management than on factors clearly outside the scope or control of management, such as in cases of mergers or acquisitions.

Three types of project abandonment are distinguished in this article, namely:

1. Total abandonment—the complete termination of all activity on the project prior to full implementation;

2. Substantial abandonment—a major truncation or simplification of the project to make it radically different from the original specification prior to full implementation; and

3. Partial abandonment—where the original scope of the project is reduced without entailing major or significant changes to the project's original specifications, prior to full implementation.

While all three types of project abandonment occur within organizations, our data suggest that the most prevalent is the total abandonment case.

The rest of this article is organized as follows: the next section describes the research procedures and provides a profile of the organizations of the research subjects. Then the results are presented and abandoned projects are profiled. Following that, the factors that contribute to the abandonment decisions are addressed. The final section tries to synthesize our understanding of this complex IS issue and provides some guidelines we hope may be useful to management in addressing the issue and its effects within their organizations.

## Description of the Study

## Research objectives and subjects

This study is exploratory in nature and is intended to illuminate the practice of project abandonment in organizations. The main focus of this research is twofold: first, to describe the nature of IS project abandonment within various enterprises and second, to identify the factors that contribute to project abandonment.

In order to assess the nature of the practice and its impact on organizations, the research subjects were top IS executives from the Directory of Top Computer Executives (Applied Computer Research, 1987). These executives were expected to be well-informed about project abandonment decisions within their organizations. The data collection was confined to organizations in Los Angeles and Orange Counties. The large and varied business base of these two counties made it suitable for an exploratory study of this kind because these counties provided an adequate sample-size population base.

It has been our experience that it is difficult to obtain data, in particular candid data, on abandoned projects even with the assurances of anonymity provided to the subjects. We recognize, therefore, (1) the possibility of biased responses from some of these subjects, especially if they feared that the reasons for project abandonment might reflect negatively on them and/or their IS organization, and (2) the problem of non-response bias. Given the exploratory nature of the study, we felt that our pool of subjects was indeed the most appropriate source for this kind of data on abandoned IS projects because there is no other readily identifiable group of personnel within an enterprise who have the knowledge to provide the data needed.

## Research method

A questionnaire was used to collect data to meet the research objectives. The questionnaire was refined through several iterations with selected individuals in both industry and academia. It was field-tested on a group of graduate students enrolled in a course on large-scale software development at the Claremont Graduate School.

All these students were full-time employees of companies within Los Angeles and Orange Counties. Most of them had first-hand experience of IS project abandonment within their companies.

The questionnaire had six sections and was quite detailed (11 pages), requiring at least 30 minutes to complete. The first section developed a profile of the company and its IS department. The second section provided a profile of a “typical” abandoned IS project. Sections three, four, and five assessed the extent to which time and/or budget, IS technology, and organizational issues, respectively, contributed to the decision to abandon the project. The sixth section dealt with postmortems on abandoned projects; in the interests of space, this issue will not be discussed here.

Five hundred sixty-six (566) questionnaires were sent out to the IS executives. A total of 56 completed and/or partially completed questionnaires were returned and of that total, 49 were considered useable responses. This represents a useable response rate of approximately 8.7 percent. Of these 49 usable responses, almost 50 percent (23) reported incidences of abandoned IS projects within their organizations.

Several organizations did not fill out the questionnaire (even though they may have had projects abandoned in the past) but wrote back citing security and/or confidentiality reasons for their non-cooperation in the study. It was not possible to pursue further the issue of their refusal to participate in the study because the questionnaire had no identifying characteristics, thus eliminating the possibility of tracking individual organizations. Consequently, in spite of a totally anonymous questionnaire, we formed the impression that some companies did not respond because they felt constrained, perhaps for public relations reasons, in admitting to such experience within their organizations. Or they simply did not feel comfortable filling out an 11-page questionnaire probing an issue that might be construed as reflecting “negatively” on them or their organization. This inference is based on informal conversations with some industry executives who expressed such sentiments as possible reasons for their colleagues’ refusal to participate in the study. Judging from the evidence cited by Gladden (1982) and Boddie (1987), we are inclined to accept the above explanation for what many IS professionals consider experience they would rather forget.

The results and discussions throughout the remainder of the paper relate only to the 23 responding organizations who reported abandoned IS projects. All 23 of the respondents indicated they were one of the key decision makers with IS responsibility and hence, would be in a position to know about major decisions involving the abandonment of a project.

## Profile of organizations that reported abandoned projects

Each respondent was asked to indicate the industry classification of their organization. The industry classification used for this study was also obtained from the Directory of Top Computer Executives. Table 1 shows the response rate of organizations, by industry classification, with abandoned projects.

Given the size of the sample, we judged the classification and the percentage of responses from the various industries to be fairly representative of the overall industry population. The reader, however, should be cautioned against interpreting the results too broadly given the relatively small number of responses received from the various industries. At this exploratory level it would be premature to view these results as indicative of the general incidence of project abandonment within the different industries.

Annual revenues reported by the respondents for their companies over the five-year period (1982-1986) ranged from \$10–\$100 million for two companies to over \$500 million for seven companies. The IS budgets over the same five-year period (1982-1986) ranged from \$1–\$5 million for three companies to over \$10 million for eight companies.

The number of full-time equivalent people working in IS ranged from one to 10 for one company to more than 100 for 11 companies. Eleven (the majority) of the respondents characterized their IS departments as stable, 8 described their IS departments as growing, and 4 indicated their IS departments were decreasing in size.

## Factors Contributing to IS Project Abandonment

Previous research has indicated that IS project schedule slippage and/or cost overruns have often been the cause of project failures (Brooks, 1975; Carroll, 1988; Glaser, 1984; Lyytinen and Hirschheim, 1987). Even systems that are considered technically sound run a high risk of failure when the behavioral, political, and other organizational concerns are overlooked (Ahituv and Neumann, 1990; Ewusi-Mensah, 1989; Lyytinen and Hirschheim, 1987; Turner, 1982). In addition, the technological base on which most IS projects are built is always advancing at a rate that often contributes to the obsolescence of the design requirements once the design is frozen (Brooks, 1975). It is therefore possible that, in some cases, before a project is completed and made operational, new emergent technology may become available. The technology may satisfy the needs of the organization, perhaps with some compromise, and thus may cause the original project to be discontinued or abandoned.

IS project abandonment may occur as a consequence of the changing dynamics of the organization and its IS environment, the changing perceptions or expectations of some stakeholders as to the project's value to the organization, and new technology on the market. All or any combination of these factors may adversely affect the successful completion of a project. For instance, changes in project requirements may lead to revisions of schedule as well as the associated costs in bringing the project to completion—if these changes are not sufficiently controlled and managed this may lead to the demise of the project. Also, a well-accepted axiom in IS literature and practice is that senior management support is essential to MIS success;

so for a project where such support is suddenly withdrawn, as a consequence of, for example, organizational structural changes, it is conceivable that the project may eventually be terminated if new management does not share the perceived benefits of the earlier management and/or the expected costs are considered to exceed the anticipated benefits.

Accordingly, this part of the questionnaire was divided into three sections where participants were asked to indicate their level of agreement or disagreement on a five-point scale (5 indicating strong agreement, 1 indicating strong disagreement). Included are various statements pertaining to the role of the three factors—economic, technological, and organizational—in the practice of IS project abandonment. The following sections discuss the results of the survey and describe the reasons for project abandonment.

## Economic factors in IS project abandonment

There are two main issues of economic importance in dealing with project abandonment: the cost of the project and the time needed to complete it. The overall cost of the project is usually estimated along with how long it may take to bring the project to completion. Therefore, any upward revisions of the cost and/or schedule estimates may adversely affect the perceptions or expectations of management with respect to the project. This section of the study attempts to increase our understanding of the extent to which cost overruns and schedule slippage contribute to project abandonment decisions.

## Technological factors in IS project abandonment

The second major concomitant factor in project abandonment was conjectured to be technological. The rate of advancement of information technology is such that often it may be the major cause for a project being abandoned because new technology frequently comes on the market and renders obsolete the requirements for a project under development (Brooks, 1975). A summary of the results is given in Table 9.

The responses indicate that technological factors are not important considerations. The highest average response was for statement C.1, but even this only represented a 30 percent strong agreement. What emerges from the results is that in most, if not all, of the projects the IS technical environment existing in the companies was more than adequate to ensure the successful completion of the projects. These findings are consistent with Lyytinen (1988), who found that analysts view methods (i.e., IS techniques and tools) as not being the major reason for IS failure.

## Organizational factors in IS project abandonment

The third factor conjectured to contribute to project abandonment concerned issues arising from the organizational environment of the project. As indicated earlier, there are various claimants, that is, stakeholders, within the organization who may have a vested interest in seeing the project to its successful completion. Three groups of stakeholders are identified:

1. Company management who are the sponsors of the project,

2. End-user community who may be users of the system after its completion, and

3. IS professionals responsible for development and implementation of the system.

In our view, abandonment of the project may occur when a breach is determined to exist between the current project status and the desired or expected project status. Such a determination can be made by any of the three stakeholder groups. As Lyytinen and Hirschheim (1987) point out, the stakeholder classification identified above is quite coarse; however, we felt this grouping adequate for our purpose in that “it conveys the role prescriptions associated with the design of an IS” (p. 262). In addition, our interest in this work is centered on identifying anomalies between the intended or expected project outcome and the actual observable situation as perceived by the various stakeholders’ groups. ## Corporate Management

Table 10 provides a summary of issues that may contribute to the abandonment decision from a management perspective. An overwhelming percentage of the respondents (87 percent) indicated that senior management in their companies were committed at the start of the project to providing the resources needed to implement the project (statement D.1). This is confirmed by the assessment that senior management viewed the project as important to their organization's long-term goals (statement D.2). Despite this, more than half of the respondents indicated that organizational politics and/or disagreements associated with the project contributed to its subsequent abandonment (statement D.3).

Changes in management were also blamed (statements D.16, D.7, D.14). Respondents were divided on the issue of whether organizational structure changes had reduced the perceived benefits of the project to the organization (statement D.15): 39 percent were in agreement and 47 percent against. Finally, an overwhelming majority of the respondents (95 percent) indicated that mergers or acquisitions did not contribute to abandonment decisions (statement D.19). This figure, however, may be potentially misleading, in particular if the respondents' organizations were not subject to mergers or acquisitions. Overall, these findings corroborate Turner's (1982) assessment that IS development failures "can be [partly] attributed to behavioral, political or organizational issues rather than to technical characteristics . . ." (p.207).

## IS Professionals

The third issue examined was the role of IS professionals in decisions pertaining to abandonment of projects. The IS professional is the only stakeholder group technically responsible for the development of projects. Thus, the expectations of this group have direct bearing on the technological issues associated with the project's completion. Projects can be abandoned if the development team is unable to meet the technical specifications set forth in the requirements documents. The statements dealing with this group focused on their unique role, from a technical point of view, in successfully completing the project. The results are summarized in Table 12. The statements regarding the role of IS personnel in project abandonment were rather negative, with the possible exception of statement D.11 (17 percent in agreement). There was a low 13 percent agreement to issues such as project staff turnover and loss of key project personnel (statements D.12 and D.13 respectively). These are possible reasons for the project's demise. An overwhelming majority (88 percent) of the respondents rejected disagreements within the project group (statement D.17) as a factor in the project's demise. Only 22 percent of the respondents indicated that the project was the first one of its kind or size attempted by IS/user departments (statement D.20). This led us to surmise that in most of the abandonment cases reported the role of IS professionals in contributing to a project's demise was minimal.

It is apparent from the results of the survey that in most abandonment cases the issue of cost and/or schedule overruns, although a consideration in some cases, is not a major contributor. Similarly, technology did not significantly contribute to the problem of project abandonment; the IS technical environments existing in most of the companies were judged adequate for successful project development; emergent technology was not an issue.

Regarding organizational factors, the consensus is that organizational politics and/or disagreements can be blamed for a significant part of the abandonment dilemma faced by a majority of organizations. Though end users, and to a lesser extent IS professionals, may account for some of the problem, their role is not nearly as dominant as the issues associated with management and organizational politics.

Additionally, in a preliminary investigation respondents were asked to rank, in order of importance, some of the above issues relative to each other. Only 13 percent of the respondents stated that organizational issues, did not influence the abandonment decision, as opposed to 52 percent and 56 percent for economic and technological issues, respectively (multiple responses were permitted). By using this type of ranking, respondents had the opportunity to add other reasons they considered responsible for project abandonment (see the Appendix). These reasons once again appear to confirm that organizational factors were dominant, but not to the complete exclusion of economic and technological considerations.

The results discussed above are preliminary and should be accepted with some reservations because of the small number of respondents who were prepared to share their experiences of IS project abandonment; therefore any generalizations based on the results should be made with caution given the exploratory nature of the study. However, although the sample of respondents is small, the diversity of organizations who actually responded to the survey lends some validity to the results. The pattern that emerges from a synthesis of the results is quite clear: project abandonment is a multidimensional or multifaceted issue with different interacting parts. As to which of the several factors potentially capable of contributing to the abandonment problem may dominate depends to a great extent on the particular organizational and IS environments. Further work is needed in order to identify factors with a potential to dominate particular organizational and IS environments leading to subsequent project abandonment decisions.

Given the above caveats, the following preliminary recommendations concerning IS project selection and management are tentatively suggested. Because organizational politics and/or disagreements seem to dominate decisions of IS project abandonment, it may be prudent on management's part to pay particular attention to the organizational behavioral issues involved in the project approval and development processes. Perhaps projects that seem to enjoy support from other managers or are more consensus-based should, all other things being equal, be viewed more favorably than non-consensus-based projects. This is not to advocate that projects associated with one manager or sponsor should summarily be rejected or treated lightly in the project approval or development processes. We think, however, that the individual sponsor of such projects should try to sell the merits of the project to other senior colleagues in other organizational units as well as to subordinates within the target organizational unit. Such an approach, we believe, will ultimately help to build a supportive base for the merits of the project within the organization. And so, in the event of the sponsor's departure, the project may not risk abandonment because of lack of internal organizational support. What form should such supportive action take?

For projects involving significant expenditures and resources where subsequent abandonment may prove to be too costly, it should be required that the individual sponsor show or demonstrate the strategic value of the project to both the target organizational unit and the organization at large. This action should serve to elucidate what the organization stands to benefit as a consequence of going ahead with the project and, in the same vein, what the organization stands to lose if the project is denied approval in the review process. The sponsor should also, where appropriate, discuss the competitive merits of the project. This indicates to his or her colleagues and other managers why the project's implementation may help improve the organizational unit's productivity by helping to make them more effective and efficient in the organization's decision-making and operational activities.

Once the project in question has been approved and funds and other resources have been released for its development and implementation, the next issue should now focus on management of the project. Project management should be thoroughly reviewed on a regular basis. Progress on the project should be closely monitored on a periodic basis to identify any potential problems which, if not swiftly and properly dealt with, might portend major trouble ahead. Failure to tackle the project management issue adequately might, in some instances, significantly contribute to subsequent abandonment of the project. Alternatively, proper and careful project management might detect potential problems with cost overruns, schedule slippage, technical difficulties, and personnel-related issues such as excessive turnovers or changes in project leadership. Early detection of such potential problems may lead management to take corrective action more quickly to forestall the project's demise, or when necessary, its early termination.

## Conclusion

We have tried in this study to identify and explore the nature and practice of IS project abandonment within various types of organizations. It was found that all types of IS projects are susceptible to being abandoned—not necessarily just high risk, complex, or unstructured ones. The major characteristics of the practice are rooted in organizational behavioral/political issues and, to a lesser extent, in economic and technical issues. This is consistent with earlier observations in the IS project management literature, e.g., Keider (1984). These preliminary results serve to confirm the existence of the practice as identified by McFarlan (1981) and have shed some light on our understanding of its importance, given the large sums of money involved in IS projects work. It is apparent that IS project abandonment is indeed a complex, multidimensional issue that defies simple explanations. Further work is needed to increase our understanding and knowledge of this practice as a facet of the general problem of IS failure. Until that happens, it is quite realistic to expect Turner's (1982) assertion that the risk of IS failure (referring to IS project abandonment) will continue to be high in organizations if management fails to adequately consider behavioral and/or political factors or because signs of resistance to the technical design are not properly handled to major stakeholder groups' satisfaction.

Future work should focus on the extent of the practice of project abandonment in organizations, in particular Fortune 500 firms, and should attempt to measure how each of the identified factors contribute to project abandonment. On the issue of organizational factors, the specific role and contribution of each of the three stakeholder groups should be identified in order to determine the dominant stakeholder group in IS project abandonment decisions. This information, we believe, will provide us with valuable insights in dealing with the organizational behavioral and/or political issues raised by Turner (1982), which we have detected in this study as significant for successful project implementation. Overall, we believe a systems approach to the IS project abandonment issue will be quite appropriate, because it will serve to “recognize the partiality and incompleteness of our understanding” of the abandonment dilemma in all sorts of organizations (Lyytinen and Hirschheim, 1987).

Finally, what lessons are learned by companies from abandoned projects should be thoroughly investigated as a related issue: Boddie (1987) and Roman (1983) suggest that project postmortems are rare and make the point that the study of past mistakes is the best safeguard against future ones. Our subsequent research will address this issue.
