## Abstract

Within information systems, the question of evaluation remains as a major contentious issue, and perhaps even more so in the field of health informatics where the traditions of medicine meet and mingle with the information systems field. A review of the literature in these areas indicates that there is little agreement on the essential role of evaluation, a ‘best way’ to evaluate, on what and how to evaluate, whom to involve and within what paradigm to proceed. Reflecting on discussion within both traditions, this paper develops an approach to evaluation broadly based on critical theory, and argues that such an approach, while not offering a solution to all the problems of evaluation, does bring into focus fundamental questions relating to evaluation process and content. To illustrate this, the paper reflects on the strengths and weaknesses of an evaluation of a primary care intranet, and suggests how a critical approach might lead to a more meaningful evaluation and provide more significant and useful findings.

## Introduction

Evaluation is a central concept in the field of information systems; new technologies are identified by organisations and assessed, they are shaped into systems to serve their needs, and these systems are then put in place and expected to perform. But do they, to what extent, and with what consequences? To answer such questions requires some evaluative activity, but as is broadly acknowledged, this is a complex undertaking in which any judgement of an information system’s potential (ex ante) or of outcomes (ex post) depends on various frequently interrelated but incommensurable factors, and any information system (IS) innovation will have diverse implications for different stakeholders and thus be judged by them differently. This means that, in practice, it is not clear how to measure the success or benefits of a system, or even what ‘success’ really means, or for whom. Indeed, as we argue here, it is not enough to just focus on ‘success’ (and by implication failure) of an IS; but also to consider the more profound implications any system may have on the way working practices are organised, daily lives are lived and society is (re)-constructed. Thus, the aim of this paper is to develop a distinct and different perspective on evaluation of information and communication technology (ICT)-based systems and services, and to propose a new evaluation programme based on critical theory principles. We argue that such a critically informed evaluation, far from being narrowly ‘academic or utopian, suitable only in limited contexts, can yield important insights for all IS researchers and practitioners as well as contributing to our understanding of the relationship between technology and society.

The critical evaluation programme developed here is one which can take into account social, political and historical conditions under which a system is constructed and used, including power relations and constraining structures that limit peoples’ understanding and the choices available to them. It acknowledges too that vested interests are active in constraining the evaluation process itself, since evaluation, potentially, redistributes power. Thus, in the role of researcher or evaluator (or both), we need to consider how our activity exposes, expresses, challenges or enforces some interests, while downplaying others, and to question our own alliances and assumptions. Evaluation in these terms will always be problematic and political, but, through reflective engagement with the subject and communicative action among stakeholders, it can lead to a more profound learning and understanding and serve the goal of emancipation.

The paper starts with a discussion of evaluation approaches that emerge from both the IS and health informatics perspectives. It then proposes an approach to evaluation that draws on theoretical positions that originate from social theory, particularly critical theory. Using an example taken from a health sector study, the evaluation of a primary care intranet, the paper illustrates how a critical approach might have helped to conduct a less limited evaluation, reaching beyond immediate concerns of traditionally understood user satisfaction.

## Evaluation: issues and challenges

Approaching any evaluative activity we need to consider the context of evaluation (who is evaluating and why), the process (how) and the content (what) (Symons, 1991). In this way we confront the issues of how to establish criteria for assessing outcomes ex post or ex ante, constructing and operationalizing measures, and finding a ‘point of view’ that any evaluation is to reflect. Addressing these questions quickly becomes confusing, complex and often controversial – see for example, Heathfield et al. (1999) discussing evaluation of an IS in a hospital setting. A further dimension that compounds the situation is when the implementation of technological innovations around ICT is closely intertwined with, or even indistinguishable from, organisational change, particularly when ISs are introduced explicitly to support strategic agendas of organisational reform. For example, in the U.K. health sector, policy has proposed over a number of years and formulations that ICT can serve as a fundamental driver for reform and modernisation of the National Health Service (NHS) (DOH, 1997, 2002). The situation becomes yet more challenging when an organisation, like the NHS, is engaged in a range of complimentary or interdependent change programs at the same time, and the consequences and outcomes of any particular project cannot be isolated from all the other changes taking place. Moreover, new ISs and the organisational change they are bound up with may bring about unintended, but significant, changes that must be at some point captured and assessed, see for example Lau et al.’s (1999) discussion of Orlikowski and Hofman’s improvisational model (1997).

Such problems, of interrelated change programs and unexpected consequences, are particularly evident in infrastructure and similar projects that attempt to deliver enabling resources for local appropriation by diverse work groups. This is increasingly the case in the health domain, but is equally relevant in many other IS application areas, for example in projects that seek to enable knowledge management. We certainly see that many contemporary health ISs are complex and aiming to be both technologically and organisationally innovative, involving a large number of different stakeholders, spanning institutions and professions, and (potentially) changing both the way people and organisations work and the ways in which services are delivered and experienced.

Against this background it is not surprising that evaluation, although discussed extensively in both the IS and health informatics literature, remains controversial and in practice often stumbles into trouble. Many accounts indicate how it can be incomplete, biased or just ‘touching the surface’ (Smithson & Hirschheim, 1998; Farbey et al., 1999). Traditionally, evaluation of ISs has focused on technical aspects of a system, its performance, reliability, robustness and security, cost–benefit, and immediate questions of usability. As ISs have become more pervasive, ambitious, complex and interactive, evaluation emphasis has, to a degree, shifted to concerns with how and to what extent ISs innovations serve ambitions of organisational change. This in turn leads to political, cultural and organisational aspects being seen as necessarily playing a major role in shaping the evaluation activity (Walsham, 1993). Thus, issues of alignment with business goals and institutional interests, understanding of existing work practices in formal and informal senses, and the diverse power bases and competing stakeholder groups and their information needs, have all been given attention in the IS field (Symons, 1991; Smithson & Hirschheim, 1998). Ethical questions, to what extent and in what way a new IS may effect peoples’ legitimate interests, also no longer appear to be irrelevant or simple to accommodate (Cornford & Klecun-Dabrowska, 2001). Finally, we must also note that, in practice, ISs projects are often not evaluated at all, or are not adequately evaluated (Powell, 1992; Anderson & Aydin, 1994; Smithson & Hirschheim, 1998; Willcocks & Lester, 1999; Wyatt & Wyatt, 2003). Evaluation may then be conceived as some corrupted or impotent process, for example as a tactic of legitimisation rather than aiming to elicit real knowledge or support learning (Introna, 1997; Jones & Hughes, 2001).

In response to such critiques the scope and content of evaluative activity, as seen in the IS literature, has begun to change. The application of rigorous ‘scientific’ methods, for example, cost and benefit analysis, or return on investment, have been often questioned, although such questioning has been equally robustly refuted (Farbey et al., 1993, 1999; Smithson & Hirschheim, 1998; Bannister, 1999; Land, 2000; Wilson & Howcroft, 2000). The result has been the emergence of a number of alternative or complimentary methods, including critical success factors, value analysis, and other ‘subjective’, multiobjective, multicriteria methods, for example measuring users’ satisfaction levels. A further dimension is introduced to the discussion if we acknowledge that any scheme of judgement about a system will be defined differently by different stakeholders in an organisation, representing an inevitable bias (Avgerou, 1995), and the assessment of a system will be in particular influenced by various stakeholders’ expectations (O’Keefe, 1989; Remenyi & Money, 1991).

The use of such a stakeholder approach, and the adoption of users’ satisfaction as a key evaluative measure, raises an interesting fundamental question in the hypothesis that there exists a strong relationship between users’ satisfaction and system effectiveness (Srinivasan, 1985; Powell, 1992). For example, Miller (1993) argues that the achievement of alignment between organisational priorities and IS performance is more fundamental. Indeed, it could be argued that user satisfaction is a poor measure for success of strategic systems or systems introducing substantial change – the intention may indeed be that users are not satisfied. Yet, despite doubts raised about the validity of user satisfaction as an indicator of a system’s effectiveness, it is still a widely applied basis for measurement, albeit in conjunction with other methods, and it was used, together with other approaches, in the case study discussed in this paper. After all, although user satisfaction does not necessarily indicate that a system is effective in any broader terms, it is hard to see how a system can have significance as an intervention (be used and appropriated) without people wanting to, or being able to, use it. In the words of Ives et al. (1983 p 786) ‘A good IS perceived by its users as a poor system is a poor system’.

The discussion of evaluation within the IS field has been paralleled in the last decade by a discussion within the health informatics discipline. See Kaplan & Shaw (2004) for a recent review of different streams in evaluation research and their theoretical underpinnings. The debate in this community focuses, in particular, on the applicability of ‘scientific’ methods of evaluation stemming from the medical tradition, for example, randomised controlled trials (RCT), for the assessment of ICT-based applications in health care (Heathfield et al., 1998, 1999; Kaplan, 2001a, b; Ammenwerth et al., 2003; Littlejohns et al., 2003; Stoop & Berg, 2003; Wyatt & Wyatt, 2003). The arguments put forward against RCT in particular, and more generally the claim for an objective scientific approach, is that they do not allow for complex contextual issues beyond experimental control to be addressed (Heathfield et al., 1998; Kaplan, 2001b). RCT is based on an assumption, often critiqued as dubious or discredited, that different factors or parameters (such as hardware performance, training, etc.) can through experimental design be disentangled from the social processes and wider cause–effect relationships established around a system in use (Stoop & Berg, 2003). Critics suggest that such studies, when undertaken, are unreal, difficult to generalise and have not yet provided any major indication of improved patient outcomes or cost effectiveness (Heathfield et al., 1998).

Acknowledging these arguments and the difficulties of evaluating health ISs, a number of international projects have set out to work on producing new guidelines, these include IMIA WG13 and 15, AMIA (Van Gennip & Lorenzi, 1999) and the European led VATAM project (Talmon et al., 1999). Talmon et al. (1999), for example, following Friedman and Wyatt (1996), suggest that evaluation in this context extends beyond assessment of safety, efficacy and feasibility, to usability, costeffectiveness, and then beyond to social, economic and ethical concerns, and the VATAM guidelines aim at supporting such a broad approach (Talmon et al., 1999; Banta, 2001). Southon (1999) suggests that evaluation of ICT in health needs to be more organisation focused, and in particular to be undertaken so as to support existing organisational decision making processes. Similarly, within the field of telemedicine, Taylor (1999) proposes that assessments should consider if telemedicine is first safe, second practical and third worthwhile. Thus, the focus of evaluation develops from the (technical) system, to the services delivered and experienced, and finally to the health outcomes achieved and societal welfare benefits. Field (1996) also suggests that evaluation should include questions about the clinical process of care, on patient status, health outcome, accessibility, costs, and acceptability when compared to alternative health care strategies. He calls, significantly, for evaluation to be considered as an integral part of project design, implementation, and redesign, and understood as a continuous learning process going beyond a single project. In a similar vain, Anderson & Aydin (1994) and Kaplan (1997, 1998) point out that for evaluation activity to support a learning process, and in order to improve future out comes, it is not sufficient to evaluate around selected but isolated criteria, be the technical, economic, user, or medical, but rather it is necessary to pursue the complex processes that contribute to outcomes (to ask why those outcomes come about), and to consider relationship between system characteristics, individual characteristics, organisational characteristics, and effects among them. Other voices call for explicit consideration of patient interests, their preferences, their overall well-being, and accounts of health outcomes in wider terms than just as medical interventions or clinical outcomes (Hillier, 1987; Bowling, 1997; Cribb & Barber, 1997).

Striving to provide an encompassing programme for evaluation of health ISs, and to draw upon both the ISs and health informatics traditions, Cornford et al. (1994) provide an evaluation framework in the form of a matrix with structure, process and outcome on one side, and system functions, human perspectives (customer, actor, owner) and organisational context on the other (Figure 1). This matrix adapts a framework developed by Donabedian (1966, 1978) for evaluating quality of care, and is similar to a U.K. Department of Health framework for evaluating ISs projects (IMG, 1996).

This framework not only encompasses technical, individual and organisational characteristics, as well as a societal perspective (‘effect in the wider world’), but also offers a rich template for generating measures and data sources when evaluating ISs in many settings. It seeks to address the long-term prospects of a project, that is, its sustainability, as well as changes to work practices. It also implies an ethical dimension, through human perspective as well as organisational context. We used this framework in the case study discussed below, and discuss its limitations there.

From this brief review we can see that there is an overlap between the IS discipline’s concerns with evaluation and the evaluation issues discussed within some parts of the health informatics field. This may be due, in part, to the limited but useful interaction between the broader IS community and some parts of the health informatics community, with some health informatics researchers who critique the discipline’s over reliance on RCT being also active researchers within the wider IS field, for instance the works of Berg and Kaplan. However, in both the IS and health informatics disciplines there still exists a clear division between, on the one side, the formalist or rational functionalist tradition and on the other the various interpretative schools of evaluation (Introna & Whittaker, 2002). Indeed, the methodological debate is often polarised (Stoop & Berg, 2003) and there appears to be little direct engagement between those doing more traditional evaluation and those developing the new approaches.

Nevertheless, as evaluation is increasingly seen as being concerned with and needing to address organisational change and change processes (or their absence), and in health care, the patient’s experience of care, the formalist tradition has been increasingly challenged. Although health informatics has focused historically on verifying the clinical efficacy of systems and their safety, increasingly organisational, social and ethical implications of such systems are included in proposed evaluation frameworks, even if such challenges are still far from being universally acknowledged, far less resolved (Ammenwerth et al., 2003). More generally, we can detect a shift towards an understanding that there are real choices to be made as to evaluation techniques and measurements, and that these choices should be made based on appreciation of relevant criteria such as the technological basis of a system, its context of use, its processes of development and deployment, the goals of a project, as well as the overall aim of the evaluation and the mix of interests it represents (Ginzberg, 1978; Farbey et al., 1995).

## The need for a critical approach to evaluation

Despite the acknowledgement of the need for increasing the scope of evaluation and constructing it carefully within any given context, in practice the assessment of health informatics projects, as in other areas, often shows a narrow focus, limited to questions considering feasibility, performance and (in the case of clinical systems) safety (Lobley, 1997; Wallace, 1998). Many approaches to evaluation, even those eschewing the formalist or rational functionalist tradition, seem to focus on immediate implications and privilege an organisational/ managerial dimension. We seek here to take a broader perspective acknowledging that ISs permeate not only organisations and work practices but also society and social activities (the ‘lifeworld’). They thus may have far reaching consequences (good or bad) for the fabric of society and for people’s daily lives. This is not to say that technologies are autonomous and have ‘trajectories’ or predefined effects; we acknowledge that technologies are shaped by society (MacKenzie & Wajcman, 1985), that they are, to a certain extent, malleable, and their characteristics come into play or are revealed when they are enacted (put to use, performed), and through such enactment they may be appropriated in different ways in different settings with different outcomes (Woolgar, 1996; Orlikowski, 2000).

Nevertheless, and as a significant impetus to our shift to critical theory as a basis for evaluation activity, we do believe that technologies have ‘accumulative’ powers that limit or adsorb opportunities for change, making some choices, representing powerful interests, more natural than others (Winner, 1977). Technologies thus serve to legitimise some actions and to avoid responsibility for others. We are then concerned not only with efficiency and effectiveness of any innovative IS, or narrowly defined measures of outcomes or users’ satisfaction, but also with implications of IS innovation for society. We thus look for an evaluation approach that can address and reflect the real complexities of implementing IS and achieving innovative organisational change.

In pursuit of this goal we advocate here an approach to evaluation based on critical theory. To arrive at such a critically focused programme for evaluation, we first consider work on evaluation that draws on the critical perspective. Noting the relative dearth of such work, we review some other noninstrumental (and often crossfertilising) approaches to evaluation, seeking to highlight their commonalities and their distinct aspects. The approaches we are interested in here draw on sociotechnical perspective, social constructivism, actor network theory and hermeneutics (Davis et al., 1992; Avgerou, 1995; Berg, 1999; Bryans et al., 1999; Wilson & Howcroft, 2000; Jones & Hughes, 2001; May & Ellis, 2001; McAulay et al., 2002).

## The critical perspective

Critical theory (CT) is not a unified theory but rather a set of loosely linked principles. It has many flavours and proponents, but overall we can state the aim of a critical theorist or researcher as being to expose and undermine the status quo, thereby contributing to the emancipation of those who are (in some way) oppressed. The central idea underpinning the critical perspective is that all social phenomena are historically created and conditioned (Horkheimer, 1972; Horkheimer & Adorno, 1972[1944]). Social and technological conditions, often constraining individual or group efforts for emancipation and limiting human potential, are created and recreated by man (Horkheimer, 1972). From this perspective, technology is not autonomous, nor benign or neutral, but is an instrument of social control placed in the hands of the ‘vested interests’ that control society (Marcuse, 1970; Feenberg, 1991).

There are relatively few critically inspired works on IS evaluation. Drawing on Habermas’ (1987) theory of communicative action, O’Donnell & Henriksen (2002) seek to develop philosophical foundations for a critical evaluation of the social impact of ICT. They suggest that the social impact of ICT can be evaluated from a critical stance, by investigating the influence of ICT on the process of communicative action within a ‘lifeworld-insystem’, and propose that the validity claims of comprehensibility, objective truthfulness or efficiency, normative rightness and sincerity can provide appropriate empirical entry points for evaluation. In a somewhat similar vein, Bryans et al. (1999) propose a model of evaluation based on learning and dialogue, reflecting Habermas’ (1979) notion of ideal speech situations, noting that evaluation needs to be conducted in the environment of open communication, with clarity of purpose and trust. The aim, they suggest, is to develop an environment where people are not only able to engage in reflexive judgement but are also willing to re-visit their views and beliefs and to conceive of new possibilities for themselves and their organisations.

Yet, as McAulay et al. (2002) illustrate in a case study of evaluation of outsourcing decisions, different stakeholders have different personal interests and views, and thus the construction of shared criteria to support evaluation in these terms is problematic and the notion of autonomous and responsible dialogue may too easily be undermined by powerful stakeholders. Their study shows that the criteria implicated in the evaluation of outsourcing represent concepts that have superficial plausibility, for example, service quality and costs, but these are based on assumptions that are either ambiguous, theoretically implausible or difficult to substantiate. For example, a belief in improved service quality might be ‘an optimistic wish for the future’, often expressed in terms of intangibles of trust and partnership, rather than grounded in experience. The decision on outsourcing, they suggest, is essentially ideologically driven (economic interest) and as a consequence has different meanings for different stakeholders given their perspectives.

## Socio-technical approach

Stepping back from the critical approach we briefly review the contributions of socio-technical school of thought to our understanding of evaluation. Theorists of this school were among the first to highlight the social as well as technical nature of IS and to advocate the design of IS that would enhance job satisfaction (Mumford, 1983). Working within this tradition, Land (2000) argues for methods capable of valuing both social and technoeconomic goals. He suggests, for example, the use of a modified version of the balanced scorecard (Kaplan & Norton, 1992) that would include different perspectives; customer, financial, internal-efficiency, learning and growth, as well as the employee perspective, where questions about improving the quality of working life are posed. Such a socio-technical evaluation, as proposed by Land aims to accommodate corporate goals and preconceptions of the established managerial community, to make such an approach more acceptable for business users. In this its philosophy is substantially different to that of critical theory. However, sociotechnical approaches do advocate systems that enhance workers’ skills and job satisfaction, and base this on information gathering and evaluative activity. Land then suggests that the balanced scorecard might be used to arbitrate between the working of the managerial and the humanistic welfare paradigms – an attempt at ideological resolution.

## ‘New’ socio-technical perspectives: social shaping of technology and social constructivism

The socio-technical approach has evolved considerably in the past decade and IS researchers emerging from this tradition have reached out to different social theories, including social shaping of technology (SST), social constructivism and actor-network theory (ANT), seeking a better fundamental understanding of the nature of IS innovations within organisational settings. For example, Berg (1999) describing his perspective as sociotechnical (without a hyphen) explicitly draws on such theories. He suggests that an IT application in healthcare should be seen as forming a seamless web rather than being perceived as ‘Technology in an Organisation’, and suggests that ‘social’ and ‘technical’ should not be pried apart. He also argues that design and evaluation of IS (e.g. setting of meaningful evaluation criteria) must be based on deep empirical insights into the work practices in which ICT will be used. Wilson & Howcroft (2000) propose an approach to evaluation based on SST, maintaining that technology is a product of a society, influenced by the conditions of its creation and use (MacKenzie & Wajcman, 1985; Williams & Edge, 1996). From this, Wilson & Howcroft (2000) infer that evaluation (or evaluative processes) are deeply ingrained in technologies being, and that evaluation in the formal sense should not be seen as disjoint from, but as a part of a wider constitutive dynamic through which a technology is interpreted by relevant social groups and becomes stabilised (or not). Through a case study of a nursing IS, they illustrate how the meaning of success or failure of an IS can be constructed (and re-constructed) through the evaluation process. Similarly, drawing on the sociology of medical knowledge and practice, the social constructivist programme (SCOT), ANT and Foucault, May & Ellis (2001) suggest in their study of a proposed telemedicine system how different professionals find it difficult (or even impossible) to agree what knowledges and practices ‘count’ when developing a protocol and evaluating a clinical intervention. May et al. (2003) conclude that evaluation should not be seen as a politically neutral set of research techniques, but rather as a set of social practices that generate knowledge which may then contribute towards the embedding (or institutionalisation) of a technology.

## The hermeneutic tradition

Evaluation approaches drawing on the hermeneutic tradition shift the focus – to meanings rather than interests – emphasising the historical dimension of meaning and the role of an interpreter (reader of the text) in the process of understanding. This draws on Gadamer (1976), who suggests that a text can have many meanings depending on the context in which it is read, and that understanding is in this sense situated, both historically and culturally. If we consider an IS as a text, and an evaluation as an interpreting process, then we see that an IS may have many meanings – meanings created by (read by) the various interpreters and other ‘readers’, that is, participants in the formal evaluation process but also in the every-day work situation that a system is embedded in. The aim of such an evaluation is then to strive for understanding of those multiple, spread out in time and space, meanings; the meanings the system is given by different readers of the text and how those meanings are constructed and re-constructed.

Jones & Hughes (2001) advocate the use of situated hermeneutic evaluation, and point out that people are ‘active makers of physical and social reality’ and evaluate things in their everyday life. These evaluations, informal opinions and gossip are important to individuals and peer groups, and thus should be important to organisations and evaluators. Davis et al. (1992) discussing IS assessment in the context of failure also utilise the concept of Gadamer’s (1976) hermeneutic circle of understanding, acknowledging the need to understand the parts of a text through the understanding of the whole, while the understanding of the whole is determined by understanding of its parts. This understanding is drawn from lived experience (not isolated and detached metrics), and achieved through dialogue with the subject, rather than through technique and judgement. Davis et al. suggest that to understand the underlying causes of, in their case, IS failure, the evaluator may need to go through a repeated cycle of reinterpreting ‘facts’ or data already recorded. These reinterpretations will often deconstruct those ‘facts’ and perhaps explain confusing results that initially seemed unreasonable. New interpretations may then result, and lead to the need to pursue more observations (readings) that might lead to further interpretations – see for example, Klecun-Dabrowska & Cornford (2000).

## Summary of the approaches introduced

The four approaches to evaluation briefly introduced here (see Table 1) are informed by different but crossfertilised theoretical schools, and have a number of common themes. They all see evaluation as a broadly political process and emphasise the need to evaluate in context and using social, not only techno-economic, factors. They also have distinctive features and each places emphasis on some aspects more than others. More fundamentally perhaps they are based on somewhat different ontological assumptions and critical theory may be seen in these terms as either a separate paradigm (Burrell & Morgan, 1985) or as a type of interpretative research (Alvesson & Willmott, 1992; Denzin & Lincoln, 1994).

In particular, taking a critical perspective, we assume that ISs do exist independent of social actors, but we see them as manifestations of social interests serving dominant social groups or dominant ideologies, for example, managerialism, rather than just as neutral technological artefacts or social constructions. Furthermore, critical theory invites us to take a stand, to be more than a narrator of different stories, to challenge the status quo and to strive for emancipation, for example, from overreaching techno-economic rationality, oppressive work conditions, and technologies that facilitate these. It also encourages the exploration of the links between the local (e.g. a work setting) and macrosocietal trends and interests, taking into account how these have been historically constituted. In this it can offer insights into different forms of domination, power and knowledge. What critical theory shares with the other approaches (e.g. social constructivism and hermeneutic) is a belief in epistemic relativity of science, that knowledge is historically and socially produced. This leads us to believe that knowledge of an IS (based for example on an evaluation) will always be relative, subjective and open to improvement. This also suggests that formative models of evaluation are more desirable than summative, and implies that the choice of evaluation methods is equally not objective but is historically and socially conditioned (Bryans et al., 1999). The process of evaluation then should be based on reflexive dialogue that aims to explicate, and when possible resolve differences, in values and understanding of the system and, building on this, can establish contextually powerful criteria and methods of evaluation.

We should be clear, however, that a belief in epistemic relativity does not imply judgmental relativity (Mingers, 2000). Thus, although evaluation is necessarily partial and influenced by historical and social constraints governing the evaluator and other participants, there are more appropriate or less appropriate accounts of any IS under study. Furthermore, what critical theory demands is that the IS itself (and the process of evaluation) be judged against some normative values. We may acknowledge that those values are themselves socially constructed, but this does not deny them validity. Thus, a health IS could be assessed in terms of its contribution to improving health and quality of life of particular groups of patients (those directly affected by the system), or the population (by increasing efficiency and supporting better use of resources) and such a choice must be made, and made explicit. Equally a critical evaluation would consider the effects of a system on working conditions and job satisfaction, and on the practice and nature of medicine and care, for example, raising questions about medicalisation of social life, or the managerialisation of clinical care.

In summary, an evaluation inspired by critical theory builds on the following principles:

1. Evaluation should be guided by critical-emancipatory cognitive interests. Evaluation is normative, not ideologically neutral or apolitical, and needs to be guided by critical-emancipatory cognitive interests. It should strive to expose historically created conditions (including technological conditions) that limit people’s lives, and consider how a system under study may alter them (in a positive or negative way) and bear in mind the accumulative power of technology. Evaluation should give voice (striving for an ‘ideal speech situation’) to all persons and groups that have interests in and are potentially affected by a technology or system being evaluated, or by the evaluation itself.

2. Social, material and historical conditions should be reflected in evaluation. Social, material and historical conditions must not be treated as a mere ‘context’, but as constitutive of the system under study and the evaluation itself. Issues of competing interests and power, knowledge, dominant rationalities and ideologies, as well as alienating and constraining practices should be central to evaluation.

3. Methods adopted should be critically informed. In critical research the choice and manner of using methods (logics-in-use) cannot be separated from the theoryinforming method or the focal activity of a study (Morrow & Brown, 1994). This implies that the evaluation process and content (e.g. the construction of criteria and methods of evaluation) cannot be separated from the critical principles and the situation under study. Building on the hermeneutic tradition, critical interpretation is based on the evaluators’ experience and knowledge.

4. Evaluation must capture enactment. Since enactment is a temporal process, this implies that technology needs to be evaluated over time. Attitudes to IS, the interests represented in them, the way they are appropriated and used by individuals and groups, the extent to which they become embedded (or not) in work practices and institutionalised in organisations will all change over time. However, in a critical evaluation it is not the comparative snapshots that are important, but the processes of enactment that are at work.

5. Evaluation is fundamentally reflexive. Overall, the power of critical evaluation, and its utility, is based on an active engagement between the evaluation process, the interests represented in a system, and the evaluator. This is created through an explicit reflexivity, in which learning, deeper meanings and revealing of hidden rationalities are harnessed to emancipatory intent and action.

## Case study: the primary care intranet

Bearing in mind the discussion above, this section reflects briefly upon an evaluation of a primary care intranet project we conducted in 1999, and on this basis outlines its strengths and weaknesses. It is our reflection on this prior work that has challenged us to look beyond established models of evaluation and beyond the frameworks we used then, and to develop the critical evaluation framework presented above.

## Background

The evaluation of the intranet was commissioned during a period of radical reforms of the U.K.’s National Health Service (NHS) incorporating a restructuring of the NHS and a drive towards ‘modernisation’, which gave a prominent role to ICT and to evidence-based medicine. As part of this restructuring primary care groups (PCGs) linking family doctor’s practices in local areas were created and given budgets and power to purchase health services from the secondary care sector.<sup>1</sup> This gave primary care doctors an opportunity to influence the secondary care providers (mainly hospitals), but also added to their responsibilities and necessitated a close cooperation and exchange of information between different doctors’ practices within one PCG.<sup>2</sup>

This evaluation considered a new intranet connecting the family doctors’ practices within one PCG in an inner city area in London, and linking them to hospitals and health authorities as a part of the nation wide NHS intranet – NHSnet. It was hoped that the intranet would facilitate the growth of different applications, and that it would be used for exchange of patient-based information and for clinical governance. The Health Authority, the projects sponsors, also perceived that the intranet would offer a good opportunity for facilitating cooperation within the PCG and with external agencies, as well as encouraging the practice of evidence-based medicine; in this way, they saw it as leading towards better care for patients. The evaluation’s aim, as conveyed to us at the outset, was to assess the deployment of the intranet in this primary care setting. Specific goals included ascertaining what applications were used, for what purpose, were they perceived as beneficial to the practices, and if so, in what way? Views on current and future information needs were also solicited.

## Research approach

In the study we adopted an interpretative approach. Interviewing users seemed an obvious choice and well suited to collecting rich, contextual data that lent itself to addressing ‘why’ questions. Although a number of stakeholders within the PCG were identified, due to the limited scope, budget and time scale of the evaluation, it was not possible to fully consult representatives of all user groups. The interviews were semistructured, consisting of open-ended questions. They varied in length, lasting between 15 min to 1 h. One primary care manager, six practice managers or assistant practice managers, one data inputter and one administrative assistant were interviewed (one on the phone), as well as three family doctors (one in person, one on the phone and one completed a short questionnaire). In total, nine practices took part in the evaluation out of approximately 50 participating in the pilot. These were selected to represent different areas (e.g. a deprived council estate and an area of privately owned houses) and doctor’s practices (single-handed and larger partnerships). In addition, two representatives from St Thomas’ hospital, who were at the same time piloting a referral system between the hospital and one of the practices, were invited to comment. A representative from the local medical school’s Primary Care Skills Centre, who conducted training sessions for the pilot, was asked for comments on participants’ IT skills and their general attitudes towards the intranet.

Having in mind the aim and goals of the evaluation and the tight time scale, as well as the interactive nature of the system, the choice we made at the time as to what exactly to evaluate was informed by the mainstream literature on the subject. Thus, the assessment accommodated the six major categories that are included in DeLone & McLean’s (1992) taxonomy: system quality, information quality, use, user satisfaction, individual impact, and organisational impact. A template for the content of the evaluation was provided by the SPO framework presented earlier in Figure 1. In this study the framework was adopted for both information gathering and analysis. However, not all of the matrix items were investigated to the same degree. Since user satisfaction was central to the evaluation mandate, the intranet was evaluated principally through the users eyes. For example, technical performance was subjectively assessed by the users rather than measured through ‘objective’ techniques, similarly with altered delivery and practice.

## Outline of findings

The detailed results of the evaluation are discussed in other papers, which focus on social and organisational issues of the intranet usage, rather than on the process of evaluation itself (Dabrowska, 1999; Klecun & Cornford, 2003). Here, we only outline the main findings.

The majority of the respondents demonstrated a positive attitude towards the system and considered it beneficial, although usually email was regarded as more useful than the web-based information. All agreed that as more applications became available (particularly a referral system to work with local hospitals and links to laboratories for patient test results), the intranet would become very useful. The majority of respondents also stressed the need for the intranet to be integrated with clinical systems and not to stand alone (as it did in this pilot implementation). Most (but not all) said that they had at least satisfactory understanding of the system’s capabilities. This indicates that support workers (practice managers, office workers) and GP (primary care doctors) do not dismiss new technologies out of hand. However, these technologies must either fit with their working practices or show significant improvements (e.g. an online system simplifying referral procedures). Overall, time, skills, access, services available and technical performance of the system were identified as the most important factors influencing use of the intranet.

No significant changes to the working practices and the practice of medicine were identified at the time, although these were anticipated by many for the future. One respondent mentioned that although the intranet may keep people better informed, it may also lead to alienation and loosening the ties between the practices if it was to replace face-to-face or telephone contact. Surprisingly, the respondents voiced no concerns regarding security and confidentiality of patient records, these were only considered when prompted by the interviewer. This may be because the majority of interviewees were administrative or managerial staff.

There were significant differences in the Internet usage between large practices (those including a number of doctors, nurses and other staff) and single-handed practices. Not surprisingly single-handed practices were using the Internet the least and staff had received hardly any training at all. They were struggling with day-to-day tasks and had no time for ‘extra’ work (i.e. searching for medical information on the Internet). Similar findings were discovered in a contemporary and larger scale project of intranet usage by doctors (Howcroft & Mitev, 2000), and a study of a similar system in another London Borough (Abdulrasul, 2001).

## Reflections on the evaluation from a critical perspective

This study illustrates that, limitations not withstanding, a qualitative approach as part of a pilot implementation, can lead to meaningful, if somewhat predictable findings that, if fed back, could benefit the process of full-scale implementation or expansion (formative evaluation). It is because of the choice of the conceptual framework (SPO) and methods (discussions rather than questionnaires), the willingness to listen to different voices, and the scope of the evaluation (covering technical, human and organisational perspectives), that this evaluation might be differentiated from the usual ‘user satisfaction’ studies. It might also be said then that the evaluation answered the questions posed by its sponsors and fulfilled their agenda. However, reflecting upon the evaluation we have come to question its process and methods and, more importantly, its agenda. In the reminder of this section we thus evaluate the evaluation according to the principles of critical evaluation proposed in a previous section.

## Fulfilling critical-emancipatory cognitive interests

Reflecting on the first principle, we ask if criticalemancipatory cognitive interests guided this evaluation. The answer to this question is not straightforward. The evaluation’s aims were not articulated as emancipatory but the evaluation was an exercise to give medical and administrative staff an opportunity to ‘speak their mind’ on the current system and to make suggestions for the future. But is this really an emancipatory aim (even with a small ‘e’)? Were the users identified really given a chance to express their views? And more importantly, were their views going to be considered when decisions were made? After all, the broader NHSnet project and PCGs were going ahead regardless of this or any other evaluation results.

We would argue that this was a valid exercise – having (some) input into the future design of the potential system and its operational support. The commissioning manager read the evaluation report and used it in his presentations, so we could say that the evaluation had some impact. However, we are not sure if the recommendations were enacted in any way. More importantly, the participants had limited influence on the evaluation agenda and scope. Although they were asked open-ended questions and were encouraged to express their views, they were not asked to put forward their own agenda for evaluation. Furthermore, some participants may have been constrained in contributing by their lack of knowledge, for example of the system’s potentials, as well as other factors. We believe that in this study the participants felt able to speak freely, but we have no real basis for such a belief other than the social cues of the moment. Finally, in this study we are clear that the representatives of other stakeholder groups, for example, nurses and most significantly patients could have been given a stronger voice.

## Reflecting social, material and historical conditions

Moving on to the second principle, we see that social and historical conditions were treated as little more than a mere ‘context’. Issues of competing interests, power, knowledge creation, dominant rationalities and ideologies, although acknowledged, were not central to our evaluation effort. Almost unreflected in the evaluation, the implementation of this intranet was highly political, in terms of local organisational politics and the national agenda of the then new Labour government. For example, conflicting visions of the system were presented by the Health Authority and the hospital involved in the pilot. The hospital representatives wanted the new system to be linked to existing clinical systems and be implemented in fewer, but better prepared practices – serving their desire to pilot a referral system and ‘prove’ a technology. However, the Health Authority, who had the budget for the project, pushed forward its vision by involving more practices and, for security reasons (as they claimed), offering only stand-alone computers unintegrated with other systems. Should we have pursued further the relations between the Health Authority, PCG, and hospitals, and placed them within national policy agenda, to understand how this influenced the system at hand? We are left with a feeling that without deeper understanding of this context (i.e. socio-political structures and their historical context), the evaluation could only have a limited validity and serve a limited purpose.

We have already noted above that not all of the matrix items incorporated in the SPO framework were investigated to the same degree. Specifically, organisational context was not given much attention in the fieldwork, and ‘Effects in the world’ was only marginally considered. However, the evaluation was not limited to immediate concerns as to the technical performance of the system, its ease of use, and the appropriateness and usefulness of its information content and different applications. Issues of its influence on work practices, patient care and effects on individuals (e.g. alienation from others) were addressed and raised by some respondents. However, not all respondents felt able to comments on these. This is partly because at the time of evaluation the intranet was new, included only a few applications (email and web-based information on the NHS) and its use was limited. Thus, some respondents felt that they could only speculate on potential impacts and were hesitant to do so. Nevertheless, we could have focused more on the perceived role of the broader NHSnet initiative, asking more about how it might alter existing relationships and working practices, for example, between doctors and patients, and have consequences for the practice of medicine more generally, probing beyond more obvious changes to patient experience on the administrative level.

## Adopting critically informed methods

Considering the third principle that the choice and manner of using methods (logics-in-use) cannot be separated from the theory-informing method and the problem to be clarified, we reflect on the appropriateness of our methods of evaluation and the choice of criteria (the SPO evaluation framework adopted in this study). We have already acknowledged the limitations of user satisfaction studies and the partial stakeholder coverage, but we believe that a user perspective, even if not always ‘scientific’ is a valid one. It is important not only to measure how a system performs but also how it fits in with the work lives of its users and is perceived by them – the lifeworld-in-system. Taking such an approach may be accused of producing biased and inaccurate results – doubly subjective as users attempt to account for their experience of the systems, and the evaluators themselves interpret this stream of data. However, it would have the advantage of being embedded in working practices, and seeking to capture what is and what is not important for users, thus potentially lending itself to emancipatory aims.

The chosen method, semistructured interviews, did enable us to elucidate details and interpretations that would not have been available from questionnaires or system generated data. The semistructured format helped to ensure that standard questions would be answered, but at the same time encouraged the participants to express additional ideas. Furthermore, the participants were visited in their working environment and asked to describe, and when possible to demonstrate and talk through, the tasks they performed. Thus, we conclude that the chosen methods were broadly appropriate for critically led studies.

Although, the SPO framework is encompassing, from the critical perspective it (and our use of it) has a number of limitations. First, organisational context was only understood as organisational ‘effects’ of the system, but the organisational and societal context that conditioned the development and use of the system needed to be more strongly represented. Thus, any discussion of ‘impacts’ or ‘effects’ of such systems are potentially misleading, and should be better understood as consequences of underlying social conditions linked to processes by which a technology or systems innovation is developed, implemented and appropriated (MacKenzie & Wajcman, 1985; Kling, 1996; Doolin, 1998). Indeed, as discussed above, it was this ‘context’ that created and conditioned the particular project that we were evaluating, through national politics, institutional rivalry and competing goals, etc.

Our next point follows directly: the SPO framework as used here does not explicitly address issues of power relations in the organisation (and beyond), or the power shifts that may come about (intended or otherwise) through a systems implementation. These issues might be identified and boxed under ‘organisational context’, as process and outcome, but they certainly could have been made more explicit in the study. Thus, one of the issues to investigate more directly would be ‘did people perceive their interests being challenged or served?

Finally, the SPO framework somewhat artificially divides System Functions (i.e. the technical) and Human/Organisational perspective (i.e. the social). This is perhaps done for clarity and in order to simplify the model, but such socio-technical boundaries are inherently unclear. Problems and issues may be constructed as technical or social at will, and different interests may do this to serve their own aims, as illustrated by Bloomfield & Best (1992). Perhaps, drawing on ANT, we should rather aim for some symmetry in the way we treat the technical and social (i.e. not making a priori distinctions between these two). In the study reported here, our analysis of problems encountered by people using the system thus could have paid greater attention to such concerns, particularly in situations where the social was redefined as technical and visa versa. As a simple example, usability can be defined as a social problem and addressed through recruitment, training and work processes, or as a technical problem and approached by (re)designing an interface.

## Capturing enactment

The fourth principle suggest that an evaluation should capture the process of enactment, appropriation and change, thus what is evaluated is not a moment in time or a snap shot, but should be able to reflect a dynamic process at work and to provide an account of particular events within an ongoing narrative and informed by a hermeneutic perspective. Against this principle our work has clear failings. The study was undertaken in a short space of time, with fieldwork undertaken over a period of 6 weeks. The interpreting of the collected data was to a greater extent iterative, and did employ some hermeneutic elements influenced by Davis et al. (1992), but no follow-up data collection was carried out, thus we only partially satisfied this principle.

## Achieving reflexivity in evaluation

The last principle proposes that evaluation processes must be reflexive. This paper, an evaluation of an evaluation, is a part of our attempt at reflexivity. We question our and other participators interests, beliefs and assumptions, and consider how these might influence the choice of criteria and methods, the evaluation process and its findings. We acknowledge that being commissioned to do the work, given an agenda for evaluation and having limited resources (in terms of time and money) resulted in obvious shortcomings of the evaluation, as discussed here. It may also be suggested that because we were commissioned by the Information Director of the Health Authority involved, we were not impartial evaluators. This is true, but being aware of this fact, we consciously tried to report our findings as they were presented to us by the participants, without emphasising/deemphasising different points. Nevertheless, although reporting others opinions, we acknowledge that we had a principal role in the constructing the evaluation, for example, in terms of how and what questions we asked and how we tried to reflexively analyse the stream of data generated. We sought to take into account the participants previous experience with computers, the nature of their job, familiarity with procedures, and general perceptions of computers, and thus tentatively tried to consider why they told us what they did.

These reflections based on the five principles have led us to conclude that, from the critical theory perspective, there are a number of areas of this particular study that we would wish to redesign.

Preparation Recognising that evaluation is not ideologically neutral or apolitical (principle one) suggests the need for reflection on the place of an evaluation in the wider societal and organisational context (e.g. to ask why evaluate, to what purpose, as well as when and by whom it should be done). This also involves the evaluators questioning their own beliefs and assumptions, and their role in the evaluation process (as advocated in the fifth principle).

Designing/planning of the case study Following the first principle, this entails seeking to identify and actively involve all relevant groups in planning the evaluation process and contributing to the agenda, and ensuring (as far as possible) that conditions for an open communication processes are met, a Habermasian approach.

Content Having in mind the third principle, the SPO framework applied in this study is judged as an appropriate starting point, but the points discussed earlier in this section need to be addressed. In particular, emancipatory aims should be explicitly stated (rather than implied). For example, participants in such an evaluation could be asked to state their own priorities and those of the health care system and then consider how (if at all) the proposed intranet may support the priorities identified. Such a process requires reflexive questioning of (sometimes ingrained) assumptions and opening of other possibilities (e.g. through making both technological and managerial rationality a valid topic for discussion), as indicated in principle five. Furthermore, as stated in principle two, issues of competing interests, power, knowledge creation, dominant rationalities and ideologies, as well as (alienating) work practices should be central to an evaluation. In this case, for example, we should challenge a privileged role given to technomanagerial rationality.

Process Reflecting on the third principle, the research methods chosen (interviews, observations), we still judge as appropriate for the aims of the evaluation, but a more reflexive interpretative account (and reinterpretations), as well as a dialogue between stakeholders, might have brought more insightful, critical results (see principles four and five). Such reinterpretations should go beyond the immediate and consider in what way participants’ responses were socially and historically conditioned, and ask the evaluator to be aware of the inherent ambiguity of language.

## Summary and conclusion

In this paper, we have sought to introduce some theoretical perspectives into the field of evaluation with the aim of providing a basis for a new, more substantial and richer, even provocative, evaluation tradition. Drawing on social theory, we have outlined a point of departure for evaluation efforts that builds upon fundamental principles of critical theory, and in doing so combine elements of the hermeneutic, socio-technical and social constructivist positions. This approach is encapsulated in a set of five principles for a critical evaluation programme, indicating in each case what the principle might imply in practical situations (see Table 2). We acknowledge that such ideas, of course, are not easy to carry into practice and that such a programme will not (indeed cannot) ‘solve’ all the problems discussed in the previous sections and may well appear to present some new ones (perhaps a lot) as it reopens questions of underlying assumptions and brings to attention controversies and diverse interests. Healthcare, with its strong ethical underpinning, public service ethos and often strongly expressed professional interests, appears to be a natural setting for the evaluation programme we are proposing. It might be easier to convince those sponsoring an evaluation in this field of the need to move beyond a narrowly understood managerial (or ‘scientific’) issues. Nevertheless, we would argue that the ideas we have presented are relevant in other settings too. The evaluation that ignores or brackets the issues discussed throughout the paper and operates within restricted constructs such as technical performance and ‘user satisfaction’ risks leaving unrevealed, but deeply embedded, conflicts that may jeopardise the achievement of the real benefits that are potentially available through ISs innovation. The emancipatory potential of technology, inherent in the critical analysis, will never be achieved if it is not brought into the debate.

The programme proposed is based on the broad assumption that all social phenomena, including ISs innovations, are socially and historically conditioned, and that any evaluative activity has to both reflect and respond to such conditions. Such a perspective presupposes that neither information and communication technologies, the systems into which they are incorporated, nor the evaluation criteria and assessment processes that are applied to them are neutral. In this sense we see evaluation as inherently political, a contested domain where contentious ideas are put in play. Reflecting this view, and drawing on critical theory, we seek to emphasise the reflexivity that is, or should be, at the core of evaluative activity, and give a new emphasis to evaluation activity through a questioning of common assumptions and beliefs, and an acknowledgement of the embedded and sedimented power relations and vested interests that evaluation might, with benefit, uncover.

By describing and deconstruction an evaluation that we undertook, and discussing how it might have been reshaped within the proposed critical programme, we have tried to illustrate how such an endeavour might be undertaken. More fundamentally, we argue, this proposal is driven by a belief that reframing evaluation may have the potential to reframe our relations with technology itself. We are thus drawn back to the observation of Wilson & Howcroft (2000), building on a constructivist approach, that evaluation (or evaluative processes) are deeply ingrained in technologies’ being and are one of the key elements of the broader process by which any technology or IS achieves a distinctive identity and becomes stabilised in use (see also Bijker, 1997).
