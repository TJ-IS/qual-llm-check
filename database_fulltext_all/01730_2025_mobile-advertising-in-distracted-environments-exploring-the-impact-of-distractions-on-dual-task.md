# MOBILE ADVERTISING IN DISTRACTED ENVIRONMENTS: EXPLORING THE IMPACT OF DISTRACTIONS ON DUAL-TASK INTERFERENCE<sup>1</sup>

Siddharth Bhattacharya Information Systems and Operations Management Area, Costello College of Business George Mason University, Fairfax, VA, U.S.A. {sbhatt22@gmu.edu}

Heather Kennedy Department of Management, Gordon S. Lang School of Business and Economics, University of Guelph, Guelph, ON, CANADA {kennedyh@uoguelph.ca}

Vinod Venkatraman Department of Marketing, Fox School of Business, Temple University, Philadelphia, PA, U.S.A. {vinod.venkatraman@temple.edu}

Sunil Wattal Department of Management Information Systems, Fox School of Business, Temple University, Philadelphia, PA, U.S.A. {swattal@temple.edu}

It is increasingly common for consumers to engage with various tasks on their personal devices amid other distractions such as watching television at home, shopping at malls, or attending concerts. While this split in attention poses challenges, it also opens valuable opportunities for advertisers to strategically push targeted advertisements based on information about the user’s environment. Across a series of controlled lab experiments using a custom app developed for this study, we demonstrate how marketers can optimize pop-up advertising on consumers’ personal devices within distraction-filled environments. In doing so, we extend traditional insights from dual-task interference studies that have previously focused on corresponding tasks in isolation, without considering any stimuli from the environment. Our results indicate that, in the presence of additional stimuli from the environment, a facilitating relationship exists between the attention paid to a task and the effectiveness of pop-up advertisements interrupting the task. However, this relationship is moderated by the extent of attention diffusion from the environment. As the distance between the task and the environment increases, consumer attention to the task is more diffused, resulting in poorer encoding of the pop-up advertisements. Critically, optimizing the content and timing of pop-up advertisements to the environmental content can significantly improve their effectiveness. Our results have important implications for helping marketers develop actionable strategies for mobile advertising in distraction-filled environments.

Keywords: Dual-task interference, multiscreen, mobile, environmental distractions, advertising

## Introduction

The ubiquity of mobile devices has created a boom in mobile advertising, with recent reports estimating mobile advertising spending to increase to \$399.6 billion by 2024 (Statista, 2022).

However, the effectiveness of mobile advertising is complicated by the fact that mobile users are frequently situated in dynamic environments, which can be sources of continuous distractions (Ehmke, 2021; Elgan, 2017; Griffey, 2018). Examples of such distractions include performing tasks such as watching television, driving a car, watching a child at a playground, reading a research article, or walking—to name a few (Rabbit, 2018). The success of mobile advertising relies on capturing the attention of consumers, which becomes challenging when they use mobile devices amid distractions that divert part of their focus away from the screen. As a result, the response to the advertisements (ads) users encounter on their mobile devices is influenced not only by the attention the users dedicate to their devices but also by the attention diverted towards distractions. Consequently, understanding how contextual information about these distractions affects the consumer response becomes crucial for mobile advertisers, as it has the potential to enhance the effectiveness of their advertising efforts.

Anecdotal evidence suggests that mobile advertisers are adopting technologies such as location-based targeting and cross-device tracking to account for contextual distractions that consumers may face while seeing ads on their mobile device (Feldman, 2018). For example, advances in mobile technology allow specific ads to be served to users at a given time by predicting their likelihood of watching a particular TV program from their online behavior (Hinz et al., 2022). Similarly, programmatic advertising (Multiview, 2019) and cross-device ad targeting (Hynes, 2022) are being used to increase reach among users engaged across multiple platforms. Furthermore, location-based targeting is being used in entertainment venues (Arkenberg et al., 2019; Unacast, 2022) to enhance user engagement and customer retention through discovery and brand awareness campaigns. Despite its emerging prevalence in the industry, little research exists on how tailoring ads to the contextual information extracted from environmental distractions impacts the effectiveness of mobile advertising.

Ads on mobile devices are often served in the form of popup interrupts when a user is performing a task such as listening to music, reading news, or playing games on their mobile device (Mobile Action Team, 2019). The theoretical lens of dual task interference (DTI) has been applied across past research studies to explain how users respond to such interrupts while performing a particular task (e.g., Heninger et al., 2006; Jackson et al., 2023; Jenkins et al., 2016; Mark et al., 2008; McFarlane, 2002; Wu et al., 2020). These studies have consistently demonstrated that performance is negatively impacted within dual-task experiences, be it when performing two continuous tasks (task-task interference— e.g., Segijn & Eisend, 2019) or interrupting tasks (taskinterrupt interference) (e.g., Jenkins et al., 2016). Recent studies in IS literature (e.g., Jenkins et al., 2016; Wu et al., 2020) have primarily focused on examining competition for attention between the task-interrupt ensemble in isolation.

The contexts in which these experiments are carried out are treated as “static” since participants are asked to focus only on the task at hand without any additional environmental distractions. However, we contend that in the real world, these types of dual-task interferences occur in the presence of additional distractions. For instance, security alerts interrupting a user who is checking their email or reading the news on their computer occur while the individual may also be engaged with activities such as listening to music playing on the same device or watching television. We label these as “dynamic” contexts since additional stimuli from the environment can further influence and moderate the tasktask or task-interrupt interference. In other words, individuals’ attention is not exclusively confined to the traditional task-task or task-interrupt interference but can be further diffused based on various external stimuli coming from the environment. In this study, we focus specifically on dynamic task-interrupt contexts, where the user is engaged with a task that is being interrupted periodically in the presence of additional changes in the environment that also compete for the user’s attention.<sup>2</sup> Thus motivated, we ask our main research question: How can mobile advertisers optimize the design and delivery of ads (interrupts) on a mobile device when the user is engaged with a task on the device, given that additional stimuli from the environment can also compete for the user’s attention?

To examine this question, we collected data using a combination of three independent online and lab-based experiments, using a custom app developed especially for this study where we simulated a typical user experience of performing a task on a personal device (such as mobile phone) that is interrupted periodically with pop-up ads while also being engaged in a task in the environment. Our results indicate that users who perform well on the task on the personal device are also likely to have higher recall of the interrupts (in our context, pop-up ads), indicating a facilitating relationship between the task and the interrupts in the presence of external distractions. Moreover, we found that strategically timing the interrupts on the personal device based on the content of the environment improves its effectiveness. Varying the congruence of the content of the interrupts with respect to the environment also influences interrupt effectiveness. Finally, our results indicate that the delivery and design effects are moderated by the extent of attention diffusion between the task-interrupt ensemble and the environment.

Our research makes several contributions to the growing body of literature on DTI and mobile advertising. We contribute to knowledge about DTI by evaluating the role of environmental distractions on DTI performance. Most of the current DTIfocused studies in the IS literature focus on a task-interrupt ensemble in isolation. Mobile advertising, however, often exists in dynamic contexts where the interrupt on the mobile device appears when a user’s attention can shift back and forth between a task on the mobile device and the environment or between multiple tasks on the mobile device. While traditional DTI research often suggests an inhibiting relationship between tasks and interrupts in static contexts<sup>3</sup> (Jenkins et al., 2016; Kao & Pierro, 2022; Plummer et al., 2015), our results reveal a facilitating relationship between a task and interrupts in the presence of a distraction. The intuition is that in the context of distractions, consumers process the task and interrupt as a single entity with increased automaticity (Pashler, 1994). The greater the attention paid to the task, the more likely the user is to process the interrupt more efficiently in an involuntary manner. We also add to our understanding of DTI by investigating how inputs from the environment can be used to improve DTI performance. For instance, strategically timing an alert relative to or making it congruent with the environment’s content improves ad performance.

Finally, we also contribute to DTI knowledge by showing that the impact of task engagement on the effectiveness of interrupts is influenced by the extent of attention diffusion to the environment. We demonstrate that increasing the spatial distance between the environment and the task-interrupt ensemble results in an increased diffusion of attention, which subsequently affects the performance of the interrupt. This adds to Pashler’s (1994) work by highlighting that it’s not just the timing and complexity of tasks but also their physical setup—specifically, their spatial arrangement—that influence the extent of dual-task interference. Theoretically, our findings also inform future mobile advertising scholars on the importance of considering consumers’ environments and their potential to be leveraged in the optimization of mobile ads.

Our research also provides insights for marketers and organizations seeking to effectively improve consumers’ responses to advertisements on their mobile phones when they are distracted by interactions with the environment. At the highest level, our results indicate that when designing mobile ads, advertisers should consider mobile tasks, which are likely to consume more user attention, as this increases ad performance. Advertisers should also account for the context in which a user is likely to consume a given ad. For example, advertisers should seek to make mobile ads congruent with surrounding distractions, such as featuring relevant spokespeople (e.g., a player on the local sports team for ads appearing during a sporting event). Alternatively, it could suggest advertising particular products on a mobile device at times that are likely to be most congruent with environmental content. For instance, a large company with multiple product lines (e.g., PepsiCo) targeting fans using their mobile devices at a sports event might choose to feature its most congruent products (e.g., Gatorade as opposed to Tropicana). Moreover, with advances in ad syncing and automated content recognition (ACR) technology,<sup>4</sup> advertisers can estimate the distance between a mobile user and a distraction. For instance, in multiscreen viewing contexts, advertisers can detect the kind of medium users are engaged on (i.e., whether it’s a single medium such as a splitscreen TV or two separated mediums with multiple screens). This information can then be used to strategically push ads to consumers’ mobile devices while considering the perceived distance from the distraction.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In the following section, we review the relevant literature in the areas of DTI and mobile advertising. Then, we develop our hypotheses. Next, we provide an overview of our research design, followed by results from the three studies. This is followed by a discussion of the robustness checks. Finally, we summarize the main results, discuss our theoretical contributions and managerial implications, and suggest future directions for our research.

## Literature Review

## Dual-Task Interference

Dual-task interference (DTI) refers to the performance deficits that occur in component tasks when two tasks are performed simultaneously (Watanabe & Funahashi, 2014). Understanding the nature and mechanisms of these performance deficits has been an area of research interest for over 30 years (Pashler, 1994). Many of the early studies on DTI involved cognitive psychology experiments where participants had to perform two tasks concurrently (e.g., walking and talking, watching television while working, toggling between two tasks when working on a computer). Subsequent studies, particularly in information systems and marketing, have focused on the effect of interrupts when performing a primary task—e.g., notifications while working (Jenkins et al., 2016), pop-up advertisements while engaging with a mobile app (Wu et al., 2020), etc. Most studies have consistently demonstrated that performance is negatively impacted within dual-task experiences, be it continuous tasks (e.g., Segijn & Eisend, 2019) or interrupting tasks (e.g., Jenkins et al., 2016). Table E1 in Appendix E provides an overview of key representative DTI studies along these two dimensions, namely, (1) simultaneous continuous tasks (task-task interference) and (2) interruptions during a continuous task (task-interrupt interference). These studies, however, assume a static context, where the interference is evaluated in controlled environments with no additional distractions. However, little prior work exists in characterizing how these DTI instances are impacted by dynamic contexts, where attention is diffused across various stimuli coming from the environment (Figure 1).

Across prior studies involving static contexts, DTI has been shown to be associated with negative implications such as reduced productivity (McFarlane, 2002), increased stress (Jackson et al., 2023; Mark et al., 2008), and poor task performance (Segijn & Eisend, 2019). Specifically, interference and diminished performance in these dual-task scenarios occur from the need to share limited and finite cognitive resources across multiple tasks (capacity sharing), the inability to use certain resources simultaneously across tasks (bottlenecks), and/or the cross-task resulting from engaging in two similar tasks (Pashler, 1994).

Given that humans have limited ability to simultaneously process multiple sources of information and balance attention between sources (Kahneman, 1973; Lang, 2000), multitasking, compared to single-tasking, becomes increasingly demanding in terms of cognitive resources (Jackson et al., 2023). With every additional task, there is less cognitive capacity available for each task, particularly from an attention perspective. Though sensory selection and central processing operate independently, the need for attention creates a bottleneck and limits the amount of parallel processing that can occur, resulting in negative effects of DTI (e.g., Jackson et al., 2023; Lang & Chrzan, 2015; Segijn et al., 2019).

A second critical factor that influences DTI is automaticity, which reflects the automation of activities wherein the mental operation proceeds without voluntary control and does not require processing capacity (Pashler, 1994). With tasks that are familiar or practiced (e.g., having a conversation with a passenger is less disruptive for an experienced driver compared to a novice driver), the demands on attention and cognitive processing associated with performing tasks are reduced, leading to efficient processing (Logan, 1979). This is also consistent with studies that show a habituation effect with DTI, where the effect of interference between tasks diminishes over time as individuals start to process the tasks in a more automated manner (Vance et al., 2017).

Finally, DTI research has found that increased similarity between tasks may affect dual-task performance positively or negatively. When two tasks have coherent inputs, common processing routines, or compatible stimulusresponse mappings or goals, task performance may improve because the coordination or integration of subtasks is facilitated (e.g., Duncan, 1979; Peters, 1977). On the other hand, task similarity may hamper task performance when tasks are independent and do not employ coherent or common processes (Korteling, 1993; Navon & Miller, 1987).

More recently, scholars (e.g., Jenkins et al., 2016; Vance et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2020) have begun to investigate ways of improving or optimizing performance within the taskinterrupt interference. For example, studies have focused on optimizing the design and/or distribution of interrupts such as security alerts while using a computer or mobile device to improve the effectiveness of the interrupt. These studies have found that strategically timing the interrupt relative to the task (Jenkins et al., 2016) or using disruptive designs (Wu et al., 2020) improved the performance of the security alert. Moreover, avoiding oversaturation or habituation to the alert can positively increase desired alert performance (Vance et al., 2017).

Despite these findings, the literature is largely silent on a commonly encountered real-world scenario—namely, situations in which an interrupt is served to a user who is engaged in a task but is also engaged with another activity or activities in the environment. In these dynamic taskinterrupt interference contexts, the degree of engagement with the environment may change over time, which then moderates the performance of the task and the interrupt itself. We seek to address this gap in the literature by explicitly evaluating how the degree of engagement with the environment influences the performance of the interrupt.

## Mobile Advertising and DTI

The advent and adoption of mobile devices has created new advertising opportunities, with researchers and marketers alike seeking to understand their effectiveness. Several studies have evaluated consumers’ attitudes towards mobile advertising (e.g., Ghose & Han, 2014), consumers’ mobile browsing behaviors and e-commerce strategies (e.g., Zheng et al., 2019), factors affecting consumers’ use of mobile advertising (e.g., Rafieian & Yoganarasimhan, 2021; Todri et al., 2020), strategies to improve mobile advertising recommendations (e.g., Awad & Krishnan, 2006), and design factors affecting mobile advertising success (e.g., Abbasi et al., 2021).

We argue that mobile advertisements can also be evaluated using a DTI lens, where mobile ads interrupt the user’s engagement with a different task on the mobile device (similar to the context of information security studied in DTI literature where a security alert interrupts the task that a user performs on a device). In this mobile task-interrupt context, Abbasi et al. (2021) found that in-game pop-up ads can be optimized to improve ad effectiveness through various ad design options including incentives, personalization, entertainment, and credibility. Extending this further, studies on location-based mobile advertising have examined the impact of ads that are presented to a user through their mobile device based on their current location (e.g., entering a geo-enclosed area) (e.g., Andrews et al., 2016; Kurtz et al., 2021; Lin & Bautista, 2020; Wang et al., 2017). Although location-based mobile advertising suggests an impact of the environment (i.e., location) on ads, the focus of the existing literature in this area is exclusively on how characteristics of the location impact interrupt effectiveness (in the context of mobile advertising, this is characterized by how consumers react to pop-up ads). This research does not capture how characteristics of the location impact the task-interrupt ensemble (i.e., how does the location interact with the specific tasks that the user is engaged in on their mobile device when the pop-up ads occur). We seek to evaluate how the degree of attention paid to the environment outside the focal task systematically affects the effectiveness of the interrupt.

Finally, a third stream of research has focused on evaluating the effect of media multitasking on ad evaluations in multiscreen contexts (Chinchanachokchai et al., 2015). These studies have consistently found a decrease in memory recall and recognition, but have largely focused on comparing performance in multiscreen groups against reference groups such as single screen groups (e.g., Lang & Chrzan, 2015; Segijn et al., 2019) rather than trying to maximize performance within multiscreen environments. Additionally, the majority of these studies have focused on TV as opposed to mobile phone advertising (e.g., Chinchanachokchai et al., 2015; Duff & Sar, 2015)—for example, ads embedded into a TV show during commercial breaks (e.g., Kazakova et al., 2016) or perimeter billboard ads during a sport event (e.g., Angell et al., 2016). Such approaches ignore mobile screen advertising, despite the fact that it is increasingly commanding individuals’ attention (Lopez-Gonzalez et al., 2018).

In summary, extant studies on mobile advertising examine factors such as location or multiscreen viewing but do not consider how the attention of the consumer is consumed by the consumers’ tasks versus the environment. For instance, how can marketers optimize the presentation of pop-up ads while a consumer is engaged in a task on their mobile device based on dynamic changes in the environment (such as watching a live sporting event) and on the relative attention paid to the mobile screen versus the environment. In this study, we seek to address this gap by evaluating how the effectiveness of pop-up ads during a mobile task can be optimized based on knowledge about the level of engagement with the environment.

## Hypothesis Development

We first evaluated the effect of task engagement on interrupt processing (similar to task-interrupt interference in previous studies), in the presence of distractions (i.e., a second task on the same device, such as listening to music; a second task on a different device, such as watching television; and/or watching a child at a busy playground). Several past studies, particularly in cross-model contexts, suggest that the involuntary attention activated by the first stimulus can impact the processing of the second stimulus. For example, Shen and Sengupta (2014) proposed a two-stage model of involuntary and voluntary attention where even an aversive noise can produce an initial facilitation effect for visual stimuli (because of an involuntary appraisal mechanism) before a more deliberative attentionallocation produces impairment. This impairment in performance is seen in traditional DTI scenarios in the IS literature (Jenkins et al., 2016; Vance et al., 2017), where attention is divided between a task and an interrupt on a single device (i.e., computer). Therefore, focusing on the task may draw attention away from the interrupt, as individuals actively seek to avoid the distraction from the interrupt when performing the task. However, in the presence of additional distractors, we theorize that the task and interrupt will be processed as a single entity with increased automaticity (Pashler, 1994). Hence, focusing on the task-interrupt ensemble draws attention away from the environment. Thus, when individuals pay more attention to the task relative to their environment, they are also more likely to process the interrupt more efficiently in an involuntary manner.Thus, if an individual is more engaged with the task in the face of environmental distractions, they are more likely to be aware of interrupts that occur during the task. Hence, in contrast to past studies on DTI, we expect a facilitating relationship between attention paid to the task and interrupt effectiveness.

H1: There is a positive relationship between task engagement and interrupt effectiveness.

Next, we explore how the degree of engagement with the environment affects the effectiveness of processing interrupts during a task. Attention and engagement with the environment can be influenced by a variety of factors. We focused specifically on two key factors that moderate the engagement levels of the environment—the content and the distance of the distraction in the environment—holding all other factors constant.

Recent DTI research has shown that manipulating the interrupt to make it more dynamic, visual, or salient can increase the performance of the interrupt (Jenkins et al., 2016; Vance et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2020). This body of research has largely focused on the singular task-interrupt context and has shown how making the interrupt more salient/dynamic improves the performance of the interrupt. However, when the consumer’s attention is split between a task and another activity in the environment, the relative attention to the task versus the environment is likely to impact the performance of the interrupt. In such dynamic contexts, users often do not want to miss important contextual information; thus, it is important that their attention is allocated to the right activity at the right time (Vanattenhoven & Geerts, 2017). For example, research has shown that employees show the highest level of work engagement in the morning (Sonnentag & Kühnel, 2016) and are more likely to notice a security alert on their computer at that time. Consequently, we extend the existing IS literature related to task-interrupt interference (e.g., Jenkins et al., 2016; Vance et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2020) by showing that strategically manipulating the timing of the interrupt relative to the environment should also maximize the interrupt’s effectiveness. Specifically, at times when a user is likely to be highly engaged with the environment, they will pay less attention to the interrupt. Therefore, our second hypothesis is:

H2: Lower levels of engagement with the environment lead to higher interrupt effectiveness.

Prior literature has shown that accurately contextualizing information makes information appear more beneficial, as close agreement between message and context improves message efficacy (Cannon, 1982; Tripathi & Siddiqui, 2011). From the DTI perspective, prior research has highlighted that when two tasks have coherent subtasks, task performance may improve because the coordination or integration of subtasks is facilitated (e.g., Duncan, 1979; Peters, 1977). However, this body of research has focused on the impact of congruence within tasktask interference in isolation (see Figure 1 and Table E1), and it is not clear whether the impact of congruence can extend to task-task or task-interrupt ensembles in more dynamic contexts.

The relevance accessibility framework (Lynch et al., 1988) outlines how consumers are more likely to evaluate a message positively when it is both accessible and relevant (Baker & Lutz, 2013). Strategically manipulating interrupts to make them congruent relative to the activities in the environment should positively impact interrupt performance in DTI contexts. Thus, we hypothesize that when a user’s attention is divided between a task and the environment, congruence of the interrupt with the environment will lead to higher interrupt performance:

H3: A higher degree of congruence between the interrupt and the environment improves interrupt effectiveness.

Another factor that moderates the sharing of attention between a task and the environment is the physical distance between the device on which the task is performed and the distraction. The split attention effect posits that presenting learning materials at a greater spatial distance induces higher cognitive overload than presenting materials closer together (Ayres & Sweller, 2005; Mayer & Fiorella, 2014). This causes the mental integration of the content to be diluted because while processing it in working memory, users have to constantly reorientate when moving between the spatially distanced materials, imposing a high extraneous cognitive load (Ayres & Sweller, 2005; Kalyuga et al., 1998; Sweller et al., 1998). Similarly, from the DTI perspective, attention is a finite resource, and individuals have limited cognitive capacity to process information presented to them (Lang, 2000; Kahneman, 1973; Pashler, 1994). When performing multiple tasks that compete for attention, individuals must share their attentional capacity between the two tasks (Kahneman, 1973; McLeod, 1977). This graded capacity sharing, however, would be expected to work better when the spatial distance between tasks is small versus when it is large. In the context of DTI in a dynamic environment, we posit that attention allocation between the task and the distraction is moderated by the distance between the two activities, which, in turn, moderates the facilitating relation between task engagement and interrupt effectiveness. Therefore, our final hypothesis is:

H4: The effect of task engagement on interrupt effectiveness is moderated by the distance between the task and the environment, such that increasing the distance between the task and the environment reduces the impact of task engagement on interrupt effectiveness.

## Research Design

To test our hypotheses, we conducted a series of independent studies to assess the potential impact of the environment on task-interrupt interference. For all three studies, we simulated a typical task-interrupt scenario with a mobile device (i.e., a user engaged with a task on a mobile phone with occasional pop-up ads interrupting the task). To account for the presence of other tasks or distractions that a user may simultaneously be engaged with, we created a dynamic environment where users watched an NFL game while engaging in the task-interrupt ensemble. Participants were instructed to focus on both activities (i.e., mobile device task and task in the environment) and told that their memory and performance were being monitored and evaluated on both activities. Specifically, at the beginning of the study, participants were instructed that their compensation would depend on how well they performed on both tasks simultaneously (i.e., how well they watched the NFL game closely while also attempting to correctly solve as many anagrams as possible). Therefore, they were largely agnostic to our operationalizations of task, interrupt, and the environment, which increases the generalizability of our findings to other contexts.

Lab and online experiments were selected to add rigor and more control over our experimental design. At the end of the study, participants completed an online survey. For ease of exposition, an overview of the entire experimental procedure is depicted in Figure 2 below. The top horizontal bar (labeled “External”) depicts the temporal sequence of events for the NFL game (i.e., play, replay, and commentary segments interspaced with each other). The second horizontal bar (labeled “Mobile”) depicts the temporal sequence of events for a user on their mobile device playing an anagram game (represented in blue) with ad interrupts at periodic intervals (depicted by the orange). Figure 2 also depicts the anagram game as well as an example of each of the three types of ads (1) congruent, (2) semi-congruent, and (3) incongruent.

For the dynamic environment, we focused on a clip of a recent NFL game. An NFL game was selected because of (1) its dominance and popularity as a sport in the U.S. and in TV broadcasting (Schneider, 2020) and (2) its proportion of supplemental and core content in a broadcast (e.g., the average NFL game features only 11 minutes when the ball is in play, with 56% more time devoted to replays—Biderman, 2010). We selected this stimulus since live sport has been dubbed “the most valuable content on the planet” with respect to advertising (James, 2016, p. 3,11). We chose an anagram game for the mobile task since it (1) is representative of typical tasks performed on a mobile device in terms of attention and engagement, (2) has been used in past multiscreen and advertising research as a task (Segijn et al., 2019), and (3) is one of the most popular types of mobile games (Grannell, 2021). Finally, pop-up ads were chosen as the interrupt because (1) they are common in free mobile games and thus represent a typical user experience and (2) they comprise a type of mobile advertising expected to generate maximum revenue (Fortune Business Insights, 2023). Similar to prior DTI research on information security (where a security alert is the interrupt), we labeled the popup as the interrupt, and the activity that it interrupts as the task. Anything external to the task-interrupt ensemble was labeled as the environment, which we operationalized as the NFL game.

## Stimulus Development

To select our content for the environment, we reviewed games from the local NFL team’s recent regular season. Following research precedent (Breuer & Rumpf, 2012; Jensen et al., 2015), an actual clip of a previously broadcasted game was used, as it closely approximates a typical sport viewing experience. The local team was selected to increase relevance for the participants. A recent, regular season game was used so that the final clip featured similar rules and viewing experiences (e.g., same star players, head coach, etc.), but was distant enough that participants would have been unlikely to remember the game if they had watched it live. The final clip was the first 13:40 minutes coverage of the game,<sup>5</sup> providing sufficient time for a multiscreen viewing experience and presentation of the nine pop-up ads distributed across various moments of the game. The game clip itself did not feature any commercial breaks, allowing the emphasis to be placed on the game content variation. The NFL clip was manually classified second by second into one of three types of content: play, replay, and commentary using objective game elements such as the game clock, flags, and referee whistles. Since this clip was of an actual game, there was a natural flow and randomization between the segments (i.e. play, replay, and commentary segments were naturally distributed across the clip).

For the mobile gaming task, we developed a custom app using JavaScript/HTML that simulated an anagram game for this study (Appendix B, Figure B1). In the custom app, users were presented with a series of scrambled six-letter words and were asked to reorder the letters to create a word. There were no time limits for solving the anagrams, and participants could enter any six-letter word to move to the next anagram. A custom app was necessary to control what participants did on the mobile screen and the timing of the pop-up ads. We selected an anagram game because it is representative of mobile games that people play in real life. Recent industry reports have found that playing mobile games is one of the most common activities people engage in while watching TV (PCMag Staff, 2018; Ortiz-Luis, 2021). Evidenced by Wordle (a word game) becoming one of the most popular mobile games in 2022 (Stedman, 2022; Conversation, 2022), our choice of an anagram game as the DTI task reflects one of the popular game types that consumers engage in while in a multiscreen environment. The anagram game was interrupted randomly by nine popup ads at pre-determined times based on the content of the NFL game.

Each participant saw the same set of ads appearing individually at pre-set times based on the NFL clip, though the order of ads was randomized across participants.<sup>6</sup> Three ads appeared during action content (i.e., play) and six ads appeared during supplemental content (three during replays and three during commentary). The ads were distributed as evenly as possible throughout the clip. We chose the segments that featured ads to ensure an early, midpoint, and late slot for each of the three ads of a given segment category and made sure that the order of the play, replay, and commentary segment categories was varied to avoid repetition. For each ad interrupt, the user could actively close it and return to the anagram task by pressing either the “close” or “interested” button. Alternatively, the ad closed automatically (timed out) after 15 seconds. Therefore, a participant watching the NFL game exclusively could have an ad pop up and time out without ever seeing it. To ensure that the ad could pop up and time out within the same segment, the longest possible segments (i.e., at least 20- second segments) were selected for the ads’ presentation.

Finally, we varied the ads’ congruence with the NFL content to see if it influenced the processing of the ad interrupts (Myers et al., 2014). Specifically, we varied the actors (or spokesperson) and brands featured in the nine ads to generate three levels of congruence with the NFL game: (1) congruent, where both the actor and brand were relevant, (2) semi-congruent, where the brand was relevant but featured an irrelevant actor, and (3) incongruent, where both actor and brands were irrelevant to the NFL game. The app was programmed in such a way that each of the three levels of congruence were randomly distributed across the three time segments for each participant. We discuss more details about the congruence design in Appendix C.

## Measures

Data were collected from two sources: Since we developed a custom app for this study, we (1) collected user behavior from the log data and (2) administered an online survey to each participant after the experiment. Each participant was given a unique ID number that was used to match their responses between the two data sources while maintaining anonymity. The app recorded details about each anagram that was presented to the participant, the response submitted for that anagram, and the response time for each anagram. The app also recorded details about the ad, the time at which it was presented with respect to the events on the NFL game, the response to the ad (i.e., close, like, timed out), and the response time. Table A1 (Appendix A) details all measures collected using the online survey.

A surprise memory test for the content of the ad was administered to all participants at the end of the survey (Aaker, 1992; Beard et al., 2024 Venkatraman et al., 2021; Walsh et al., 2008). Participants were asked to indicate if they remembered seeing an ad for a given brand across a list of 12 brands (i.e., the nine featured brands from the ads shown to the participant and three decoy brands). Interrupt effectiveness was computed for the nine featured brands, with one point awarded for each correctly recognized brand. We refer to this interrupt effectiveness measure as ad engagement in the rest of our paper. Ad engagement scores were computed separately for each of the three time slots (i.e., replay, commentary, and action) and three congruence levels (i.e., incongruent, semi-congruent, and congruent) by summing the number of brands correctly recognized within the time slot / congruence level.

Task engagement was measured using a composite score that reflected the participant’s performance in the anagram game. Participants received one point for every anagram solved correctly and half a point for every incorrect attempt. Therefore, the composite task engagement measure reflected both quantity and accuracy, as completing an anagram, even incorrectly, takes time and attention and should be captured in the performance measure. Yet solving it correctly arguably takes more time and attention and represents greater engagement with the task.

We also collected relevant control variables. Following prior literature (e.g., Van Cauwenberge et al., 2014) we collected information pertaining to the propensity to engage in multiscreen viewing to ensure that our results were not confounded by the participants’ experience levels (i.e., multiscreen usage propensity). Since sport fandom levels are related to media consumption (Reams & Havard, 2017) and how sport viewers use their mobile devices (Rubenking & Lewis, 2016), we controlled for psychological involvement with the NFL (i.e., involvement). We also measured the perceived difficulty associated with solving the anagrams (i.e., perceived anagram difficulty). Moreover, since brand familiarity can impact ad memory in multiscreen environments (Segijn & Eisend, 2019), we controlled for it by including the average brand familiarity level (i.e., average brand familiarity). Finally, since younger consumers are more likely to engage in multitasking (Courtois & D’heer, 2012), we controlled for participant age. Similarly, since there may be differences in multitasking across genders and ethnicities, we controlled for these as well. The definition of all variables is provided in Table A1.

In Study 1A, we tested H1, H2, and H3 using data from a lab experiment featuring the use of mobile phones for the taskinterrupt ensemble and a TV screen for the NFL game. In Study 1B, we performed an online experiment on Prolific to test our hypotheses using a non-student sample. This design featured a split-screen, with the task-interrupt ensemble appearing on half the screen and the NFL on the other half. Finally, in Study 2, we tested H4 using a lab experiment featuring three conditions that varied the distance between the task and the environment, altering how the task-interrupt ensemble and NFL game were displayed across conditions.

## Results

## Study 1A

For Study 1A, we recruited 150 students at a large university in the northeast United States to participate in our study. Students are an appropriate sample for this study since they are the most likely to engage in multiscreen behavior in their everyday lives (Jensen et al., 2018; Courtois & D’heer, 2012). Moreover, recent reports have shown how multiscreen viewing has increased the most among Generation Z yearover-year (Nielsen, 2022). Twenty-seven participants were excluded due to failed attention checks or incomplete responses, leaving 123 usable participants (79 men and 44 women). Participants were between the ages of 18 and 26, with an average age of 19 (SD = 1.20). Most participants were White/Caucasian (58.5%), followed by Asian (17.9%), African American (14.6%), and then Hispanic (6.5%). Table 1 outlines the descriptive statistics from this study with respect to the focal variables.

We tested H1 by performing an OLS regression to evaluate the effect of task engagement on ad engagement. There was a marginally significant impact of task engagement on ad engagement (Table 2), both with and without control variables, providing support for H1. The intuition is that increased engagement with the anagram task in the face of environmental distractions influences both the performance in the task as well as the strength of encoding of the ad interrupts, which in turn results in improved recognition accuracy of the corresponding ads even after a delay.

To test H2, we investigated the impact of ad engagement relative to the timing of the NFL game using a one-way repeated measures ANOVA. The results indicate a significant timing effect; Wilks’s Lambda = 0.911, F(2, 123) = 5.891, p < 0.004. Tables F1 and F2 in the Appendix F report the respective means and the within-subjects contrast F-test for significant differences in means among the three levels of timing. Next, we assessed for pairwise differences among the levels (Table 3). There were significant differences between core action and the supplemental categories, but not within supplemental categories. Thus, we found that when engagement with the environment was highest (i.e., action), ad engagement was lowest, supporting H2.

Next, we investigated the impact of congruence. Tables F3 and F4 in the Appendix F report the respective means and the within-subjects contrast F-test for significant differences in means among the three levels of congruence. We used a repeated-measures one-way ANOVA to test the effect of ad congruence on ad engagement (H3) and found a significant effect; Wilks’s Lambda = 0.899, F(2, 123) = 6.814, p < 0.001. Congruent ads were recognized significantly more than semiand incongruent ads, and semi-congruent ads were recognized significantly more than incongruent ads (Table 3), supporting H3.

## Study 1B

In Study 1B, we explored the robustness of our findings from Study 1A in an online experiment using a non-student sample from the online platform Prolific. We used a split-screen design in which both the task and the interrupt (task-interrupt ensemble) appeared on one half of the screen, and the distraction from the environment was simulated on the other half of the screen. This allowed us to evaluate the effect of the environment when it was on the same device as the taskinterrupt ensemble, in addition to allowing us to conduct the experiment online, where the use of multiple screens is not feasible. We created a website with the NFL game on the left half of the screen and the anagram task (with pop-up ads) on the right half of the screen (refer to Appendix B Figure B2 for an example of the split-screen design). The split-screen design is also consistent with the advent of social TV, which has grown in popularity and received recent research attention (e.g., Cesar & Geerts, 2011; Shin, 2013). Moreover, prior advertising literature has designed experiments investigating multiscreen viewing using single split-screens rather than multiple separate screens (Segijn & Eisend, 2019). To allow for sufficient screen space and resolution, participants had to use their computers to access the website (no mobile phones were allowed for this task).

Specifically, we found a positive relationship between task engagement and ad engagement. ## Study 2

In Study 2, we replicated the experimental settings from Studies 1A and 1B with an added manipulation for the effect of the environment by changing the spatial distance between the environment and the task<sup>7</sup>. Specifically, we divided our participants randomly across three separate conditions: (1) multiscreen-projector, which featured a multiscreen set-up similar to Study 1A, where the NFL game was displayed on a projector screen and the anagram task was completed on a personal mobile phone/device (refer to Appendix B Figure B4 for example of the multiscreen-projector design), (2) multiscreen-personal, where the NFL game was shown on a personal laptop screen and the anagram tasks were completed on a personal mobile phone/device<sup>8</sup> (refer to Appendix B Figure B3 for example of the multiscreen-personal design), and (3) split-screen, which was a split-screen set-up similar to Study 1B, where both the NFL game and the anagram task were completed on a single personal laptop screen (refer to Appendix B Figure B2 for example of the split-screen design). In this set-up, the distance between the task and the environment increased across the three conditions in the following order: split-screen, multiscreen-personal, and multiscreen-projector. Figures B2, B3, and B4 in Appendix B provide visuals of each of these three treatment designs splitscreen, multiscreen-personal, and multiscreen-projector, respectively. We created a categorical variable distance to capture this such that for the split-screen condition, distance = 0 (baseline) = distance<sub>0</sub>; for the multiscreen-personal condition, distance = 1 = distance ; and for the multiscreenprojector condition, distance = 2 = distance<sub>2</sub>. This design allowed us to evaluate how the spatial distance between the task and the environmental distraction impacted consumers ability to engage with the task and the interrupts. Finally, the conditions were randomly assigned to address any concerns about differences in samples driving our findings.

We recruited 227 students at a large university in the northeast United States to participate in Study 2. Of the recruited subjects, 29 participants were excluded as they did not complete the full experiment or failed attention check questions in the survey, resulting in a final sample of 198 participants (104 men, 94 women) with 68 in the split-screen condition, 71 in the multiscreen-personal, and 59 in the multiscreen-projector. Participants were between ages 18 and 40, with an average age of 20.25 (SD = 1.87). Most participants were White/Caucasian (64.7%), followed by Asian (19.7%), African American (9.8%), Hispanic (1.2%), and other (4.6%). Table 7 shows the descriptive statistics of the focal variables for the study by distance.

Across all measures, including our focal measures (i.e., task engagement, and ad engagement), participants in the splitscreen condition performed better compared to the two multiscreen conditions (Table 7). This provides evidence that users may be able to engage better when the task and environmental distraction are on a split-screen due to spatial proximity. When the task and distraction are spatially separated on multiple screens, users must keep switching their attention from one screen due to the distance between them. We also observed this from additional survey measures in Study 2, where we asked participants “how disruptive participants thought the NFL game was on a scale of 1 (least disruptive) to 5 (very disruptive).” We show that this NFLDisruption measure varied significantly with distance (F(2, 195) = 4.56, p < 0.0115). Specifically, participants found the distraction to be lowest in the splitscreen condition relative to the multi-screen groups (Table D2, Appendix D). This aligns with the results from Study 1B showing that it is easier to allocate attention in a split-screen condition versus a multiscreen condition.

We examined the effect of task engagement on ad engagement, relative to the distance between the screens (Table 8). There was a significant positive effect of task engagement on ad engagement, consistent with previous studies and supporting H1.

Looking at the interaction effect of task engagement and distance, the effect of task engagement on ad engagement was moderated by the distance between the personal screen and the environmental distraction. In the split-screen condition, the environmental distraction was co-located with the task and the interrupts were on the same screen, making it easier for participants to divide their attention; however, the increasing distance in the multiscreen conditions impacted the ability to distribute attention between the stimuli from the environment and the task, impacting the performance of both task engagement and ad engagement. Thus, H4 is supported.<sup>9</sup>

Tables F9 and F10 in the Appendix F report the respective means and the within-subjects contrast F-test for significant differences in means among the three levels of timing. Next, as with Studies 1A and 1B, pairwise differences among the levels were assessed by employing a one-way repeated measures ANOVA to evaluate if there were significant differences in ad engagement that were dependent on ad timing (H2), but this time, we controlled for distance (by adding distance as a covariate). The results indicated a significant timing effect; Wilks’ Lambda = 0.923, F(2, 198) = 8.152, p < 0.001. There was a significant pairwise difference between action and supplemental content (Table 9). Therefore, H2 was supported as ad engagement was dependent on timing, with the highest level of recognition occurring during supplemental content.

Similarly, the level of congruence influenced ad engagement; Wilks’s Lambda =0.838, F(2, 198) = 18.153, p < 0.001 after controlling for distance. Follow-up comparisons indicated that ad engagement was significantly higher for congruent ads than for semi-congruent and incongruent ads, but there was no significant difference between semi-congruent and incongruent ads (Table 9).<sup>10</sup> Therefore, H3 was also supported.

## Robustness Checks

To further test the robustness of our results, we performed additional tests as follows: (1) a Poisson model to account for ad engagement being a count variable and (2) a repeated trials model to control for brand familiarity at the ad level.

## Alternate Specifications

Since ad engagement could be considered a count variable, as it ranges between 1 and 9, we tested our model using a Poisson specification (Table 10). The results were consistent with our previous analyses.

## User-Ad Level Analysis

In our analyses thus far, we treated each individual participant as a separate, single observation. Since participants might have already been familiar with the brands we featured, we controlled for the average brand familiarity (i.e., average brand familiarity score for the nine ads) across the studies. However, given that brand familiarity could differ substantially by participant and by ad, we confirmed our findings using a multilevel model that controlled for brand familiarity at the ad level. To do this, we transformed the data to the level of individual trials (i.e., each time a user sees an ad), resulting in nine observations for each user. We then controlled for the effect of individual brand familiarity with the brand featured in a given ad. Since task engagement was calculated at the individual level and independent from ad engagement, we focused on the effects of timing and congruence for this analysis.

With the transformed data, we defined two categorical variables: timing (i = 1, 2, 3 where 1 = action [baseline], 2 = replay, and 3 = commentary) and congruence (j = 1, 2, 3 where 1 = congruent [baseline], 2 = semi-congruent, and 3 = incongruent). We defined the dependent variable AdRecogTrial to indicate ad recognition at the user-ad level, with each user and each ad constituting one observation. In other words, AdRecogTrial<sub>ij</sub> = 0 if the user i did not recognize ad j, and 1 otherwise. We fit a multilevel logit model and controlled for brand familiarity at the trial level (AdFamilairityTrial). To account for individual differences, we added fixed effects at the individual level. We also clustered standard errors at the individual level.

Compared to action, both commentary and replay had a positive and significant effect in all three studies, supporting H2. Similarly, compared to congruent ads, both semicongruent and incongruent ads had negative and significant effects in both studies, supporting H3. Thus, the results from the repeated trials model were consistent with prior analyses across all three studies (Table 11), suggesting that our results remained robust after controlling for familiarity at the ad level. Also, as expected, the timing and congruence results remained consistent even after controlling for the distance between the screens in Study 2 (Table 11, Column 4).<sup>11</sup>

## Discussion

While the growth of mobile advertising provides opportunities for marketers to target customers, its effectiveness is complicated by the fact that mobile users are frequently distracted by other activities while using a mobile device. Researchers often rely on the theoretical lens of DTI to understand the allocation of attention in task-interrupt contexts (Jenkins et al., 2016; Kao & Pierro, 2022; Plummer et al., 2015), but this body of literature typically looks at DTI in static contexts where users’ attention is confined to the tasktask or task-interrupt ensembles in isolation and not diffused by environmental distractions. These studies generally focus on either improving the performance of the main task (Jenkins et al., 2016) or that of the interrupting task (Vance et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2020). However, mobile advertising often exists in dynamic environments where, in addition to the task-interrupt ensemble, distractions from the environment can influence the allocation of users’ attention. Our research provides important insights into how the diffusion of a user’s attention in such dynamic contexts (between the task-interrupt ensemble and the environment) impacts DTI performance. Our research addresses this critical gap and extends the existing literature by demonstrating how mobile advertisers can optimize the design and delivery of ads, given that mobile devices are often used in distraction-filled environments.

Our results indicate that when designing mobile ads, advertisers should account for the context in which the user is likely to consume that ad. Across all three studies, we found a facilitating effect between task engagement and ad engagement, suggesting that in the presence of distractions, the task and interrupt are processed as a single entity with increased automaticity (Pashler, 1994). In other words, if an individual is more engaged with a personal task in the face of environmental distractions, they are more likely to be aware of interrupts on the same device. Our results also indicate that strategically timing mobile ads based on the activities occurring in the environment and customizing ad content to make it congruent with the environment can further improve ad engagement. This finding suggests that consumers may develop coping or balancing strategies to navigate dual-task complexities in distraction-filled environments. Taken together, the impact of timing and congruence underscores the importance of marketers considering environmental factors that may impact DTI. Finally, we found that the effect of task engagement on interrupt effectiveness is moderated by the distance between the task-interrupt ensemble and the environment, wherein increasing the distance between the task-interrupt ensemble and the environment impacts the amount of attention dissipated, which in turn impacts interrupt effectiveness.

## Theoretical Contributions

Our study makes multiple contributions across various research domains. First and foremost, this research contributes to our understanding of DTI by evaluating the impact of distractions in the environment on DTI performance. Prior studies have found that strategically timing an alert, such as timing the alert between primary tasks (Jenkins et al., 2016), using disruptive designs (Wu et al., 2020), and avoiding oversaturation or habituation to an alert (Vance et al., 2017), can positively increase alert performance. While these studies build a valuable foundation of knowledge for optimizing interrupts in taskinterrupt ensembles, they examine DTI in a static context where the user is focused on the task-interrupt ensemble in isolation. However, dual-task contexts in the real world, such as mobile advertising, are likely to exist in the presence of environmental distractions. Our research provides important insights into how such dynamic contexts impact DTI performance. For example, we reveal a facilitating relationship between the attention a user pays to a task on a mobile device and the performance of an interrupt in the presence of distractions. This contrasts with traditional DTI literature, which has shown an inhibiting relationship (Jenkins et al., 2016; Kao & Pierro, 2022; Plummer et al., 2015). While Pashler’s (1994) bottleneck model primarily focused on understanding how attention is allocated between two tasks that compete for central cognitive resources within controlled experimental settings, our research extends this model by examining how such cognitive constraints operate in real-world settings where environmental stimuli add distractions to the task-interrupt context; these stimuli can vary greatly in intensity, type, and timing. Pashler’s (1994) model of sequential processing of tasks due to bottlenecks that limit the effectiveness of multitasking is exacerbated in dynamic environments compared to static ones. Our research highlights how syncing interrupts with moments of less cognitive demand or increasing their salience (via congruence) can be helpful for piercing through the bottleneck, allowing for quicker and more effective processing. The presence of distractions in the environment diffuses users’ attention outside the task-interrupt ensemble such that participants who are engaged with the task are more likely to attend to and encode the content of the interrupt (i.e., pop-up ads), compared to participants who engage more with the environmental distraction. We explored potential boundary conditions of the bottleneck effect by assessing how the distance between the environmental distraction and the task on the mobile device influences the relation between task performance and interrupt performance. In other words, we show how distance impacts the diffusion of attention, a factor not typically evaluated in laboratory settings. Thus, we extend prior DTI research by suggesting that researchers should seek to control or account for the environment when considering DTI.

Second, we contribute to the mobile advertising literature by considering ads within their overarching context. Prior research considering mobile ads, including pop-up ads, has typically examined such ads in isolation rather than as part of the complex environment that consumers generally find themselves in. While location-based mobile advertising suggests an ideal time and/or place to advertise based on the physical context, existing scholarship has yet to consider the task-interrupt ensemble as a whole, and how attention diffuses between the ensemble tasks and the environment. Our results underscore the complexity of mobile advertising, revealing that understanding how much attention is paid to a task on a mobile phone relative to distractions, can help advertisers better contextualize ads. Thus, theoretically, our findings inform future mobile advertising research on the importance of considering consumers’ environments and the potential of leveraging the environment to optimize mobile ads.

Third, this research contributes to the existing multiscreen viewing and advertising literature. We focused on optimizing ads within a multiscreen environment rather than comparing single and multiscreen conditions (Lang & Chrzan, 2015; Segijn et al., 2019), which provides insights into how consumers digest media. Our research also highlights the potential of optimizing mobile ads by making them congruent with likely distractions in consumers’ environments.

In addition to these three major theoretical contributions, our findings also provide insights into other research domains. For instance, we advance the understanding of ad congruence by demonstrating congruence with content beyond the device on which the ad is served. Specifically, we found that ad recognition was higher for ads that were congruent rather than incongruent with the environment in which they were processed. Thus, optimizing ads to make them not only synchronized but also contextually complementary with consumers’ environments can significantly influence consumer behavior. Cross-screen congruence thus represents a strategic evolution from single device congruence in terms of how advertisers can more effectively reach and engage consumers in the dynamic environments in which consumers find themselves. This approach acknowledges the fragmented nature of modern media consumption and offers a pathway to create more cohesive, impactful advertising experiences that resonate with consumers across their digital touchpoints. Our results also confirm the value of cross-device congruence within a multiscreen viewing environment, regardless of the distance between the environment and the task. Thus, theoretically, by focusing on cross-screen congruence, our work extends traditional definitions of advertising congruence—typically confined to the alignment between an ad and its immediate context—to include the coordination and harmony of advertising content across different devices that consumers may be using simultaneously. This broader view of congruence reflects the complexities of modern media consumption and provides a new lens through which to examine advertising effectiveness.

## Practical Implications

Our research has broad implications for the practice of mobile advertising. First, given the advances in location-based advertising and ad-syncing technology, advertisers can now estimate the distance between a mobile user and likely environmental distractions. For instance, in the TV watching context, companies such as Dialekta<sup>12</sup> and Goldbach<sup>13</sup> are using ACR technology<sup>14</sup> to not only detect exact times when TV content is broadcasted, triggering the delivery of relevant digital ads to the TV viewer’s mobile device, but also to detect the size and aspect ratio of the screen the user is engaged on (using screen resolution detection<sup>15</sup>) thereby giving them an idea whether a user is viewing a split-screen or multiple screens. This contextual information can then be used by advertisers to send targeted ads to users. While the distraction context for our experiment has been conceptualized as TV viewing, we believe our results are generalizable to other distractions as well.

Advertisers could thus capitalize on consumers’ relative attention depending on the distance between the consumer’s mobile device and the source of distraction to push ads to consumers’ mobile devices with a higher probability of engagement. Whereas for greater distances (such as users on multiple screens or users in a sports stadium) advertisers need to strategically place their content to catch users’ attention across distractions, for lesser distances (like split-screens) advertisers have more leeway in placing their ads, as users can more easily balance their attention between the task and the distraction.

Next, we recommend that advertisers make their ads congruent with likely environmental distractions. For example, when targeting mobile device users in sports stadiums, advertisers could feature relevant spokespeople (e.g., a player on the local sports team) or advertise congruent products (e.g., Gatorade rather than Tropicana).

Leveraging advances in synced advertising, we also recommend that ad timing be manipulated relative to external content. For instance, in the TV viewing context, marketers should strive to time their mobile ads at times when distractions are likely to be lower, such as during non-live content or during TV commercial breaks. Similarly, organizations selling ad spots/promotions could highlight the optimal times to place mobile ads and charge a premium.

Finally, synced advertising in coordination with IoT devices like Google Home and Amazon Echo could play a pivotal role in enhancing content synchronization and ad placement by providing real-time data on user engagement and environmental factors. For example, Google Home can detect ambient environmental conditions (e.g., lighting, sound levels, or the presence of multiple people) which could inform the timing and type of ads to be delivered.<sup>16</sup> Similarly, devices such as Google Home can use voice recognition and content-tracking technologies to detect the kind of show or music being played in the environment, data that could then help advertisers deliver highly targeted ads that are contextually relevant to the content being consumed.

The aforementioned strategies should also be applicable in other contexts, such as optimizing security alerts on personal devices. First, understanding the user’s environment and the likely distractions can allow for better timing of security alerts. For instance, security notifications in the workplace are more likely to be effective when they are sent at times the employee is less distracted by their work screen (e.g., during morning hours). This approach helps ensure that the alert is not only noticed but also acted upon promptly. Moreover, synchronizing the content of the alert with the user’s current activity can increase its salience. For example, if a user is conducting financial transactions online, a security alert related to potential bank fraud could be timed to coincide with these activities for immediate relevance and urgency. This contextual relevance makes the alert seem less intrusive and more integral to the user’s current focus, enhancing the likelihood of user engagement and appropriate action. Finally, as users often operate multiple devices simultaneously, coordinating alerts across devices can amplify the visibility and perceived importance of the message. For example, if a user receives a security alert on their smartphone, having a corresponding notification appear on a nearby laptop or smartwatch can reinforce the urgency and encourage quicker response actions.

## Limitations and Future Directions

Though this research makes important theoretical and managerial contributions, there are a few limitations worth noting. First, as we used surveys as one of our methodological tools, some of our measures are not objective in nature (such as direct purchase/sales). While there is a relationship between intention and behavior, future research could consider objective measures such as ad conversion, purchase intentions, or sales to confirm our results. Second, future research could seek to enhance the generalizability of our findings. While a lab setting added rigor and more control over the experimental manipulations, a field study featuring push notifications while participants view a live game could increase the generalizability of our findings. Third, for our congruence construct, we considered two dimensions (spokesperson and brand). For our research, this was sufficient since we utilized congruence as one of the ways to measure the environmental impact on DTI. However, future research could deepen the theorization of congruence and look at other dimensions mentioned in the literature and evaluate whether it extends to dynamic contexts. Fourth, we used a specific task (an anagram game) on a mobile device with interrupts (ads) as our DTI task-ensemble setting. Future studies could investigate alternative tasks to see how this impacts the balance consumers maintain between the task and the environment. Fifth, we used sports as an example of live television content as the environment task since it is one of the most popular forms of live television content (Schneider, 2020). Although we expect that the results are not context specific, future research could look at other contexts, such as other kinds of entertainment or non-media/entertainment contexts, to further confirm the results and assess the impact of task difficulty level and attention level. Sixth, in our study, distance serves as a proxy for the diffusion of attention. As distance increases, the ability to switch attention between the tasks becomes increasingly difficult, resulting in more negative performance. This is similar to the attention bottleneck (Pashler, 1994). However, future research could look at the exact distance between the task and the distraction to see how it influences ad engagement. Finally, one could easily switch the task and environment; namely, make the ads interrupt the NFL game and have participants complete the anagram game as a distraction to the task-interrupt ensemble. While we believe our results would still hold, future research could investigate different combinations of task, interrupt, and environment operationalizations. These limitations notwithstanding, our paper takes an initial step towards understanding how to optimize the design and delivery of ads on mobile devices, given that mobile consumption takes place in distraction-filled environments.

## Conclusion

In summary, our research highlights the significant implications of dynamic task environments on mobile advertising effectiveness, particularly through the lens of DTI. We demonstrate that mobile advertising strategies need to adapt to the complexities of user engagement in terms of not only their mobile device but also their environment. Our findings suggest that attention diffusion between the task-interrupt ensemble and environmental distractions profoundly affects the processing and effectiveness of mobile ads. Strategies that consider the environmental context, such as ad congruence and strategic timing relative to distractions, enhance ad engagement. Therefore, marketers should prioritize these factors to optimize mobile advertising in distraction-rich settings. This research not only extends existing DTI theories by incorporating the impact of the environment but also provides practical insights for designing more effective mobile advertising strategies in our increasingly distraction-filled world.

## Appendix C

## Congruence Design Details

Ad congruence was incorporated using nine static image ads featuring various spokespersons (three NFL players, three singers, and three movie stars) with similar popularity levels (defined by their number of Twitter followers) and brands (e.g., New Balance, Under Armour, Tide Detergent, Gatorade, etc.) that sell similar product types. This resulted in a set of nine ads, each with a unique spokesperson and brand that varied from congruent to semi-congruent to incongruent. Examples of the congruent ads are shown in Figure B2. The app picked one ad randomly without replacement from each set to show during the nine segments. This ensured randomization was maintained at both the timing and congruence levels.

Because our ads were designed to pop up on consumers’ mobile phones and for a maximum period of 15 seconds, the small landscape, along with limited time of visibility, dictated our ad design. It was essential to give consumers enough information that they would remember the brand but not be overwhelmed. This motivated our choice of the congruence dimensions (i.e., a spokesperson advertising a product/brand following prior literature; Myers et al., 2014). Myers et al. (2014) considered three dimensions of congruence (i.e., actor, plot, and language). However, since they showed actual commercials, plot was an important dimension. In our context, we showed static ads on a mobile game. The choice of a spokesperson advertising a product/brand is commonly used in mobile advertising and advertising on game apps. Since, in our context, congruence is a measure of the influence of the environment on DTI, we focused on the impact of congruent content (vs. incongruent content) rather than exploring the different dimensions of congruence. Future research could deepen the theorization of congruence and look at other dimensions mentioned in the literature. This is highlighted in our Limitations and Future Directions section.

## Appendix E

## DTI Literature Review

A review of the literature on DTI in social science literature identifies two broad categories of work. A selection of relevant studies is overviewed in Table E1. First, one stream of work focuses on simultaneous continuous tasks. In these studies, two continuous tasks (e.g., watching television and reading the newspaper) are performed simultaneously on the same device or across different devices in isolation (i.e., free of distractions, noise, stimuli, etc.). We call this task-task interference. As evidenced by the review in Table E1, these studies feature task-task interference in static contexts, failing to account for the potential influence of the environment. Our study extends this line of work by investigating the effect of DTI in more dynamic contexts, reflecting the reality of most consumers’ task-tasks experiences.

Second, the other stream focuses on interruptions during a continuous task. In these studies, a continuous task is interrupted periodically with interrupts, pop-ups, notifications, etc. (e.g., security alerts on a computer) in isolation (i.e., free of external distractions, noise, stimuli, etc.). We call this task-interrupt interference. Consistent with studies related to task-task inference, studies examining task-interrupt interference consider the task-interrupt ensemble in isolation, free of any environmental distractions. Our work extends this area of research by considering the task-interrupt ensemble within dynamic contexts. Namely, we are interested in the impact of additional stimuli from the environment on task and interrupt performance. Through this examination, we identify how interrupts can be optimized based on the environment to enhance performance (e.g., brand recognition).
