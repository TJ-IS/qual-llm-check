# WHEN AND WHO DO PLATFORM COMPANIES ACQUIRE? UNDERSTANDING THE ROLE OF ACQUISITIONS IN THE GROWTH OF PLATFORM COMPANIES<sup>1</sup>

Milan Miric Marshall School of Business, University of Southern California, Los Angeles, CA 90089 U.S.A. {mmiric@marshall.usc.edu}

Margherita Pagani EM Lyon Business School, 6013- Écully, FRANCE {pagani@em-lyon.com}

Omar A. El Sawy Marshall School of Business, University of Southern California, Los Angeles, CA 90089 U.S.A. {elsawy@marshall.usc.edu}

The success of platform companies often depends on their ability to “scale” their customer and supplier base. Existing studies have focused on a variety of approaches that platforms may use to scale but have not system atically considered that platforms might acquire other companies as part of this growth strategy. In this paper, we study the acquisition patterns of digital platform companies and contrast these with the acquisition patterns of digital non-platform companies. We find that platform companies acquire earlier (shortly after founding) when compared with non-platform companies, and they often first acquire competing platform companies from the same market niche. As platform companies mature, they begin to acquire non-platform companies from other market niches. This contrasts with how acquisitions are made by non-platform companies, as shown in our analysis.

Keywords: Acquisitions, scaling digital platform companies, double-helix framework, digital platform ecosystem

## Introduction

There is a growing prevalence of platform companies that act as intermediaries between various suppliers and customers (Jacobides et al. 2018). The success of platform companies is often shaped by network effects, whereby the platform with the largest supplier base will attract the most customers and vice versa (Parker and Van Alstyne 2005; Rochet and Tirole 2003). As a result, the strategy for platforms is to “scale up”

the platform as soon as they are established, by attracting both suppliers and customers. Existing studies have identified a variety of approaches that platforms may want to use in attracting suppliers, ranging from setting prices (Tan et al. 2020) to offering promotion schemes to make the platform attractive to suppliers (Liang et al. 2019; Rietveld et al. 2019). One aspect that has not been systematically considered is the role of acquisitions—acquiring or buying other companies— as a part of this growth strategy. As a result, there is little understanding of how platform companies use acquisitions or whether the acquisition strategies of platform companies differ from those of non-platform companies.

Acquisitions are an important strategic activity of many companies and have drawn considerable scholarly interest in understanding when and why these acquisitions occur (Ahuja and Katila 2001; Silverman 1999). These reasons for acquisitions often come down to issues of the control of key strategic assets or eliminating a competitor from the marketplace. Although we might expect many of these benefits of making strategic acquisitions to apply to platform companies, there are other features that may differ in the context of platforms. On the one hand, platform companies, just like any other company, may want to control key assets or limit competition, hence potentially wanting to acquire other companies. On the other hand, the success of platforms relies on their role as intermediaries and their ability to attract both suppliers and customers. This can create constraints when it comes to acquiring because platform companies may not want to take any action that can be seen as creating competition for suppliers because this might prevent suppliers from joining the marketplace (Huang et al. 2013; Wen and Zhu 2019). There are prominent examples of this phenomenon: When Google released its own Photos mobile app, effectively entering the complementor market on its Android platform, it affected the efforts of app developers because they did not want to compete against the platform and switched to developing other types of software (Wen and Zhu 2019). Similarly, when Amazon begins supplying its own products that compete with those sold by suppliers on its platform, those suppliers begin to limit their efforts on the platform (Zhu 2019). Typically, non-platform companies do not face the same type of constraints, and as a platform, companies are likely to acquire other firms in different ways than non-platform companies.

There are numerous examples of platform companies making acquisitions. In some cases, these examples are platform companies acquiring other platform companies to grow the platform. For instance, Freelancer.com grew its platform by acquiring similar online-work marketplaces. AirBnB fueled its early growth by acquiring other home-sharing sites, often in locations where AirBnB wanted to gain a foothold. However, there are also examples of platform companies acquiring suppliers. Microsoft and Sony acquired numerous game developers to ensure that they had exclusive content for their gaming consoles. Netflix has similarly acquired several content providers to ensure that they have exclusive content available for their streaming platform. While some of these examples are consistent with the ethos of platform companies’ need to scale by attracting suppliers and users, the remaining examples appear inconsistent with this logic.

In the current paper, we study the acquisition strategies of platform companies, specifically focusing on the questions of when do platform companies acquire and which types of companies do they acquire. We focus on the types of acquisitions that are made shortly after the founding of a company through its progression to maturity.

Using data from CrunchBase, we study 3,062 acquisitions made by digital companies founded since the year 2000, and we compare the acquisitions made by platform and nonplatform companies. We find that (1) platform companies make acquisitions earlier (most shortly after founding); (2) platform companies are more likely to acquire companies from the same market niches in the years shortly after founding but then proceed to acquire companies from other market niches as they mature; and (3) platform companies are more likely to acquire other platform companies in the years shortly after founding but then proceed to acquire nonplatform companies as they mature. Our results indicate that this differs from the acquisition patterns that we can observe for digital non-platform companies.

The present paper makes several contributions. First, existing studies have focused on a wide range of strategies that platforms can use to attract customers and suppliers (Ceccagnoli et al. 2012; Liang et al. 2019; Panico and Cennamo 2020; Rietveld et al. 2019; Tan et al. 2020) but have not considered that platforms may acquire other companies as part of that strategy. However, as our analysis shows, acquisitions can be an important part of how platform companies can scale. The current paper is among the first to consider when platform companies undertake acquisitions and which type of companies they acquire, showing that there is a substantial difference between the acquisition strategies of platform and non-platform companies.

The current paper is structured as follows: First, we provide definitions of the main concepts, highlighting the findings and the gap from the literature. We then develop our theoretical arguments and hypotheses. Subsequently, we provide an overview of our dataset and how we construct the variables for the analysis. Finally, we discuss the theoretical and practical implications of our findings and the contribution of the paper.

## Theoretical Background

## Defining Platform and Non-platform Companies

The term platform is used to describe the technologies that provide a basis for follow-on innovation (Bresnahan and Greenstein 1999), as well as for describing a business model of a company as an intermediary between buyers and sellers (Eisenmann 2006; Parker et al. 2016). Many modern platform companies often embody both of these definitions (Parker and Van Alstyne 2005).

In the current paper, platform is used to refer to the business model of a company. These are companies that act as intermediaries between suppliers and customers rather than companies that have a traditional pipeline structure (Parker et al. 2016). We are only focusing on companies in the digital realm or facilitating physical interactions through a digital medium (e.g., Uber, Airbnb). Therefore, we use the term digital platform companies throughout the paper. As a comparison group, we are referring to digital companies that use a non-platform business model. Examples of non-platform companies are software vendors, e-ommerce websites, or providers of digital goods and services that use a conventional value chain (Parker and Van Alstyne 2005).

## Gap in the Literature on Acquisitions and Platforms

## Literature on Platform Strategy

A key characteristic of platforms is that they are characterized by network effects, whereby the value and demand of the platform increases with the size of the complementor and user base (Jacobides et al. 2018). As a result, the focus of a platform strategy is to scale up quickly and build a user base to gain an advantage over competitors. Studies have considered a variety of approaches for scaling a platform, including how to set prices to attract users and suppliers (Parker and Van Alstyne 2005), using promotion strategies (Rietveld et al. 2019), or establishing partnerships with key suppliers (Cennamo and Santalo 2013). However, the literature has not considered that platforms may acquire companies as a part of these strategies. W might expect platform companies to undertake acquisitions in a way that relates to their need to scale the platform and exploit network effects. This is different from hownon-platformcompanies acquire, highlighting a need for a theory to understand the role of acquisitions in platform strategy.

## Literature on Strategic Acquisitions

There is a vast body of literature that has studied why companies make acquisitions (Ahuja and Katila 2001; Capron and Shen 2007; Wang and Zajac 2007; Zollo and Singh 2007). Explanations include the following: to realize cost synergies against increased competition, to increase pricing power, to address gaps in the product mix and asset concentration, and to create value more broadly (Bettinazzi and Zollo 2017; Choi and McNamara 2017; Fan and Goyal 2006; Maksimovic et al.

2008). Other studies have looked at the general antecedents to these acquisitions, such as environmental factors (Berchicci et al. 2017) and firm characteristics (Zhao 2009). Studies have also identified the behavioral factors driving acquisitions, such as managerial self-interest (Haleblian et al. 2009). An important factor identified by the literature is that platform companies may want to control key assets to avoid being “held up” by critical suppliers (Silverman 1999) that have bargaining power. This threat of supplier market power may encourage platform companies to control their suppliers (Pagani 2013).

## Hypothesis Development

The success of companies with platform business models depends on their ability to exploit network effects and attract suppliers and complementors (Parker and Van Alstyne 2005). We would expect that platforms might also acquire other platform companies to build their user and supplier base. There are examples of this strategy behind the growth of many prominent platform companies. For instance, AirBnB acquired a number of similar “home-sharing” platforms in its early days and brought those suppliers (complementors) into their platform. Similarly, Match.com, a prominent online dating platform, has acquired more than 24 other dating platform applications (Gilbert 2019). As a result, acquiring other platform companies in the same market niche can provide a strategy for platform companies to scale their supplier and customer base.

At the same time, platform companies may not want to acquire companies that could limit their ability to scale the platform and attract suppliers and customers. For instance, as the earlier examples of Google releasing applications within the Android Marketplace or Amazon competing against its resellers suggest, having the platform move into the market for suppliers may deter potential suppliers from joining the platform (Wen and Zhu 2019; Zhu and Liu 2018). Similarly, Facebook’s acquisition of Instagram reduced the demand for products similar to Instagram (Li and Agarwal 2017). Therefore, if a platform is going to make acquisitions early in its lifetime, it is likely to only acquire other platform companies also operating in the same market niche, hence allowing it to build its customer and supplier base. Additionally, platform companies will be less likely to acquire companies that are not platforms from the same market niche.

However, this may only prove to be a viable strategy in the early days of platforms when they are focused on rapidly scaling the platform. Competition among platform companies is often characterized by a “winner-take-all” competition, so the platform companies that survive in such markets are likely to be among the leading platform companies within a given niche. Therefore, once these platforms mature, there may be fewer other platform companies within the same market niche for them to acquire, but there may also be less of a benefit from acquiring other platform companies. This implies that as platform companies mature, they will be less likely to acquire other platform companies from the same market niche.

At the same time, we may expect that as platform companies mature, they may have an incentive to acquire other types of companies. For instance, Microsoft and Sony both acquired a number of gaming studios that would develop content (video games) for their respective gaming consoles, which has often been the canonical example of platform companies in scholarly studies (Corts and Lederman 2009; Shankar and Bayus 2003). In these cases, these acquisitions allowed the console to control key suppliers (video game developers) and ensure that their content was exclusive to their platform. This process of acquiring companies to control key suppliers is consistent with the prediction of the double-helix framework (see Figure 1), which argues that platforms will want to control key suppliers as they mature (Pagani 2013). Therefore, as they mature, platform companies may want to acquire suppliers or other companies outside of their market niche.

There may be additional benefits to a broader ecosystem for platforms to acquire other companies. In the case of Google Photos, it did create certain positive spillovers for companies because it attracted more attention to the marketplace and stimulated developers that were competing with Google to increase the quality of their products (Foerderer et al. 2018). Similarly, in the case of Facebook acquiring Instagram, there is evidence that this attracted new consumers to this market niche (Li and Agarwal 2018). As a result, platforms may be likely to acquire companies outside of their market niche. However, as the arguments above suggest, they may not want to do this in the early phase as they are attempting to scale their platform but instead wait until they are more mature.

In summary, we expect to see platform companies making acquisitions early in their life (shortly after founding) as a way of growing their platform. In this period, platform companies are likely to acquire other platforms from the same market niche, but as they mature, platform companies may become less likely to acquire other platform companies and instead acquire non-platform companies from other market niches.

It is important to highlight that these patterns are also likely not true for non-platform companies. As discussed in earlier, there has not been a lot of research into when companies acquire, and very few studies on the acquisition strategies of entrepreneurial companies. In fact, much of the literature on platforms has focused on large, established companies and has highlighted that acquisitions are a common growth strategy for such companies (Klepper 1996; Kim et al. 2011). This suggests that non-platform companies often acquire when they are more mature, not as part of their early growth strategy. As a result, we expect the acquisition strategies of platform companies to differ from those of the non-platform companies that have typically been studied.

Based on the arguments above, we summarize our empirical hypotheses as follow:

H1. Digital platform companies make their first strategic acquisition earlier than digital non-platform companies.

H2. Digital platform companies are likely to first acquire companies from the same product market niches but then proceed to acquire companies from different product market niches as they mature.

H3. Digital platform companies are likely to first acquire other platform companies but then proceed to also acquire digital non-platform companies as they mature.

## Data and Sample

## Data Sources

We use data on acquisitions from CrunchBase. These data contain a profile for each company, including a text description of the business area of the company, information about the number of employees, date of founding, sources of venture financing, and a list of acquisitions made by each company. This dataset does not contain SIC-based industry classifications but does contain tags that describe the general business area of each company (e.g., cloud storage).

The sample of companies in CrunchBase favors technology and digital firms. This provides a large sample of digital companies, including those with platform business models and early-stage companies that have not yet gone public. This is particularly important for studying platform business models because many platform companies only go public once they become large. Meanwhile, in the current paper, we focus on the acquisition strategies of companies in their first few years after founding. CrunchBase’s data have been used in a number of earlier studies (Nuscheler et al. 2019; Shi et al. 2016).

## Sample

The CrunchBase dataset contains information on many companies across a variety of industries. We omitted companies from non-digital industries, as well as companies founded prior to the year 2000. We omitted companies that did not have text descriptions (or had very short text descriptions) because we relied on these descriptions to calculate some of our variables. These were typically very small companies that did not survive very long and typically did not make any acquisitions. Therefore, omitting these observations did not influence our conclusions. Out of a broader sample of 123,044 digital companies that were in our dataset but did not make any acquisitions, our final sample consisted of 1,933 companies that made strategic acquisitions and were founded after the year 2000. Of the acquiring companies, 278 were platform companies. Our final sample consisted of 3,062 acquisition events.

## Variable Construction

## Identifying Acquisitions by Platform Companies

Our main theoretical distinction is between the acquisition strategies of platform and non-platform companies. This refers to the business model of the company. The challenge in identifying platform companies is that platform companies may exist in a wide range of settings, from real estate (e.g., Zillow) or tourism (e.g., AirBnB) to online sales (e.g., eBay), and there are no tags or indicators in the data to easily indicate these companies. We identified platform companies by employing student coders to manually classify the sample of companies that undertook an acquisition. In the parts of the analysis where we wanted to test for selection, we had to classify the entire population of companies. This was done using a supervised classification algorithm based on the text descriptions. We performed a number of robustness checks in our analysis to ensure that our classification was correct and that this was not biasing our results.

## Measuring Firm Age

Our theoretical arguments are based on whether companies are likely to acquire earlier or later in their lifetime. Our measure of Firm Age reflects how many years after founding a firm makes a particular acquisition.

## Identifying Companies from Same or Different Niches

Following earlier studies (Hoberg and Phillips 2016; Kim et al. 2016; Shi et al. 2016), we utilize a text-based approach to capture the degree of overlap in market niches. Below, we summarize the process for constructing such a measure. As a first step, we take the text descriptions of each company and convert the description into a TF-IDF vector. We remove the keywords that occur frequently. These vectors reflect how much particular keywords occur in each text description. We then compare each pair of acquirer and target firms to identify the extent by which they overlap in terms of these keywords; we do this by using cosine similarity. We also test the robustness of this approach by using topic modeling to reduce the dimensionality of these vectors (following Shi et al. 2016) or by comparing the overlap based on industry tags (e.g., cloud storage, etc.).

## Control Variables

We control for industries or business areas (Industry FE) using dummy variables to indicate whether a company is assigned a particular tag in its profile. These tags are used to describe the business of the company, as described above (e.g., cloud storage). We control for Firm Size based on the number of employees that the company employs. This is reported as a range (e.g., 50–100 employees) in our dataset, so we use dummy variables to indicate the different groups. Controlling for Firm Size helps account for the differences in the resources that companies may have, which could translate into their acquisition strategies. As an additional control, we include Funding Controls, which contains dummy variables for the number of venture funding rounds that the firm has received. This provides a measure for the financial resources that a company has at its disposal to undertake acquisitions. The decision of firms to undertake acquisitions may be shaped by the availability of acquisition targets. To control for this, we include Market Controls, which is the average cosine similarity between a company and the 10 companies with the highest similarity score in the marketplace. We include Year FE as dummies for each year that an acquisition was made to account for macroeconomic trends in acquisition patterns.

## Results: When do Platform Companies First Acquire?

Hypothesis 1 predicts that platform companies are likely to make acquisitions earlier than non-platform companies. Empirically, this implies that the Firm Age (years since founding) of when a platform makes its first acquisition would be lower on average than for non-platform companies. In Table 3, Part A, we report these values. On average, platform companies made their first acquisition 4.6 years after founding, while non-platform companies made their first acquisition on average 6.1 years after founding. This difference is statistically significant $( \varDelta = 1 . 8 ; S . E . = 0 . 2 7 ; t = 6 . 5 2 )$ based on a simple t-test, providing evidence in support of Hypothesis 1.

Our goal is to account for differences in terms of industry (market niche), Firm Size, Funding, and Competition Controls, which may in part explain the difference between platform and non-platform companies. ## Discussion of Findings and Looking Forward

In the current paper, we study the acquisition of platform companies and ask when and which type of firms platform companies acquire. Existing studies have not considered the idea that acquiring other platform companies may be a way for platforms to scale their base of customers and suppliers. The current study is the first to focus on the role of acquisitions in this process. As our results show, platform companies often acquire other platform companies earlier, and as they mature, they then proceed to acquire other non-platform companies such as suppliers or complementors.

These results contribute to the literature on platform strategy, which has attempted to understand the factors that determine the success of digital platform companies and the nuanced strategies through which digital platform companies grow their base of users and complementors (Boudreau 2010; Cennamo and Santalo 2013; Huang et al. 2017; Jacobides et al. 2018; Karhu et al. 2018; Parker and Van Alstyne 2005; Wareham et al. 2014;). Additionally, the results show that there is a substantial difference between the acquisition strategies of platform and non-platform companies. This suggests that unlike the literature on acquisitions by non-platform companies, which often centers on large and established companies (Ahuja and Katila 2001; Kim et al. 2011), acquisition patterns by platform companies are distinctive and an integral part of the entrepreneurial strategy of platform companies.

## Implications for Theories of Industry Evolution

Another potential implication for these findings is that they can inform our understanding of industry evolution. The double-helix model of industry evolution, which was first developed by Fine (1998) and expanded on by Pagani (2013), considers how industries evolve with respect to their need for companies to balance competing needs for intermediation versus control. These studies have documented how companies have evolved from emphasizing control over their suppliers toward more loosely coupled coalitions, increasingly moving toward being platforms. They theorize how the shift from vertically integrated structures toward platforms was motivated by a need for flexibility and the need for product variety (Pagani 2013). However, they also theorize that these platforms also eventually require greater control of their suppliers, particularly as those suppliers grow in influence. As a result, they theorize that platforms may eventually return to a phase where they require greater control of complementors or suppliers (this evolution is framed in Figure 1). As a result, they predict that although platform companies may benefit from being intermediaries between different actors, they eventually may want to control certain elements of the ecosystem.

As the present results suggest, platform companies may want to acquire other platform companies early on and often from the same market niche. However, as they mature, platform companies may want to control non-platform companies, including those from other market niches. This is consistent with the theory from the double-helix model, which predicts that platform companies may eventually act less like intermediaries and be more likely to exert control over other companies, including potential suppliers. This raises questions for future research that may consider the evolution of platform companies when they want to exert greater control over various partners. This also highlights the need for future work to consider the challenges that platforms face in balancing between intermediation and control, and rather than acting as pure intermediaries, they often need to control other companies in their ecosystem. Future research is needed to better understand the various ways that companies make acquisitions and the reasons behind platform acquisitions.

## Managerial Implications

The results for the current paper have considerable implications for managers and their understanding of how platform companies grow. Academic studies have typically highlighted that platforms grow by attracting users and suppliers. As the present study has documented, acquiring other companies may also play a very important role, especially early on in the life cycle of companies when they need to scale the platform. Additionally, once companies become prominent platforms, they often need to manage the relationship between users and suppliers and, therefore, need to shift away from simply attempting to scale the platform by acquiring competitors.

## Implications for Future Research

In the current paper, we provide evidence for when and who platform companies acquire. However, this raises new questions for subsequent research. First, questions remain regarding the reasons behind the acquisitions made by platform companies, particularly the specific types of assets that platform companies need to acquire. Second, questions remain regarding the extent to which different types of acquisitions by platform companies impact performance and allow companies to attract users through network effects. Third, the present paper signals how platform companies have evolved from pure intermediaries to exercise greater control over suppliers and other members of the ecosystem, as predicted by the double-helix framework (Pagani 2013). Future research can build further on this to understand how digital platform companies manage the balance between intermediation and control.
