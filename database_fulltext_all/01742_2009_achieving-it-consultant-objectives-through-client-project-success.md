# Achieving it consultant objectives through client project success

## A B S T R A C T

Improving project performance is an important objective in IS project management. In consultantassisted IS projects, however, consulting organizations may have additional objectives, such as knowledge acquisition and future business growth. In this study, we examined the relationship between client and consultant objectives and the role of coordination in affecting the achievement of these objectives. A research model was developed and tested using 199 consultant-assisted projects. The results showed that the achievement of consultant objectives was dependent upon the achievement of client objectives and that coordination had a positive impact on both client and consultant objectives. - 2009 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

## 1. Introduction

Increasingly, the use of IT consulting services has become an important part of corporate IT strategy. According to the Gartner group, US companies spent about \$45 billion on IT consulting services in 2004 and expenditures were expected to grow to \$58 billion in 2009 [8]. As more companies engage IT consulting firms in IS implementation projects, consultant-assisted IS projects increase. However, these projects differed from normal outsourcing ones because close collaboration between the client and consulting organizations was required throughout the entire course of the consultant-assisted projects [23]. In outsourced IS projects, systems requirements should be well defined and technologies quite mature so that outsourcers can be contracted to carry out well designed systems. However, consultant-assisted projects often involve ill-defined business problems and/or new or emerging technologies. Therefore, consultants must provide technical expertise as well as strategic guidance. Thus IT consulting firms offer not only technical implementation but also aid in requirements analysis, systems integration, and solution services. The management of consultant-assisted projects is also more complex because project team members from client and consulting firms may have somewhat different objectives and incompatible work practices. Since consulting fees represent a large part of IS implementation budgets and companies rely on consulting firms for a wide range of expertise and experience, it is crucial that companies effectively manage consultant-assisted projects [4,10].

Research in IT project management has generally focused on improving project performance. Overall performance involves both process and product performance. Process performance reflects the quality of project management, project control, and group process, while product performance depends on the quality of the implemented IS applications and the satisfaction of business users. However, studies have found that different constituents in a project team may evaluate product and process performance differently because they have varying interests in the project and have access to different project information [11]. For example, business managers may be more concerned with product performance because a new system will help them enhance and implement business strategies and they are in a better position to evaluate how well it is working. Process performance, on the other hand, may be of greater interest to the IS group who use it as a feedback mechanism for project management and control.

While improving project performance may be the primary objective of the clients, consulting firms may have additional objectives beyond their currently contracted-engagement, related to the growth and survival of their business. For example, Gefen [9] identified engagement success as a performance measure for consulting firms, defining it as the client’s willingness to develop a long-term business relationship with the consultant. Thus consultants need to demonstrate their value to their counterparts on the project team while making sure that higher level decision makers are satisfied with their services. Also, operating in a knowledge-intensive industry, consulting firms must constantly create and update their technical and business knowledge and expertise so that they stay ahead of their competition. Indeed, an important source of new knowledge derives from their consulting: the skills and experience they gain in their engagements [6]. Therefore, another performance measure, from the consultant’s perspective, would be the knowledge gained from the project. It is true that these consultant objectives are related to project performance but they are distinctly different from the client’s objectives and they are not necessarily shared by the client organization.

Naturally, clients want consultants to put project performance as their first priority and are annoyed if consultants pursue their own interests at the expense of the client; especially if consultants recommend cutting-edge technology solutions that do not really fit the needs of the client [7]. On the other hand, consultants have to respond to performance measures set by their employer, such as project profitability, client satisfaction, and knowledge gained [2,12,22]. Failure to perform on such measures could reduce their income and impede their consulting careers. So managing the differences in interests and objectives between clients and consultants represents a challenge and added complexity to consultant-assisted projects.

We studied the relationship between client and consultant objectives within the consultant-assisted IT project context. We focused on two questions:

(1) How is project performance related to consultant’s objectives, such as knowledge acquisition and future business growth, and (2) How does coordination affect these objectives?

To answer these, we produced a model that links coordination to both client and consultant objectives and then empirically tested its relationships in 199 consultant-assisted projects.

## 2. Prior work on consultant-assisted projects

Successfully managing consultant-assisted projects requires that both clients and consultants work to ensure that they can achieve all of their objectives (separate and joint). There are two possible approaches:

\- By taking an agency theory perspective, align client and consultant objectives so that they are complementary [15].

\- Making sure that client and consulting organizations pursue coordinated actions that favorably impact both their objectives.

Agency theory proposes a number of ways to address this when the goals of the agent are in conflict with those of the principal and the actions of the agent are difficult for the principal to observe. In the IT project management, it has been applied to explain the relationships between project managers and developers in internal IS projects [3,16], and between clients and vendors in outsourced IS projects [20]. The relationship between clients and consultants can be characterized as an agency relationship and their goals may not be compatible.

To address the agency problem, the principal can link the agent’s compensation and reward to the achievement of its goals and objectives. This offers the agent an incentive to act in the principal’s best interest but an agent may weigh that against its own goals and behave accordingly. The client organization in a consultant-assisted project will also assess how project performance is related to consultant objectives, such as growing future business and acquiring knowledge, and establish incentive mechanisms to align these objectives.

A number of studies have examined the effect of team-level coordination on project performance. Nidumolu [17] assumed that the effect of vertical coordination on project performance was mediated by project uncertainty and project risk, while horizontal coordination had a direct effect on improving project performance. Although both coordinations represented different coordination techniques and tools, they were required to achieve high project performance. Andres and Zmud [1] compared the impact of different coordination strategies in lab experiments. They found that an organic (decentralized, informal) coordination strategy produced significantly greater satisfaction and higher productivity than a mechanistic (centralized, formal) strategy: the interaction effect between organic coordination and task interdependence was especially effective when the projects involved highly interdependent tasks. Sabherwal [20] examined how coordination mechanisms were used in outsourced IS projects. He found that clients and vendors had rather different perspectives on the appropriate coordination mechanisms and when they should be used: both clients and vendors used coordination as a tool to improve project performance. Thus coordination has generally positive direct effects on project performance as well as indirect effects through project uncertainty and task interdependence.

The agency and coordination approaches represent two distinct but complementary theoretical perspectives. No studies have integrated both perspectives in a single model. In addition, most of the research has focused on improving project performance and treated it as the only dependent variable. In our study, we proposed a model that explicitly considered both client and consultant objectives through the lens of agency and coordination perspectives. Project uncertainty was treated as a factor that mediated the effect of coordination.

## 3. The research model

The model is presented in Fig. 1. We proposed that consultant objectives (business development and knowledge acquisition) were dependent on the achievement of the client objective (project performance) and postulated that coordination can affect client and consultant objectives both directly and indirectly through project uncertainty.

Project performance, as a client objective, can be measured in term of process quality, product quality, and the extent to which the project budget and schedule are met. Process quality is a measure of the quality of the client–consultant interactions, governance, and learning, while product quality is to the quality of the implemented IS applications and satisfaction of business users.

Possessing a comprehensive and relevant knowledge base is an important source of competitive advantage for consultants. Consulting firms require their associates and principals to update and improve their knowledge base through engagements and other learning means. Here, knowledge acquisition means the amount of new knowledge consultants are able to obtain from their current engagement, including technical know-how plus organizational and project management expertise. Consultants can also acquire project management knowledge that encompasses skills that enable them to lead, collaborate with, and better serve future clients. Finally, there is technical knowledge about IT systems and platforms and how they should be integrated and implemented in a business environment. It should be noted that such new knowledge is different from the information needed to complete a project.

While a failed or marginally successful project can certainly offer some insights on what went wrong and should be avoided in the future, successful projects offer consultants more direct learning experience and could yield more knowledge acquisition. For example, knowledge about the successful use of new IT can be readily transferred to another engagement and can dramatically increase the chance of its success. Also, knowledge about successful work practices and interaction with clients can be documented as best practices to guide future engagements. Thus,

H1. Higher levels of project performance will lead to higher levels of knowledge acquisition by consultants.

To survive and thrive as a business, consulting firms must contract for a steady flow of engagements. Here, business development is the ability of consulting firms to secure future engagements. Given the distinct differences between project performance and business development, clearly stipulated contractual terms and monitoring mechanisms could prevent potential agency problems. In addition, the linkage between client and consultant objectives can also provide incentives for consultants to act in the client’s best interests. Clients are more likely to award future projects and employ the same consulting firm if an earlier project was successful. Also, consultants rely on reputation and referrals to acquire new clients and extend their business. Satisfied clients and impeccable track records are crucial for obtaining positive references leading to an expanded client base and the ability to charge higher fees. Therefore,

H2. Higher levels of project performance will lead to higher levels of business development for consultants.

Knowledge about the current client is invaluable for establishing a long-term relationship and securing future consulting projects. New technical expertise can help consulting firms attract new clients and extend their service offerings. Knowledge on project management best practices enables consultants to deliver excellent service and maintain client satisfaction in a consistent manner.

H3. Higher levels of knowledge acquisition by consultants will lead to higher levels of business development for consultants.

Client–consultant coordination deals with activities and techniques used by client and consultants to manage their interdependence in achieve project objectives. Nidumolu showed that higher levels of coordination led to higher levels of project performance. Consultant-assisted projects are similar to internal IS projects—project success depends on multiple participants working together effectively. However, as consultant-assisted projects are complex because project team members come from different organizations, client–consultant coordination tends to be critical to project success.

H4. Higher levels of client–consultant coordination will lead to higher levels of project performance.

Client–consultant coordination can enhance the interactions among clients and consultants on the project team and allow them to share information. Every organization is unique and presents great learning opportunities for the consultants. As a result, consultants can gain a great deal of knowledge about the client organization through coordination, which is a learned, critical project management skill. The more consultants are able to practice and apply coordination techniques, the better they are able to perform in future projects. Finally, although consultants are usually more technically competent than their clients, the client organization may possess certain in-depth knowledge in niche areas. Client–consultant coordination may help consultants discover such niches and learn from the clients. Furthermore, coordination could highlight the knowledge processes [14] and facilitate knowledge transfer within project teams [19].

H5. Higher levels of client–consultant coordination will lead to higher levels of knowledge acquisition by the consultants.

In addition to gaining knowledge, client–consultant coordination provides an important means by which consultants can identify and resolve conflicts and disagreements with clients arising over the course of the project [13]. Coordination also offers a venue for social interactions which can promote trusting relationships. The ability to resolve conflicts and establish personal relationships with key decision makers in the client organization would allow consultants to build long-term relationship with clients and thus secure future work.

H6. Higher levels of client–consultant coordination will lead to higher levels of business development by the consultants.

From a coordination theory perspective, project uncertainty is an important mediating factor between coordination and project performance [21]. Project uncertainty can be broken into two parts: requirements and technical uncertainty. The first refers to vagueness in the application specifications of the client organization, whereas the second refers to uncertainty about the client and consulting organizations’ experience with the software and its implementation. In internal IS projects, higher levels of coordination have been found to reduce the levels of project uncertainty.

In consultant-assisted projects, consultants and clients contribute different types of knowledge and expertise to the project team: consultants have the technical and project management skills while clients have knowledge about their business processes and needs. Therefore, knowledge sharing and exchange are critical. Client–consultant coordination promotes knowledge sharing and exchange by helping team members recognize the need for expertise, locate, and apply it.

Coordination can have a similar effect on technical uncertainty. Consultants usually possess more technical experience and knowledge on the systems to be implemented than clients. On the other hand, clients are more familiar with their own technology infrastructure and environment. Thus, close coordination would allow the transfer of technical experience and knowledge and reduce the complexity and ambiguity associated with technical uncertainty. Therefore,

H7. Higher levels of client–consultant coordination will lead to lower levels of technical uncertainty.

H8. Higher levels of client–consultant coordination will lead to lower levels of requirements uncertainty.

Technical uncertainty can increase the time and cost variances in completing a project if the team is unfamiliar with the technology. Inexperience may also lead to implementation mistakes that impact system quality. Similarly, project requirements need to be clearly specified before they can be implemented. Therefore, uncertainty and indecision in requirements specifications could be a major source of project delay, cost overrun, and dissatisfaction with the completed system.

H9. Higher levels of technical uncertainty will lead to lower levels of project performance.

H10. Higher levels of requirements uncertainty will lead to lower levels of project performance.

Projects with higher technical uncertainty often involve cutting-edge technologies and/or new business environments. While such technical uncertainty could increase project risks and present significant challenges for the project team, consultants may view this as an opportunity to learn new technologies and business environments. In addition, consultants can gain knowledge by solving problems and overcoming challenges.

H11. Higher levels of project technical uncertainty will lead to higher levels of knowledge acquisition by consultants.

## 4. Research method and data analysis

To test the research model, we conducted a survey among members of two special interest groups (IS and IT & Telecomm) within the Project Management Institute (PMI), the largest project management professional organization in the world (see http:// www.pmi.org/). PMI members were invited to participate in the survey via two electronic newsletters. Following a link provided in these, PMI members were directed to the survey website with a welcome page explaining the purpose and expected value of the study. They were then asked to focus on one IS implementation project in which they had actively participated. In addition, the candidate project had to meet the following criteria: 1) have significant participation of one or more external consulting organizations providing technical and/or project management expertise; 2) involve the implementation of an enterprise system or an enterprise system module; and 3) be completed within, or be nearing completion in, the last 12 months. If the members did not have experience with such a project, they were directed to an exit page where they were thanked for visiting the site. Otherwise, they would enter the survey web site. Those members entering the site who identified themselves as consultants were directed to the survey questionnaire. As an incentive, all respondents were entered into a lottery drawing for three Amazon Corporation gift certificates.

## 4.1. Development and pre-test of the survey instrument

The survey instrument consisted of items adapted from prior studies in our review of the literature. Coordination measures were based on the items used in [18]. Project performance was measured using items that reflected the quality of the project management process (process quality), the quality of the application (product quality), and items that evaluated the extent to which the project was over or under budgeted time and cost.

The draft questionnaire had first been reviewed by academics with extensive experience in survey methodology, and then pretested by a senior vice president at a major IT consulting firm. The survey was then subjected to pilot testing using member organizations on the CIO council of the authors’ business school. These organizations included manufacturing and service companies, as well as major IT consulting firms. The CIOs provided access to project managers who participated in the pilot study. The questionnaire was modified as a result of the findings and resulted in 28 items used to measure six constructs, as shown in Appendix A.

## 4.2. The survey

We received a total of 219 responses from the survey. After eliminating duplicate submissions (hitting the submission key several times) and responses with one or more sections of missing data, there were 199 usable projects. The project characteristics are presented in Table 1. The projects reported in the survey consisted of a variety of enterprise applications, including complete ERP implementations and ERP modules (Financial, Human Resource, CRM, SCM, BI/KM, Operations, Databases/Infrastructure). Total budgeted costs of these projects ranged from less than \$100,000 to over \$50 million, with the median cost falling into the \$1–5 million category. Project length also varied greatly with a range of less than three months to over 24 months, with the median length falling into the 12–14 months category. All respondents to the survey were consultants. As shown in Table 2, the respondents ranged from senior executives to business analysts, but slightly less than half (44.7%) were project managers. The average working experience of the respondents was over 18 years, with more than 50% having 10–25 years of working experience. Taken together, most respondents were seasoned consultants with substantial working experience and managerial responsibilities. To assess possible sample bias, we compared large projects (over 1 million dollars in budget) and small projects in terms of cost overrun and time overrun. No significant differences were found.

Since all variables are measured through self-reported items, common method variance could be a potential source of bias. Harman’s one-factor test was used to examine the threat of common method biases [5]. An exploratory factor analysis was performed on all items measuring both independent and dependent variables. Six factors were extracted using the principal component analysis. The first factor accounted for 24.5% of the total variance. The result showed that there were multiple factors and no one single factor could explain the majority of the variances among variables. Therefore, common method variance was not a cause for concern in our data set.

Exploratory factor analysis was also performed on the data set to assess the initial convergent and discriminant validity. Items that loaded on multiple factors or had a loading value of less than 0.40 were removed and the analysis was performed again. The collected data set was then analyzed and tested using the structural equation modeling approach to validate the research model. The testing of the model involved the evaluation of both the measurement and structural models. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was performed to test the measurement model. The psychometric properties of the measurement model were evaluated for convergent validity, discriminant validity, and overall model fit. After the measurement model was shown to possess satisfactory psychometric properties, the structural model was used to investigate the direction and significance of causal relationships between various latent variables.

Fig. 2. Results of the structural model.

Fig. 2 shows the standardized path coefficients between the constructs. Nine out of eleven hypothesized relationships were statistically significant. Project performance was found to have significant positive effects on knowledge acquisition (0.526, p = 0.002) and business development (0.574, p = 0.002), thus supporting H1 and H2, respectively. Knowledge acquisition had significant positive impact on business development (0.351, p = 0.000), supporting H3. As expected by H4 and H6, client– consultant coordination was found to improve project performance (0.363, p = 0.000) and business development (0.204, p = 0.034). However, the impact of client–consultant coordination on knowledge acquisition (H5) was not significant (0.023, p = 0.809).

Higher levels of client–consultant coordination were found to reduce technical uncertainty (0.241, p = 0.003) and requirements uncertainty (0.469, p = 0.000), supporting H7 and H8 respectively. As stated in H10, requirements uncertainty, on the other hand, has significant negative effects on project performance (0.110, p = 0.01). Finally, technical uncertainty showed significant positive effects on knowledge acquisition (0.266, p = 0.005) as hypothesized in H11 but H9 was not supported because there was no significant effect of technical uncertainty on project performance (0.023, p = 0.809). The squared multiple correlations (SMC) showed that the model accounted for 55 percent of the variance in business development, 41 percent of the variance in project performance, 22 percent of the variance in knowledge acquisition, 12 percent of the variance in requirements uncertainty, and 7 percent of the variance in technical uncertainty. Thus, the results, in general, provide support for the research model.

## 6. Discussion

We examined the relationship between client and consultant objectives and the role of coordination in consultant-assisted projects from the consultant’s perspective. Project performance, the client’s primarily objective, was found to have significant positive effect on the consultant’s objectives of knowledge acquisition and business development. As our research focused on the consultant’s perspective, this finding is reassuring for client organizations: if consultants perceive that their objectives are linked to the client’s objectives, they are less likely to focus on their own objectives. Consequently, client organizations may utilize this linkage to manage the agency relationship and improve project performance.

Our study showed that the impact of successful project performance can

\- allow consultants to acquire new knowledge more directly and quickly,

\- enable consultants to develop business relationships with new clients,

\- establish a track record for the consulting organization, via success stories, and thus help in obtaining new clients,

\- provide consultants with the expertise necessary to broaden their services,

\- reduce requirements and technical uncertainty,

\- allow the project team to evaluate a fuller range of options and identify the best solution,

\- build trust, resolve goal conflicts, and manage better relationships throughout the project and thus build a long lasting relationship between the two organizations.

Our study contributed to agency theory by establishing positive linkages between client and consultant objectives. We generally presume that client and consulting organizations would have different and conflicting objectives, so the agency theory lens tends to focus on outcome-based contracts and monitoring mechanisms. Although the positive linkages do not suggest that contracts and monitoring mechanisms are less important, they do provide an alternative way to view conflicting objectives. The results also showed the importance of coordination for achieving both client and consultant objectives.

Client managers should realize that consultants may have different objectives than project performance. Consultants recommend cutting-edge technology that may increase the technical uncertainty of the project because they see technical uncertainty as an interesting challenge rather than a risk.

We note three limitations in our study. First, we focused only on knowledge acquisition and business development as the consultant objectives. Second, we examined the relationships between client and consultant’s objectives only from the consultant’s perspective rather than from matched-pair responses from the consultant and client. Third, although it allowed us to obtain a large sample size and a variety of consultant-assisted projects, the online survey methodology did not allow us to explore the depth and evolving nature of the relationships between clients and consultants. It also limited our ability to measure some of the constructs, e.g., project performance, at a more detailed level.

## 7. Summary

We developed a model for examining the relationships between client and consultant objectives in consultant-assisted IS projects and the role of coordination in achieving their objectives. Using data from 199 consultant-assisted projects, we showed that project performance, a primary objective of the client, had a significant impact on the consultant objectives of knowledge acquisition and business development. Coordination had positive effects on project performance as well as business development. Furthermore, consultants perceived that technical uncertainty could have a positive effect on knowledge acquisition. Our study contributed to the theory and practice of IS project management by linking client and consultant objectives in consultant-assisted IS projects, and suggested ways of improving the effectiveness of managing such projects.
