## A R T I C L E I N F O

## A B S T R A C T

Despite the extensive proliferation of Information Technology Outsourcing (ITO). firms often obtain unsatisfactory outsourcing outcomes due to the lack of appropriate outsourcing cap abilities of a client or their vendor. ITO clients strive to enhance outsourcing performance by establishing their own outsourcing capabilities as well as contracting with capable vendors to meet their strategic needs. Thus, for the success of an ITO project, it is imperative to <sup>fi</sup>nd an e<sup>f</sup>ective combination of complementary outsourcing capabilities on both sides. However, knowledge of how to identify and develop a set of appropriate outsourcing capabilities of both client and vendor has yet to be developed. This study aims to <sup>fi</sup>ll this knowledge gap by ela borating on the con<sup>fi</sup>gurational mechanisms of outsourcing capabilities from a bilateral perspective, which explicate how multiple types of client and vendor outsourcing capabilities combine into con<sup>fi</sup>gurations simultaneously to produce high ITO performance. First, based on the ITO literature, we develop a conceptual framework that identi<sup>fi</sup>es three key types of outsourcing capabilities. Then, with the matched dataset collected from a client and their vendor, we conduct a fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA), which is a set-theoretic con<sup>fi</sup>gurational method to investigate the systemic and holistic interdependencies among key outsourcing cap abilities that produce high outsourcing performance. On the basis of the conceptual framework and empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings, we present four viable propositions to build con<sup>fi</sup>gurations of client and vendor ITO capabilities that can achieve ITO success with theoretical and practical implications.

## Introduction

Information Technology Outsourcing (ITO) has been in the limelight in the past few decades because of its considerable potential to substitute for or complement in-house IT operations and management (Gopal et al., 2003; Han and Mithas, 2013). The recent proliferation of cloud computing and business process o<sup>f</sup>shoring demonstrates the increasing popularity of ITO (Dhar, 2012; Schneider and Sunyaev, 2016). Previous studies have inferred that ITO primarily enables a <sup>fi</sup>rm to reduce IT costs by increasing operational e<sup>fi</sup>ciency and to focus on the core business by rearranging critical <sup>fi</sup>rm resources (Lacity and Willcocks, 2013). At the same time, as often reported by practitioners, ITO may also lead to higher managerial costs because of the complex nature of cooperation between a client and their vendors, which can result in unsatisfactory outsourcing outcomes (Cabral et al., 2014; Clark et al., 1995). Besides, the lack of appropriate managerial expertise as well as contracts with incapable vendors are reported to have negative in<sup>fl</sup>uences on outsourcing performance (Liang et al., 2016, Weigelt and Sarkar, 2012).

As a viable solution for enhancing ITO performance, clients have striven to develop their own outsourcing capabilities and/or search for capable vendors who can e<sup>f</sup>ectively meet their strategic needs (Han et al., 2013; Pratap, 2014). Many ITO studies have built on the resource-based view (RBV) to shed light on the ITO-related capabilities of clients and vendors that can appropriately explore and exploit <sup>fi</sup>rm resources and achieve ITO success (Aral and Weill, 2007; Balaji and Brown, 2005; Handley, 2017; Ranganathan and Balaji, 2007). For example, Feeny and Willcocks (1998) suggested nine core IT capabilities that derive potentia value from <sup>fi</sup>rm resources, and other researchers have also suggested various types of capabilities relevant to ITO (Balaji and Brown, 2005; Bharadwaj, 2000; Han et al., 2013; Handley, 2017; Lacity et al., 2010; Lee, 2008; Levina and Ross, 2003; Palvia et al., 2010; Ranganathan and Balaji, 2007; Su and Gargeya, 2012). In the current study, we focus on explaining ITO capabilities that enable <sup>fi</sup>rms to achieve high outsourcing performance (Helfat and Peteraf, 2003; Hitt et al., 2016; Holcomb and Hitt, 2007; Makadok, 2001). By synthesizing extant ITO studies, we categorize the diverse types of ITO-related <sup>fi</sup>rm capabilities into management, technical, and relational capabilities.

Prior research has advanced our knowledge of these three types of ITO capabilities, but there is still a lack of understanding, especially about the complementary nature of the ITO capabilities on both the client and vendor sides (Liang et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2018). The knowledge gap could be partly but importantly attributed to the tendency of extant studies to focus on the net e<sup>f</sup>ect of the individual capabilities of the client or vendor, paying less attention to the complementary or holistic e<sup>f</sup>ects of both sides’ capabilities on outsourcing performance (Han et al., 2013). Building and exploiting appropriate capabilities for successful ITO require a client to consider both their own capabilities and their vendors capabilities simultaneously (Levina and Ross, 2003) because outsourcing performance is determined by their successful collaboration (Lee et al., 2008). ITO performance is determined not by the unilateral e<sup>f</sup>orts of either side but by how to maximize the synergy from both sides. Therefore, we argue that it is imperative to understand how to con<sup>fi</sup>gure the two sides’ ITO capabilities in a way to maximize ITO performance. This idea is fundamental for achieving the current study’s aims to elaborate on <sup>fi</sup>t and complementarity by uncovering congruent patterns in the viable con<sup>fi</sup>gurations of ITO cap abilities of both client and vendor (Barua et al., 1996; Fiss, 2011; Han et al., 2013; Liu et al., 2018).

To achieve this objective, we adopt a con<sup>fi</sup>gurational approach with the premise that ITO performance is better explained by the systemic interaction of the two sides’ outsourcing capabilities (Fiss, 2007, 2011; Park et al., 2017). On the basis of a comprehensive literature review, we develop a conceptual framework that consists of the three types of ITO capabilities (i.e., management, technical, and relational capabilities) and three contextual factors (i.e., project period, project amount, and prior experience) for successful ITO. Then, with the matched dataset collected from a client and their vendor, we conduct a fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA) and uncover multiple con<sup>fi</sup>gurations that produce high ITO performance (Fiss, 2007; Ragin, 2008). Based on these <sup>fi</sup>ndings, we develop four propositions that prescribe multiple alternative ways to configure the ITO capabilities on both sides to achieve ITo success, depending on the contextual factors.

Thus, this study makes contributions to the ITO literature by developing new theoretical insights into the complex nature of combinations of multiple ITO capabilities of both client and vendor that produce high ITO performance. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, our conceptual framework and a matching con<sup>fi</sup>gurational approach with fsQCA can guide IS researchers for investigating holistic, systemic relationships among key ITO capabilities from the bilateral perspective. Furthermore, our prescriptive propositions help practitioners to con<sup>fi</sup>gure their own capabilities as well as their partners’ capabilities to e<sup>f</sup>ectively achieve ITO success.

## Theoretical background

Outsourcing capability refers to the ability to deploy sourcing-related resources in an outsourcing arrangement (Balaji and Brown, 2005). Ranganathan and Balaji (2007) de<sup>fi</sup>ned ITO capabilities as a set of e<sup>f</sup>ective organizing processes that a <sup>fi</sup>rm uses to exploit internal and cross-border IT resources. As such, prior studies have primarily investigated and conceptualized outsourcing capability based on the RBV (Barney, 1991; Handley, 2017). From the RBV, a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s capability is emphasized along with its dominant roles in manipulating <sup>fi</sup>rm resources to achieve business objectives. In a similar vein, outsourcing capability can be recognized around its roles and value in exploring and exploiting internal and external <sup>fi</sup>rm resources to achieve high outsourcing performance (Han et al., 2008).

Outsourcing capabilities can be categorized into two classes, according to who possesses them: client-side outsourcing capability, de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a client’s ability to acquire, distribute, and leverage outsourcing-related resources and assets, and vendor-side outsourcing capability, de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a vendor’s ability to identify, respond to, and manage the outsourcing-related needs of a client utilizing outsourcing-related resources and assets (Han et al., 2013). These two-sided types of outsourcing capability not only in<sup>fl</sup>uence the entire outsourcing performance gauge, but also a<sup>f</sup>ect the entire ITO process and outcomes by causing complementary e<sup>f</sup>ects based on the reciprocal nature of the relationships between a client and their vendors (Kishore et al., 2003). Conceptually, complementarities between activities indicate that the increase in the level of one side’s abilities (i.e., client outsourcing capability and its pertinent utilization) leads to higher marginal returns from the increase in the level of the other side’s capabilities (i.e., vendor outsourcing capability and its pertinent utilization) (Han et al., 2013). A client and their vendors in an ITO project are usually tied together in that they may share a common goal of ITO success and thus must closely collaborate with each other. Actively shoring up where a partner is weak and enduring short-term disadvantages on account of the partner may be essential for achieving the shared goal of high outsourcing performance (Lee, 2001).

The RBV of <sup>fi</sup>rms (Barney, 1991) considers ITO capability of clients as consisting of IT related resources or assets and management ability. Bharadwaj (2000) de<sup>fi</sup>ned the speci<sup>fi</sup>c IT resources of client <sup>fi</sup>rms as IT infrastructure, human IT resources, and IT-enabled intangibles. Ross et al. (1996) classi<sup>fi</sup>ed the client capability into a re-usable technology base (technical asset), competent IT skills (human IT asset), and an intimate relationship between IT and business units (relationship asset). The study by Lee et al. (1995) on IT professionals suggested that to lead organizational integration and process innovation activities effectively. firms would demand a set of skills in the areas of technical knowledge, business operations, management, and interpersonal relationships. Feeny and Willcocks (1998) characterized nine distinct IT functions as a set of core capabilities, namely, IT governance, business systems thinking, relationship building, designing technical architecture, making technology work, informed buying, contract facilitation, contract monitoring, and vendor development. They mapped the nine capabilities with the skill and knowledge requirement framework proposed by Lee et al. (1995). Given that client <sup>fi</sup>rms possess various IT resources, they must carefully choose which ones should be prioritized in creating synergetic capabilities. Considering that the RBV explicitly recognizes the importance of intangibles (Bharadwaj, 2000), we utilized the RBV to identify measurable constructs that re<sup>fl</sup>ect the client capability for ITO success. Speci-<sup>fi</sup>cally, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the three most strategic areas of client capability a<sup>f</sup>ecting outsourcing performance; management, technical, and relational capabilities. These constructs are the same ones identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the study by Han et al. (2008), which were conceptualized based on the extant RBV studies that operationalized the client outsourcing capability (Feeny and Willcocks, 1998; Lee et al., 1995; Newbert, 2007). These constructs were de<sup>fi</sup>ned based on the Valuable, Rare, Inimitable, and Organizational (VRIO) framework to assess the performance implications of the resources and capabilities for sustaining competitive advantage (Barney, 1991; Black and Boal, 1994). Furthermore, we used the resource categorization framework of Mills et al. (2003) to ascertain whether the three given constructs comprehensively re<sup>fl</sup>ect the client outsourcing capabilities.

The ITO literature has paid much less attention to the vendor’s outsourcing capability and how it generates value in ITO projects, focusing instead on corporate asset requirements and the skills required for IT personnel. For instance, Kim and Chung (2003) explain that vendor capability includes experience, track record, technical competence, and financial status. Although many studies on IT resources and capabilities a<sup>f</sup>ecting business performance have been conducted (Straub et al., 2008), few studies have holistically explored the capability of vendors except for some studies, for example, Levina and Ross (2003) that investigate the value proposition from the vendor perspective through the case study approach, and argue the importance of the expertise of vendors in managing a healthy client–vendor relationship. In general, capabilities grow through the <sup>fi</sup>rm-wide experience that vendors gain from implementing a large number of diverse projects. Levina and Ross (2003) identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the three major areas of the core competence of vendor <sup>fi</sup>rms: personnel development, methodology development and dissemination, and customer relationship management, which are key enablers in creating and maintaining the competitiveness of vendors and in realizing the outsourcing objectives of clients. This study developed the three strategic areas of vendor outsourcing capability based on Levina and Ross’s study, namely, man agement, technical, and relational capabilities, which mostly pertain to human and organizational resource categories rather than to physical or <sup>fi</sup>nancial categories. We adopt a con<sup>fi</sup>gurational approach to e<sup>f</sup>ectively investigate the interrelated outsourcing capabilities from the two sides to uncover how the multiple types of outsourcing capabilities combine into con<sup>fi</sup>gurations to produce high outsourcing performance.

Based on this conceptual framework, we suggest our research model (Fig. 2). This research model is constructed on the theoretical assumption that <sup>fi</sup>rms are constituted by relevant attributes, and the way that these attributes are combined matters in relation to <sup>fi</sup>rms achieving the outcomes of interest. Thus, this con<sup>fi</sup>gurational perspective allows us to investigate the holistic, systemic e<sup>f</sup>ect rather than the net-e<sup>f</sup>ect of individual attributes (Fiss, 2011; Ragin, 2008). Furthermore, we incorporate a complementarity perspective into the model on account of the reciprocal nature of outsourcing relationships between a client and their vendors (Lee, 2001; Lee and Choi, 2011; Liu et al., 2018). This perspective indicates that both parties generally share the same objectives and hence would bene<sup>fi</sup>t by actively collaborating to achieve the shared goal.

## Predictors: outsourcing capability

Management Capability: Management capability is a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s ability to recognize, acquire, and coordinate resources to achieve business objectives (Feeny and Willcocks, 1998; Ranganathan and Balaji, 2007). Firm resources required for successful ITO are usually dispersed across <sup>fi</sup>rm boundaries; thus, the ITO success depends partly on a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s management capability to recognize and integrate fragmented resources and maximize complementarities among them.

As aforementioned, in the process of ITO project implementation, the management capabilities of both sides matter for outsourcing performance. The client-side management capability focuses on coordinating the relationships between ITO project teams and business groups within the firm so that ITO project can garner active cooperation and resources from business groups. In addition, a client <sup>fi</sup>rm needs to adequately manage and control the previously secured resource pools.

On the other hand, the vendor-side management capability emphasizes standardized rules and procedures for e<sup>fi</sup>cient ITO implementation and systematic problem solving (Han et al., 2013). A vendor’s management capability enables ITO projects to e<sup>f</sup>ectively exploit resources in a way to enhance operational e<sup>fi</sup>ciency and satisfy the strategic needs of client <sup>fi</sup>rms. Similar to management capability, each side’s technical capability has unique roles and functions. First, a client <sup>fi</sup>rm should decide on an appropriate level of technical capability depending on their strategic needs. If the strategic objective of ITO is to reduce IT-related costs by augmenting non-core business/IT functions to vendors, the client may not need to maintain a high level of technical capability. Rather, they should focus on identifying a list of business/IT functions to be outsourced (including technica expertise) to vendors. However, if a client <sup>fi</sup>rm intends to actively operate core business/IT functions with technical support from vendors, it may have to develop a high level of technical capability so that it can prevent vendors from engaging in opportunistic behaviors (Hirschheim and Lacity, 2000).

On the other hand, regarding a vendor-side technical capability, it is imperative to retain and exploit a set of technical knowledge and skills required for each ITO project. In general, a vendor s level of technical capability is directly associated with its level of competitiveness in the market. A higher level of technical capability enables a vendor to provide the appropriate level of service and cope with potential technical hurdles. Furthermore, technical capability increases the chance for the vendor to seize future business opportunities by signaling its technical ability to successfully implement ITO projects (Michell and Fitzgerald, 1997).

Relational capability: Relational capability refers to a <sup>fi</sup>rm s ability to facilitate and maintain reciprocal relationships to achieve business obiectives (Balaji and Brown. 2005). By building an appropriate level of relational capability, both a client and their vendors may look beyond existing contractual arrangements and ful<sup>fi</sup>ll long-term business value (Han et al., 2013; Kern and Willcocks, 2002). Despite having partially shared characteristics, distinct di<sup>f</sup>erences exist between the relational capabilities of clients and vendors. Clients usually want to access and manage a su<sup>fi</sup>cient pool of competent vendors so that they can e<sup>f</sup>ectively cope with unexpected contingencies without incurring much switching cost (Whitten et al., 2010). The literature emphasizes that a client's relational capability covers controlling, coordinating, and maintaining relationships with the current vendors and simultaneously building and managing replacement strategies (Balaji and Brown, 2005). Thus, it is one of the critical factors for ITO success (Feeny and Willcocks, 1998).

On the vendor side, maintaining amicable relationships with multiple clients is essential, not only for the current business in terms of maintaining su<sup>fi</sup>cient pro<sup>fi</sup>tability through economies of scale but also for long-term business continuity. Interestingly, relationa capability has unique characteristics distinguished from the other two capabilities. A considerable amount of time and e<sup>f</sup>ort from both sides, based on mutual trust and partnership, is indispensable for developing a high level of relational capability (Lee and Kim, 1999); thus, relational capability is di<sup>fi</sup>cult to develop with only unilateral e<sup>f</sup>orts; a bilateral reciprocal relationship should instead be sought and secured (Han et al., 2013).

## Outcomes: project performance

In the current study, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne ITO project performance as an outcome of the con<sup>fi</sup>gurations of the outsourcing capabilities of a client and their vendors (Bharadwaj, 2000; Peppard and Ward, 2004). ITO project performance can be de<sup>fi</sup>ned and measured in terms of two key aspects of the ITO project. The <sup>fi</sup>rst one is process performance, which re<sup>fl</sup>ects how well the ITO implementation process has been undertaken (i.e., the extent to which the project is delivered on schedule and within budget), and the second is product/ service performance, which refers to the quality of a product or service delivered to clients (Nidumolu, 1995; Wallace et al., 2004). The two factors represent the distinct aspects of ITO project success and should be considered simultaneously when measuring outsourcing performance, because one factor may not fully represent ITO success (Nidumolu, 1995). For example, ITO projects completed within the given time and the budget may result in poor products or services. On the contrary, even with problems in terms of project schedule and budget, the quality of delivered products or services may be better than expected. Thus, in assessing ITO project performance, we take the two factors together into consideration.

## Contextual factors

Based on the ITO literature, we assume that depending on the context there are di<sup>f</sup>erent ways to con<sup>fi</sup>gure ITO capabilities to achieve high outsourcing performance. We include the period and the amount of an ITO project and prior experience between a client and their vendors as contextual factors which are known to have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on ITO project success (Currie and Willcocks, 1998; Gopal and Gosain, 2010; Koh et al., 2004; Quinn, 1999, Willcocks and Feeny, 2006).

Project period and amount: According to the ITO literature, the period and the amount of an ITO project, mostly speci<sup>fi</sup>ed in a contract, a<sup>f</sup>ect ITO project performance (Currie and Willcocks, 1998; Gopal and Gosain, 2010; Koh et al., 2004). In a similar vein, the project period and amount are important for deciding the appropriate level of ITO capabilities (i.e., management, technical, and relational capabilities) for high ITO project performance. First, the project period signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly a<sup>f</sup>ects the relationships between a client and their vendors and the required types of outsourcing capabilities to achieve the ITO success. For example, in a short-term ITO project, clients tend to have contract-based relationships so that they can enforce their bargaining power (Yao et al., 2010), whereas reciprocal partnerships are more preferred in a long-term ITO project because they facilitates mutual trust, which provides a solid basis for long-term collaboration (Grover et al., 1996: Lee. 2001: Lee and Kim. 1999). The effect of relational capability can vary depending on the project period. A long-term ITO project may require a relatively high level of relational capability, whereas the precise ful<sup>fi</sup>llment of a short-term project may not (Chen and Bharadwaj, 2009). Second, regarding the project amount, clients with a large-sized ITO project may intend to improve their operational stability and focus on core business by rearranging <sup>fi</sup>rm resources (Barthélemy and Geyer, 2004). On the other hand, clients with a small-sized ITO project may attempt to negotiate with vendors by signaling a possibility of switching vendors anytime to reduce short-term operational costs (Lacity et al., 1996). This indicates that <sup>fi</sup>rms conventionally decide the project amount in accordance with their strategic goals; thus, the required level of management and technical capabilities can be di<sup>f</sup>erent depending on the ITO project amount. Therefore, we incorporated the project period and amount as contextual factors that may have potential impacts on the ways to configure outsourcing capabilities to achieve high outsourcing performance.

Prior experience: Prior experience refers to the direct engagement a client has had with their vendor prior to starting a new ITO project. Prior experience helps to establish communication channels between a client and their vendors, which in turn enables them to build reciprocal relationships and enhance their mutual understanding of business requirements. According to the ITO literature, the length and depth of prior experience between a client and their vendors have been treated as one of the critical factors in estimating the level of IT service provision at the organizational level (Chang and King, 2005; Weill and Olson, 1989). In addition, the source of knowledge is emphasized as a determinant of future behavior (Fishbein and Ajzen, 2011) and a critical means to form organizational actions in developing a path-dependent absorptive capability (Zahra and George, 2002). Thus, we incorporate prior experience as an additional contextual factor to facilitate a further understanding of the con<sup>fi</sup>gurations of ITO capabilities that produce high ITO project performance.

## Research method

We adopt fsQCA, which is particularly useful for empirically investigating multiple alternative con<sup>fi</sup>gurations that emerge from the interactions of interdependent conditions, to achieve outcomes of interest (Fiss, 2011; Lee et al. forthcoming; Misangyi and Acharya, 2014; Ragin, 2008). In general, the con<sup>fi</sup>gurations uncovered by an fsQCA are translated into causal recipes, which prescribe how to con<sup>fi</sup>gure all elements simultaneously to produce a target outcome (Ragin, 2008). Thus, we take a holistic perspective equipped with fsQCA to investigate which con<sup>fi</sup>gurations of ITO client and vendor capabilities produce high ITO project performance. In the following, we describe the data collection in detail and the fsQCA steps brie<sup>fl</sup>y.<sup>1</sup>

## Sample and data collection

The research design is a <sup>fi</sup>eld study of Korean <sup>fi</sup>rms that have been engaged in ITO projects. A <sup>fi</sup>eld study is known as a usefu research method to make a more generalizable theory across di<sup>f</sup>erent settings (Stone, 1978). ## Data analysis with an fsQCA

As the <sup>fi</sup>rst step of fsQCA, we calibrated the set memberships for all variables. Calibration is a process to transform conventional interval measures to membership scores using three qualitative thresholds for full membership, crossover point, and full nonmembership (Ragin, 2008). For the calibration of each element, we decided on three thresholds based on extant literature and substantive domain knowledge. Thereafter, we utilized the direct method of calibration in the fsQCA software to transform original measures into set membership ranging from 0 to 1, in which a value closer to 0 means lower or no membership in the group of interest and a value closer to 1 means higher membership in the group (Fiss. 2011: Ragin. 2008). For instance, set membership score 1 means full membership in the group of <sup>fi</sup>rms with high outsourcing performance, and 0 means the opposite. That is, a <sup>fi</sup>rm with set membership near to 1 achieved higher outsourcing performance, a firm with membership score near to 0 did not achieve the desired outsourcing performance, and a <sup>fi</sup>rm with 0.5 is neither in nor out of the high ITO performance group.

Before calibration, measures for project performance and capabilities were <sup>fi</sup>rst averaged with their multiple item scores, and then average values were calibrated into fuzzy-set scores. We set interval scale 6 as the anchor for full membership, 2 for full nonmembership, and 4 for the cross-over point by following suggestions in earlier fsQCA studies (Fiss, 2011; Park et al., 2017). For prior experience, following the recommendation of extant QCA studies (e.g., Campbell et al., 2016; Fiss, 2011), we set 75, 50, and 25 percentiles as three anchors for full membership, cross-over, and non-full membership, respectively. That is, <sup>fi</sup>rms with prior experience in the lower quartile of the entire sample (i.e., ≤25th percentile) were calibrated as “fully out” of the set of high prior experience; <sup>fi</sup>rms with prior experience in the upper quartile (i.e., ≥75th percentile) were coded as “fully in”. We used the halfway mark between the two anchors as the crossover point. We also applied the same calibration scheme using percentile values for project period and amount. With the calibrated set membership scores, fsQCA proceeds to the next step with a truth-table algorithm that determines con-<sup>fi</sup>gurations that su<sup>fi</sup>ciently produce the outcome (Greckhamer et al., 2013; Ragin, 2008). To identify con<sup>fi</sup>gurations that consistently produce the outcome, we set two thresholds for minimum consistency and the frequency of cases per row in the truth table. First, we set the minimum acceptable frequency to one case per con<sup>fi</sup>guration by considering 50 cases in our dataset.<sup>2</sup> Second, by following recommendations (Park et al., 2017; Ragin, 2008), we set a cuto<sup>f</sup> at 0.8 for raw consistency and 0.75 for PRI consistency. This designation means that a row with raw consistency above 0.8 and PRI consistency above 0.75 is identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as consistently producing high project performance. In Appendix B, we present the resulting truth table. There are 16 rows (i.e., combinations) satisfying both frequency and consistency thresholds (marked “1″ in Project Performance column in the table), which is normal, similar to those found in extant QCA studies (e.g., Fiss, 2011; Lee et al., forthcoming; Park et al., 2017). Although mathematically there are many possible rows in the truth table (in our study 512 possible rows, i.e., 2 to the power of 9), in reality cases have memberships only in a small portion of rows/con<sup>fi</sup>gurations because of some empirical restricts, industry best practices such as enterprise systems, institutional pressures, and organizational logics (e.g., DiMaggio and Powell, 1983; Hannan and Freeman, 1989; Miller, 1987) that force <sup>fi</sup>rms to choose one of only a few alternative viable con<sup>fi</sup>gurations. Furthermore, a con<sup>fi</sup>gurational perspective assumes that all elements are interdependent with each other and simultaneously combine into con<sup>fi</sup>gurations, which mostly result in a small number of parsimonious con<sup>fi</sup>gurations covering a large portion of the empirical cases (i.e., limited diversity) (Meyer et al., 1993; Park and Mithas, forthcoming).

In addition, fsOCA uses counterfactual analysis to overcome the limitations of a lack of empirical instances (Ragin. 2008, p. 162). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, a counterfactual analysis further minimizes the number of causal conditions in a con<sup>fi</sup>guration by handling rows without empirical cases (Fiss, 2007, 2011). The “easy” counterfactual analysis deals with an empirically unobserved combination by adding a condition known to produce the outcome to a combination, whereas the “di<sup>fi</sup>cult” counterfactual analysis deals with an empirically unobserved combination by removing a redundant condition from a combination (Ragin, 2008, p. 162). By applying easy and di<sup>fi</sup>cult counterfactuals, the truth-table algorithm makes the most parsimonious solution, in which elements have a stronger causa relationship with the outcomes (Fiss, 2011) and thus become core conditions for the outcome. In addition, with only easy counterfactual analysis, the truth-table algorithm makes an intermediate solution that includes elements with weaker causal relationships with an outcome in addition to the core conditions found in the parsimonious solution. Thus, with intermediate solutions, we can explain which conditions of a con<sup>fi</sup>guration play a core role or a peripheral role in producing the outcome of interest.

We uncover the con<sup>fi</sup>gurations of outsourcing capabilities with three contextual factors that produce high project performance.<sup>3</sup> Table 4 summarizes the fsQCA results in the form of the Boolean expression of parsimonious and intermediate solutions (Ragin, 2008), in which + means logical OR, \* means AND, and \~ means negation. For project performance, three parsimonious combinations producing high ITO project performance are uncovered: vtec\*\~pexp (the vendor’s high technical capability without long prior experience), crel\*vman (the client’s high relational capability and the vendor’s high management capability), and crel\*vtec (the client’s high relational capability and the vendor’s high technical capability). The intermediate solution includes eight con<sup>fi</sup>gurations for high project performance. Particularly, elements in the parsimonious solution are embedded in the intermediate solution as core conditions. Elements that only appeared in the intermediate solution are treated as peripheral conditions that may complement core conditions in producing the outcome (Fiss, 2011).

Fig. 3 graphically depicts the intermediate solution of eight con<sup>fi</sup>gurations in Table 4 by following the notation system of Ragin and Fiss (2008). Each rectangle corresponds to one con<sup>fi</sup>guration in which a large circle indicates a “core” condition and a small circle a “peripheral” one. A dark circle indicates that it must be present, a crossed-out circle indicates it must be absent for the con<sup>fi</sup>guration to produce the expected outcome. Lastly, a blank space is a “do-not-care condition,” which means that it has no causal relevance to the outcome such that it can be either present or absent in the con<sup>fi</sup>guration to produce the outcome of interest (Campbell et al., 2016). This graphical expression helps us in more e<sup>f</sup>ectively comparing the similarities and di<sup>f</sup>erences within and across con<sup>fi</sup>gurations such that we can extract patterns for high outsourcing performance. The fsQCA software provides two quantitative measures that prove the validity of solutions—consistency and coverage (Ragin, 2008), which are presented in Fig. 3. The consistency of each con<sup>fi</sup>guration indicates the degree to which the con<sup>fi</sup>guration consistently results in the outcome, a measure corresponding to the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant level alpha in regression. The coverage indicates the extent to which it covers the cases with the outcome, a measure similar to explained variance (R<sup>2</sup>) in regression. Thus, in this study, consistency shows how <sup>fi</sup>rms sharing the same set of outsourcing capabilities consistently produce high project performance. Moreover, the coverage of a con<sup>fi</sup>guration implies its empirical relativit and importance (Fiss, 2007; Ragin, 2008).

Thus, con<sup>fi</sup>guration S1 with a highest coverage is empirically most relevant, meaning that more <sup>fi</sup>rms achieving high ITO project performance have this con<sup>fi</sup>guration. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, S1 re<sup>fl</sup>ects a general solution—<sup>fi</sup>rms with high levels of all six ITO capabilities achieve high ITO project performance regardless of contextual factors, which can be expected and thus may not be surprising. However, except for this general con<sup>fi</sup>guration S1, in all other con<sup>fi</sup>gurations contextual factors matter for achieving the outcome.

Now, by comparing similarities and di<sup>f</sup>erences among the con<sup>fi</sup>gurations, we can <sup>fi</sup>nd some unique patterns. First, except for S5, all con<sup>fi</sup>gurations exhibit high relational capability of client as a core element, meaning that a client s relational capability has a critical role in achieving high project performance. On the other hand, for vendors, technical capability and management capability are core elements for most con<sup>fi</sup>gurations to achieve high project performance. We also found patterns di<sup>f</sup>ering over contexts such that successful ITO projects share a similar combination of ITO capabilities depending on project period and amount, for example, con<sup>fi</sup>guration pairs of (S2, S3), (S4, S5), and (S6, S7). First, the common structure re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the intersection between S2 and S3 is high levels of ITO capabilities except for client technical capability in a short-term, small-sized ITO project without a long prior experience. Second, S4 and S5 are con<sup>fi</sup>gurations of high performance in a short-term, large-sized ITO project without a long prior experience. S4 lacks high technical capability in the vendor side, whereas S5 lacks high relational capability in the client side. Lastly, S6 and S7 represent successful ITO cases in a long-term and large-sized outsourcing. S6 represents cases without long prior experience, and S7 represents cases with long prior experience. Lastly, although empirically less relevant, S8 shows a surprising pattern of low management capabilities on both sides for a long-term, small-sized ITO project in a case with no prior experience. Particularly, with the intersection analysis (Park et al., 2017), we can <sup>fi</sup>nd the common structure between S2 and S3, especially the same structure for all ITO capabilities of both sides. Furthermore, based on the coverages of S2 and S3, and the very small coverage of S8, we can decide that S2 has more membership in small & short in quadrant 3. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the common structure of con<sup>fi</sup>gurations S2 and S3 implies that when carrying out a short-term, small-sized ITO project, a client can rely on a vendor’s overall high outsourcing capability and it does not need to have a high level of technica capability to achieve high ITO project performance. Meanwhile, a vendor with high technical and management capabilities can quickly respond to technical concerns and complete ITO projects within a given schedule and budget (Han et al., 2013). In particular, the common pattern identi<sup>fi</sup>ed from S2 and S3 shows prior work experience between a client and their vendor is unnecessary in this context. One typical example may be a supply of <sup>fi</sup>nished IT products (e.g., server, storage, packaged software, and network device), that a vendor is responsible for the very detailed process that the ITO project entails, and that all vendor-led <sup>fi</sup>nal products are delivered to their client (Pinnington and Woolcock, 1997). By conducting multiple small-sized ITO projects performed over a short period of time, a client can reduce a vendor s opportunistic behavior and monitor and control the vendor s project implementation based on contract-based relationships accrued from their high management capability (Chang et al., 2017). As a core condition, a client s high relational capability also provides more <sup>fl</sup>exible project implementation to guarantee the ultimate quality of <sup>fi</sup>na outcomes (Ye et al., 2014). Given these patterns from empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings of S2 and S3, we suggest the following:

Proposition 1. For a short-term and small-sized ITO project, regardless of prior experience, a client’s relational capability and a vendor’s management and technical capabilities are essential to achieve high project performance. In addition, a client must equip management capability but does not need to have high technical capability

Con<sup>fi</sup>guration S8 in quadrant 4 (Table 5) shows that in a long-term and small-sized ITO project, high technical and relationa capabilities of both a client and their vendors are important to achieve high project performance. A typical example of S8 may be cloud computing, which is often based on a small-sized, long-term contract. As the pattern in S8 shows, the successful implementation of cloud computing generally depends on a vendor s technical capability rather than a client s management capability. When speci<sup>fi</sup>c IT areas are partially outsourced over a long period of time, a client’s technical capability is required to select a right ITO area and harmonize it with other non-outsourced areas (Han et al., 2013). Here, a vendor s technical capability plays a critical role in leading their client throughout the entire project by enabling an accurate and stable provision of contracted products or services over a long period of time against frequent changes of IT trends as well. At the same time, a client s relational capability is essential for creating and maintaining reciprocal partnerships with their vendors to handle unforeseen risks and to ensure a successful ITO implementation in the long run (Lee and Kim, 1999). By contrast, if a small-sized ITO project is relatively simple, a high level of management capability on both sides may not be necessary, as we witness in cloud computing outsourcing which does not need a high level of management capability but rather a high technical and relational capability (Schneider and Sunyaev, 2016). In addition, as shown in S8, this combination of outsourcing capabilities does not require prior experience between two parties, but rather they can build through a long-term ITO project based on technical and relational capabilities. With these insights, we suggest the second proposition

## as follows:

Proposition 2. For a long-term, small-sized ITO project, both clients and vendors do not need to rely on management capability and prior experience but on technical and relational capability to achieve high project performance. In addition, a client’s relational capability and a vendor’s technical capability should be made and centered in the configuration.

In a long-term and large-sized ITO project in quadrant 2 (Table 5), a client should have overall high levels of all ITO capabilities. In particular, a client’s high level of relational capability is essential to facilitate a stable project execution through reciproca relationships with vendors. On the other hand, a vendor needs to have a high level of management capability to systematically manage a large-scale project over a long period of time. Further, depending on the presence of prior work experience with their client, a vendor must have high technical or relational capability. Con<sup>fi</sup>gurations S6 and S7 suggest two pathways to achieve high project performance depending on prior experience between a client and their vendors. The pathways show the complementary nature of a vendor’s outsourcing capability according to prior work experience with their client (Teo and Bhattacherjee, 2014). First, as shown in S7, when a client has a long prior experience with a same vendor, critical technical knowledge has been already transferred from the vendor to the client. Thus, when a client outsources IT from a vendor with whom it has a long prior experience, a vendor s technica capability may not be critical for an ITO project to succeed. A representative example is total outsourcing that outsources most IT areas to one dominant vendor (Koo et al., 2017). In such a situation, the creation and maintenance of a reciprocal partnership become essential to achieving high outsourcing performance. Thus, the relational capability of both sides plays a key role over an entire project execution (Lee and Kim, 1999). By contrast, without prior experience between the two parties as is the case of con<sup>fi</sup>guration S6, vendors must lead the ITO project with high technical and project management capability, whereas client relational capability is critical to building close collaboration. Thus, we suggest the following:

Proposition 3. For a long-term and large-sized ITO project with no prior relational experience with vendors, a client’s relational capabilities and a vendor’s management and technical capabilities are essential for achieving high project performance. However, in the situation when a client has a long prior working experience with vendors, it does not require a vendor’s high technical capability

Doing a large-scale ITO project in a short period tends to be riskier and thus requires intensive e<sup>f</sup>ort and collaboration between a client and their vendors. Con<sup>fi</sup>gurations S4 and S5 in quadrant 1 show two alternative ways to con<sup>fi</sup>gure outsourcing capabilities to achieve high outsourcing performance. First, regardless of a vendor’s prior work experience with their client, the speci<sup>fi</sup>c patterns of outsourcing capabilities can be di<sup>f</sup>erent depending on the substitutable relation between a client’s relational capability and a vendor’s technical capability. S5 represents a vendor leading ITO projects that emphasize the importance of a vendor’s technica capability which other capabilities complement. As numerous tasks must be implemented in a short period of time, a vendor needs to have a sufficient level of technical expertise to cope with unexpected technical issues. By contrast. S4 represents a client leading an ITO project, in which a client’s relational capability coupled with a vendor’s management capability represents core conditions for high project performance (Pinnington and Woolcock, 1997). On the basis of a high level of relational capability, a client may reduce switching costs and minimize a vendor’s opportunistic behavior without locking in any speci<sup>fi</sup>c vendors (Huo et al., 2016). A vendor’s high management capability enables it to e<sup>f</sup>ectively complete within a given schedule and budget. A typical example is IT consultation or an ERP project that covers most IT areas (Oesterle et al., 2016). As these projects must be completed within a relatively short period of time coupled with intensive resource exploitation, managing the project based on overall high outsourcing capabilities and equipping it with appropriate outsourcing capabilities in accord with individual project situations are critical. Thus, we suggest the following:

Proposition 4. For a short-term, larger-sized ITO project with no prior relational experience with vendors, a vendor’s management and relational capabilities can complement a client outsourcing capability to achieve high project performance, in which a vendor’s management capability and a client’s relational capability play an essential role. Alternatively, a client working with a vendor equipped with all outsourcing capabilities, particularly strong technical capability, does not need to have relational capability.

## Theoretical and practical implications

This study makes several theoretical contributions to the ITO literature by developing a holistic understanding of complex dynamics among interdependent multiple outsourcing capabilities of both clients and vendors. First, with substantial support from prior studies, this study conceptually identi<sup>fi</sup>es three types of outsourcing capabilities on both sides and elucidates their nature and interdependent e<sup>f</sup>ects on outsourcing performance. Our categorization of bilateral ITO capabilities covers most resources required for successful ITO projects and reveals that each capability has its own roles and implications in deriving potential value of ITOrelated resources. Thus, the three outsourcing capabilities not only advance our understanding of ITO-related <sup>fi</sup>rm capabilities, but also provide a solid foundation that enables researchers to investigate diverse phenomena related to outsourcing capability in ITO projects.

Second, this study extends existing capability perspectives of both clients and vendors to ITO and its empirical validation (Handley, 2017). Most ITO studies have concentrated primarily on clients that decide to outsource, overlooking the importance of vendor capability. Although understanding of both parties in ITO relationships is critical to achieve ITO project success (Kern and Willcocks, 2002), studies on the role of vendors have been mostly conceptual and exploratory, lacking in empirical investigation (Levina and Ross, 2003; Pinnington and Woolcock, 1997). In this study, we conceptualize the capability of vendors and empirically tested its impact, combined with client capability, on outsourcing performance. Thus, this study, as an early empirical attempt, provides a better understanding of the role of client capability, vendor capability, and their combined and synergistic e<sup>f</sup>ects on ITO success.

Lastly, this study adopts a con<sup>fi</sup>guration approach and empirically supports it with a matching fsQCA method to investigate the complex combinations of multiple ITO capabilities from the bilateral perspective. In ITO research, having a bilateral view of client and vendor sides has been recognized as important for its business value (Kern and Willcocks, 2002; Liu et al., 2017; Saunders et al., 1997). However, only a few studies have concretized this view. The concept of <sup>fi</sup>t has been applied, but a simple “<sup>fi</sup>t” has been insu<sup>fi</sup>cient to explain the sophisticated relationship between vendors and clients. Accordingly, we conceptualized con<sup>fi</sup>guration and empirically validated it in the ITO context. The con<sup>fi</sup>guration approach is e<sup>f</sup>ective because it not only simultaneously considers both parties but also speci<sup>fi</sup>es the impact corresponding to the capability level of each party. While prior research merely stated the existence of synergic e<sup>f</sup>ects, this research, by adopting the con<sup>fi</sup>guration approach, was able to predict and explain the impact of such synergy in a more systemic manner.

This study also o<sup>f</sup>ers practical insights into the boundary of necessary assets or skills that need to be cultivated within client <sup>fi</sup>rms to leverage vendor <sup>fi</sup>rms’ capabilities. While most IT-related corporate resources could be outsourced and are mandatory in some sense, client capability, including management, technical, and relational capabilities, should be <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c valuable assets that deserve to be secured internally. Furthermore, on the basis of the con<sup>fi</sup>gurational approach with fsQCA, we empirically found multiple con<sup>fi</sup>gurations and extracted meaningful patterns to explain how outsourcing capabilities work together to achieve high outsourcing performance. By developing four context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c propositions, we suggested speci<sup>fi</sup>c ways to develop and con<sup>fi</sup>gure the outsourcing capabilities of both a client and their vendors according to the project period and project amount and considering the relative length of prior work experience of both parties. Despite some variances, the propositions reveal some equi<sup>fi</sup>nal pathways to high outsourcing performance with the support of the e<sup>f</sup>ective con<sup>fi</sup>gurations of outsourcing capabilities. Practically, these propositions and their implications may enable <sup>fi</sup>rms to formulate and implement an appropriate ITO strategy to con<sup>fi</sup>gure outsourcing capabilities of a client and their vendors for high outsourcing performance.

## Limitations and future research

Our research has also some limitations. First, this study focused on the three types of outsourcing capability that were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed through a comprehensive literature review. However, we admit that other ways of categorizing outsourcing capabilities may also help uncover a richer understanding on the outsourcing capability con<sup>fi</sup>guration (see some examples capabilities in Lacity et al., 2010). Second, we include the three contextual factors with the aim to <sup>fi</sup>nd more context speci<sup>fi</sup>c patterns for ITO capability con-<sup>fi</sup>gurations of high performance. Future research can include other contextual factors, such as industrial category, <sup>fi</sup>rm size, and IT types to be outsourced, which may produce more <sup>fi</sup>ne-grained patterns. However, to include more conditions, researchers should recognize the recommended data size that is appropriate for the number of conditions (e.g., Greckhamer et al., 2013). Third, our dataset includes Korean <sup>fi</sup>rms that adopted ITO and thus we admit that the number of cases in this study (i.e., 50 cases) may not be large enough to cover all possible combinations made by nine conditions in fsQCA. Thus, the results must be carefully interpreted when it comes to generalizability and we call for further studies that can validate and expand our <sup>fi</sup>ndings with large sample data (e.g., more than 100) and across countries, so that the founding con<sup>fi</sup>gurations may not miss important patterns. At the same time, we also admit that the large sample data does not necessarily capture all possible patterns. In addition to sample size, selection of key conditions that are most relevant for explaining the outcome of interest may be critical for finding generalizable configurations Finally, although fsQCA enables us to reveal the two aspects of causality with respect to necessity and su<sup>fi</sup>ciency of conditions for the outcome of interest, our cross-sectional data using a con<sup>fi</sup>gurational approach does not allow us to <sup>fi</sup>nd “temporal causality” which shows some causes and results in outcomes over time. Thus, follow-up research may address this issue with appropriate methodo logical tools (Ragin, 2008).

## Conclusion

The complex and competitive business environment requires <sup>fi</sup>rms to be more sensitive and agile to enhance business performance, and this also applies to the ITO context. In this study we suggest the congruent patterns of outsourcing capabilities in the pursuit of high outsourcing performance, which we empirically extracted by comparing similarities and di<sup>f</sup>erences among con<sup>fi</sup>gurations. The patterns reveal not only the complementary nature but also the synergistic mechanism between various outsourcing capabilities from both client and vendor sides. To truly understand a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s capability for ITO success, we must stop thinking about ITO capabilities in isolation, but instead more attention should be given to how to e<sup>f</sup>ectively combine various outsourcing capabilities from both sides for the intended outsourcing performance. A key implication drawn from our <sup>fi</sup>ndings is that developing outsourcing capability may need to incorporate fundamental changes in internal organizational structure, inter-organizational business process, and vendor management and control externally. We hope that our study sheds new light on how a client and their vendors can better develop the best con<sup>fi</sup>guration of outsourcing capabilities to reap the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts in ITO projects and better position themselves in the long run.
