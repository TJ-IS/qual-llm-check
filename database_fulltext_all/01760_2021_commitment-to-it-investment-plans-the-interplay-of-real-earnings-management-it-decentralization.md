# COMMITMENT TO IT INVESTMENT PLANS: THE INTERPLAY OF REAL EARNINGS, MANAGEMENT, IT DECENTRALIZATION, AND CORPORATE GOVERNANCE<sup>1</sup>

Ling Xue Department of Computer Systems, J. Mack Robinson College of Business, Georgia State University, Atlanta, GA 30303 U.S.A. {lxue5@gsu.edu}

Sunil Mithas School of Information Systems & Management, Muma College of Business, University of South Florida, Tampa, FL 33620-5500 U.S.A. {smithas@usf.edu}

Gautam Ray Department of Information & Decision Sciences, Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, MN 55455 U.S.A. {rayxx153@umn.edu}

How does real earnings management (REM) influence the extent to which firms realize their IT investment plans? To answer this question, we study the association between firms’ engagement in REM and the extent of commitment to planned investments in IT infrastructure. We also study the extent to which this association is moderated by IT decentralization, and, in turn, how such a moderating relationship is influenced by other corporate governance mechanisms such as institutional ownership and takeover threats. We document three important findings based on our analyses of data on 2,884 U.S. firms over the period 1998–2009. First, we find a negative association between engagement in REM and IT infrastructure investment commitment, suggesting potentially negative consequences of REM for realizing IT investment plans. Second, we find that IT decentralization weakens the negative association between REM and IT investment commitment (ITIC), implying that decentralized IT governance mechanisms can serve as a potential antidote to negative effects of REM on IT investments. Finally, we find evidence that other corporate governance mechanisms, such as institutional ownership and takeover threats, can complement IT decentralization to further mitigate the negative association between REM and ITIC. Together, these findings provide new insights on how REM, IT decentralization, and other corporate governance mechanisms influence actual IT investments of firms. Our work here, which focuses on deviations from one’s own IT investment plans, makes a novel contribution by extending prior work which has so far focused only on deviations from industry norm in IT investments.

Keywords: IT investments, earnings management, IT governance, corporate governance, decentralization, institutional ownership, board independence, takeover threats

## Introduction

The importance of information technology (IT) investments to execute digital strategies and digital transformation initiatives has prompted many firms to allocate significant parts of their capital budgets toward IT. However, often such plans are compromised because of other corporate priorities and managerial initiatives. For example, even if firms initially allocate substantial dollars toward IT investments, many IT projects are postponed or canceled mid-stream (Mieritz 2012). In other words, even if firms initially make rational plans for levels of IT investments, the actual realization of such investment decisions is not always guaranteed. Managers may be driven by short-term motives, sometimes driven by self-interest, to compromise the realization of planned IT investments. For example, Graham et al. (2005) report that 80% of surveyed CFOs would like to cut discretionary expenditure on technology, advertising, and maintenance to deliver expected earnings, while 55% would postpone new projects to meet earnings targets, even if such delay may cause small losses.

Although prior research in accounting suggests that corporate managers use real earnings management (REM), short-term alteration of normal business activities to meet earnings performance targets, the extent to which REM influences realization of IT investments remains unknown. Prior research in information systems (IS) has studied determinants and consequences of IT investments using two dominant perspectives; however, neither of these perspectives include a consideration of REM. First, a value-creation perspective considers how firms’ operational scale, scope, and firm boundary decisions influence IT investments (e.g., Dewan et al. 1998; Hitt 1999; Kobelsky et al. 2008; Ravichandran and Liu 2011). The main idea is that firms choose IT investments to support their valuecreation activities. Second, an institutional perspective considers effects of industry norms and competitive actions on IT investments (Ho et al. 2017; Mithas et al. 2013; Salge et al. 2015; Ravichandran et al. 2009). The main idea here is that firms choose IT investments to either conform to or deviate from industry norms as a response to competitive pressure. Although extant studies provide useful insights on determinants of actual IT investments, we know very little about why firms may fall short of their planned IT investments, and how IT governance such as the extent of centralization versus decentralization, and other corporate governance mechanisms influence the realization of actual IT investments.

Against this backdrop, our goal in this study is to understand the link between REM and firms’ commitment to their IT investment plans. We start with a baseline conjecture that firms with greater engagement in REM are less likely to realize their planned IT investments. Next, we consider how the relationship between REM engagement and realization of planned IT investments is moderated by IT decentralization, or the decentralization of IT purchasing rights to lower-level divisional managers. We hypothesize that to the extent that IT decentralization reduces the top management control, it can constrain the effect of REM on the realization of planned IT investments. Our focus here on the effect of IT decentralization in balancing the control power between top-level corporate managers and mid-level divisional managers departs from the extant literature, which has primarily theorized IT decentralization as a governance arrangement for local information processing and knowledge-decision co-location (e.g., Sambamurthy and Zmud 1999; Tiwana 2009). Finally, we examine how various corporate governance mechanisms (e.g., institutional ownership, board independence, and takeover protection) influence the strength of the moderating effect of IT decentralization on the association between REM and realization of IT investment plans. We follow prior work that argues for the general logic that corporate governance helps address the issue of REM (e.g., Bertrand and Mullainathan 2003), and serves as a mechanism for shareholders to regulate the behavior of managers (Cohen et al. 2008).

Our empirical analyses of data on IT infrastructure development for 2,884 U.S. firms over the period 1998–2009 suggest a negative association between REM and the extent to which firms achieve their planned IT infrastructure investments. This finding supports the view that the actual realization of IT investment plans can be affected by managers’ proneness to alter normal business activities in REM. We also find that REM has a weaker influence on the extent to which firms achieve their planned IT infrastructure investments for firms with more decentralized IT decisions. In addition, two corporate governance mechanisms—institutional ownership and takeover protection—complement the mitigating role of IT decentralization on the negative association between REM and the extent to which firms achieve their planned IT infrastructure investments.

Together these findings make important contributions to IT investment and IT governance research by pointing to the important roles of REM, IT decentralization, and corporate governance mechanisms in shaping actual IT investments of firms. Our study also contributes to the understanding of deviation behavior in IT investment. The extant literature has considered deviations from industry peers in investing in IT (e.g., Ho et al. 2017) and what drives managers to follow such a deviation strategy (e.g., Xue et al. 2017). However, the focus of these studies is on deviation from industry norms. We provide a novel perspective by focusing here on the deviation from the firm’s own IT investment plan,<sup>2</sup> and the role of IT decentralization and other governance mechanisms that amplify and mitigate such deviations. Our insights complement the IT governance literature by highlighting the challenges in realizing planned investments in IT, and the roles of potentially mitigating mechanisms.

## Background and Theory

## Real Earnings Management and IT Investments

We draw on two streams of literature to argue for a potential link between REM and IT investments. First, we draw on accounting literature related to earnings management (EM), defined as managers’ intentional use of accounting discretion and manipulation of real business activities to influence shortterm reported earnings such as meeting or beating earnings targets, and thereby affect investor perception and financial reporting-dependent outcomes (Healy and Wahlen 1999; Xu et al. 2007). EM strategies primarily include AEM and REM. AEM refers to the purposeful alteration of reported earnings by changing the accounting methods or estimates used to present given transactions in the financial statements without altering the underlying real business transactions (Zang 2012). In contrast, REM refers to the purposeful altering of reported earnings by manipulating the timing or structuring of real business operations, investments, or transactions, which may have long-term suboptimal business consequences (Graham et al. 2005; Roychowdhury 2006).<sup>3</sup>

Although early research focused more on AEM, REM has become more important and prevalent in practice due to increasingly tough regulation (e.g., the Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002, referred to as SOX) and it is relatively harder to detect for outsiders and auditors (Cohen et al. 2008). REM is typically evidenced by abnormal business activities such as heavy promotions to temporarily boost revenue growth, overproduction that reduces costs of goods sold, and reductions of discretionary spending to improve short-term profitability (Roychowdhury 2006; Xu et al. 2007). Prior work suggests that REM practices are relatively harder to detect by outsiders, compared to AEM, due to heightened scrutiny of firms’ accounting practices (Cohen et al. 2008), and likely questioning of aggressive accounting estimates by auditors (Zang 2012). While REM involves managerial manipulation of business activities, it does not always harm firms and sometimes it can be well-intentioned, such as to manage growth expectations and build market confidence.

Second, we draw on prior work on IT investments which suggests that IT infrastructure investments hold the key to long-term corporate strategies (Weill et al. 2002) and have important implications for firm performance (Chatterjee et al. 2002). The successful implementation of IT investment plans is critical for maintaining strategic IT planning priorities (Galliers 1994; Lederer and Sethi 1988). However, the long duration and significant resource requirements make IT infrastructure development subject to risks of cancellation and delay (Mieritz 2012).

We conceptualize IT investment commitment (referred to as ITIC hereafter) as the realized level of IT infrastructure development relative to the planned level. Firms with low ITIC are those that fall short of their planned levels of IT infrastructure investments, and firms with high ITIC are those that achieve or exceed their planned investments. Our focus on ITIC complements the existing IT investment literature (e.g., Hitt 1999; Kobelsky et al. 2008), which takes IT investment as a single decision and does not explicitly distinguish the planning of IT investment from the actual realization of investment plans (Mintzberg and Waters 1985).

We argue that REM may influence the realization of IT infrastructure investment plans due to at least two reasons. First, managers may prioritize other capital investments if they believe (rightly or wrongly) that other capital investments more directly contribute to primary business activities (e.g., production expansion) than IT infrastructure investments (Dewan et al. 1998). Investments in IT infrastructure are often associated with uncertainty and risks (Dewan et al. 2007), making their long-term value difficult to justify using traditional financial metrics such as NPV or IRR (Kumar 2004). Also, CIOs usually lack sufficient political status and bargaining power to secure resource allocation (King 2011) if other senior managers question the strategic role of IT (Bensaou and Earl 1998; Plant 2013).

Second, engaging in REM and thereby reducing IT infrastructure investments can boost short-term earnings in several ways. For example, a significant portion of IT infrastructure investments (e.g., purchasing computer equipment) can be capitalized, and the depreciation expenses of these capital investments can lower earnings. Forgoing planned capital investments on IT infrastructure helps improve short-term earnings by reducing capital depreciation expenses. In addition, building and expanding IT infrastructure can incur various related expenditures on software and accessories, IT labor, warranties and services, etc. (e.g., Gao and Hitt 2012). These expenditures are often directly expensed (Mithas et al. 2017) and can lower the overall earnings. Forgoing or even temporarily postponing IT infrastructure investment helps cut related IT expenses and improve short-term earnings. Moreover, reducing IT infrastructure investment can also result indirectly from the alteration of other business practices in REM. For example, other short-term REM activities, such as overproduction and over-promotion, can shift resources and efforts away from long-term IT infrastructure development. The large operations of many discretionary activities, such as R&D, are dependent on building IT infrastructure because IT facilitates firms to effectively manage the complexity of these activities (Ravichandran et al. 2018). The reduction of these activities in REM reduces the need for scaling up IT infrastructure in the short term.

Based on the foregoing discussion, we expect a negative association between REM and ITIC as a baseline. In other words, we expect that firms engaging in REM are more likely to compromise their ITIC. However, our main interest in the paper is on how IT decentralization and corporate governance mechanisms moderate this relationship.

## Theory Development and Hypotheses

We consider IT decentralization, the decentralization of IT purchasing decisions, as a moderator that influences the relationship between ITIC and REM.<sup>4</sup> Our arguments for potentially beneficial effects of IT decentralization in weakening the association between REM and ITIC focus on how IT decentralization may influence the balance of power between top managers and middle managers (Xue et al. 2011).<sup>5</sup> Argu ably, due to their compensation structures and job positions, top managers have stronger incentives than midlevel local managers to alter normal business operations in REM. Because top managers receive significantly more equity and option compensation than local managers (Guidry et al. 1999), and their income is tied more to the overall corporate earnings performance, top managers can derive more benefits from manipulating earnings through REM (e.g., better stock performance). Also, since top managers face boards of directors and investors more directly, they are under more pressure to deliver expected earnings performance, maintain investor confidence, and signal their management capabilities. Therefore, relative to local managers, top managers may be more willing to boost earnings performance through short-term alteration of activities in REM. Given managers’ different incentives, the organizational structure that balances managerial power may influence the extent to which short-term alteration of business operations in REM is actually carried out.<sup>6</sup>

We further expect corporate governance mechanisms to also influence the moderating effect of IT decentralization on the relationship between REM and ITIC. We specifically consider three corporate governance mechanisms—institutional ownership, board independence, and takeover threats—as they reflect the roles of the three key parties (i.e., corporate owners, the board, and the top management) in corporate governance theories (Walls et al. 2012). Institutional ownership reflects the extent to which the shares of the firm are owned by institutional investors. Board independence reflects the extent to which the corporate board consists of outside directors (e.g., from external partners, investment institutions, and nonprofit organizations) who are less tied to the internal management. Takeover protection reflects the extent to which corporate managers are protected by anti-takeover provisions and are less disciplined by shareholders in corporate control.

Corporate governance is primarily concerned with how corporate owners (e.g., corporate board and external shareholders) control and motivate the top management (Shleifer and Vishny 1997), and prior work shows its relevance in the context of IT initiatives (Ho et al. 2011; Raghupathi 2007). We characterize corporate governance as tight or loose depending on the extent to which corporate owners monitor and control top managers’ behaviors. In tight corporate governance, corporate owners closely monitor and control corporate decisions and managerial behavior to address the agency conflict (Jensen and Meckling 1976). For example, the corporate board can play a significant role in scrutinizing and replacing corporate CEOs (Hermalin 2005). It can also actively oversee managerial performance and reward management accordingly (Hermalin and Weisbach 2003). Large outside shareholders (e.g., institutional owners) with enough voting control can put pressure on management or even oust management through a proxy fight or a takeover (Shleifer and Vishny 1986). In contrast, in loose corporate governance, corporate owners exercise less control power over management (e.g., using takeover protection) and rely more on the latter’s self-discipline in corporate decision making.

Corporate governance mechanisms can be viewed from the control/monitoring and the incentive perspective. From the control/monitoring perspective, tight corporate governance can constrain top managers’ self-interested behavior and curb myopic managerial behavior in earnings management. From the incentive perspective, however, the effects of tight corporate governance are harder to predict. This is because, on one hand, tight corporate governance mitigates the agency problem and reduces manipulation behavior (Bertrand and Mullainathan 2003) by aligning top managers’ interests with those of shareholders; on the other hand, tight corporate governance can create performance pressure that drives managers to manipulate business activities through more sophisticated measures in earnings management (Jensen 2004).

We posit that corporate governance mechanisms can complement the effect of IT decentralization on the relationship between REM and ITIC (see Table 1 for a summary of the main arguments). The control/monitoring mechanism of corporate governance can force top managers, when scaling back IT investments, to base their decisions on legitimate reasons rather than their private interests. Likewise, the incentive mechanism of corporate governance can align managers interests with long-term firm interests and discourage shortterm behavior of sacrificing planned IT investments.

We next develop specific hypotheses for the role of IT decentralization in moderating the effect of REM on ITIC, and subsequently how corporate governance mechanisms influence the moderating effect of IT decentralization. In other words, we posit three-way interactions among REM, IT decentralization, and other corporate governance mechanisms.

## Moderating Role of IT Decentralization

We argue that IT decentralization limits the control power of top managers to alter the planned IT infrastructure investments in REM because the alteration of normal business practices in REM usually spans different organizational units and requires coordination between different levels of managers. Centralized power makes it easy for top managers to override prior investment plans and compromise IT infrastructure investments because top managers have more incentives for activity alteration in REM. Centralization also allows top managers to tighten the control over various organizational units to ensure the manipulation of IT investment. Centralization can force local managers to cave to pressure from top management (Vafai 2005) and facilitate the compromise of ITIC. In addition, top managers serve as an intermediate layer between corporate owners and local units. Centralization enables them to filter more information and further reduce information transparency about IT investment activities, making the manipulation of IT investment more difficult to detect in REM. This logic is consistent with the argument in the principal–supervisor–agent framework that additional layers of agents lead to more severe collusion and agency problems (Tirole 1986).

In decentralized firms, the autonomous organizational environment enables IT decisions to be made in a less interrelated manner across divisions, making it difficult for divisions to collude (Spagnolo 1999) and manipulate overall IT activities toward a common goal of manipulation in REM (which is often beyond their responsibilities). Although some local managers in decentralized firms may also face relatively strict performance pressure from the top management (McNeil et al. 2004), a decentralized environment can allow local managers to act as more effective internal monitors of each other (Lee et al. 2007), which makes it more costly for top managers to push for the overall ITIC manipulation. Decentralization of decision rights may also result in a flatter organizational structure that improves overall transparency of IT operations in the internal firm environment. Thus, we hypothesize that:

H1: The negative association between REM and IT investment commitment is weaker in firms with more IT decentralization.

## Institutional Ownership, IT Decentralization and IT Investments

We focus on the effect of overall institutional ownership (e.g., Aghion et al. 2013; Crane et al. 2016) because from the control/monitoring perspective institutional investors have the incentive to monitor and discipline managers and are often capable of limiting myopic managerial behavior (Chung et al. 2002; Monks and Minow 1995). While institutional investors with different horizons may create different pressure for managers to pursue short-term performance (Bushee 1998), past studies have found that in the context of long-term strategic initiatives such as innovation, the effect of overall institutional ownership is largely consistent with the effects of disaggregated types of institutional ownership (e.g., dedicated investors and transient investors) (Aghion et al. 2013). Past studies have documented that overall institutional ownership can mitigate short-term managerial behavior, such as the use of REM (Malik 2015) and undercutting of R&D investment (Bushee 1998).

IT decentralization and institutional ownership can jointly constrain short-term manipulation of IT investments in REM. Centralized power enables top managers to control information flows and hide more firm-specific information, and the resultant information asymmetry can undermine the effectiveness of monitoring by external institutional investors and make the manipulation of IT investments less detectable. In contrast, IT decentralization limits top managers’ interference with detailed IT operations and information processing. Certain decentralized structures (e.g., M-form) can also remove redundant organizational layers in IT decisions and activities, and make information less subject to manipulation when it moves up the hierarchy and is shared with external institutional shareholders. Therefore, from the external monitoring perspective, IT decentralization and institutional ownership can complement each other to foster effective external monitoring and make ITIC less susceptible to compromise by the short-term alteration of business operations in REM.

Even if institutional ownership may sometimes create market pressure for top managers to pursue short-term performance (Bushee 1998), IT decentralization can undermine such incentive effect on IT manipulation by constraining the power of top managers. With decentralized IT governance, the detailed execution of IT investment plans, such as IT infrastructure expansion, is delegated to lower-level managers, leaving less space for top managers to intervene in operational-level IT activities and accomplish REM through the manipulation of IT investments. Overall, the joint effect of IT decentralization and institutional ownership should further mitigate the compromise of ITIC in REM. We therefore hypothesize that:

H2: Institutional ownership and IT decentralization are complementary in mitigating the compromise of commitment to IT infrastructure investment in REM.

## Board Independence, IT Decentralization and IT Investments

Board independence. Board independence is often considered key to effective corporate governance (Beasley 1996) because independent outside directors are less subject to conflicts of interest and they have greater access to external information compared to the internal directors. Independent board members are expected to scrutinize managerial decisions and protect shareholders’ interests (Baysinger and Butler 1985). From the perspective of control/monitoring, board independence can constrain short-term managerial opportunistic behavior in REM (Visvanathan 2008); and technology investment decisions are also within the monitoring scope of corporate boards (Kor 2006). In this regard, board independence can facilitate optimal decisions in IT investment for the long-term benefit of the firm (Ho et al. 2011). However, the market pressure perspective suggests that scrutiny by outside independent board members also imposes pressure on managers to meet or beat earnings targets (Ge and Kim 2014). Top managers may be forced to use more REM as they can leverage their information advantages to make the manipulation of real activities less detectable by the board (Osma 2008).

From the control/monitoring perspective, IT decentralization reduces the scope and complexity of IT decisions of top managers. This helps reduce the knowledge gap between corporate boards and top managers and facilitate effective board monitoring. In governing IT decisions, independent board members often rely on complementary IT knowledge attained from outside the firm (Ho et al. 2011; Zahra and Pearce 1989). Such complementary IT knowledge is usually more relevant to high-level strategic IT decisions rather than firmspecific IT routines. As IT decentralization makes top managers less involved in routine IT operations, independent board members can better utilize their relevant knowledge to understand and scrutinize the IT decision-making of top managers. In this regard, IT decentralization and board independence should be complementary in constraining the manipulation of IT investments in REM.

From the market pressure perspective, if tight monitoring by a relatively independent board forces top managers to use more sophisticated approaches for altering business operations, such as REM to boost earnings, IT decentralization can still limit the power of top managers to implement such manipulation in the area of IT. As top managers are less involved in operational-level IT activities, they are less likely to employ IT manipulation as part of their REM strategies. Overall, the joint effect of IT decentralization and board independence should further mitigate the compromise of ITIC in REM. We therefore hypothesize that:

H3: Board independence and IT decentralization are complementary in mitigating the compromise of commitment to IT infrastructure investment in REM.

## Takeover Threats, IT Decentralization and IT Investments

Takeover threats or less use of anti-takeover provisions reduces the power of incumbent managers due to entrenchment (Gompers et al. 2003). Examples of anti-takeover provisions include tactics for delaying hostile takeover, restrictive shareholder rights in voting, and director/office protection. Higher use of anti-takeover provisions reserves more power for management and restricts shareholders’ ability to replace managers. Control-related perspectives (e.g., the entrenchment view; Fama and Jensen 1983) suggest that takeover protection empowers managers to alter business operations in REM, as they are less concerned about the adverse outcomes (e.g., job loss) of their myopic behavior. However, incentiverelated perspectives (e.g., the incentive alignment view; Zhao et al. 2012) suggest that takeover protection improves incentive alignment, as job security provides managers more discretion to maximize long-term firm value (Ge and Kim 2014). In contrast, takeover threats, or the use of less takeover protection, can increase the pressure for managers to alter normal business activities as poor short-term performance exposes them to the risk of replacement.

We expect that takeover threats can complement IT decentral ization in mitigating the compromise of ITIC in REM for two reasons. First, while IT decentralization balances managerial power by distributing decision rights, entrenched top managers who are less disciplined may still leverage their authority to influence lower-level managers in REM (Cheng et al. 2016). In this case, disciplining top managers with less takeover protection helps avoid power abuse and further ensures the effectiveness of IT decentralization. Second, when takeover threats misalign managerial incentives and create pressure for top managers to boost earnings performance using short-term business alternation, IT decentralization can constrain the power of top managers to realize such alteration in the IT domain. With less involvement in operational-level IT activities, top managers are less capable of manipulating ITIC even if they are under the pressure of takeover threats, and they are driven to focus more on longterm IT strategies. Therefore, overall, the joint effect of IT decentralization and takeover threats (the use of less takeover protection) should help further mitigate the compromise of ITIC in REM. We therefore hypothesize:

H4: Takeover threats and IT decentralization are complementary in mitigating the compromise of commitment to IT infrastructure investment in REM.

## Research Design

## Data

We combined the data for this study from multiple sources. First, we obtained firm-level data on planned and realized IT infrastructure investments from the Computer Intelligence Technology database (CITDB) maintained by Harte-Hanks. Second, we obtained the data on IT decentralization from CITDB. CITDB has been recognized as an authoritative source of firm-level and establishment-level IT data, and has been used extensively in IS research (e.g., Forman 2005; Zhu and Kraemer 2002). The sample from CITDB consisted of 3,961 unique firms over the period 1998–2009.

Third, to assess the extent of REM engagement, we followed the accounting literature (e.g., Roychowdhury 2006) to use data from Compustat database. Fourth, to assess corporate governance, we followed the literature (e.g., Walls et al. 2012) to obtain data on institutional ownership from Thomson/Reuters Institutional Holding database, and obtained data on boards of directors from the Institutional Shareholder Services (ISS) database. Fifth, we obtained the data on takeover protection from the RiskMetrics database (the former IRRC database) (Bebchuk et al. 2009; Gompers et al. 2003). RiskMetrics database reports 24 corporategovernance provisions implemented at the firm level to protect managers from the shareholders’ disciplinary actions or takeovers. The use of more provisions means that managers have greater potential for managerial entrenchment, and are more protected from shareholder discipline. Finally, we used the Compustat database to derive other firm-level control variables.

The matching of the CITDB with the Compustat database resulted in a sample of 16,393 firm-year observations, with 2,884 unique firms, for our dynamic panel data analysis. The matching between the CI-Compustat dataset with Thomson Reuters Institutional Holding database reduced the sample size to 13,714 firm-year observations. The matching between the CI-Compustat dataset with ISS database reduced the sample size to 7,003 firm-year observations. Matching between the CI-Compustat dataset with RiskMetrics database reduced the sample size to 6,334 firm-year observations.<sup>7</sup> In the matching process, we followed the literature to eliminate firms in the regulated industries (SIC codes between 4400 and 5000) and the firms in banking and financial industries (SIC codes between 6000 and 6500) (Roychowdhury 2006).

## Measurement

IT investments Commitment in Infrastructure Development (ITIC). Following prior work (e.g., Chwelos et al. 2010; Kleis et al. 2012), we consider IT infrastructure development in three areas: (1) decentralized computing equipment (measured by the total number of PCs and workstations), (2) centralized computing equipment (measured by the total number of servers), and (3) network communication equipment (measured by the total number of network hubs and routers). The CI database records the numbers of these three types of IT infrastructure components that firms plan to increase in the next year. The CI database also records the actual numbers of these components in every year. For each of these three types of IT infrastructure components, we constructed an IT investment commitment measure as [(realized IT ) – (planned IT<sub>t</sub>)]/IT , where IT and $I T _ { t - 1 }$ represent the total number of the component in year t and t-1, respectively.<sup>8</sup> A high level of this measure means that the firm realizes more of its planned development, or, commits to its development plan to a greater extent. It is possible that the firm may invest more than its planned level. As our focus is on whether the firm meets its plan, we consider investing more than the planned level also as being committed to the plan. The three individual measures exhibited good internal reliability, with a Cronbach’s Alpha of 0.720. We then constructed a composite measure (using factor analysis) that combined the three types of IT infrastructure investments.

Real Earnings Management (REM). We followed the accounting literature (e.g., Cohen et al. 2008; Kim et al. 2012; Roychowdhury 2006) to use three measures of REM: (1) abnormal discretionary expenses (ABDX), (2) abnormal production costs (ABPC), and (3) abnormal cash flows from operations (ABCF). These measures are captured by the residuals or the reverse residuals from the corresponding regression models estimated by year and the two-digit NAICS industry (more details in Table 2).<sup>9</sup> The underlying idea is that when firms engage in REM to meet short-term earnings targets, they are likely to alter normal business practices by having abnormally low levels of cash flows (e.g., due to heavy promotions to boost sales revenue to lower per unit costs), high levels of production costs (e.g., due to overproduction to apportion overhead and improve per-unit margin), and low levels of discretionary expenditure (e.g., due to aggressive cuts in discretionary spending). The REM literature has also validated these measures by showing that firms that are likely to engage in REM have these measures changing in the expected directions (Cohen et al. 2008; Kim et al. 2012).<sup>10</sup> Therefore, although these individual measures may not be exhaustive enough to capture all aspects of REM, we can use them to assess a general extent of REM engagement and managerial proneness to alter normal business activities in REM. As explained in Table 2, we constructed a composite measure of REM engagement (denoted as REM) by linearly aggregating the three individual standardized measures of REM (Cohen et al. 2008; Kim et al. 2012), with higher values representing more engagement in REM.<sup>11</sup>

IT Decentralization (ITDEC). We considered IT decentralization as the extent to which firms delegate the decision rights of IT investments to local establishments (i.e., business units), rather than keep these decisions at the corporate level. Following prior studies (McElheran 2012; Xue et al. 2011), we used establishment-level data from the CI database to assess the decentralization of IT infrastructure decisions. The CI database records three types of IT infrastructure decisions for each establishment: PC/workstation decisions, server decisions, and network decisions. For each decision, the CI database indicates whether it is made by the headquarters or by the establishment itself. Therefore, we first developed three establishment-level measures for these three decisions, each with a value of 1 (0) if the corresponding decision is made by the establishment (made by the headquarters). We aggregated the establishment-level measures, weighted by the establishment size (measured by employee counts), to develop firm-level measures for each decision. The three firm-level measures of IT infrastructure decentralization exhibited reasonably good internal reliability, with a Cronbach’s Alpha of 0.762. We then used factor analysis to develop a composite measure of overall IT decentralization (referred to as ITDEC), with higher values meaning more decentralized patterns in IT infrastructure decisions.

Institutional Ownership, Board Independence, and Takeover Threats. Following prior studies, we measure institutional ownership (referred to as IO) as the percentage of shares held by institutional investors using data from Thomson/Reuters. Board independence (referred to as BI) is measured as the number of outside directors over total directors, using data from Risk-Metrics (Daily and Dalton 1994). To assess takeover threats, we followed the finance literature (e.g., Dittmar and Mahrt-Smith 2007; Johnson et al. 2009) to employ the “Governance Index (GI)” defined in Gompers et al. (2003). Specifically, RiskMetrics database reports whether firms put in place 24 governance provisions to protect managers from being constrained by the shareholders’ disciplinary actions or from being removed by takeovers.<sup>12</sup> In GI, a point is added for every provision that restricts shareholder rights. Therefore, the GI values can potentially range from 0 to 24, with higher values representing stronger managerial power, more entrenched managerial position, and less disciplinary governance placed by the shareholders. To make the interpretation of the results more intuitive, we transformed the GI by subtracting the original GI value for each firm-year from the maximum GI value observed in the data. The resulting measure, referred to as TT hereafter, has higher values when the managers are restricted by higher shareholder discipline.

Control Variables. We followed IT investment studies (e.g., Kobelsky et al. 2008) to include control variables that might provide rival explanations for IT investments. First, we included a measure of IT intensity (ITINT) to capture potentially heterogeneous IT needs across organizations. Based on these three measures (with a Cronbach’s Alpha of 0.706), we used factor analysis to develop a composite measure of overall IT intensity. Second, we included advertising expenditure (ADV), R&D expenditure (RND), and capital expenditure (CAPX), as these control variables capture how firms may allocate discretionary expenditure resources to other strategies and, therefore, potentially influence IT investments. We also included lagged values of several firm characteristics that are likely to be the determinants of planned and actual IT investments (Kobelsky et al. 2008). These firm characteristics include: (1) industry-adjusted return-on-asset (AROA), as defined as the difference between firm ROA and the corresponding fourdigit NAICS industry-average ROA, to control for firm performance; (2) firm leverage (LEVR), as measured by total debt to total assets, to control for potential constraints on investment budget; (3) market-to-book (MTB) ratio, as measured by total market value to book value of equity, to control for potential long-term growth opportunities; (4) diversification (DIVR), as measured by the entropy measure as in Palepu (1985), as a control for IT needs for internal coordination; (5) firm size (SIZE), as measured by natural log value of total employees, to control for operational scale; (6) sales growth (SGRW), as measured by the natural log value of one-year sales growth rate, to control for potential IT needs or competing cash flow needs to sustain operational expansion; (7) size growth (SZGW), as measured by the natural logarithm of one-year growth rate of employees, to control for organizational growth that may need more IT; (8) surplus cash (SPLS), as measured by cash flow from assetin-place to total assets (Richardson 2006), to control for higher availability of investment resources; and (9) headquarters employee ratio (HQ Emp), as measured by the total employees at the headquarters divided by that of the firm, to control for the general tendency of organizational decentralization.

## Empirical Models and Estimation

Given the nature of our model and data, we adopt a dynamic panel model estimator (Arellano and Bond 1991) for the main analysis, consistent with similar approach in IS research (e.g., Goes et al. 2014; Menon and Kohli 2013). The first model examines how the relationship between REM and ITIC is contingent on IT decentralization,

$$
\begin{array}{r l} I T I C _ {i t} & = \alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {1} I T I C _ {i, t - 1} + \alpha_ {2} R E M _ {i t} + \alpha_ {3} I T D E C _ {i t} + \\ & \quad \alpha_ {4} R E M _ {i t} \times I T D E C _ {i t} + \gamma [ O t h e r C o n t r o l s ] + v _ {i t} \\ v _ {i t} & = u _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i t} \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

In Eq. (1), the lagged term $I T I C _ { i , t - 1 }$ is included as a predetermined regressor, which captures the potential intertemporal correlation of ITIC (e.g., certain IT investments may be delayed in the present period but may increase the realized investment in the next-period). $R E M _ { i t }$ is included as an endogenous regressor. $I T D E C _ { i t }$ is a focal explanatory variable, and the interaction term $R E M _ { i t } \times I T D E C _ { i t }$ is used to test H1. Other Controls include all other exogenous regressors that may provide rival explanations of ITIC. The error term $\nu _ { i t }$ is made up of both a random error $\varepsilon _ { i t }$ and an unobservable firm fixedeffect $u _ { i } ,$ which may be correlated with both the predetermined regressor and the endogenous regressor. Firstdifferencing Eq. (1) removes both the fixed-effect $u _ { i }$ and its associated omitted-variable bias; that is,

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \Delta I T I C _ {i t} & = \alpha_ {1} \Delta I T I C _ {i, t - 1} + \alpha_ {2} \Delta R E M _ {i t} + \alpha_ {3} \Delta I T D E C _ {i t} + \\ & \quad \alpha_ {4} (R E M _ {i t} \times I T D E C _ {i t}) + \gamma \Delta [ O t h e r C o n t r o l s ] + \Delta \varepsilon_ {i t} ^ {(2)} \end{array}
$$

We employ a system-GMM (SGMM) approach to estimate the parameter sets á and $\gamma .$ As an augmented version of the classic Arellano–Bond GMM approach for dynamic panel data models, SGMM is appropriate in our case given key considerations in our analysis, including those of (1) a “small T, large $\mathbf { N } ^ { \ast }$ data panel; (2) a dynamic dependent variable (which may be dependent on its past realization); (3) endogenous regressors (which may be correlated with the realization of the error term); (4) individual fixed effects that incorporate unobservable heterogeneity; and (5) potential heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation within individual firms’ error terms.

A key aspect of the Arellano–Bond GMM approach is the adoption of “internal” instruments that are based on lagged levels of the instrumented variables (i.e., predetermined and endogenous variables). The Arellano–Bond GMM estimators also allow the inclusion of external instruments. In our case, in addition to internal instruments, we adopt several external instruments. Specifically, we first adopt a measure of abnormal accruals (ABA) as an external instrument. This measure captures the level of AEM (e.g., Roychowdhury 2006). As an alternative earnings management approach, AEM is related to REM, as firms may balance between the uses of these two approaches. However, since AEM primarily relies on ex post accounting representation of existing activities, there is no obvious reason to believe that AEM should be directly related to IT investment activities. We also adopt an indicator of whether firms are audited by one of the biggest audit companies (Big4) as an external instrument. The idea here is that the monitoring by the major audit companies should force firms to shift from AEM to REM, which is more difficult to detect (Zang 2012). However, there is no obvious reason to believe that general auditing should necessarily be related to IT investment. In addition, we also adopt an indicator of the post-SOX period. Firms are found to shift from AEM to REM due to the passage of SOX (Cohen et al. 2008). However, the passage of SOX should not be directly related to IT investment.

SGMM extends the base Arellano–Bond GMM estimators by incorporating both the levels equation and the differences equation (i.e., Eq (1) and (2)) in the system, and employing both lagged levels and lagged differences as instruments. This helps increase efficiency by using additional moment conditions, and addresses the issue of lagged levels as potentially weak instruments for first-differenced variables (Blundell and Bond 1998). We use a two-step SGMM estimator with better asymptotical efficiency than the one-step SGMM in the presence of potential heteroscedasticity. We also apply a finite-sample correction on the asymptotical variance of the two-step SGMM estimator (Windmeijer 2005) to address their potential downward biases caused by the dependence of the weight matrix on estimated parameters in the two-step approach.

We also use specification tests to ensure model validity. First, we use AR (1) and AR (2) tests (Arellano and Bond 1991) to check first and second order serial autocorrelation. For SGMM, only the absence of second-order serial autocorrelation is needed, as Eq. (2) is in first differences. The tests (as illustrated in result tables) indicate that we cannot reject that the differenced error terms are second-order serially uncorrelated, although they are first-order serially correlated. Second, we use Hansen’s J statistic of over-identifying restrictions to check the overall instrument validity. The Hansen’s J statistic is used in place of the Sargan test of overidentifying restrictions because of its consistency in the presence of autocorrelation and heteroscedasticity (Roodman 2009). The results show that Hansen’s J tests fail to reject that the instrument sets are exogenous overall and therefore valid for use. In addition, we use Difference-in-Hansen tests (Baum 2006) to further check the exogeneity of subsets of instruments (i.e., levels, differenced, and standard IV instruments). These tests suggest that we cannot reject that any of these subsets of instruments are exogenous and therefore proper instruments.

To examine how IT decentralization and corporate governance may be complementary in mitigating the adverse effect of REM on ITIC, we specify the following model,

$$
\begin{array}{l} I T I C _ {i t} = \alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {1} I T I C _ {i, t - 1} + \alpha_ {2} R E M _ {i t} + \alpha_ {3} I T D E C _ {i t} \\ + \alpha_ {4} R E M _ {i t} \times I T D E C _ {i t} + \alpha_ {5} C G _ {i t} + \alpha_ {6} R E M _ {i t} \times C G _ {i t} \\ + \alpha_ {7} R E M _ {i t} \times I T D E C _ {i t} \times C G _ {i t} + \gamma [ O t h e r C o n t r o l s ] \\ + u _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i t} \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

Where $C G _ { i t } \in \{ I O _ { i t } , B I _ { i t } , T T _ { i t } \}$ . In Eq. (3), we use the threeway interaction terms to examine the complementarity between each of the three corporate governance mechanisms and IT decentralization. A significant coefficient of the three-way interaction suggests that ITDEC and a corporate governance mechanism (IO, BI, or TT) may jointly reduce the impact of REM on ITIC.

## Additional Sufficiency Test for Complementarities

Note that a significant coefficient of the three-way interaction term is a necessary, but not a sufficient condition for comple mentarity (Brynjolfsson and Milgrom 2013; Tambe et al. 2012) because a certain level of a three-way interaction term may correspond to multiple combinations of its components. A significant coefficient of the three-way interaction term does not guarantee that the overall marginal effect of REM (on ITIC) with high levels in both ITDEC and the corporate governance factor (IO, BI or TT) is significantly lower than the overall marginal effect of REM with other combinations of ITDEC and the other factor.

Therefore, we follow the approach of complementarities tests in the recent literature (e.g., Aral et al. 2012; Tambe et al.

2012) to assess complementarity between ITDEC and the other three corporate governance factors. We assume that ITIC = F[REM, ITDEC, CG], where CG is one of the three corporate governance factors; that is, CG 0 {IO, BI, TT}. The estimate of the function F[.] is specified as in Eq. (3) (with all coefficient parameters at their estimated values, and other variables at their mean levels).

Because our main focus is on the impact of REM on ITIC, we first compare the case when ITDEC and CG are both high (i.e., one standard deviation above the mean) and the case when ITDEC and CG are both at their average levels. As REM, ITDEC and CG are all standardized in the sample (i.e., with standard deviations of 1), we test the following relationship:

$$
F [ 1, 1, 1 ] - F [ 0, 1, 1 ] <   F [ 1, 0, 0 ] - F [ 0, 0, 0 ]\tag{4}
$$

Eq. (4) essentially states that when ITDEC and CG are both high, a high level of REM generates less (adverse) impact on ITIC than when ITDEC and CG are at their average levels. We use the parameter estimates of Eq. (2), as in Table 3, to test Eq. (4).

Next, we test the following two relationships,

$$
F [ 1, 1, 1 ] - F [ 0, 1, 1 ] <   F [ 1, 1, 0 ] - F [ 0, 1, 0 ]\tag{5}
$$

$$
F [ 1, 1, 1 ] - F [ 0, 1, 1 ] <   F [ 1, 0, 1 ] - F [ 0, 0, 1 ]\tag{6}
$$

Eq. (5) states that when ITDEC and CG are both high, REM causes less (adverse) impact on ITIC than when only ITDEC is high. Eq. (6) states that when ITDEC and CG are both high, REM causes less (adverse) impact on ITIC than when only CG is high.

Finally, we test the following relationship:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \left(F [ 1, 1, 0 ] - F [ 0, 1, 0 ]\right) - \left(F [ 1, 0, 0 ] - F [ 0, 0, 0 ]\right) \\ <   \left(F [ 1, 1, 1 ] - F [ 0, 1, 1 ]\right) - \left(F [ 1, 0, 1 ] - F [ 0, 0, 1 ]\right) \end{array}\tag{7}
$$

Eq. (7) essentially states that when CG is high, increasing ITDEC (from average to high) weakens the negative impact of REM to a greater extent than increasing ITDEC when CG is at an average level. With trivial algebra, it can be shown that the testing of Eq. (7) is essentially equivalent to testing the significance of the coefficient of the three-way interaction term $E M \times I T D E C \times C G$

## Main Results

Table 3 reports the SGMM results for the relationship between REM and ITDEC. The negative and significant coefficient of REM (p < 0.001) suggests that when firms engage in REM, they are less committed to their preplanned IT investment. The coefficient value -0.121 in Model 1 suggests that compared to a low-REM firm (i.e., with REM at one standard deviation below the mean), a high-REM firm (i.e., with REM at one standard deviation above the mean) undercuts ITIC by 24.2% of its standard deviation, which is equivalent to undercutting IT infrastructure increase (from the previous year) by 6.2% (24.2% × 0.256, where 0.256 is the standard deviation of ITIC; see Table 2).<sup>13</sup> In addition, the results in Model 2 shows that the overall effect of ITDEC on ITIC is not significant. This suggests that in general, decentralized firms do not significantly differ from centralized firms in their ITIC.

We find support for H1, because the results in Model 3 show that the coefficient of the interaction term REM × ITDEC is positive and statistically significant (p < 0.01). This suggests that IT decentralization weakens the detrimental effect of REM on ITIC. For example, for firms with an average level of IT decentralization (ITDEC = 0), the increase of REM by one standard deviation is associated with the decrease of ITIC by 11.6% of standard deviation. For firms with a high level of IT decentralization (ITDEC = 1), the increase of REM by one standard deviation is associated with the decrease of ITIC by 5.5% of standard deviation. This finding is consistent with the explanation that when top managers (who have more interest in REM) have less control over the actual IT spending processes, firms are able to realize more of their commitments to their planned IT investments.

Turning to our tests for H2, H3 and H4, Table 4 shows that the effect of board independence (BI) is distinct from those of institutional ownership (IO) and takeover threats (TT) when it comes to moderating the effect of ITDEC on the relationship between REM and ITIC. Table 4 shows that the coefficients of the three-way interaction terms REM × ITDEC × IO and REM × ITDEC × TT are significant, suggesting necessary (but not sufficient) conditions to support H2 and H4 (i.e., the complementarity between ITDEC and institutional ownership, and the complementarity between ITDEC and takeover threats in mitigating the impact of REM).<sup>14</sup>

Table 5 presents the results of complementarity tests to check sufficient conditions for H2 and H4, and we find support for meeting the sufficiency conditions. In particular, regarding institutional ownership, the test IO1 supports Exp. (5) and suggests that with both high ITDEC and high IO, the negative impact of REM on ITIC is weakened to a greater extent compared to the case with both average ITDEC and average IO. The test IO2 and the test IO3 support Exp. (6) and Exp. (7), respectively, and suggest that with both high ITDEC and high IO, the negative impact of REM on ITIC is weakened to a greater extent than the case when only ITDEC or IO is high. The test IO4 supports Exp. (8) and suggests that when IO is high, the marginal effect of increasing ITDEC (from average to high) in weakening the negative impact of REM is greater than that of increasing ITDEC when IO is only at an average level. Therefore, H2 is supported, which suggests that ITDEC and institutional ownership are complementary in mitigating the negative impact of REM on ITIC. The significance of complementarity can also be assessed using the estimated coefficients in Table 4. When IO is high (IO = 1), the increase of ITDEC from the average level (ITDEC = 0) to the high level (ITDEC = 1) reduces the marginal detrimental effect of REM by 92.3%. In contrast, with an average level of IO (IO = 0), the increase of ITDEC from the average level to the high level only reduces the marginal detrimental effect of REM by 42.9%.

Likewise, regarding takeover threats, the test TT1 supports Exp. (5) and suggests that with both high ITDEC and high TT, the negative impact of REM on ITIC is weakened to a greater extent compared to the case with both average ITDEC and average TT. The test TT2, which supports Exp. (6), and the test IO3, which supports Exp. (7), suggest that with both high ITDEC and high TT, the negative impact of REM on ITIC is weakened to a greater extent than the case when only ITDEC or TT is high. The test IO4 supports Exp. (8) and suggests that when TT is high, the marginal effect of increasing ITDEC (from average to high) in weakening the negative impact of REM is greater than that of increasing ITDEC when TT is only at an average level. Therefore, H4 is supported, which suggests that ITDEC and takeover threats (or the use of less takeover protection) are complementary in mitigating the negative impact of REM on ITIC. Using the estimated coefficients in Table 4, when TT is high (TT = 1), the increase of ITDEC from the average level (ITDEC = 0) to the high level (ITDEC = 1) reduces the marginal detrimental effect of REM by 73.0%. In contrast, when TT is intermediate (TT = 0), the increase of ITDEC from the average level to the high level only reduces the marginal effect of REM by 39.6%.

We do not find support for H3 because the results on board independence in Table 4 show that the coefficient of the threeway interaction REM × ITDEC × BI is not significant. Therefore, the necessary condition for complementarity for H3 does not hold. In addition, the direct effect of BI on ITIC is positive and significant $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , and the coefficient of the interaction ITDEC × BI is negative and significant $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ suggesting that board independence is likely to directly foster IT infrastructure investment, and such effect is more effective in centralized firms. The extant literature suggests that board independence can be a facilitating factor for the firm to benefit from IT investments (Ho et al. 2011). In line with this view, our findings suggest that board independence may also facilitate the firm to follow its long-term strategy of investing in IT infrastructure. Without considering REM, this benefit of board independence appears to be more prominent in centralized firms, possibly because IT centralization allows board members to more easily govern top-level IT decisions. However, when REM is taken into account, board independence does not take effect, either by itself or jointly with IT decentralization, to mitigate the negative impact of REM. These results are aligned with the existing perspective that top management facing tight board monitoring may use more REM (Ge and Kim 2014), and that board independence is not very effective in curbing the use of REM (Malik 2015).

The complementarity tests in Table 5 show that all the conditions for the complementarity between IT decentralization and institutional ownership, and for the complementarity between IT decentralization and takeover threats, are satisfied. In particular, the combination of a high ITDEC and a high IO is more effective in attenuating the negative effect of REM on ITIC than other combinations, suggesting the complementarity between ITDEC and IO. A high ITDEC and a high TT is the most effective combination in attenuating the negative effect of REM on ITIC, suggesting the complementarity between ITDEC and TT.

## Robustness and Additional Analyses

We conducted several additional analyses for robustness and additional insights. First, we used an alternative measure of REM, in place of our aggregate measure to capture the firm’s engagement in REM. To alleviate the concern that our aggregate measure based on certain operational activities (e.g., abnormal cash flows, overproduction, and reduction in discretionary expenses) may not accurately capture other complex manipulation activities in REM, we follow prior literature (Cohen et al. 2008; Kim et al. 2012) to employ an alternative approach. Specifically, we identify firm-year observations in which firms just met one or both of two critical benchmarks to capture REM—a zero earnings benchmark and a nonnegative change in earnings; we call these observations SUSPECT firms.

The main logic for operationalizing REM using SUSPECT is that firms are expected to have strong incentives to manage their earnings to avoid reporting a loss or reporting stagnated growth. Therefore, compared to non-SUSPECT firms, SUSPECT firms are more likely to manage their earnings using REM, or to engage more in REM. The difference between SUSPECT firms and non-SUSPECT firms therefore can reflect the effect of REM. Following prior research (e.g., Kim et al. 2012; Zang 2012), we identified all firm-year observations with net income before extraordinary items (NIBEI) scaled by total assets that lay in the interval [0, 0.005) (i.e., just meeting zero earnings), or with the change in NIBEI scaled by total assets that lay in the interval [0, 0.005) (i.e., just meeting non-negative earnings growth), as SUSPECT firms. We then specify the following model:

$$
\begin{array}{l} I T I C _ {i t} = \alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {1} I T I C _ {i, t - 1} + \alpha_ {2} S U S P _ {i t} + \alpha_ {3} I T D E C _ {i t} \\ + \alpha_ {4} S U S P _ {i t} \times I T D E C _ {i t} + \alpha_ {5} C G _ {i t} + \alpha_ {6} S U S P _ {i t} \times C G _ {i t} \\ + \alpha_ {7} S U S P _ {i t} \times I T D E C _ {i t} \times C G _ {i t} + \gamma [ O t h e r C o n t r o l s ] \\ + u _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i t} \end{array}\tag{8}
$$

In $\mathrm { E q . ~ } ( 8 ) , S U S P _ { i t }$ is an indicator with a value 1 if the corresponding observation of firm i in year t (firm i in year t-1) is a SUSPECT firm-year observation, and a value 0 otherwise. $C G _ { i t } \in \{ I O _ { i t } , B I _ { i t } , T T _ { i t } \}$ . Other controls include all control variables used in the main analysis. The results are summarized in Table 6.

Table 6 shows results broadly similar to those in the main analysis. In particular, SUSPECT firms have significantly lower ITIC compared to non-SUSPECT firms, and this difference is mitigated by ITDEC. These results suggest that the likely REM activities in SUSPECT firms negatively affect ITIC, and IT decentralization weakens such negative effect of REM. The coefficients of the three-way interaction involving institutional ownership and that involving takeover threats are positive and significant. The coefficient of the three-way interaction involving board independence, however, is not significant.

The subsequent complementarity tests, as summarized in Table 7, indicate that all the conditions for the complementarity between IT decentralization and institutional ownership, and for the complementarity between IT decentralization and takeover threats, are satisfied. Therefore, IT decentralization is complemented by institutional ownership and takeover threats in mitigating the negative effect of REM on ITIC.

It is worth noting that SUSPECT firms have different options to manage earnings, including both REM and AEM. However, AEM is less relevant to ITIC; in a separate analysis, we also confirm that AEM does not affect ITIC in our context (Appendix D). Therefore, in our context, the difference between SUSPECT firms and non-SUSPECT firms is relevant to ITIC mainly because of the different REM activities by these two types of firms.

Second, we conducted another robustness analysis using an alternative measure of takeover threats. Specifically, we use the “Entrenchment Index (EI)” (Bebchuk et al. 2009) instead of GI to measure takeover threats. EI is constructed using 6 of the original 24 provisions from the RiskMetrics database that are considered to be most important from a legal and operational standpoint.<sup>15</sup> As we did for GI, we constructed EI Scores by subtracting the EI value for each firm-year from the maximum EI value, and use this reverse-coded measure as the measure for takeover threats (TT). Firms with higher EI Scores, therefore, are firms that use less takeover protection, or impose more takeover threats. The results of the analysis using EI, as presented in Appendix $\mathrm { E , }$ are consistent with the main analysis using GI.

Third, to gain more insights on different types of IT infrastructure, we separately examined the three types of IT infrastructure equipment in our sample and their corresponding decentralization. In particular, we perform the same SGMM analysis, with the aggregate measures of ITIC and IT decentralization replaced by the corresponding investment commitment and decentralization measures in each of the three IT infrastructure areas (i.e., PC, server, and network). The results are summarized in Appendix F. We find that the results on PC and server are very consistent with the main results. That is, firms engaging in REM compromise more of their commitment to PC investment and server investment. Decision decentralization in PC investment and server investment alleviates the negative impact of REM on the corresponding investment commitment. The results also provide some evidence on the complementarity between decentralization and institutional ownership, and the complementarity between decentralization and the use of less takeover protection, in alleviating the impact of REM on PC investments and server investments. In contrast, the results also show that the impact of REM on network investment commitment is weaker. Moreover, the decision decentralization and corporate governance mechanisms do not significantly alleviate the negative effect of REM. A potential explanation is that firms may prioritize the building of communication infrastructure due to the communication needs. The building of communication infrastructure is also likely to be interrelated across business units and coordinated more from the corporate perspective. Therefore, firms are less likely to sacrifice network investment, and decentralization makes less difference in the case of network investment.

## Discussion

## Main Findings and Contributions

Our goal in this study was to assess how IT decentralization and other corporate governance mechanisms influence the link between firms’ engagement in REM and extent to which they realize their planned investments in IT infrastructure. Our analyses of data on more than 2,884 U.S. firms over the period 1998–2009 suggest three key findings. First, we document a negative association between REM and the extent to which firms realize their planned IT investments. Second, IT decentralization weakens this link implying that decentralized IT governance mechanisms can ameliorate potentially negative effects of REM on IT investments. Finally, the findings suggest that corporate governance mechanisms, such as institutional ownership and takeover threats, complement IT decentralization to further mitigate the negative association between REM and IT investment realization. These findings are robust and qualitatively similar even if we use alternative measures of REM, takeover threats, and IT decentralization.

The study makes three important contributions. First, we respond to the recent call for research on determinants of IT investments (e.g., Salge et al. 2015) by pointing to a hitherto ignored mechanism of managers’ proneness to alter normal business operations in REM. In doing so, we complement extant value-generation perspective and institutional perspectives on IT investment in the literature. Second, we contribute to the IT governance literature by showing the underexplored value of IT decentralization in addressing the negative impact of short-term alteration of business operations in REM on IT investment commitment, complementing other explanations for IT decentralization based on local information-processing and knowledge-decision co-location. Finally, the study provides useful implications for further research related to IT governance by pointing to complex interplay of corporate governance, IT decentralization, and IT investments decisions as a fertile area of research.

## Research Implications

Our findings suggest several important research implications. First, ours is the first study to illuminate the important role of IT and corporate governance mechanisms in understanding the transition from IT investment plans to IT investment realization. Our study reveals that this transition is subject to the influence of short-term managerial actions, such as the alteration of normal business activities in REM. This finding provides a more complete picture of how firms make essential investments in IT, and contributes to recent research that has called for more in-depth investigation of the actual realization processes of IT investment (e.g., Salge et al. 2015). Similar to the distinction between intended and realized strategy, our research here explored the distinction between intended and realized IT investments (see Mintzberg and Waters 1985). An implication of this research is to further delve into deliberate IT investments (realized as intended) and emergent IT investments (realized, despite or in the absence of, intentions), and their implications for firm performance.

Second, a key message from our findings is that REM makes managers more prone to alter normal business activities that can compromise IT investment plans. Since REM is often driven by managers’ private interests (Graham et al. 2005), our finding points to the influence of managerial self-interest as a relevant input that shapes IT investment decisions. Our finding suggests that the relationship between IT investment and business performance is more nuanced than suggested by the existing theoretical lenses (Gurbaxani and Wang 1991; Nault 1998; Ravichandran et al. 2009). Prior work suggests that IT investments can be viewed as real options because they provide strategic flexibility (Sambamurthy et al. 2003), such flexibility can also be exploited by engaging in REM to enhance short-term business performance even though that may compromise planned IT investments. In this regard, the research on the relationship between IT investment and business performance should pay more attention to whether business performance results from the long-term value of IT investment or short-term managerial behavior. While we recognize that REM at times may not be driven by bad motives,<sup>16</sup> our study underscores the role of short-term managerial behavior in REM in associating IT with business performance.

Third, our finding on the effect of IT decentralization provides a new theoretical lens because the existing literature views IT decentralization from the perspective of a tradeoff between organizational controls and local information processing (Sambamurthy and Zmud 1999; Tiwana 2009). We extend that view by revealing that from a governance perspective, the contribution of local managers is not just limited to their local information processing capabilities. Instead, IT decentralization can enable local managers to play a more important role in internal monitoring to constrain short-term managerial behavior that may jeopardize the realization of planned IT investments. In this regard, the design of IT governance arrangements should be aligned with general corporate governance goals to address various managerial motivations and balance the managerial control power.

Fourth, ancillary to the above point, our joint examination of IT decentralization and various corporate governance mechanisms further illustrates the important complementarity between IT governance and general corporate governance. In the context of REM, our findings illustrate that a decentralized organizational structure and proper corporate governance arrangements can jointly protect IT infrastructure investment plans from being compromised by short-term managerial behavior. The implications of these findings are applicable to not only IT but also to other discretionary expenditures such as R&D.

## Managerial Implications

Among implications for practice, our study suggests that investors and boards should closely scrutinize changes in IT investments, particularly when such changes are likely to be related to REM. Boards may consider, when appropriate, using decentralized IT governance as a potential way to curb the compromise of IT investment. It would be in the interest of boards to put in other complementary governance mechanisms, such as dedicated IT governance committees and more direct engagement of boards in overseeing IT infrastructure and capability building (e.g., Nolan and McFarlan 2005; Turel et al. 2017; Van Peteghem et al. 2019; Weill et al. 2019); because such mechanisms are not only profitable (Mithas et al. 2012), but are eventually rewarded by the stock and bond market (Kim et al. 2017; Mithas et al. 2014). Boards may also require firms to be more transparent about their IT practices (Bloch et al. 2012; Desmet and Mesøy 2009; Willmott 2013) and tighten the link between the development of digital strategies and firm performance. Institutional investors, regulators and financial analysts should also demand IT-related information from firms, for a more complete assessment of performance and to reduce risks due to poor management of IT. Regulators can also consider mandating higher levels of disclosure of IT spending and IT initiatives in annual reports and financial statements because, despite the importance of managing information flows within and across firms, very few firms volunteer such information on their own. Mandated IT disclosure may force firms to commit to their planned IT investment and prevent IT resources from manipulation or undercutting when managers engage in REM.

## Limitations and Future Research

Some limitations of this study also create opportunities for future research. First, this study focuses on how firms may fall short of their investment plans in REM. Sometimes firms may overinvest in IT (Ho et al. 2017). Overinvestment may be driven by different mechanisms (e.g., empire-building) and also deserves future research attention. Some fudge factors in budgeting may also confound IT investment commitment. For example, technological trends may drive firms to gradually shift from a capital investment budget (to build IT infrastructure internally) to an operating budget (e.g., using more cloud-based services) in building IT infrastructure. This may alter their internal investment commitment.<sup>17</sup> Also, if managers strategically inflate their planned IT budgets for risk avoidance or negotiation purposes and actually spend at their desired levels, the lack of IT investment commitment may be less of an issue. While our data lacks the granularity to examine such fudge factors, future research with alternative data sources may address them.

Second, the lack of data availability prevented us from assessing firms’ investment commitments in other IT areas such as software. Future research with such data may examine firms’ commitments to other types of IT investments and how they may also be affected by short-term managerial behavior in REM. Third, in addition to IT decentralization, future studies may consider how other IT governance mechanisms (e.g., strategic IT planning processes) may help mitigate the impact of short-term managerial behavior on IT investment commitment.

Fourth, the actual relationship between IT investment commitment and REM may be subject to many organizational and environmental boundary conditions. For example, firms may face fixed-term contracts or other legally binding obligations that compel them to stick to their original budget. A more transparent environment, for example, with more disclosure of IT initiatives in financial reporting, can also limit the room for IT investment manipulation in REM. Similarly, private or not-for-profit organizations that are under less performance pressure from external stakeholders compared to public firms may have less motivation to manipulate IT investments for the short-term earnings purpose. Even in REM, managers may also be influenced by the inclination to protect IT (e.g., by avoiding IT outsourcing or IT department downsizing). From a real option perspective, sometimes it may also be desirable for firms to reserve the flexibility of temporally adjusting (e.g., postponing) their IT investments. Therefore, future research may study these organizational and environmental factors that add nuance to the relationship between REM and IT investments.

Finally, although our study provides useful insights when firms invested in traditional information technologies (e.g., corporate internal IT infrastructure), more recent technological development, such as cloud-based on-demand computing, may reshape the relationship between REM and IT investment by altering the way that firms makes IT investment. Arguably, while on-demand computing may reduce the need for planning large-scale IT infrastructure investments in advance and therefore make the deviation from plans less salient, some industry reports indicate that cloud computing may increase long term IT infrastructure costs. We call for further studies on the relationship between REM and IT investment in the new technological contexts when firms are planning more investments in cloud computing, artificial intelligence, and machine learning. To the extent such technological choices may have implications for power balance between IT department and other organizational units, they may raise new questions about how such power balance increase or decrease levels of IT investments at the firm level even if IT department loses control on firm-level IT spending.

## Concluding Remarks

This study examined the role of REM, or the managerial proneness to alter normal business activities in the short term, in shaping IT investments. We find evidence that firms engaging in REM are more likely to fall short of their IT investment plans. Interestingly, decentralized IT governance can mitigate the potentially negative effects of REM. Furthermore, corporate governance mechanisms, such as institutional ownership and takeover threats, can complement the beneficial effects of IT decentralization. Overall, these findings provide new insights on the benefit of IT decentralization in fostering corporate commitment to IT investment, as well as practical implications regarding how different corporate governance and IT governance mechanisms can help to realize optimal investments in corporate digital resources.

## REM and Cash Constraints

We conduct a robustness check to see if the association between REM and ITIC is stronger in cash-constrained firms. The main idea is that cash-constrained firms should be more likely to sacrifice IT infrastructure investment in REM. We follow the literature (Denis and Sibilkov 2010; Khieu et al. 2014) to measure cash constraints in two ways: dividend payout ratio (DevdPay) and debt rated (DebtRated). DebtRated has a value of 1 (i.e., constrained) if a firm observation has debt outstanding that year, but have never had their public debt rated before (or the long-term debt rating is unavailable). DebtRated has a value of 0 (i.e., unconstrained) if the firm has no debt outstanding, or has had its long-term debt rated by Standard & Poor’s and its debt is not in default. The results, as summarized in Table C1, are consistent with expectations. ## Appendix D

## Assessing the Effect of AEM on ITIC

The purpose of this robustness analysis is to examine if AEM also affects ITIC. We rerun the main analysis with AEM included as an independent variable. The results are summarized in Table D1.
