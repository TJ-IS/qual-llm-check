# Research Note

# The Cost Impact of Spam Filters: Measuring the Effect of Information System Technologies in Organizations

Marco Caliendo

Chair of Empirical Economics, University of Potsdam and Institute for the Study of Labor (IZA), D-14482 Potsdam, Germany, caliendo@uni-potsdam.de

Michel Clement, Dominik Papies

Institute for Marketing and Media, University of Hamburg, D-20354 Hamburg, Germany, {michel@michelclement.com, dominik.papies@uni-hamburg.de}

Sabine Scheel-Kopeinig University of Cologne, D-50923 Cologne, Germany, scheel-kopeinig@wiso.uni-koeln.de

D <sup>ealing</sup> <sup>with</sup> <sup>spam</sup> <sup>is</sup> <sup>very</sup> <sup>costly,</sup> <sup>and</sup> <sup>many</sup> <sup>organizations</sup> <sup>have</sup> <sup>tried</sup> <sup>to</sup> <sup>reduce</sup> <sup>spam-related</sup> <sup>costs</sup> <sup>by</sup>installing spam filters. Relying on modern econometric methods to reduce the selection bias of installing a spam filter, we use a unique data setting implemented at a German university to measure the costs associated with spam and the costs savings of spam filters. Our methodological framework accounts for effect heterogeneity and can be easily used to estimate the effect of other IS technologies implemented in organizations.

The majority of costs stem from the time that employees spend identifying and deleting spam, amounting to an average of approximately five minutes per employee per day. Our analysis, which accounts for selection bias, finds that the installation of a spam filter reduces these costs by roughly one third. Failing to account for the selection bias would lead to a result that suggests that installing a spam filter does not reduce working time losses.

However, cost savings only occur when the spam burden is high, indicating that spam filters do not necessarily reduce costs and are therefore no universal remedy. The analysis further shows that spam filters alone are a countermeasure against spam that exhibits only limited effectiveness because they only reduce costs by one third.

Key words: spam; spam filter; selection bias; propensity score matching

## Introduction

Spam is defined as the use of electronic communication channels to indiscriminately send unsolicited bulk messages with commercial content to recipients (OECD 2005). Potentially high profits resulting from low market entry barriers and low operating costs continuously attract spammers, with the effect that since October 2003, more than 50% of total global e-mail traffic has been continuously classified as spam. In 2010, the average share was around 89% (MessageLabs 2010).

Spamming leads to negative externalities that primarily affect e-mail service providers and recipients of spam (Goodman et al. 2007). E-mail providers must handle billions of “abusive mails” (Message-

Labs 2010); individuals must constantly deal with spam; and organizations complain about the burden placed on employees who must make an effort to deal with spam, making investments in technical infrastructure necessary. In summary, spam affects individuals, organizations and economies on a global scale (Hanke and Hauser 2008). In response, technical, market, and legal actions have been undertaken to reduce the costs of spam (Zhang 2005, Joseph and Thevaranjan 2008)—however, success has been limited so far.

Despite the strong interest in reducing the impact of spam, we find only a limited amount of academic research addressing the cost effectiveness of antispam actions (Joseph and Thevaranjan 2008). Although we find a few management-related studies conducted by consulting companies (e.g., Vircom 2004) that seek to measure spam-related costs, precise measures of individual or corporate costs assessing the magnitude of the problem have not been subject to academic consideration. More importantly, no study that we are aware of assesses the impact of technical countermeasures on the costs associated with spam. Instead, most spam-related research focuses on mechanisms intended to reduce spam or its impact. This type of literature can be grouped into four streams. First, theoretical economic papers analyze and model market mechanisms that can be used to overcome the externalities of spam by increasing its cost for the sender (e.g., Kraut et al. 2005). Second, a significant amount of research focuses on legal methods of increasing risk (and cost) for spammers (e.g., Zhang 2005). A third stream of literature addresses user perceptions of spam (e.g., Morimoto and Chang 2006). Fourth, papers in the IS literature contribute to enhancing filtering technologies by identifying, marking, and filtering unsolicited e-mails (e.g., Cormack and Lynam 2007). Whereas most individuals and organizations use filter technologies to reduce the amount of spam in their in boxes, market mechanisms and legal actions are typically beyond the control of IS managers. However, despite the widespread use of spam filters, we know of no study that empirically addresses the question of whether or not spam filters or other countermeasures have the desired effect and indeed reduce costs.

This dearth of research is surprising for two reasons. First, on the corporate level, company investments in information technology involve high risk and unclear outcomes (Dewan et al. 2007, Dewan and Ren 2007), which should result in a strong interest in measuring the effectiveness of IS investments. However, such outcomes are not easy to measure. Although experimental settings would be theoretically appropriate, a control group is rarely available to serve as a benchmark because it is often impossible to conduct experiments that measure the cost effectiveness of IS innovations (Mithas and Krishnan 2009). Thus, alternative approaches are required. Second, although spam filters are designed to reduce the spam burden and thus to decrease spam-related costs, this desired effect is not necessarily achieved. In general, five cost components can be identified (European Union 2004). Direct costs include (1) loss of working time caused by the need to delete spam and install and train filters and (2) central costs that accrue in data centers and information technology (IT) departments as a result of the installation of countermeasures. Further, (3) direct costs arise if Internet service providers must adapt their capacities to respond to increased spam. Indirect costs refer to the effect of spam on e-mail usage: e-mails (4) can be erroneously identified as spam (false positives) or (5) might contain viruses or other potentially harmful features. Several of these cost components are directly caused by the decision to adopt a spam filter: the installation and training of filter mechanisms, the central costs, and the control of the spam filter (especially for false positives). In sum, the net benefit of the adoption and usage of spam filters depends on whether the gain in efficiency outweighs the costs of installation and the sum of time losses as a result of filter maintenance as well as checking the filter results for potential misclassifications (false positives/ negatives). Therefore, the individual net cost effect remains an open issue a priori, and whether spam filters do indeed reduce costs remains unclear. Although one would expect the overall net effect of spam filters to be positive, this might not be the case for all users.

We address this research gap and pursue two major research aims. First, we measure the central and individual costs of spam in an organization to evaluate the magnitude of the spam problem. Second, we take a preliminary step toward evaluating the efficiency of countermeasures by focusing on spam filters. Without a controlled experimental setting, evaluating the cost effects of spam filters in cross-sectional samples requires rigorous control for the presence of a selection bias, which could arise because respondents using a spam filter may differ from those who do not. For example, we find that users of spam filters spend more time dealing with spam than those who do not use a filter. This raises the question of causality: Do users install filters because of high spam costs, or do filters add to costs rather than reducing them as considered above? Ignoring this selection effect would lead to biased results in a typical cross-sectional sample using simple ordinary least squares (OLS), for example. Therefore, we reduce this selection bias by drawing on a propensity score matching procedure (Smith and Todd 2001, 2005; Mithas and Krishnan 2009). This procedure has been recently introduced to the IS literature by Mithas and Krishnan (2009, p. 309) as effective for “answering research questions that focus on the estimation of causal effects of binary and sharp interventions on firm performance.”

To measure the effect of spam filters on the individual costs of spam, we collected data from a German university and differentiate between the individual costs for the employees (N = 960) and the costs to the university’s central data center. The nature of our data allows us not only to indicate implications for the specific organization but also to extend our findings to other organizations with similar properties. We evaluate under which circumstances our findings can be generalized beyond the specific context based on the characteristics of our measurements.

Our research contributes to the IS literature in two dimensions. First, the nature of our data set enables us to provide an indication of the costs of spam on both the individual and corporate levels. We show that central IT costs are of less relevance because the majority of spam costs are accrued by employees who spend working time identifying and deleting spam—on average, 5.07 minutes per employee per day. We find that the time required to deal with spam can be reduced by roughly one third through the installation of a spam filter. Contrary to our expectations, a spam filter does not reduce costs under any circumstances, but its efficiency depends on the amount of spam an individual receives. Thus, heterogeneity with regard to the cost-saving effect of a spam filter is a key to targeting antispam actions toward the right individuals.

Second, the measurement of the consequences of the installation decision is affected by sample selection bias, as is most likely the case in many IS research settings focused on the impact of IT interventions implemented in organizations (e.g., the introduction of an innovative accounting software). We demonstrate the importance of controlling for this selection bias when measuring the value of IT adoption; otherwise, the results are confounded by inconsistent estimates that could lead to inferior investment decisions. For example, in our study, failing to control for selection bias would have resulted in the finding that a spam filter does not reduce costs. We therefore rely on propensity score matching and show in the next section how the procedure introduced into the IS literature by Mithas and Krishnan (2009) can be used to measure the effect of optional IT adoption on individual-level variables such as costs when experiments are not feasible.

## Method

The potential outcome approach, also known as the Roy–Rubin model, is a suitable framework for addressing the question of whether the installation of a spam filter reduces employees’ working time losses (Roy 1951, Rubin 1974). D is a treatment indicator and equals one if a spam filter is installed and zero otherwise. The second term on the righthand side of Equation (1) is unobservable; i.e., we do not observe the potential outcome without a filter for those who have installed a filter. Hence, we must find a proper substitute for this counterfactual component. Solely comparing a situation at play today (installed spam filter) with a situation observed in the past (no spam filter) could be misleading because of unobserved effects over time. In an experimental setting, where the installation of spam filters is randomly assigned to employees (Harrison and List 2004), we could compare the mean working times of those employees who have installed a spam filter (called the treatment group hereafter) and those who have not (the control group) because E6Y 405  D = 17 = $E [ Y ( 0 ) \mid D = 0 ]$ . However, such an experimental setting is not an option for most companies in the case of spam filters or comparable IS investments.

Furthermore, our empirical data (see Table 2) indicate that users with a spam filter differ in more aspects than just the decision to install a filter, such that simply comparing mean working times for the treatment and the control groups would yield a biased estimate of the ATT known as self-selection bias. For instance, one can assume that users with high spamrelated costs have a higher propensity to install spam filters. If this selection is caused only by observable characteristics, we can rely on the conditional independence assumption (CIA) as an identification strategy. We assume that potential working time losses are independent of the installation decision given a set of relevant, observable variables X (Imbens 2004). By relevant we mean that we need to observe all variables that simultaneously influence the decision to install a spam filter and the outcome variable. In general, this is a very strong assumption that needs to be justified case by case given the data and the research setting at hand. Even though we are confident that our data cover the X variables that are crucial to justify the conditional independence assumption, we test the sensitivity of our results to this assumption.

Based on the conditional independence assumption, we use propensity score matching (PSM) to estimate the ATT (Imbens and Wooldridge 2008). The basic idea behind PSM is to approximate the counterfactual working times of individuals in the treatment group by finding similar users in terms of their probability of installing a spam filter $P ( D = 1 \mid X )$ in the control group. One important feature of PSM—in contrast to OLS regression—is that it only compares individuals whose propensity score values overlap to a certain extent (common support).<sup>2</sup> To ensure that users from the control group have a positive probability of belonging to the treatment group, we must further assume that P 4D = 1  X5 < 1 (overlap assumption). ## Research Design and Data

We conduct our research at a German university with approximately 8,000 employees (including the university’s hospitals), whose size resembles that of a medium-sized company.<sup>3</sup> An advantage of this setting is the integrated structure of the institution, which combines most sources of spam-related costs into one organization: the university serves as an e-mail provider, operates its own data center, and employs a sufficiently high number of computer users. Our data collection focuses on two points of measurement within the university: (1) we measure all central costs incurred by the university data center, where all computer-related tasks are centralized, and (2) we contact 5,000 university employees (excluding the university’s hospitals) to measure the individual costs and a set of covariates using an online questionnaire.<sup>4</sup>

We differentiate between two main cost elements: infrastructure (software and hardware) and IT service and maintenance costs (Table 1). We asked the IT experts and the head of the university’s IT department about their time invested in spam protection (in hours). We used the average salary (<sup>E</sup>4,500 per month and 38.5 hours per week) of the employees working in the data center (in German public universities, wage groups are standardized and publicly available) to calculate the costs (<sup>E</sup>30 per hour).

## Individual Measurement

Sample. Our second measurement is designed to capture the individual costs to the employees. We invited all of the approximately 5,000 university employees to fill out an online questionnaire by e-mail in the winter term of 2004/2005. This included administrative staff as well as faculty members, Ph.D. students employed by the university, and Ph.D. students in graduate schools. Bachelor’s and master’s degree students were not included. We received exactly 1,000 responses (20% response rate) of which 960 are usable cases. For validation purposes we contacted all respondents one year later for a brief follow-up study (n = 421 respondents).

In the sample of study 1, 67% of all respondents are faculty or Ph.D. students, which compares well to approximately 60% of all employees at this university being faculty or Ph.D. students. Although this indicates a slight overrepresentation of administrative staff, it appears to be a reasonable representation of university employees. We received responses from individuals between the ages of 20 and 82 years with a mean of 40.8 years (SD = 11022); 54% of the respondents were male. Hence, the sample contains respondents from all professional and demographic segments. We therefore assume that the sample of respondents is not structurally different from that of the nonrespondents.

Costs. We separate recurring from nonrecurring costs. Recurring costs refer to the time per day allocated to deleting spam and controlling the spam filter. We therefore asked respondents how much time they spend on an average working day dealing with spam. For the 960 respondents, these activities take up an average of 5.07 minutes per day (Table 2).

The nonrecurring costs include inquiries about spam filters and installation by the respondent or an assistant. On average, installing a filter requires approximately one hour of working time. Compared to the recurring costs, they are relatively small (Table 2) because the nonrecurring costs constitute a one-time investment, whereas users face the recurring costs every day. We therefore focus our analysis on the daily recurring costs of dealing with spam and measure these costs using the time that an individual user allocates to these tasks. We later assess whether the results change when we include the nonrecurring costs of installation.

Selection of Covariates. The decision to adopt a spam filter was made autonomously by the employees such that the users willing to adopt one had to activate a central filter and define an automatic classification of e-mail in their local inbox or install a local spam filter. Of the respondents, 54.2% had already adopted a spam filter (D = 1). To characterize and predict the installation decision, we collected several covariates that characterize the e-mail user and his or her behavior pertaining to e-mail and computer usage.

First, adoption research (e.g., the technology acceptance model) suggests that the perceived utility of an innovation drives adoption behavior—in this case, spam filter installation. Although the initial costs of finding and installing a filter are small when we compare them to the recurring costs of dealing with spam, they are greater than zero (Table 2) and thus relevant for an individual’s decision-making process. We therefore assume that the installation decision is a function of the perceived net utility of the filter; i.e., the propensity to install a spam filter is in part driven by the amount of spam an individual receives.

Second, we control for heterogeneity that may arise because of different educational and occupational backgrounds. One reason is that faculty members have typically received more years of education, which may be reflected in different habits of computer usage (e.g., Bozionelos 2004). Further, administrative staff members have different tasks and therefore specific usage requirements that may affect the amount of spam as well as their sensitivity with regard to spam.

Third, we include the department affiliation of respondents because different departments are characterized by different needs to use computers. Faculty in departments such as natural science or economics are likely to have adopted computers and related means of communication earlier than other departments (e.g., humanities), resulting in a different “age” of e-mail addresses, different penetration rates of e-mail usage, and different degrees to which computer-related classes are part of the curriculum.

Fourth, we take into account the respondents’ computer configuration with regard to the operating system that is in use as well as the Internet browser. This captures, for example, the degree to which a user keeps track of new developments or makes distinct choices with regard to computer usage (e.g., uses a Macintosh computer although university standard procurement procedures make it more convenient to obtain a PC). Further, Windows-based operating systems have been exposed to a higher number of malicious attacks, which might result in Windows users taking specific countermeasures against unwanted external influences (e.g., spam, malware, viruses) to restore a perceived sense of security (Microsoft 2010).

## Analysis of Cost-Saving Effects

## Group Characteristics Before Matching

Comparing the average working time loss per day for users with spam filters (6.25 minutes) to those without filters (3.68 minutes) suggests that a spam filter significantly increases working time losses by approximately two and a half minutes per day. However, as displayed in Table 2, the groups differ not only in the amount of time they allocate to spam but also in their observed characteristics. These differences in observed characteristics indicate that a simple mean comparison of working time losses between the two groups cannot yield an unbiased estimate that answers the question of how much a spam filter can reduce working time losses.

Performing a simple OLS regression of working time losses for our set of explanatory variables X yields an insignificant regression coefficient for the spam filter dummy variable.<sup>6</sup> Even though OLS regression establishes a considerable difference from the mean comparison, it still does not solve the selection problem satisfactorily (Mithas and Krishnan 2009), and this finding implies that installing a spam filter does not reduce the daily time a user must allocate to handling spam. Standard OLS regression does not implement a “common support” condition, so users with diverse characteristics are compared and estimates are extrapolated even to regions in which common support is low. We therefore implement propensity score matching (PSM).

## Matching Results

We present three different matching estimators in our analysis: single nearest neighbor (NN) matching without common support (matching estimator A hereafter), single NN matching with common support condition (matching estimator B), and KM (kernel matching) with common support condition (matching estimator C). KM estimators extend pairwise matching approaches by using multiple control subjects as potential matches and are well established in the econometrics literature (Heckman et al. 1998). For the KM estimator, we use an Epanechnikov kernel function with a bandwidth parameter of 0.06 according to Silverman’s (1986) rule of thumb.<sup>10</sup> We present our matching results in Table 4.

All estimated effects are negative, which indicates that the installation of a spam filter lowers average working time losses regardless of the algorithm chosen. However, the magnitude of the effects differs between the matching algorithms.

The interpretation of the results should be preceded by an evaluation of matching quality. A first indication is given by Table 2, which shows that no covariate significantly differs between the two groups after matching. To determine more systematically whether the matching procedure balances the distribution of covariates between the two groups, Rosenbaum and Rubin (1985) propose using standardized biases (SB). ## Validation and Robustness Checks

Conditional Independence Assumption. The validity of our estimates depends on the conditional independence assumption. For this assumption to be fulfilled, we must observe all variables that simultaneously influence both the propensity to install a spam filter and the outcome variable. We validate whether unobserved heterogeneity might alter our results by applying the bounding approach proposed by Rosenbaum (2002) and implemented in state by Becker and Caliendo (2007). The basic idea of this approach is to determine how strongly an unobserved variable must influence the decision to install a spam filter in order to alter the significance of our matching results (Table A3 in the online appendix). In our case, with a negative estimated treatment t we only need to consider the situation in which we have underestimated (denoted as $\prime \prime \mathit { t } \mathrm { - h a t - \mathit { \Omega } } ^ { \prime \prime }$ in Table A3 in the online appendix) the true treatment effect. Gamma is a measure of the influence of the unobserved component where Gamma = 1 is a situation free of any hidden bias. For each value of Gamma, we can calculate the new p-value, showing that our results only become insignificant when Gamma equals 1.8. This in turn means that the influence of the unobserved component can only render our results insignificant if it is almost (80%) as strong as all our observed covariates. Given the depth of our data, this is rather unlikely.

Aggregation to Annual Costs. In the preceding analysis, we evaluated whether the installation of a spam filter reduces the daily recurring costs of dealing with spam. This does not take into account that those users who use a spam filter experience the one-time costs of installing the filter. Taking into account these initial efforts should reduce the cost-saving effects of a spam filter. To empirically address this question, we performed additional analyses that include the onetime effort of searching for and installing a filter (see installation effort in Table 2). We cumulated the daily measure of recurring costs to the annual time invested in dealing with spam and added the one-time cost of installation and related activities using two different measures: (1) We multiply the daily time by 365, which follows the assumption that spam e-mails enter the user’s in-box regardless of his or her presence at the workplace and require the user’s attention after vacation, weekends, and sick days. Further, this measure will be appropriate if many users attend to their e-mails on weekends. (2) We multiply the daily time by 220 days, hence using the average number of days an employee is present in the office. Although cumulating the daily costs for one year entails the danger of measurement error,<sup>12</sup> the estimation contains useful information by indicating the length of a time frame that is required for an investment in spam filter technology to pay off. Assuming a time horizon of 365 days, we find a significant effect of 555 minutes per employee and year. Aggregating the daily recurring costs over 220 days yields a treatment effect of 312 minutes per employee and year (Table A4 in the online appendix). However, the latter effect fails to be significant (with p = 0012). This suggests that the installation does not pay off in the short run, whereas in the long term it does. This result is indeed plausible because the shorter the time frame that is considered, the larger the nonrecurring costs that will arise only for adopters relative to the daily recurring costs. Hence, we can conclude that—assuming a time frame of one year or more—the benefits of installing a spam filter outweigh the costs of installing, using, and training the filter. As we will discuss next, these effects also indicate that spam filters alone are not a sufficient countermeasure against spam.

Survey Measures. Although the use of survey measures is common in many research areas, it entails the risk of measurement error. We therefore invited all respondents from study 1 to take part in a brief follow-up survey (study 2) one year later. The 421 responses from study 2 allow us to perform additional validity checks on the measures used in the main study. To assess the reliability of our dependent variable, we compute the correlation between the working time losses in studies 1 and 2 reported by those respondents who did not invest in (additional) countermeasures after study 1 and find a strong and highly significant correlation with $r = 0 . 6 7$ . We take this as evidence that this variable constitutes a sufficiently reliable measure. One potential problem for our econometric identification strategy could arise from the cross-sectional nature of the data if the respondents’ installation decision affects those variables that we use as a predictor of the installation decision in the logit model. The variables that capture “spam prevention” may be the ones that exhibit the highest probability of being affected. We explored whether these variables significantly change between studies 1 and 2 for those users who installed a filter in this time frame and found no significant changes. We therefore conclude that these variables are not affected by the installation decision, and therefore our identification should not be confounded by these variables. Lastly, we checked whether the assumption that working time losses because of spam are constant over time is justifiable by assessing the development of these variables for those users who did not change their countermeasures between studies 1 and 2, and we do not detect significant changes. This suggests that we do not observe learning effects over time that translate into significant time savings. We therefore view it appropriate to assume that working time losses are constant over the time frame we analyze. In sum, these checks do not provide indications that the data we used to estimate the cost-saving effects are confounded by severe measurement errors. Details for these analyses are listed in the online appendix (Tables A5–A7).

## Discussion and Conclusion

## Summary

Our analysis shows that the existence of spam creates new expenses for organizations, primarily in the form of working time losses such that on average an employee invests approximately five minutes per day in dealing with spam. When an organization decides to react by using a spam filter, the organization and the users incur further installation expenses. The net effect of this trade-off has been unclear so far. Our research sheds light on this effect and shows that spam filters can reduce individual spam-related costs by approximately one third. The findings are significant and relatively insensitive to unobserved heterogeneity. Furthermore, we find that the desired cost-saving effects of spam filters only occur for users whose spam load is high.

## Generalizability

This paper analyzes the cost-saving effectiveness of IS innovations using spam filter installation in one specific organization as an application. However, we believe that key implications can be extended beyond the specific case if the organization is comparable to other organizations with regard to at least three key dimensions: (1) the amount of spam to which the organization is exposed, (2) the technological solution deployed to deal with this spam burden, and (3) the users who use the technology and have to deal with spam.

Ad (1): The university we studied is accessible on the Internet via a top-level domain (.de), suggesting that the probability that the e-mail addresses in this organization will be identified by spammers is not confounded by a specific domain-level construction. This is a requirement for external validity (Melville et al. 2006). Furthermore, the comparison of the spam rate in our study (the university’s data center reported a spam rate of about 90% at the time of our study) to the reported 89.1% global spam rate in 2010 and to the 82% rate from early 2005 (MessageLabs 2010) shows that the amount of spam is in the same range and is stable over time. Thus, the organization under study is exposed to a spam load that can be considered typical.

Ad (2): Because the university uses one of the globally most widespread filter solutions (Spam Assassin), the technological infrastructure is comparable to that of many other organizations.

Ad (3): Although respondents with higher education levels are more likely to be present at a university, the office work performed at a university is comparable to that of many other organizations. Because university employees are hired from the free market and companies hire employees who have been previously employed by a university, it is unlikely that university employees will systematically differ in their ability to deal with spam.

We therefore assume that our results are comparable and implications can be extended to samples of users whose value creation primarily entails computer-assisted work, whose structure is dominated by personnel with higher education, and who work in a midsized company that uses a widespread spam filter solution such as Spam Assassin.

Furthermore, our findings with regard to the daily time spent on dealing with spam do not substantially differ from those of other studies. For instance, the National Technology Readiness Survey finds losses of 3 minutes per day for private users (Rockbridge 2005). Similarly, McAfee (2003) reports losses of 40 minutes per week, which equals 5.7 minutes per day, and Nucleus Research (2003) reports time losses of 6.5 minutes per day. Thus, we trust that our finding of 5.07 lost minutes per day because of spam has face validity. This indication of convergent validity also suggests that the university and the employees we study do not systematically differ with regard to unobserved characteristics that render the results incomparable to those of other organizations.

One additional concern could pertain to the longevity of the results because we study a dynamically evolving market. Spam distributors and spam filter developers have been in constant and strong competition such that new technologies from spam distributors lead to new filter technologies and vice versa. With either the filter developers or the spam distributors being one step ahead, this competition constantly forces organizations to react (Goodman et al. 2007, Kanich et al. 2009). One of the main reasons for this constant competition may be that although individual users learn how to efficiently deal with spam, so do spam distributors. Consequently, the global spam rates have constantly remained far above 70% for many years, with only temporary effects after innovative antispam countermeasures and no downward trend (MessageLabs 2010). Thus, spam is likely to remain an issue for the individual user and will continue to remain a significant issue in the IS setup in many organizations.

## Implications

One finding that we presented above pertains to the different magnitude of the central costs and the individual working time losses. That the central costs are small compared to the cumulative sum of the costs caused by individual working time losses suggests that the primary concern in organizations should be the effectiveness of filter mechanisms because the cumulative time savings—if expressed in monetary terms—easily outweigh the central costs of the installation of antispam measures. Hence, in our study, the university’s investment in antispam measures paid off—and it is likely to pay off for comparable organizations. Note, however, that one would have arrived at the opposite conclusion if one had used an estimation technique that does not reduce the sample selection bias.

Because cost-saving effects only occur for those users with a high spam load, companies should primarily address these users to reduce costs through spam filters. For these users, the installation of a spam filter will lead to the desired effect. If a user is not strongly affected by spam, a company should not encourage the implementation of technical countermeasures. In this case, manual inspections appear to be more efficient than are filter mechanisms. Thus, we find no homogeneous effect of spam filters such that costs are reduced for every type of user, which suggests that a spam filter solution is no universal remedy.

Furthermore, our results show that spam filters reduce the individual costs by roughly one third. Although the magnitude of the effect justifies investments in countermeasures, the spam filter leaves two thirds of the costs being externalized by the spam distributors to the companies and e-mail recipients. This indicates that although spam filters are technically advanced, technical solutions such as filters cannot be considered a sufficient solution to the spam problem. In combination with the effect heterogeneity that we discussed above, this underlines that spam filters are no universal remedy. Their insufficiency requires legal and economic measures to complement the technical measures.

We derive our conclusions by applying an econometric matching approach that controls for selection bias based on observable characteristics and also test the sensitivity of our results with respect to deviations from this identifying assumption. It is unlikely that the selection bias is unique to our sample. Rather, selection bias probably poses a problem for a multitude of other IS or IT value research questions (Chan 2000, Mithas and Krishnan 2009). Within organizations, this effect might arise in evaluations of the effectiveness of optional IT innovations for which an experimental setting is not available. To evaluate the effectiveness of the innovation, the company cannot use a simple cross-sectional approach because the estimation cannot distinguish whether the outcome measure (e.g., efficiency) causes or is affected by adoption. Comparisons between several organizations entail a similar problem. Consider the introduction of an innovative accounting software system. An efficiency evaluation cannot occur without correcting for sample selection bias because cross-sectional estimation procedures cannot distinguish whether efficient companies tend to be early adopters of new software or whether the adoption of new software enhances their efficiency. Thus, when experimental settings are infeasible, we recommend the application of a quasiexperimental setting that draws on matching procedures. This provides a viable and economic way to correct for sample selection bias. However, it is important to acknowledge that the applicability of propensity score matching as a method for correcting sample selection bias depends on the availability of the most important variables that influence treatment and outcome variables.

Finally, we note some limitations to our study beyond the potential limitations regarding generalizability that we noted above. First, we use selfreported measures. Although this is common practice in research and provides generally accepted validity, we do note that self-reported cost measures are likely to entail a certain measurement error. Thus, a comparison between these measures and observed measures, perhaps in an experimental setting, might enhance generalizability. Second, although we demonstrate the positive effects of spam filters on the individual and organizational levels, we cannot extend our findings so far as to propose general welfare implications. Whether spam filters represent the most efficient way to deal with spam in the long term remains unclear. Some theoretical evidence suggests that the widespread use of filters might even increase the overall amount of spam (Melville et al. 2006). Therefore, this question should be addressed in research to derive long-term recommendations about spam policy.
