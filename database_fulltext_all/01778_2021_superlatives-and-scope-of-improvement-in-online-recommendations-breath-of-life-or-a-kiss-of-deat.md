# SUPERLATIVES AND SCOPE OF IMPROVEMENT IN ONLINE RECOMMENDATIONS: BREATH OF LIFE OR A KISS OF DEATH?<sup>1</sup>

Rohit Aggarwal David Eccles School of Business, University of Utah Salt Lake City, UT, U.S.A. {rohit.aggarwal@eccles.utah.edu}

Vishal Midha College of Business, Illinois State University Normal, IL, U.S.A {vmidha@illinoisstate.edu}

Nicholas Sullivan School of Business Administration, University of Dayton Dayton, OH, U.S.A. {nsullivan2@udayton.edu}

Online professional networks are important tools used by recruiters to find qualified candidates for job openings. Within these networks, professional recommendations are used to supplement profiles and add credibility. These recommendations tend to be overly positive, full of superlatives, and lacking in critical statements (referred to as scope of improvement). We draw on the theory of online trust to argue that having scope of improvement and superlatives may affect various dimensions of trust and to show how online trust, in turn, can affect the usefulness of a recommendation and the likelihood of receiving an interview. We contribute to the body of work on online trust both theoretically and empirically. From a theory perspective, we explain why including scope of improvement and superlatives in recommendations on online professional networks may help certain candidates in getting an interview but hurt others. From an empirical perspective, we provide a unique empirical setting that allows us to observe not only the effect of scope of improvement and superlatives, but also validate the theoretically argued underlying process. Furthermore, through discussion with recruiters, we identify then test contextual factors that differentiate recommendations on online professional networks from traditional recommendations. In this study, we use a scenario-based, quasiexperimental survey to test the effects of superlatives and scope of improvement on the usefulness and effectiveness of recommendations. Further, we test the mediating role of trust and how the experience levels of the recommendee affect the sign and strength of these relationships. Our findings indicate that including scope of improvement increases the effectiveness and usefulness of recommendations for candidates at low- and middle levels of experience. For the most experienced candidates, including scope of improvement has a negative effect on effectiveness. Superlatives negatively affect the perceived competence of the recommender and thus should be avoided. This negative effect is reduced when combined with scope of improvement.

Keywords: Online trust, social networks, recommendations, superlatives, negative messaging

## Introduction

A 2016 national survey by Jobvite (2016) confirms that social media is a widely used tool for recruiters and those seeking jobs. According to the survey, 59% of individuals actively seeking a job use social media as part of their search.

Perhaps more importantly, 87% of recruiters report using LinkedIn to find potential job candidates. The number of recruiters using other, similar platforms (Xing, Viadeo, Branchout, etc.) is likely even higher. This underscores the importance of having an up-to-date presence on these online professional networks (OPNs) in order to increase the chances of improving one’s current employment situation. A typical OPN allows users to maintain professional profiles with education, work background, skills, certifications, and other qualifications that would make them an attractive job candidate. One common feature allows users to give and receive professional recommendations from other users. This provides an opportunity for other users to confirm listed information and endorse the user for a job position. This endorsement can increase the user’s value on the job market and help to reinforce the professional image the user wishes to maintain (Van Dijck 2013). The professional image a user maintains is important because recruiters often base hiring decisions not only on the quantifiable qualifications of the applicant, but on the perceived fit of the individual with the job and organization (Chiang and Suen 2015; Tsai et al. 2011). Recommendations have the potential to boost users’ visibility on the network, improve users’ chance of getting a job, and add professional credibility to their profile (Butow and Taylor 2008). Recommendations on networking sites have been proven to be an effective tool in matching resources with those who value them (Garg et al. 2011). These recommendations are published to a user’s profile page at the discretion of the user and made visible to anyone on the network. To differentiate these kinds of professional recommendations from those delivered directly to the recipient, we refer to them as OPN recommendations and refer to the latter as traditional recommendations.

When recommenders agree to write a recommendation, a primary objective in writing the recommendation is often to persuade readers to see the recommendee in a positive light (Cascio and Aguinis 2010). This desire to portray a recommendee positively often leads recommenders to inflate their recommendations (Ryan and Martinson 2000). This can present in different forms, such as intentionally leaving out negative information or including excessive positive information such as an overuse of superlatives. A text analysis we performed on 13,620 OPN recommendations revealed that recommendations are overwhelmingly positive and use superlatives at a rate four times higher than news articles and twice as high as online movie reviews. The analysis did not uncover a single instance of a recommender sharing potentially critical information about an area where the recommendee could improve. The lack of critical information and a high rate of superlatives may cause trust issues, which is problematic because the usefulness and effectiveness of any persuasive message depend upon the trustworthiness of the content and its source (Chaiken 1980).

Trust is an important concept, especially in online behaviors, and is needed for electronic relationships, especially those with financial implications (Gefen 2000; Jarvenpaa et al. 1999; Pavlou et al. 2007; Reichheld and Schefter 2000). OPN recommendations have financial implications because recruiters use them to make decisions about which personnel to invest time and money in, and poor decisions can lead to a loss of both of these valuable resources. When dealing with professional networking sites, users must decide whether to trust specific content based on information available to them (McKnight et al. 2002). This means when they read recommendations, they use what is written to determine whether they can trust the writer and the message. Therefore, it is critical for the writer to give some indication that they are credible and can be trusted.

Writers of recommendations have a decision to make. Should they try to establish trust with readers by including weaknesses of the recommendee (referred to as scope of improvement) or should they avoid risking the potential damage this information could cause and discuss only positive aspects? Would a recommendation containing scope of improvement be more effective at establishing credibility and influencing behavior, or would it act as a “kiss of death” (Knouse 1983) to any hope the candidate may have of receiving an interview. If including scope of improvement does cause damage, will the damage be more pronounced for candidates with high levels of experience than for others?

In this paper, we use a scenario-based, quasi-experimental survey to study the broad research question of whether and when including scope of improvement and superlatives may be beneficial, and under what circumstances they should be avoided. Specifically, we study the effect of scope of improvement and superlatives on the likelihood of a recruiter being willing to interview a candidate and the usefulness of the recommendation. We also consider the mediating role of recommender trust and the moderating role of the candidate’s experience level on these effects.

To the best of our knowledge, there are no other papers that theorize the underlying process of how scope of improvement and superlatives may affect a candidate’s propensity to get an interview or the usefulness of a recommendation, nor any papers that empirically test the effect of scope of improvement or superlatives. Previous papers on the general topic of letters of recommendation largely provide qualitative discussion and viewpoints on the problem of inflation in recommendations without using any theoretical framework such as trust, and do not empirically test their effects. For example, Ryan and Martinson (2000) largely present their views on how inflated recommendations are not a victimless crime, and mostly focus on increasing awareness of the harm caused by inflated recommendations in academic circles.

Consequently, this paper makes multiple contributions: It is the first attempt to theoretically propose the underlying process and the first attempt to empirically test the effect of scope of improvement and superlatives on interview propensity along with the underlying process. Furthermore, through our discussions with recruiters, we were able to identify and capture contextual factors that differentiate OPN recommendations from traditional recommendations. Specifically, we found that recruiters place lower trust in OPN recommendations because they believe that recommenders often withhold information when they provide recommendations to recommendees for posting on OPNs. Another contribution of this work is that it captures this difference and shows how including scope of improvement in OPN recommendations can make up for the loss of trust, in comparison to traditional recommendations.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. First, we look at literature related to topics associated with our study. Then, we develop the theoretical foundations for our hypotheses. Next, we discuss the design of the study and the data used to test our hypotheses. After discussing the results and analysis used, we provide a discussion of the findings and managerial implications of the findings and end with a brief conclusion.

## Theoretical Background

## Letters of Recommendation

Traditional letters of recommendation have been a topic of study for decades. Researchers have sought to determine how valid these letters are and how well they predict future performance (Aamodt et al. 1993; Baxter et al. 1981; Browning 1968; Judge and Higgins 1998; Leising et al. 2010). Letters of recommendation have played an important role in placements in college, graduate school, internships, employment, and other programs (Range et al. 1991). One survey found that top-ranked graduate programs ranked the letter of recommendation at a higher level of importance than GRE scores or GPAs when making admission decisions (Purdy et al. 1989).

In general, people are not asked, nor do they agree to write a letter of recommendation for another unless they have positive things to say; as a result, most letters are highly favorable to the recommendee (Cascio and Aguinis 2010). Consequently, letters can come across as being inflated, full of superlatives, and so positive that they risk becoming uninformative (Range et al. 1991). While inflation of recommendations appears to be an issue with the practice, extant literature only qualitatively discusses the issue without ever testing the effect that inflation in the recommendation has on the likelihood of the recommendee getting an interview. For example, Ryan and Martinson (2000) largely present their views on how inflated recommendations may give an unfair advantage to undeserving candidates, may force others to inflate their recommendations, can embarrass faculty when inadequate skills are discovered later, can set unrealistic employer’s expectations for candidates, and may weaken the employer’s organization. Their paper mostly focuses on increasing awareness of these harms caused by inflated recommendations in academic circles. They conclude their work with a survey that captures the perceived prevalence of inflated recommendations and ethical issues regarding the practice. Their focus is neither to discuss nor empirically test how such inflations in recommendations affect candidates job chances.

Similarly, Rosovsky and Hartley (2002) discuss factors that have led to recommendation inflation, reasons individuals may inflate letters they write, as well as potential legal issues that may exist when writing letters of recommendation. Suggestions are then provided on how to potentially remedy the issue of inflation. However, the actual effect of inflation on an individual’s likelihood of receiving an interview is neither discussed nor tested. In an opinion piece, Smith (1998), suggests that letters of recommendation have become so inflated that readers look for anything that can be construed as criticism and take it as such. Extant literature, besides providing mostly qualitative discussion, often provides conflicting viewpoints on the effectiveness of not withholding negative information; some suggest that any mention of weakness may disqualify even the most qualified candidate (Larkin and Marco 2001; Smith 1998), while others say it may have a positive impact but add that more research is needed on the topic (Knouse 1983). Furthermore, because previous papers that mention superlatives do not actually test their effects (Range et al. 1991; Ryan and Martinson 2000; Smith 1998), they are unable to provide any insight about the effect on perceptions of the letter writer. Consequently, some previous papers have noted that despite the wide breadth of literature on letters of recommendation, there remains a lack of empirical research on how a recommendee’s candidacy is affected by elements within a recommendation (Mason and Schroeder 2012; Mason and Schroeder 2014).

## Trust

Trust in online environments is paramount because traditional cues that we often rely on in face-to-face interactions are not available (Reichheld and Schefter 2000). With much of our activities in today’s society being of an impersonal nature, it has become essential to determine which sources should and should not be trusted (Smith 2003). As such, trust has become an important topic in information systems research (e.g., Benbasat et al. 2008). While there are different ways to define and represent trust (Kanawattanachai and Yoo 2007; Lewis and Weigert 1985; McAllister 1995), the most common approach is to break down the construct of trust is into three components: competence, benevolence, and integrity (Mayer et al. 1995; McKnight et al. 2002). Competence refers to one’s ability and expertness to perform a task. Benevolence refers to one’s motivation not to be self-serving, but rather, to help others. Integrity refers to one’s commitment to being honest and truthful.

Distinguishing these different dimensions of trust is important because it is possible for one or two to be present without the remaining being present as well. One may trust their physician to give medical advice but not to give legal counsel. While the physician may have one’s best interest at heart (benevolence) and has no intention to deceive (integrity), a physician is likely not an expert in various legal matters (competence). Likewise, one might be wary of taking the advice of an opposing counsel because despite being competent, they may not have their best interest in mind (benevolence). Benevolence and integrity both represent ethical traits (McKnight et al. 2002) while competence is an indication of ability.

In the IS literature, trust has been shown to play an important role in the adoption of new technologies (Gefen et al. 2003), the use of e-commerce tools, and online marketplaces (Fang et al. 2014; McKnight et al. 2002; Pavlou and Fygenson 2006; Pavlou and Gefen 2004). Trust has also been found to be a valid predictor of customer retention (Gefen 2002). That is to say, if users trust an entity, then they are more likely to engage in risk-related behaviors associated with the entity. Additionally, trust has been found to play an important role in the reception of useful information (Levin and Cross 2004) with information coming from trusted sources being more influential and desired.

## OPN Recommendations

A large focus of online word of mouth research has been on online product and service reviews (Ho et al. 2017; Hu et al. 2017; Huang et al. 2009; Huang et al. 2015; Li and Hitt 2010; Mudambi and Schuff 2010). The body of research on this topic is quite extensive, and there are some core similarities between online reviews and OPN recommendations. Online service reviews can be especially useful because services may lack the quantifiable attributes that many products have, which can make evaluating a service prior to experiencing it difficult. Reviews allow for customers of a service to post their experiences with the service and their assessment of its quality. Likewise, OPN recommendations allow for an individual with firsthand experience working with a user to post their experiences and their evaluation of the recommendee’s quality as a professional. Therefore, we believe it is likely that many of the same components that make for a useful and effective service review will carry over to making for a useful and effective professional recommendation.

Another stream of work explores what kind of reviews are trusted and found to be most credible. Some postulate that one of the most important factors influencing the usefulness of a review is the credibility of its source (Ladhari and Michaud 2015). To this end, studies have investigated different ways that trust can be increased in online reviews (Kim et al. 2008; Ku et al. 2012; Racherla et al. 2012; Sherwani and Stumpf 2014). These studies have found that review valence is a significant contributor to trust in online reviews. Reviews that are too positive are trusted less than reviews that contain a mix of positive and negative information (Riasanow et al. 2015).

## Theory Development

## Scope of Improvement

Establishing trust is important because trust plays an especially critical role in the decision-making process of users interacting with technology and operating within online environments (Fang et al. 2014; McKnight et al. 2002; Pavlou and Fygenson 2006; Pavlou and Gefen 2004). Past studies have argued that when people see a promotional message, they can interpret the information provided as either an attempt to sell a particular product or an attempt to accurately describe characteristics of the specific product (Crowley and Hoyer 1994; Settle and Golden 1974). One could argue that when a recruiter reads the information contained in an OPN recommendation, they may believe the motivation behind providing the information is either an attempt to accurately represent characteristics of the recommendee, or an attempt to simply promote the recommendee by withholding information. If people believe information is being withheld, it may decrease the level of trust they have in the source of information (Cvetkovich 1999). If a recommender provides information regarding a weakness of the recommendee, readers will likely be inclined to believe that the recommender is not withholding information and is attempting to accurately describe the recommendee. More precisely, we define scope of improvement as providing information regarding a weakness of the recommendee in the form of an opportunity for improvement, as opposed to criticism of a specific behavior.

Providing scope of improvement in an OPN recommendation should increase each of the established dimensions of trust that readers (namely recruiters) have in the source of the information because they will be inclined to believe that the recommender is providing the information in an honest attempt (Crowley and Hoyer 1994; Golden and Alpert 1987)

to be objective and unbiased (Settle and Golden 1974). By providing such information, readers are likely to view the recommender as more honest (Aggarwal et al. 2012). This pursuit of honesty and objectivity should increase the perceived integrity of the recommender. Competence can be established by demonstrating the ability to make valid assertions and provide critical evaluation (Hovland et al. 1953). Evaluators that include critical statements within their evaluations are often perceived as more competent than evaluators that provide purely positive evaluations (Amabile 1983). For this reason, recommenders that supplement their recommendations with information on scope of improvement will likely come across as more competent than those who do not. Writing a recommendation takes time and effort, and it is not compulsory for a recommender to provide a recommendation. Recommenders give recommendations of their own volition, mostly because they are interested in helping others (Cascio and Aguinis 2010). Providing information regarding a specific opportunity for improvement is a signal that the recommender is engaged and interested in the recommendation writing process (Knouse 1983; Weisinger 1989). Thus, including scope of improvement can increase the perception of the recommender’s benevolence towards others. Therefore, we formally hypothesize the following:

H1a: Including scope of improvement has a positive effect on the perceived integrity of the recommender.

H1b: Including scope of improvement has a positive effect on the perceived competence of the recommender.

H1c: Including scope of improvement has a positive effect on the perceived benevolence of the recommender.

## Superlatives

Studies on traditional letters of recommendation have found that it is not uncommon for writers to inflate the abilities of those they recommend by using questionable superlatives (Smith 1998). In advertising, the practice of overinflating positive attributes about a product is known as puffery. Puffery can involve the overuse of superlatives and exaggerated descriptions of the positive attributes of a product. This excessive use of superlatives in messages has been found to lead to lower levels of source credibility and message believability (Goldberg and Hartwick 1990; Kamins and Marks 1987).

Superlatives may also be an indication of deceptive messaging. In the field of computational linguistics, much work has been done to develop techniques to automatically identify deceptive reviews (Ott et al. 2013; Ott et al. 2011).

These reviews do not contain legitimate information and are not from a trustworthy source. Classifiers use the words of the reviews as input to determine whether the text is genuine. The generated models indicate that deceptive opinions contain more superlatives (Ott et al. 2011). This is consistent with previous research that has found that deceptive writing often contains more exaggerated language (Buller and Burgoon 1996; Hancock et al. 2007). Despite the potential negative implications of superlatives, they are often used in letters of recommendation (Range et al. 1991) and can create problems for those tasked with determining which portrayals are genuine and accurate. The overuse of superlatives in letters of recommendation tarnishes the credibility of the source and can make it difficult for others to believe the claims made (Smith 1998). Therefore, we expect superlatives to negatively affect the perceived integrity of the recommender.

As discussed above, in the context of professional recommendations, the use of superlatives would suggest that either the candidate is exceptionally good or the recommender is being deceptive (Ott et al. 2011). In addition, the use of superlatives in writing has been linked to lower perceived competence of the writer (Foster 2008; Tench 2001). Ralph Waldo Emerson, in his essay, The Superlative, described the overuse of superlatives as an indication of the poverty of skill (Emerson 1899). In other words, a recommender using excessive superlatives may be perceived as someone who lacks understanding of the full spectrum of possibilities or as someone with grandiose thinking who is prone to jumping to extreme conclusions. However, when recommenders also note scope of improvement, they present a more balanced view and demonstrate they have the ability to evaluate critically both the good and the bad, which results in them being perceived as objective and unbiased (Settle and Golden 1974). For this reason, the negative effect of superlatives on perceived competence should be moderated by the presence of scope of improvement. Formally, we hypothesize the following:

H2a: Using superlatives in the absence of scope of improvement has a negative effect on the perceived integrity of the recommender.

H2b: Using superlatives in the absence of scope of improvement has a negative effect on the perceived competence of the recommender.

H2c: Using superlatives in the absence of scope of improvement has a positive effect on the perceived benevolence of the recommender.

H2d: The presence of scope of improvement moderates the effect of superlatives on the perceived competence of the recommender.

## Trust and the Likelihood of an Interview

Trust plays an important role in online communities and in any e-commerce environment. Perceptions of trust contribute to attitudes regarding making purchases and have direct effects on purchase intentions (Verhagen et al. 2006). These findings have been replicated in different contexts (Gefen 2000; Gefen and Straub 2004) and across different cultures (Jarvenpaa et al. 1999). When advertising messages are trusted, they help people make decisions and raise purchase intentions (Eisend 2006). When people trust the source of a message, it positively affects their attitudes towards the product and their intention to purchase (Goldsmith et al. 2000; Lafferty et al. 2002; Wu and Wang 2011). The conclusion drawn from these studies is that the more consumers trust, the higher their purchase intentions (Gefen and Straub 2004).

In our context, there are no physical products being marketed; instead, it is the skillset and experience of the recommendee. Similarly, the messages are not meant to appeal to consumers looking to purchase a product, but rather, recruiters looking to hire an employee. Therefore, having a purchase intention is demonstrated by the recruiter being willing to take the next step in the hiring process, which is to grant the candidate an interview.

Consequently, higher levels of trust should positively affect the likelihood of the recommendation leading to an interview. More specifically, we hypothesize that each component of trust has a positive effect on the likelihood of receiving an interview.

H3a: Perceived integrity of the recommender has a positive effect on the recruiter’s likelihood of giving an interview to the recommendee.

H3b: Perceived competence of the recommender has a positive effect on the recruiter’s likelihood of giving an interview to the recommendee.

H3c: Perceived benevolence of the recommender has a positive effect on the recruiter’s likelihood of giving an interview to the recommendee.

## Trust and the Usefulness of a Recommendation

The usefulness of any information is dependent on the trustworthiness of its source (Sussman and Siegal 2003). When information comes from a credible source, it is generally perceived as being more useful (Ko et al. 2005; Mizerski et al. 1979). If a source of information is not trusted, the recipient will likely discount the information and deem it as not useful (Eagly et al. 1978). When it comes to online review helpfulness, source credibility is a significant factor in contributing to review helpfulness (Connors et al. 2011; Li et al. 2013). In this context, helpfulness is generally defined as the information’s ability to aid in the decision-making process. If one does not trust the source of information, it is unlikely that they will allow the information to influence their behavior or regard the information as useful.

In the present context, usefulness means that the recommendation provides information and insights that can aid recruiters in making decisions about the employability of the recommendee. If a recruiter questions the trustworthiness of the recommendation’s source, then regardless of the content of the recommendation, it would likely not be useful in making hiring decisions. Therefore, higher levels of trust in the message’s source should have a positive effect on the perceived usefulness of the recommendation. Specifically, we hypothesize that each component of trust has a positive effect on the perceived usefulness of the recommendation.

H4a: Perceived integrity of the recommender has a positive effect on the usefulness of the recommendation.

H4b: Perceived competence of the recommender has a positive effect on the usefulness of the recommendation.

H4c: Perceived benevolence of the recommender has a positive effect on the usefulness of the recommendation.

## Moderating Effect of Experience

The higher the level of investment a specific action requires, the more hesitant people are to pursue that action when there are known drawbacks of doing so (Chebat and Picard 1985; Lang et al. 1999). When choosing candidates to fill positions requiring prior experience in specific levels within an organizational structure, it is reasonable to expect that the varying levels of experience needed would be associated with different pay scales. From the perspective of the recruiter, this represents varying levels of financial commitment associated with pursuing candidates for different jobs. When scope of improvement is included in recommendations for candidates with experience at different levels, the overall effect of the information on interview propensity may be different depending on their prior experience. This does not mean, however, that the negative information is not useful. On the contrary, people typically find negative information to be more useful than positive information (Ahluwalia and Shiv 1997; Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006; Skowronski and Carlston 1987).

Recruiters may be put off by negative information about high experienced candidates (those with experience at senior-level positions) because bad hires at top-level positions at a company can cause more damage than lower-level positions and represent a significantly larger financial commitment (Cowherd and Levine 1992). Not only will they require a higher salary, but also their potential organizational impact will likely be much higher (Denis and Denis 1995). Shortcomings that may be tolerated, even expected, at one level of experience may not be at another. When recruiters are dealing with job candidates with management experience, they may expect that weaknesses have been overcome and be less forgiving of shortcomings than they would be for candidates that lack this experience. When recruiters come across weaknesses of candidates that lack high levels of experience, they may understand that these candidates are still learning and have not yet had extensive experience as their teacher. Therefore, we expect the effect of scope of improvement on the likelihood of an interview to be different for candidates that have high levels of experience than for candidates who do not. Formally, we hypothesize the following:

H5a: Scope of improvement has a direct negative effect on the likelihood of interviews for candidates with high levels of experience.

H5b: Scope of improvement does not have a direct negative effect on the likelihood of interviews for candidates who lack high levels of experience.

## Methodology

Since our model posits that the presence (or absence) of superlatives and scope of improvement influences decision makers’ evaluations of a candidate’s recommendations, we designed a scenario-based experiment that manipulated the presence (or absence) of superlatives and scope of improvement in the recommendations within the scenarios.

## Experimental Design

Twelve different scenarios were developed with possible combinations of presence (or absence) of superlatives and scope of improvement in recommendations for candidates with experience at three different levels within a company (3 experience levels x 2 scope of improvement x 2 superlatives). Each scenario contained a brief background of the candidate, including current job and past work experience, and one open recommendation. Depending on the experimental condition, the open recommendation presented was from possible combinations of presence (or absence) of superlatives with presence (or absence) of scope of improvement. The three different jobs represented candidates with experience at three different levels: entry level, middle management, and senior management. Each scenario was followed by a short questionnaire (see Appendix A).

To collect the data, 407 industry professionals, who were directly or indirectly involved in recruitment processes at their place of work, were approached for participating in this study. Each subject was provided with a set of three scenarios, one from each level of experience. That is, one scenario each from entry-level (E), middle-level (M), or senior-level (S) categories as seen in Table 1 below. Each completed scenario was used in the data analysis (for example, if a subject only completed one of the three, then we used only the one completed scenario in our analysis).

The survey followed complete randomization, starting with the order in which the three scenarios—entry, middle, and senior experience levels—were shown. For each scenario, whether or not superlatives were present was randomized, and whether scope of improvement was present was randomized as well. Since the assignment of subjects to treatments was completely random, we did not expect any significant difference in the profile of participants across treatments. The number of participants in each treatment is reported in Table 2 below. We statistically compared the profiles of participants across treatments and did not find any significant differences across age (p-value: 0.88), gender (p-value: 0.30), education (p-value: 0.81), or experience (p-value: 0.93).

We also statistically compared gender (p-value: 0.60), level of education (p-value: 0.87), and experience of the subjects (pvalue: 0.59) who took the survey (218) and those who did not (32). We did not find any statistically significant differences between the two groups. Plus, the order of scenarios, whether to show scope, and whether to show superlatives were also random. All this suggests that selection bias is not a problem in this analysis.

## Base Case Recommendations

Using these profiles as a guide, we developed a total of nine recommendations, three for each level of experience. These recommendations were designed with low superlatives and without any scope of improvement. The recommendations were then shown to 44 graduate students who were asked to score the usefulness of the recommendations and the likelihood of interviewing the candidate with these recommendations. The scoring was done on a 7-point Likert scale using the items shown in the instrument provided in Appendix A. From these, three base recommendations, one in each category, whose scores on both dimensions were statistically similar to each other, were selected. These three recommendations served as the base case recommendations for the three levels of experience (i.e., low superlative and no scope of improvement for each level of experience).

## Experimental Manipulation Case Recommendations

To design the recommendations with high superlatives, the three selected recommendations were infused with appropriate and similar superlatives. The overall count of superlatives was kept comparable in all the recommendations. The scope of improvement sections of the recommendations were prepared after interviewing 11 experienced hiring managers and recruiters. The recommendations that were prepared, as well as the recommendations that were initially extracted from LinkedIn profiles, were shown to the 11 hiring managers and recruiters. The focus of these interviews was to identify dimensions for scope of improvement that they typically see or would like to see in candidates’ recommendations.

We then ranked the dimensions that emerged during these interviews according to the number of times they were mentioned in the interviews. We used the top two dimensions in developing the scope of improvement content for the recommendations. We prepared two different variations of the scope of improvement and appended one of the two variations to the appropriate recommendations to prepare recommendations with scope of improvement.

We confirmed the recommendations’ design using a cardsorting test in which six individuals grouped the list of recommendations into four predefined categories. The sorting resulted in a satisfactory classification of the items into four different dimensions—2 superlatives x 2 scope of improvement.

## Measures

Independent variables: The key independent variables for the present study are the presence (or absence) of superlatives in the recommendation, presence (or absence) of scope of improvement in the recommendation, and experience level of the job. The presence of superlatives in the recommendation was operationalized by a dummy variable Superlative. It takes the value of 1 when superlatives were present in the recommendation and takes the value of 0 otherwise. Similarly, the presence of scope of improvement in the recommendation was operationalized by a dummy variable Scope, which takes the value of 1 when scope of improvement was mentioned in the recommendation and takes the value of 0 otherwise. The three job levels were recorded using a combination of two dummy variables, Mid-Exp and Hi-Exp. A value of (0, 0) for (Mid-Exp, Hi-Exp) represents experience in an entry-level job; (1, 0) for (Mid-Exp, Hi-Exp) represents experience in a middle-management job; and (0, 1) for (Mid-Exp, Hi-Exp) represents experience in a senior-level management job. These position level dummies in the model captured position-specific fixed-effects including the difference in the level of trust in recommenders who left recommendations for recommendees at various positions.

Dependent variables: The key dependent variables are the likelihood of interviewing the candidate (Interview) and the usefulness of the recommendation (Usefulness). The likelihood of interviewing and the usefulness of the recommendation were measured on a 7-point Likert scale of Strongly Disagree to Strongly Agree.

Mediating and control variables: The items for measuring the mediating variables (perceived integrity, competence, and benevolence) were adapted from preexisting items and were measured on a 7-point Likert scale of Strongly Disagree to Strongly Agree. The gender, age, years of education, and years of experience of each participant were recorded and controlled for in each analysis.

## Test of Constructs

The appropriateness of using factor analysis was determined by using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy and Bartlett’s test of sphericity. For the collected data, the KMO is 0.80, and the chi-square statistic is 3785.11 (p < 0.000), thus indicating that factor analysis is appropriate (Hair et al. 1998). The next step in factor analysis is to perform principal components analysis (PCA). PCA examines the factor loading of each indicator, extracting items that do not significantly load on the construct of interest. An item is extracted if it does not load above 0.60 on its own construct or above 0.40 on another construct. After determining the appropriate number of factors, the final factor matrix was rotated using Varimax rotation. As seen in Table 3, all of the factor loadings are greater than 0.6, the accepted standard.

Reliability analysis used Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability to assess internal consistency (Chin 1998). Composite reliability (similar to Cronbach’s alpha) of 0.70 or higher is considered adequate (Fornell and Larcker 1981) and is considered more robust than Cronbach’s alpha because it weighs items differently depending on factor loading considerations (Agarwal and Karahanna 2000). Looking at Table 2, both composite reliability and Cronbach’s alpha are greater than 0.70, indicating high reliability and stability for the measurement items of each construct.

Convergent validity used the three standards recommended by Bagozzi and Yi (1988) to assess the measuring model: (1) all indicator factor loadings should exceed 0.5 (Hair et al. 1998); (2) CREL should be above 0.7; and (3) the average variance extracted, AVE, of every construct should exceed 0.5 (Fornell and Larcker 1981). As Table 3 shows, the indicator factor loading of every item in the measuring model of this study exceeded 0.7. Composite reliability of constructs ranged from 0.868 to 0.922. AVE ranged from 0.621 to 0.799, therefore meeting all conditions for convergent validity.

In discriminant validity, as Fornell and Larcker (1981) suggested, the AVE of the construct should exceed other correlation coefficients of the construct. Table 3 shows the matrix of correlation coefficients for all constructs in this research. Diagonal elements are the square roots of average variance extracted for the constructs. The correlation coefficients between any two constructs are smaller than the square root of the average variance extracted for the constructs. Constructs in the measurement model of this research indeed are different from one another, indicating that all constructs in this research carry sufficient discriminant validity. Therefore, the measurement model in this research shows satisfactory reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity. As seen in Table 4, offdiagonal elements are the correlations among constructs and diagonal elements are larger than off-diagonal elements demonstrating discriminant validity.

## Hypothesis 1 Result: Scope of Improvement on Trust

Hypothesis 1 deals with the relationships between scope of improvement and the three components of trust: integrity (1a), competence (1b), and benevolence (1c). We hypothesized that including scope of improvement in the recommendation would have a positive effect on each element of trust. The regression results, as seen in Table 6 fully support Hypothesis 1a as the coefficient of scope of improvement and perceived integrity is positive and significant $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . The effect of scope of improvement on perceived competence, as seen in Table 6, is not significant. This means that Hypothesis 1b is not supported. However, it is noted that the effect of scope of improvement on perceived competence is positively moderated by superlatives and is significant $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . This can be seen by the fact that the coefficient for scope of improvement is not significant, the coefficient for superlatives is negative and significant $( p \ < \ 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , and the coefficient for the interaction of scope of improvement and superlatives is positive and significant $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . Therefore, scope of improvement has a significant positive effect on perceived competence when superlatives are present, and a nonsignificant effect when superlatives are not present. Finally, as seen in Table 6, the effect of scope of improvement on perceived benevolence is positive and significant $( p = 0 . 0 2 4 )$ , supporting Hypothesis 1c.

## Hypothesis 2 Result: Superlatives on Trust

Hypothesis 2 looks at the relationship between superlatives and each component of trust: integrity (2a), competence (2b), and benevolence (2c). The results, as seen in Table 6, indicate that the relationship between superlatives and perceived integrity is not significant. Hence, Hypothesis 2a is not supported. The effect of superlatives on perceived competence is negative and significant $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ providing support for Hypothesis 2b. The support for Hypothesis 2a and the lack of support for Hypothesis 2b suggests that recruiters are more prone to attribute possible exaggeration not as an intent to deceive but rather to the recommender’s inability to accurately assess and communicate reality. It is the recommender’s perceived competence that is negatively affected by superlatives, not their perceived integrity.

## Mediation

The effect of scope of improvement on usefulness is mediated by the dimensions of trust, particularly integrity. This mediation is established (Baron and Kenny 1986) by the following observed results: (1) Scope of improvement significantly affects perceived integrity (as seen in Table 6). (2) Perceived integrity significantly affects the usefulness of the recommendation (as seen in Table 7). (3) Scope of improvement significantly affects the usefulness of the recommendation when perceived integrity is not included in the model (as seen in Table 8). (4) When perceived integrity is included in the model, the effect of scope of improvement on usefulness is no longer significant (as seen in Table 8). Similar results are seen with the effect of scope of improvement on the likelihood of an interview. As discussed, scope of improvement significantly affects perceived integrity (Table 6), perceived integrity significantly affects the likelihood of an interview (Table 7), and the significant effect of scope of improvement on the likelihood of an interview becomes nonsignificant when perceived integrity is added to the model (Table 8). Overall, it seems there is a tradeoff associated with having scope of improvement in a recommendation. Scope of improvement increases the credibility of the recommendation, which in turn increases the chance of getting an interview; hence, this indirect effect of scope of improvement benefits the candidate.

## Robustness Check

We used two different (A and B) wordings for scope of improvement. This not only allowed us to avoid using the same wording for scope of improvement in two scenarios within the same packet, but also allowed us to test the robustness of our methods. Out of the 308 records with scope of improvement, 161 used Version A and the remaining 147 used Version B. We ran two separate analyses, (1) by excluding Version A (sample size 300+147), and (2) by excluding Version B (sample size 300+161). Results from both the analyses were qualitatively similar to our main results.

Within the design of the study, each participant could potentially provide up to three different data points. This presents a potential opportunity for unobserved individual heterogeneity to affect our analysis. To control for this effect, we ran a fixed-effect model that controlled for the fixed effects of each participant. The results were quantitatively similar to our initial analysis. Likewise, because participants provided up to three different data points, each corresponding to a different treatment condition, we first took preliminary precautions to prevent sequencing or priming effects by randomizing the order in which participants received the scenarios. Afterwards, we performed additional analysis to ensure the randomization was effective in eliminating these effects. Additional regression analysis using dummy variables to indicate whether the first recommendations shown to each individual participant contained superlatives (coded as 1 if superlatives are present and 0 otherwise), and whether the first recommendation shown contained scope of improvement (coded as 1 if scope of improvement was present and 0 otherwise). Appropriate interaction variables were also included in the analysis. The results consistently showed that these dummy variables are not significant, providing further confirmation that no sequencing or priming effects were present in our analysis.

We also tested for further relationships, such as whether experience also moderates the effect of superlatives, using interaction variables between experience levels and superlatives. Results showed that these interaction variables are not significant, indicating that experience does not moderate the effect of superlatives in the same way that it moderates the scope of improvement effect. Likewise, we tested for the direct effect of usefulness on the likelihood of an interview. Its effects proved to be insignificant, which makes sense, considering that a recommendation can be useful when it supports candidacy and also when it hurts candidacy.

## Traditional versus OPN Recommendations

The focus of this study is to theoretically propose and empirically test the underlying process of how scope of improvement and superlatives affect the effectiveness and usefulness of recommendations appearing on OPNs (referred to as OPN recommendations). However, the question remains of how OPN recommendations differ from traditional recommendations. Traditional recommendations are those that are written to be provided directly to the recipient, regardless of the actual delivery mechanism. To gain further perspective on contextual factors, we discussed the differences between online and traditional recommendations with a few recruiters and found that a primary distinction they make when dealing with traditional letters of recommendation they receive and those that appear on OPNs, is that in the OPN setting, the candidate has the discretion and control to stop the recommendation from showing up on their profile. However, with traditional recommendations, candidates send a request to recommenders to write recommendations and recommenders can then submit the recommendation to recruiters directly. A candidate does not have the same level of control to block a recommendation in traditional channels, compared to an online recommendation displayed on an OPN. This leads to the perception that recommenders are more likely to withhold information when writing OPN recommendations. Therefore, recruiters generally place lower trust in OPN recommendations, as compared to traditional recommendations. However, if recruiters see scope of improvement in an OPN recommendation, then it may restore their trust in the recommendation. Whereas in a traditional recommendation, where it is not as unusual for recruiters to see scope of improvement, seeing this information may not increase the trust as much. Therefore, scope of improvement would be expected to have a stronger effect on trust in OPN recommendations than in traditional recommendations.

To capture this effect, we conducted an additional study. In this additional study, we used two levels of experience: entry-level and senior-level. We still manipulated (not) including scope of improvement, and (not) including superlatives, and studied their effect on the three dimensions of trust and interview propensity. We also manipulated how the recommendation would be provided to the recruiter: specifically, online versus traditional. For the online scenario, we showed the recommendation on a candidate’s online profile. For the traditional scenario, we provided the recommendation separately to a candidate’s resume and explicitly noted that this was submitted by the recommender directly without the candidate’s review. We utilized standards of randomization that were similar to the first study.

The results of this study can be seen in Table 9. How the recommendation was presented was captured by the dummy variable Traditional, which took a value of 1 if presented as a traditional recommendation and a value of 0 otherwise. The other variables presented are the same as in the first study. Results showed that recommendations presented online suffer from lower levels of perceived recommender integrity, indicated by the positive and significant value of Traditional in both instances (for both entry- and senior-level jobs). Precisely speaking, perceived integrity in the case of OPN recommendations is 0.34 less than in the case of traditional recommendations for an entry-level position. For senior positions, OPN recommenders receive a much larger penalty to perceived integrity than their traditional counterpart— specifically, a penalty of 0.83. Nevertheless, when scope of improvement is added, online recommenders receive a significant bump in perceived integrity. This can be seen by looking at the interaction variable Traditional\*Scope, which has a value of 1 if the recommendation is presented as a traditional recommendation with scope of improvement present, and a value of 0 otherwise. The significant negative value of this variable in both instances suggests that the positive net effect of adding scope of improvement is larger for OPN recommendations than for traditional recommendations. Therefore, adding scope of improvement in OPN recommendations is an effective way to make up for the loss of perceived integrity in comparison to traditional recommendations. Analysis also showed that the perceived competence and benevolence of recommendations presented online versus traditionally were quantitatively similar to one another with no significant differences.

## Controlling for Respondents’ Experience in Recruiting at a Given Experience Level

In this additional study, a question was also added to explicitly capture whether participants had experience making hiring decisions for candidates at a given experience level. With this information, an analysis was performed to compare the results of the full sample with the results of the subsample that had direct experience with a given scenario. As seen in Table 10, the analysis shows that the results for the full sample and the subsample are quantitatively similar with no significant differences between the two groups.

In this study, we also provided a second operationalization of the benevolence construct. In the first study, benevolence was measured from the perspective of the recommender being benevolent towards the recommendee. That is, with the perception that the recommender was acting in the best interest of the recommendee. However, it is possible that participants in the study may have been less concerned with the recommender’s benevolence towards the recommendee than they were with the recommender’s benevolence towards themselves (the recruiter). From their perspective, the recommender may only appear as benevolent if they have the recruiter’s best interest in mind. Therefore, we added this second operationalization of the construct to the study to determine if there is a significant difference in the effects of benevolence on the likelihood of an interview when operationalized differently. The results provided in Table 10 indicate that the two different versions of benevolence are both insignificant and are quantitatively similar to one another.

## Conclusion and Discussion

At first blush, many people may presume that exposing someone’s scope of improvement would hurt their chances of getting an interview. However, our study indicates that this is not necessarily true. One of the key findings is that writing about scope of improvement in OPN recommendations is not always a bad thing. Under certain conditions, it can actually help candidates significantly. Using the theoretical framework of online trust, we argued for the conditions under which including scope of improvement in OPN recommendations can benefit recommendees. We not only tested the conditions under which recommenders will want to write about scope of improvement but also tested the underlying theoretical process. We theoretically evaluated our hypotheses. Findings show that including scope of improvement has a positive effect on all three dimensions of perceived trust of the recommender. We found that superlatives, when present in conjunction with scope of improvement, positively affect the perceived competence of the recommender; however, superlatives, when used without scope of improvement, adversely influence the perceived competence of the recommender. The lack of support for the hypothesis that superlatives (without the presence of scope of improvement) would negatively impact the perceived integrity of the recommender may suggest that if a recruiter suspects that a recommendation is inflated, they may not attribute this to the recommender’s lack of integrity but rather to the recommender’s lack of competence. If accurate, this is consistent with Hanlon’s razor, the adage that one should not attribute to malice that which can be adequately explained by incompetence (Raymond and Steele 1996). Perceived integrity and competence of the recommender have a positive effect on the likelihood of a recruiter being willing to interview the recommendee as well as the usefulness of the recommendation. Lastly, results show that the experience level of the recommendee moderates the effect of scope of improvement on the likelihood of an interview, with more experienced recommendees being hurt severely while less experienced recommendees are not.

We contribute to the growing literature on online trust and show how online trust plays an important role in the context of online recommendations published on professional networks that allow users to hide recommendations if they choose to. While there does not seem to be any indication that OPNs will remove the ability of users to hide recommendations, should this occur, our findings may not extend to those situations. A second study confirmed that recommendations presented online suffer from lower levels of perceived recommender integrity than recommendations presented traditionally. Many will agree that online recommendations on professional networks are becoming increasingly more important in recruitment decisions. Our results, which show that there is a significant difference in how recruiters receive these recommendations vis-à-vis traditional recommendations, demonstrates the need to further investigate this domain.

The proposed model (Figure 1) draws upon established definitions of trust (Mayer et al. 1995) as a three-part construct and proposes that dimensions of trust act as a mediator between the independent variables of scope of improvement and superlatives and the dependent variables of the likelihood of an interview and usefulness.

Hypothesis 5 anticipated the moderating role of experience, which was confirmed by testing. As shown in the findings for Hypothesis 2b, using superlatives has a significantly negative effect on the perceived competence of the recommender. However, adding scope of improvement can reduce this effect, as indicated by the Hypothesis 2d testing results. As seen in Table 6, and illustrated in Figure 2, the presence of scope of improvement and superlatives has a significantly positive effect on perceived competence.

The use of OPNs such as LinkedIn, Xing, Viadeo, and others will continue to be used by hiring managers, recruiters, and job candidates for the foreseeable future in the job search process (Jobvite 2016). As such, it is essential for users to understand how to maximize the impact of such tools and improve performance in active or passive job search activities. Using the professional recommendation features of these sites may provide the edge a user needs to take the next step in the process. Both job candidates, as well as those who have been asked to write professional recommendations for others, can use the findings presented to improve their online image.

Because OPNs allow users to choose which recommendations they display about themselves, it may be tempting for recommenders to refrain from including information that may reflect weakness or areas of improvement. Nevertheless, these findings illustrate that this may be a mistake. Given proper circumstances (i.e., entrylevel positions), including such information to be shared can actually increase the likelihood of a recruiter being willing to interview the candidate. However, these findings also offer a word of caution for those in senior management positions: what works for less experienced positions might not work here. Including scope of improvement has a negative direct effect at the senior management level and thus should be avoided. While it may be reasonable to believe this occurs because recruiters are especially cautious with senior-level hires or perhaps they simply expect those with more experience to have fewer weaknesses, determining the exact reason for the penalty would be a potential extension of this work. Regardless, addressing weaknesses in a professional recommendation for senior management positions may be the kiss of death to any hope of receiving an interview.

A second and perhaps less intuitive application of these findings is the effect the recommendations may have on the perception of the recommender. Some propose that a letter of recommendation can say as much about the writer as it does about the subject of the letter (Knouse 1983). In our case, we measured the perceived integrity, competence, and benevolence of the writer, not the subject. As hiring managers and recruiters read professional recommendations, they not only form opinions and make judgments about the recommendee, but also about the recommender. This is especially impactful because information about the recommender is often easily accessible on the OPN. Hence, recommenders should be careful in how they present themselves in writing recommendations for others. Unlike traditional letters of recommendation, recommendations given on an OPN are readily available to the entire network and it is easy to access all of the recommendations a given user has written. Our findings warn that using excessive superlatives can lower the perceived competence of the writer, so they should be used with caution. If superlatives are warranted, their negative effects can be reduced by including scope of improvement.

There are a number of avenues for future research that could address the limitations of this study or provide natural extensions to it. This study used two different variations of scope of improvement that were developed based on our interviews with hiring managers. It is reasonable to expect that there are some weaknesses that, if mentioned, would cause more harm to the recommendee than good. Likewise, there may be other factors beyond experience that moderate or provide boundary conditions to the effects observed in this study. Manipulating the kind of weakness mentioned, how skillfully the recommender communicates the weakness, as well as the overall writing style of the recommender would all be worthwhile extensions of this work. Also, as the gender and race of the applicants and recommenders were not manipulated, it would be a worthwhile pursuit to study how manipulating these variables might affect our findings.

Past research has found that there may be significant differences in perceptions of different races and genders in various aspects of professional interactions (Adams and Funk 2012; Chan and Wang 2017; Igbaria and Wormley 1992; Pondorfer et al. 2016). Future research could also focus on the effects of racial and gender stereotypes in the reception and interpretation of OPN recommendations.

Furthermore, many of the studies that have been done on service reviews could be replicated with appropriate modifications using professional recommendations. However, a good portion of studies on online service reviews rely on voting functionality (i.e., was this review helpful?) to operationalize review quality or helpfulness. Since this functionality is not yet used by the sites that publish OPN recommendations, appropriately operationalizing quality and helpfulness will be much more labor intensive, as it was in this study.

Another potential for future research is the manipulation of information in opinion forums (Dellarocas 2006), which could be applied directly to professional recommendations. Poorly worded or intentionally deceptive recommendations that project doubt regarding the integrity of the writer could have an impact on the organizations and firms represented, not just the individuals. We believe this study can introduce OPN recommendations as a rich source of data for a number of intriguing and pertinent research questions in the future.
