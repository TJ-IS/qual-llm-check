# ENDING THE MENDING WALL: HERDING, MEDIA COVERAGE, AND COLOCATION IN IT ENTREPRENEURSHIP<sup>1</sup>

Brad N. Greenwood Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, MN 55455 U.S.A. {wood@umn.edu}

Anandasivam Gopal Robert H. Smith School of Business, University of Maryland, College Park, MD 20742 U.S.A. {agopal@rhsmith.umd.edu}

The advent of the Internet and the maturation of the information technology (IT) industry has significantly decreased transaction and coordination costs. However, there is still a strong preference for being geographically proximal, notably when accessing capital markets for innovation activities, such as the formation of funding ties between venture capitalists (VCs) and new IT ventures. While VCs prefer to support colocated entrepreneurs, significant evidence suggests that the funding of nonlocal IT entrepreneurs does occur. In this work, we investigate how trends in the form of herding and media coverage, which are not specific to the entrepreneur but pertain to the technology sector in which the entrepreneur operates, may influence VCs to reach across geographic boundaries. Using a series of matched sample methodologies, results suggest that the influence of media is significantly stronger for VCs considering funding non-colocated, as opposed to colocated, IT entrepreneurs. However, and strikingly, support for the effect of herding is notably weaker. Managerial and policy implications, particularly for technology entrepreneurs and policy makers seeking to support such innovators, are discussed within.

Keywords: Venture capital, entrepreneurship, herding, media coverage, coarsened exact matching, colocation, information technology

## Introduction

It is well known that information technology (IT) entrepreneurship and the activities of venture capitalists (VCs) are deeply intertwined. Gompers and Lerner (2001), for example, estimate that more than 70% of venture capital disbursements in the United States during the 1980s and 1990s went to IT startups. Unsurprisingly, these investments were heavily concentrated in hotbeds of entrepreneurial activity such as Silicon Valley, Route 128, and New York City. However, as the IT industry has matured, transaction and coordination costs have fallen, thereby allowing IT entrepreneurs to locate their firms outside of the traditional regions (Feldman 2001).<sup>2</sup> And, as these new ventures grow, they still require early-stage funding, which has been historically provided by the very VCs who tend to operate in entrepreneurial hotbeds. Thus, IT entrepreneurs face a critical dilemma: procuring early VC funding from non-colocated VCs. As long as venture capital remains important to IT entrepreneurship, understanding when VCs may be inclined to fund remote IT entrepreneurs is an essential question. More formally, under what conditions are VCs willing to provide early-stage funding to an IT entrepreneur who is not colocated with them?

Within the broader literature that addresses entrepreneurship and geography, the central question of location has been examined through many lenses (Bresnahan et al. 2001; Klepper 2007; Porter 2000; Sorenson and Stuart 2001), a consistent finding being that new ventures are more likely to receive venture capital funding from VCs with whom they are colocated (Gompers and Lerner 1999; Sorenson and Stuart 2001). This strong colocation preference, which we call the colocation constraint, arises from the significant benefits that accrue to both parties. For the VC, colocation allows access to superior information about the entrepreneur through overlapping social networks ex ante (Sorenson and Stuart 2001), as well as easier monitoring ex post (Gorman and Sahlman 1989). For the entrepreneur, colocation provides access to the VC’s network of capabilities, leading to superior outcomes (Gorman and Sahlman 1989) and additional financing (Gompers and Lerner 1999).

In this work, we begin with the observation that, in spite of the benefits of colocation, many funding relationships between VCs and entrepreneurs are not colocated. Indeed, Chen et al. (2010) show, in a remarkably counterintuitive result, nonlocal VC investments tend to provide higher returns than local investments, all else equal. Thus, it is not inconceivable that non-colocated funding ties can be attractive under certain circumstances. Sorenson and Stuart (2001) provide one context when non-colocated funding may occur: colocation by members of the VC’s investment syndicate. They find that colocation by one member of the syndicate allows access to reliable information about the entrepreneur at reduced cost. Effectively, the colocated syndicate member acts as a pipe (Podolny 2001), carrying relevant information to other members of the funding syndicate about the entrepreneur. However, this still requires at least one VC to be colocated with the IT entrepreneur. What if this were not true, since VCs tend to operate in concentrated clusters? Would relocation then be the only option for the entrepreneur?

We argue in this paper that, beyond specific information about the entrepreneur or the VC, there are likely to be broader environmental influences that induce VCs to accept, or even seek out, non-colocated entrepreneurs. These environmental factors are likely to be of particular interest in the context of IT ventures because the IT industry is characterized by rapid clockspeeds (Mendelson and Pillai 1998), quick development cycles (McAfee and Brynjolfsson 2008), and markets which mature and fall away quickly (Baskerville and Myers 2009). In such environments, where a significant role is played by VCs, we hypothesize that decision makers may respond to environmental trends as well as the actions of their peers (Lerner 2002, Petkova et al. 2013). In contrast, fields such as biotech and pharmacology (which constitute a much smaller proportion of VC funding) are characterized by strong linkages to local economies that preclude entrepreneurial relocation, while also requiring large capital investments, collaboration across multiple partners even in the early-stage, and dependence on basic science facilities (Powell et al. 2002). As a result, environmental changes will be slow to occur and variation in the demand for VC funding will be minimal (Baum and Silverman 2004, Gompers and Lerner 1999). We therefore examine two sources of influence that have not yet been studied in the context of the location preferences: media discourse and herding based on peer VC funding decisions.

The crux of our arguments for why these two factors may affect VC decision making relies on the observation that VC outcomes are highly uncertain (Gompers and Lerner 1999, 2001). In such contexts, the presence of media coverage and the observed patterns of funding by peer VCs are likely to introduce significant bias into the VCs decision making process through the pathways of legitimacy and salience. In effect, VCs are likely to allow “optimism … [to] overcome prudence” in the presence of such environmental trends, as stated succinctly by Sorenson and Stuart (2008, p. 271). Herding, as an instance of observational learning, refers to situations where individuals “follow the crowd” by ignoring private information when the behavior of others has been revealed (Banerjee 1992; Cai et al. 2009). We argue that VC funding decisions, made in the face of considerable uncertainty, are influenced by previous funding decisions made by peer VCs. These decisions are observable, salient, and have been identified as influential in previous work (Lerner 2002). Therefore, when there is significant peer investment in technology sectors, and these are observed by the focal VC, there is a higher probability that she will be willing to relax the colocation constraint. In a related vein, increased media attention brings legitimacy to tech sectors while also making them highly salient (Sunstein 2002). The effect of this salience and legitimacy is to make investments in new IT ventures within these technology sectors more attractive, while also leading to systematic biases in the assessments of costs associated with such investments (Kuran and Sunstein 1999). As a result, VCs will be more willing to fund noncolocated IT entrepreneurs operating in the discussed technology sector.

We conduct our empirical analysis using data from several sources on funding of new IT ventures. Data on colocation and VC funding decisions is drawn from the VentureXpert dataset, which provides detailed information on VC deals and funded entrepreneurs in the United States over the past 30 years (Sorenson and Stuart 2001). We use, as our base dataset, IT related VC-entrepreneur investment dyads in the United States between 1985 and 2006. Additional data from VentureXpert also allows us to measure the herding behavior of peer VCs. This dataset is then augmented with information from 11 national newspapers in the United States by assessing the frequency of media reports on the technological sector of the entrepreneur in the years preceding the funding decision. Our analysis is then conducted using three matching methods frequently used in extant literature: the naïve matching methodology described by Sorenson and Stuart (2001), a coarsened exact match (Blackwell et al. 2009), and a propensity score match. Results indicate consistency with extant work showing the strong influence of colocation (Sorenson and Stuart 2001). Further, results indicate that while media consistently moderates the effect of colocation (i.e., the benefits of media accrue more quickly to non-colocated entrepreneurs than to colocated entrepreneurs), the effect of herding is equivocal and dependent upon the matching method used, indicating ambiguity in the extent of the effect.

Our work adds to existing literature in three specific ways. First, it has been argued that, in the context of knowledge or technological work, the constraints imposed by location are becoming superfluous (Mithas and Whitaker 2007; Sohn 2004). The ability to reduce transaction costs, as well as the enhanced capabilities for coordination and collaboration, allow IT entrepreneurs to utilize their locational advantages in setting up their firm while accessing customers remotely (Dahl and Sorenson 2012). However, firm growth necessitates funding, typically from VCs who still hold colocation as critical. This raises an important question for IT entrepreneurs: Is relocation to a VC hub, or even to a location close to a preferred VC, essential? We augment prior work that has focused exclusively on attributes specific to the entrepreneur or the VC (Sorenson and Stuart 2001, 2008) by showing that environmental influences, such as from media discourse, can allow non-colocated funding ties to occur, thereby extending the analysis of funding decisions to environmental constructs outside the direct influence of either the VC or the entrepreneur.

Second, even within the domain of IT-enabled innovation, the issue of location has been addressed in depth, notably in work that examines how entrepreneurs access capital markets and how individuals choose to invest in projects located across the world (Agrawal et al. 2013). In recent work in crowdfunding, for example, both Kim and Hann (2015) and Burtch et al. (2014) study how location affects project success, the former finding that projects in areas with less local potential are more likely to acquire funding, and the latter finding that geographic and cultural distance does stymie the acquisition of funds. Our work adds to this growing literature examining the ramifications of location in technology-related entrepreneurial activity.

Finally, we contribute to the literature studying the effects of media discourse on decision-making, albeit with a focus on venture capital. Prior work in strategy has addressed these questions in the context of IPO markets (Pollock et al. 2008), the choice of analysts on which firms to cover (Rao et al. 2001), and fashionability of IT systems (Baskerville and Myers 2009). Even within the IS domain, Aggarwal et al. (2012) study how discourse on blogs (as an alternative form of media) may influence the funding of new ventures. While the bulk of this work is predicated on media discourse around the focal firm, there is little treatment of how more generalized media discourse about the technology sector may matter. Our work addresses this gap in the literature through our theorizing of the effects of media. Further, our findings question current wisdom regarding the apparent effect of herding on VC decision making. While herding has been long identified with investors in general, and with VCs in particular, our results indicate that the influence of herding on expanding the geographical distribution of VC funding ties appears limited.

## Theory and Hypotheses

## VC Funding Decisions and Colocation with New IT Ventures

VCs face considerable uncertainty when evaluating new ventures for early funding (Gompers and Lerner 1999, 2001). Early-stage technology firms, almost by definition, possess many untested and untried technologies, representing a significant risk for investing firms (Gorman and Sahlman 1989). Thus, the VC faces a pressing adverse selection problem, since identifying the true quality of an entrepreneur ex ante is difficult. Beyond the decision to fund, VCs must also consider the costs of maintaining the relationship with the entrepreneur through exit (e.g., acquisition or IPO). These post-funding costs can include bringing in advisors, potential customers, experienced managers, and financial underwriters (in the event of an IPO), as well as by providing valuable advice and access to their social networks (Gompers and Lerner 1999; Gorman and Sahlman 1989). Effectively, VCs considering first-round funding for an entrepreneur encounter a significant judgment task, where ex ante entrepreneur quality as well as ex post costs of relationship management and exit are difficult to evaluate (Gorman and Sahlman 1989).

Funding colocated entrepreneurs has emerged as one partial solution to these problems, because it allows the VC to manage both the adverse selection as well as moral hazard problems. Regarding adverse selection, colocation allows the VC to observe the entrepreneur more easily and tacit information about her is more easily gathered through intersecting social networks (e.g., customers, employees, or stake-holders; Sorenson and Stuart 2001). Further, the costs of gathering this information is lower (Chen et al. 2010) and the information is typically more trustworthy (Stuart et al. 1999). Postfunding, it is easier for the VC to deploy assets, such as managers or financial specialists, when assisting the entrepreneur develop her venture toward eventual exit (Gompers and Lerner 1999; Sorenson and Stuart 2001). Thus, from a preand post-funding perspective, it is not surprising that VCs prefer to fund entrepreneurs who are colocated.

When viewed from the entrepreneur’s perspective, location represents a significant decision with serious implications for future success. Prior research has implicated location as a key factors that affects the survival of new firms (Dahl and Sorenson 2012) and has argued for why entrepreneurs should consider location-specific resource munificence when starting the venture (Dubini 1989; Lomi 1995), in addition to local policy and regulation. Consider, for instance, Fairfax County,<sup>3</sup> an affluent suburb of Washington DC. The Fairfax County Economic Development Board explicitly incentivizes new firms to relocate to Fairfax in order to gain the benefits of favorable policies regarding access to equity, real estate, customer organizations, and human resources. Relevant to our work here, one of the key resources that locations offer is, of course, access to VCs, as it is well-accepted that VCs prefer to support colocated firms.

The preference for colocation notwithstanding, empirical evidence indicates a non-trivial proportion of funding ties manifest when the entrepreneur is not colocated (Sorenson and Stuart 2001). What might drive these non-colocated funding decisions when the advantage of colocation is so unambiguous? Clearly, one candidate factor is entrepreneur quality. It is possible that a specific non-colocated entrepreneur provides outstanding value for the VC (Chen et al. 2010). However, prior research suggests that establishing such compelling value in an early-stage venture is difficult, given the uncertainty inherent in early-stage technology firms (Gompers and Lerner 2001; Kaplan and Strömberg 2003). Indeed, The Wall Street Journal reported in 2012 that 75% of VC investments do not provide positive ROI, despite of VC due diligence (Gage 2012).

We identify two such factors in this paper: media coverage from news outlets and herding in the form of peer VC investments. We argue that these factors may influence the focal

VC to fund non-colocated entrepreneurs. To the extent that media attention enhances the appeal of certain events, firms, or technologies (Pollock and Rindova 2003), while also ensuring salience, VCs are known to track media trends assiduously as part of their professional activities (Petkova et al. 2013). Thus, when VCs observe significant media attention dedicated to certain technological sectors, they are likely to perceive these technological sectors as attractive. Similarly, the presence of funding trends by peer VCs in certain technology sectors, made salient by the networks in which VCs operate (Batjargal and Liu 2004), are likely to relax the colocation constraint; thereby increasing the probability that VCs fund non-colocated entrepreneurs in those technology sectors. While prior work has shown herding behavior among VCs (Lerner 2002), we consider the effect of herding and observational learning on the formation of non-colocated funding ties.

Our analysis of the role of herding and media discourse in relaxing the colocation constraint is particularly apposite in the case of IT entrepreneurs. First, unlike capital-intensive domains such as biotech or pharmaceuticals, where earlystage ventures require deep IP protection, collaboration across multiple partners, and dependence on the local basic science facilities (Baum and Silverman 2004; Powell et al. 2002), IT entrepreneurs have low capital requirements and the potential for disaggregated work (Rai and Tang 2013), allowing them to operate outside of VC hubs. As a result, although IT entrepreneurs may find it advantageous to utilize local resources and support initially, they are likely to need VC funding from now non-colocated VCs. Second, the role of environmental factors such as media and herding in affecting VC decision making are particularly relevant in fast-moving contexts like IT, where trends change rapidly (Baskerville and Myers 2009). Indeed, McAfee and Brynjolfsson (2008) argue that technology-intensive industries experience faster turbulence and change in market structure. This extends to the development and dissemination of new technology platforms as well as the business models associated with these technologies (Bharadwaj et al. 2013). The effects of media discourse and herding are therefore likely to be of significant influence in the evaluation of IT entrepreneurs by VCs, in comparison to biotech or pharma, which have experienced steady but unchanging interest from the VC industry over time (Baum and Silverman 2004).

## The Funding of Non-Colocated IT New Ventures

## The Role of Media Discourse

Considerable research has addressed the influence of media on decision making (Abrahamson 1991; Pollock et al. 2008;

Sunstein 2002; Tversky and Kahneman 1973), with two dominant mechanisms being identified: greater salience (Tversky and Kahneman 1973) and increased legitimacy (DiMaggio and Powell 1983). Both of these mechanisms have implications for how VCs may relax their preference for colocated funding ties.

In the first stream, researchers have argued that increased media coverage can act as a legitimizer of topics (Aggarwal et al. 2012; Pollock and Rindova 2003). Consider, for example, Abrahamson’s (1996) seminal work on management fashion. In this work, he asserts that the development of rhetoric and increased public discourse is essential both for managerial “trend setters” to maintain their status and to provoke the adoption of new management fashions (Abrahamson 1991; Abrahamson and Fairchild 1999). The argument is that public discourse (i.e., media) legitimizes the firm, manager, or technology by providing a tacit or explicit endorsement of it. For example, researchers have argued that increased attention granted by the media can influence investor behavior at IPO (Pollock and Rindova 2003). In these contexts, the presence of media attention is viewed as enhancing the legitimacy of the object of discussion, making it more desirable and appealing (Baskerville and Myers 2009; Benders and Van Veen 2001). This perception of legitimacy in turn drives decision making regarding the topic receiving the media attention, making it more legitimate and attractive.

Alternatively, research linking media discourse to decision making has also related it to the salience of a subject (e.g., firm, technology, industry). This literature argues that as the amount of discourse on a subject increases bias is introduced, rendering the individual unable to form accurate judgments of the benefits and risks associated with the subject (Heath and Tversky 1991). The impetus for such biased judgment is the availability heuristic (Tversky and Kahneman 1973, 1974), in which the individual connects “the probability of an event [and] the ease with which instances or occurrences can be brought to mind” (Tversky and Kahneman, 1974, p. 1127). This original finding has sparked a host of research in experimental psychology (Carroll 1978) and strategic management (Pollock et al. 2008) where, in each context, evidence suggests that individuals tie the salience of events to the probability of those events occurring (Sunstein 2002).

Applying these arguments to the VC context, we contend that when certain technology sectors receive media attention, VCs are likely to relax the colocation constraint in funding entrepreneurs in that technology sector for two reasons.<sup>4</sup> First, when a technology sector receives media attention, VCs will be incentivized to take note of the new ventures in that sector (Kirsch et al. 2009), even if they are outside the VC’s traditional geographical preference. The dual effects of legitimacy and salience make these new ventures appear on the VC’s “radar” even if they are not colocated. In other words, the presence of media discourse is likely to reduce the costs faced by non-colocated entrepreneurs in reaching VCs, while also reducing the search costs incurred by VCs for entrepreneurs. Thus, media discourse contributes to a first-order reduction in search costs incurred by both VCs and entrepreneurs, but in particular for non-colocated dyads. Further, the benefits of salience and legitimacy do not accrue to those ventures whose sectors are bereft of media attention in the immediate time period. Media attention therefore increases the chances of an entrepreneur entering a focal VC’s “consideration set,” regardless of location, since the industry sector receives salience and appeal (colocated entrepreneurs of sufficient quality likely being already part of the consideration set).

Second, recall that VC funding decisions are built on a calculus of pre-funding payoff judgment and post-funding relationship management costs (Gompers and Lerner 1999, Gorman and Sahlman 1989). Increased salience from media is likely to make that industry sector more attractive, thereby biasing judgments of costs (both pre- and post-funding). Effectively, increases in media discourse on technology sectors enhances the perceived value of new ventures associated with that technology sector (Abrahamson 1996), while also systematically biasing downward the costs associated with the investments (Baskerville and Myers 2009). Since these judgments are more uncertain, and therefore riskier in the case of non-colocated entrepreneurs, the judgment task is more complex. Simpler tasks are less prone to cognitive biases, as they are less cognitively taxing (Heath and Tversky 1991). More complex tasks, in contrast, necessitate cognitive shortcuts, such as the availability heuristic, which results in greater bias (Kuran and Sunstein 1999, Sunstein 2002). Because the process of evaluating the non-colocated entrepreneur is more difficult, a greater amount of bias is introduced by the invocation of the availability heuristic. Thus, compared to colocated entrepreneurs, where the costs are easily benchmarked and information is more reliable, evaluating the costs of the non-colocated entrepreneur is more likely to induce significantly more bias, on the margin.

Although we propose these mechanisms through which increased media discourse on specific technology sectors is likely to moderate the colocation constraint, we cannot make a case for which mechanism dominates in this setting.<sup>5</sup> However, as each mechanism posits the same direction of influence of media on the colocation constraint, we propose

H1: Increases in media discourse will negatively moderate the influence of colocation on VC first-round funding decisions.

## The Role of Prior Peer VC Funding Decisions through Herding

Herding is broadly defined as a form of social influence wherein decision makers imitate group behaviors rather than acting solely on private information (Baddeley 2010, 2013). Further, it is often a response to uncertainty and an individual’s recognition of his own lack of information. The implicit logic, therefore, is that individuals follow the crowd because they believe that the crowd “knows better” than they do. Indeed, early treatments of herding are often attributed to Keynes (1930). However, more rational models of herding have also been proposed using Bayesian updating (where agents observe actions taken by others and then update probabilities and expectations). These Bayesian models, formally known as information cascades, are formed when decision makers view a series of decisions taken on similar tasks (Banerjee 1992; Bikhchandani et al. 1992), and rationally act against their private information. More recently, herding has been assimilated into a broader set of studies on observational or social learning (Cai et al. 2009; Zhang and Liu 2012), defined as any mechanism by which individuals learn from the actions or decisions of others. While this learning can be classified as rational (when the agent models decisions on those of previous decision makers) or irrational (where the agent is influenced by psychological or task characteristics that induce bias) (Zhang and Liu 2012), the end result is the same. By either directly or indirectly observing the actions of others, individuals ignore their own private information and follow the herd.

Why would the effects of herding disproportionately accrue to non-colocated entrepreneurs? Recall from H1, we argued that salience and legitimacy are likely to moderate the colocation constraint in VC funding. To the extent that prior funding decisions by peer VCs can also induce salience and conformity effects, we would expect a similar moderation of the colocation constraint in the herding context. Thus, if peer VCs are observed making funding decisions in a specific technological sector, these decisions will appear salient (as VCs are a community of peers; Sorenson and Stuart 2008). Moreover, by virtue of operating in a highly institutionalized environment, VCs have similar sets of preferences and goals, with little tolerance for maverick behavior (Gompers 1996). From a legitimacy perspective, peer VC investments will also moderate the preference for colocation. Indeed, prior work studying observational learning has argued that herding behavior can include the confounding effects of salience and the desire for conformity (Cai et al. 2009; Zhang and Liu 2012). Clearly, in the VC context, salience and conformity are relevant. To the degree that the actions of the crowd will bias the perceptions of the decision maker, VC perceptions of costs will be biased downward and perceptions of revenues upward, leading to biased perceptions of ex ante information asymmetry and the severity of ex post costs. Therefore, we expect herding to induce the same effect as media: to moderate the preference for colocation when faced with the choice of funding an entrepreneur.

Moreover, the direct effects of herding (i.e., observational learning) are also present. If VCs observe their peers making heavy investments in a specific technology, they are likely to find it compelling, thereby leading them to invest in the same technology sector (Bikhchandani et al. 1992; Cai et al. 2009). Entrepreneurs who are not colocated with the focal VC will experience higher odds of being included in the consideration set as a result. Additionally, by virtue of herding, VCs may find it easier to ignore their private signals or private information, focusing instead on the decisions of others. Under such conditions, the true costs of maintaining post-funding relationships with a non-colocated entrepreneur may be ignored or underplayed while the benefits of such funding ties may be overstated (Baddeley 2010). These effects are systematically higher in the case of the non-colocated entrepreneur in an industry sector experiencing herding rather than for the counterpart colocated entrepreneur where there is less uncertainty (Chen et al. 2010). Here again, while we cannot differentiate between pure herding, conformity, and salience in this setting, we can propose

H2: Increases in early-stage investments by peer VCs in certain technology sectors will negatively moderate the influence of colocation on first-round funding decisions.

## Data and Methodology

We draw on several sources of data to test our hypotheses. Information on VC funding decisions is derived from firstround data in the VentureXpert dataset. We augment these data with data from 11 newspapers, collected using a custom web crawler, to determine the amount of media coverage each industry subsector is receiving. To gauge media attention, we use two national newspapers (USA Today and The Wall Street Journal) to ensure that we capture discussion of different industries within both the business press and the popular press. Owing to the fact that media coverage may be concentrated geographically, we then augment these data with media coverage from within the VC’s economic zone. The economic zone is based on the U.S. Census Bureau’s Economic Census demarcations. We use nine major newspapers to accomplish this: The Boston Herald, The New York Times, The Chicago Tribune, The Minneapolis Star Tribune, The Washington Post, The Atlanta Journal Constitution, The Austin American Statesman, The Denver Post, and The San Jose Mercury News. In each case these are the highest circulation newspapers in the economic zone with the exception of The San Jose Mercury News (which is substituted for The Los Angeles Times due to the importance of Silicon Valley).<sup>6</sup>

We apply several restrictions in our sample to facilitate analysis. First, we only use entrepreneurs based in the United States as the notion of colocation, as well as the operationalization of media coverage, is more clearly defined in the United States. Second, we only include first-round funding to mitigate the confounding conditions from continued VC– entrepreneur interactions over subsequent funding rounds. Finally, we restrict our sample to the years 1985–2006, when the bulk of growth in the IT industry occurred. The unit of analysis for our investigation is the VC–entrepreneur dyad. Although entrepreneurs are often funded by groups of VCs in each round, each VC, in effect, makes independent decisions regarding the form and amount of investment, which is captured by the dyad-level analysis. Our dataset comprises 19,859 distinct entrepreneur–VC dyads for first-round funding between 1985 and 2006, consisting of 11,946 entrepreneurs funded by 2464 VCs.

## Matched Sample Methodologies

As VentureXpert provides only data on realized ties between technology entrepreneurs and VCs, there is a need to identify potential unrealized matches (i.e., funding ties between entrepreneurs and VCs that could have occurred but were not realized). This problem is pressing because no systematic dataset exists that captures information on entrepreneurs who were not funded by any VC, and individual interactions between entrepreneurs and VCs are rarely disclosed publically. Therefore, prior researchers studying VC decisions have used matching methods to create appropriate sets of unrealized funding ties as counterfactuals to those that are identified through VentureXpert (Sorenson and Stuart 2001, 2008) (similar methodologies have further been used in Azoulay et al. (2009) and Bengtsson and Hsu (2014)). We adopt three methods to perform this matching. The first method, which we term naïve matching, is consistent with that used by Sorenson and Stuart (2001). The second is a more stringent coarsened exact matching (CEM), that allows for greater balance of covariates between the treated and the matched sample (Blackwell et al. 2009). The final method is propensity score matching (PSM). In the interest of brevity, we report the results from the naïve matching approach, while describing the CEM and PSM analyses, as well as a comparison of the quality of the matches, in the appendix. The results are largely consistent between the three sets of analyses, and direct the interested reader to the appendix for further details. However, we do note that the support for H2 is greatly diminished in the CEM model, likely due to the stringency of the match and the balancing of covariates this method of matching offers. There are several benefits to using a matching approach over alternate operationalizations (such as a count model of the number of entrepreneurs funded or a time to funding model). First, by modeling the funding decision at the dyadic level we are able to control for entrepreneur-level characteristics that may influence the likelihood of the focal VC funding the entrepreneur. Second, because the VC appears multiple times in the sample, we can control for VC level characteristics in the model (up to and including a VC fixed effect). This adds the obvious benefit of controlling for heterogeneity in VC funding preferences. Third, and perhaps most importantly, executing the analysis at the dyadic level allows us to include time, industry, and location fixed effects to account for changes in the munificence of the economy/economic area over time as well as account for persistent heterogeneity between the manufactured treatment and control.

Our matching approach is as follows. In each year of our data, we match VCs that have funded an entrepreneur with every other entrepreneur, in the same industry, who was not funded by the focal VC but was funded by another VC (Sorenson and Stuart 2001). This approach effectively assumes that the focal VC chose to not fund those entrepreneurs who were funded by other VCs. As entrepreneurs tend to contact many potential VCs, and are funded by few (Kirsch et al. 2009), this assumption is not unreasonable (Sorenson and Stuart 2001). In effect, we match on year of funding, technology space, and evidence of quality through funding by another VC at that time. The technology space is denoted by matching on the industry subclass two (ISC2). ISCs are annotations within VentureXpert that provide increasing levels of granularity regarding the specific industry or technological domain in which each entrepreneur operates. ISC2 is the second most granular. This methodology offers us three distinct benefits. First, it ensures that the focal VC has capital to fund entrepreneurs (as it has elected to fund someone). Second, it guarantees that the VC is open to funding an entrepreneur in the specific technology space of the focal entrepreneur, as it has funded at least one entrepreneur in that space. Third, it ensures that each entrepreneur meets a minimum threshold of quality at the time of funding reception, as each entrepreneur has received funding from at least one other VC. Following Breslow and Day (1980) we enforce a 1:1 ratio of unrealized ties to every realized tie. Consistent with extant work (Sorenson and Stuart 2001), if multiple potential counterfactuals exist for the focal entrepreneur the unrealized tie is chosen at random from the pool or candidates. The selection of a random counterfactual ensures that there is no systematic bias entering the formation of the counterfactual set (Breslow and Day 1980; Sorenson and Stuart 2001). The final counterfactual set consists of a total of 19,850 unrealized matches (9 items not possessing suitable matches). Summary statistics are in Table 1. ## Variable Descriptions

## Dependent Variable

The dependent variable for our analysis is the dichotomous indicator of funding reception, Funded. Funded is set to 1 if the funding relationship between the focal entrepreneur and focal VC was realized and 0 otherwise.

## Independent Variables

The first independent variable of interest is the dichotomous indicator of VC–entrepreneur Colocation. Colocation of the entrepreneur–VC dyad is determined using the zip code information for both parties provided in VentureXpert dataset. We first map the entrepreneur and VC zip codes to the 176 economic areas (EA) in the United States provided by the Bureau of Economic Analysis (Alcácer and Chung 2007). As each EA encompasses several zip codes, we use the United States Postal Service dataset, which provides the most central zip code for each EA. We then match the zip code of the entrepreneur and the VC to the most central zip code in each EA using a Haversine formula. The third independent variable of interest, Media, is measured as the change in media coverage for the focal entrepreneur’s ISC3 within the VC’s economic zone. We use the VC’s economic zone because we are studying how changes in proximal media coverage influence VC decision making. Our operationalization of the Media variable is as follows. First, we use the number of articles from the two national media outlets, The Wall Street Journal and USA Today, to establish the amount of media coverage that every VC is exposed to in time t for industry i.<sup>7</sup> We then supplement this measure of media discourse by adding the coverage of the newspaper that is associated with the VC’s economic zone. Finally, we operationalize the media coverage as the change in the number of articles from these three periodicals in industry i between time periods t-2 and t-1. This variable is scaled to the change in thousands of articles to increase interpretability of the results and is lagged by one time period to limit reverse causality. We use a difference measure, which captures the relative change in discourse, as opposed to a growth metric or a simple article count, for two reasons. First, extant literature suggests that extensive discussion of topics in the media causes decision makers to be desensitized to media ubiquity by taking it “for granted” (Pollock et al. 2008). Second, as we want to capture large increases or decreases in the media coverage, operationalizations such as a growth metric, which has a natural infimum of negative one, are unsuitable. We note here that media coverage is overwhelmingly positive.<sup>8</sup>

## Controls

We control for several other variables in our analysis of VC funding decisions. Our first variable, Herfindahl, controls for the diversification strategy of the VC. This variable is operationalized as a Herfindahl Index of the ISC2 categories representing new ventures the VC has invested in over the 5 years prior to the funding decision. Two more control variables, VC Concentration and Entre Concentration, provide a measure of the concentration of VC and entrepreneurial activity in the respective locations of the focal VC and focal entrepreneur.

These variables are operationalized as the total number of distinct VCs or entrepreneurs either receiving or providing funding in the focal EA by year. Third, we include controls for measure the age of the VC (Firm Age) and the net investment of the VC (Firm Size) during the time that it has been actively investing. Fourth, we control for the recent spending activity (PrevSpending) of the VC. This variable is operationalized as the total amount of money the focal VC has invested over the previous 3 years. Fifth, to account for heterogeneity in new venture quality we include two controls. First, the novelty of the entrepreneur’s intellectual property at the time of funding (Patent Originality), is calculated using Hall et al.’s (2001) inverted Herfindahl index.<sup>9</sup> Second, we include the log of the age of the venture (ln(EntreAge)), calculated as the number of years between the new venture’s founding and the date of the first round of funding reception.

Our final control, Syndicate Colocation, is a measure of the extent to which members of the VC’s syndicate are colocated with the focal entrepreneur. This measure is included to capture the ability of the VC to gather information about the focal entrepreneur through his syndicate (Sorenson and Stuart 2001). The operationalization of this variable is as follows. We first measure the level of affiliation between the focal VC and all the investment partners within the VC’s funding syndicate by counting the number of times the member of the syndicate j has co-funded an entrepreneur with the focal VC (cofunding). We then determine which of these syndicate partners are colocated with the focal entrepreneur (colocation ). Our measure of syndicate colocation is then calculated with the following equation:

$$
\sum_ {1} ^ {j} \text { Cofunding } _ {i j} * \text { Colocation } _ {j}
$$

This measure estimates the relative influence that a syndicate member has on the focal VC and weights that influence by whether that syndicate member is colocated with the focal entrepreneur. If no member of the VC’s syndicate is colocated with the entrepreneur, this variable is 0. If some members of the syndicate are colocated with the entrepreneur, the influence of the syndicate members is weighted by their influence as determined by the extent of previous co-funding. In the case of non-realized ties, we estimate this measure by assuming that the focal VC joins the existing syndicate that has funded the entrepreneur.

Before proceeding with the empirical estimations we first inspect the datasets for persistent heterogeneity. As would be expected, the lion’s share of venture funding (39.2%) occurs in California (followed by Massachusetts (10.1%), New York (6.5%), and Texas (6.5%)), thereby necessitating the inclusion of location fixed effects. Further, as also would be expected, there is a significant spike in funding between 1999 and 2001 (the dot com boom), thereby necessitating time-fixed effects. Finally, as there are differences in the propensity of different industries to receive media coverage, as well as the treatment and control of media coverage (Table 2), we include ISC2- level industry fixed effects.

## Data Analysis

The primary regression analysis used is a logistic regression with the funding of entrepreneur i by VC j as the dependent variable. Formally, we model the correlation between our three independent variables of interest, Colocation of j and i, Media coverage of industry k in time t, and Herding behavior in industry k in time t, and funding using the following equation:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \log \left(\frac {\operatorname* {P r} \left(F u n d e d _ {i j}\right)}{1 - \operatorname* {P r} \left(F u n d e d _ {i j}\right)}\right) & = \alpha_ {1} + \beta_ {1} \text { Colocation } _ {i j} + \beta_ {2} \text { Media } _ {k t} + \\ & \quad \beta_ {3} \text { Herding } _ {k t} + X ^ {\prime} \delta_ {1} + \tau_ {t} + \varepsilon \end{array} \tag {1}
$$

where X indicates the vector of control variables, τ the vector of time fixed effects, and ε represents the error term. The terms $[ \beta _ { 1 } , \beta _ { 2 } , \beta _ { 3 } , \delta _ { 1 } , \tau ]$ are the parameters to be estimated. As we are interested in the results of the interactions between Colocation and both Media and Herding ,we introduce these sequentially into the estimation. We first present the analysis of this model from the naïve matching sample. Consistent with Sorenson and Stuart (2001), we also execute a 1:5 match and estimate the results using a rare events logistic regression (King and Zeng 2001).<sup>10</sup> The standard logistic model yields biased estimates of the standard errors when the proportion of positive outcomes in the sample does not match the proportion of negative outcomes. The rare events logit is a weighted least squares estimation which corrects the bias that arises in such cases (King and Zeng 2001). Standard errors are robust and clustered on the VC. The results are in Table 3.

One concern that emerges from the initial estimation is that individual-level VC preferences in decision making may be driving the observed effect, thereby necessitating VC fixed effects. However, due to the number of covariates required to conduct our analysis with VC fixed effects as well as the number of observations in the naïve matching dataset, the execution of this analysis becomes computationally intensive using nonlinear estimators. Moreover, due to the nonlinear nature of the estimation, interpreting the interaction coefficients is difficult (Ai and Norton 2003), notably because the tools available for a post estimation calculation of the marginal effect are designed to consider only a single interaction term at a time (thereby ignoring concomitant changes in the other variables of interest). In order to ease interpretation, we reestimate our model using a fixed effect linear probability model (LPM). LPMs offer the benefit of increasing interpretability while decreasing the computational demands inherent in large-sample nonlinear estimations with fixed effects. ## Results

Before we discuss our results, it is worth establishing the validity of the regression coefficients that we estimate in our analysis. Note that our hypotheses pertain primarily to testing whether the effect of colocation on funding changes based on the two moderators (i.e., the relevant coefficients of interest are the interaction terms). Further, because of the matched sample methodology, caution should be used in interpreting the direct effects of non-dyadically constructed variables from our models as the matched sample may induce spurious correlation between these variables and the observation of funding. Interpreting dyadic variables, such as colocation, or interaction with colocation, is reasonable since these are not biased by the construction of the matched sample (Sorenson and Stuart 2001). Our focus here is therefore the coefficients of the interactions of colocation with the independent variables.

We first examine the results shown in Table 3 from the naïve matching method. Strikingly, there appears to be little correlation between colocation of the syndicate and first round funding reception (Sorenson and Stuart 2001). Furthermore, and consistent with prior literature, it appears that increases in venture age are correlated with first round funding reception (Aldrich and Fiol 1994) (although we once again caution against directly interpreting coefficients of non-dyadic variables). When considering our proposed hypotheses, several interesting results emerge. First, as evidenced by the Colocation coefficient, there is a strong and significant relationship between colocation with the focal VC and first round funding reception. Second, as indicated by the coefficients of Media and Herding, there is a significant correlation between both variables and first round funding. We interpret this direct effect cautiously in light of the matching method. As shown in Column 3 of Table 3, the interaction term between Herding and Colocation is negative and significant, indicating that increases in herding influence the VC funding decision to a much greater degree when the entrepreneur is non-colocated. However, this coefficient is insignificant in the fully specified model (thereby providing limited support for H2). Further, as shown in Column 2 and Column 4 of Table 3, the interaction term between Media and Colocation exhibits a robust effect, indicating that VC’s are more strongly influenced by Media in the case of non-colocated entrepreneurs (thereby providing support for H1).

The results of the 1:5 match (Columns 5–8 of Table 3), as well as the fixed effect linear probability model estimates (Table 4), suggest the same results. In each case the coefficients for Colocation, Herding, and Media change are positive and significant. However, while in both sets of regressions the interaction terms between Colocation and Media are negative and significant, indicating a moderating relationship between the variables and further support for H1, there is limited support for the effect of Herding in the fully specified model (suggesting that Media has some primacy in this regard). Utilization of the LPM also allows us to show our results graphically. We use the LPM estimates, as opposed to the logit estimates, because of the relative ease of use of interpretation of the interactions. We note here that the predicted probability of funding reception with the LPM remains consistently within the acceptable range of [0..1] when independent variables are constrained to their observed values. Each model has been estimated using robust standard errors, clustered on the VC, to manage the heteroskedasticity concerns associated with linear probability models.

Referencing Figures 1 and 2, we see further support for H1, and middling support for H2. In both figures the y-axis indicates the predicted probability of the entrepreneur receiving first round funding from the focal VC. The x-axis represents the manipulation of media and herding, respectively. In both figures we see, as expected, that entrepreneurs who are colocated with the focal VC are far more likely to receive first round funding, ceteris paribus. We also observe that the slope of the non-colocated line is significantly steeper, indicating that increases in media and herding are far more influential in the case of nonlocal entrepreneurs. Translated economically, these results suggest that a one standard deviation increase in media (herding) results in a 0.8% (1.1%) increase in the probability of funding for colocated entrepreneur. However, there is a 2.5% (2.2%) increase for the non-colocated entrepreneur, more than double the effect. Extending these graphics, we next chart the marginal increase in the probability of funding the entrepreneur receives from increases in media and herding (Figures 3 and 4). In these figures the y-axis is the marginal increase in the probability of funding and the x-axis is the change in the independent variable of interest. Both graphs display the higher marginal effect of funding for nonlocal entrepreneurs, compared to colocated entrepreneurs.

## Discussion and Conclusion

In this study we explore the conditions under which IT entrepreneurs receive first-stage funding from VCs who are not colocated with them. Recognizing that VCs face a nontrivial information asymmetry when choosing ventures in which to invest, notably for first-round funding, we position colocation as a partial solution to this uncertainty. However, we also note that a considerable number of non-colocated investments are made by VCs in the IT context, thereby raising the question of why such investments are pursued. Extending previous work, we contend that macro-level environmental influences may affect the geographic distribution of the VC’s investments by biasing perceptions of the value of noncolocated entrepreneurs. These influences, independent of either the VC or the entrepreneur, relate to the general technological sector in which the entrepreneur operates, and emerge from two sources: investment decisions of peer VCs and the presence of media discourse. We further argue that the extent of these influences will be disproportionately higher in the case of non-colocated entrepreneurs, thereby easing the colocation constraint. Results suggest that media negatively moderates the effect of colocation. However, results from herding are equivocal, suggesting that peer VC behavior may have a weaker effect on easing the colocation constraint than hitherto expected.

This research makes notable contributions to three streams of literature in IS and entrepreneurship. First, although colocation remains a vital topic of interest in IS, it is often assumed that the availability of IT and the Internet should reduce transaction costs, thereby making physical distance less important (Mithas and Whitaker 2007; Sohn 2004). However, the “flat world” hypothesis remains subject to considerable debate (Florida 2005; Ghemawat 2001). Technology and reduced coordination costs notwithstanding, scholars have observed that colocation retains considerable benefits in terms of intellectual spillovers (Alcacer and Chung 2007), accessing human capital (Tambe and Hitt 2012, 2013), new product development (Kahn and McDonough 1997), and even organizational communication patterns (Van den Bulte and Moenaert 1998). In the context of VC decision making, the discussion of colocation extends back significantly as well, at least as far as Gorman and Sahlman (1989). Our work augments extant understanding of the dynamics of colocation and provides insights into the decades-old debate surrounding the flat earth hypothesis.

In the context of VC decision making, we present one plausible explanation for why VCs may extend the range of their investments. For non-colocated entrepreneurs, VCs may choose to rely on their social network partners (Sorenson and Stuart 2001) because of the benefits that accrue to VCs with extended networks (i.e., the resolution of the information asymmetry between the entrepreneur and the VC). We contend that media discourse may help non-colocated entrepreneurs attract VCs as well. Our work suggests that funding remote entrepreneurs may not be unexpected when certain technological sectors enjoy the benefits of media discourse, and to a lesser extent herding, because perceptions of the value of the focal technologies are biased by observed trends and patterns in the broader social environment (Sorenson and Stuart 2008). It therefore follows that the preference for colocation would diminish, on the margin. Further, to the extent that locally transacting parties have more effective means of resolving information asymmetries, and therefore face less complicated judgment tasks (i.e., decisions that are inherently simpler), any bias in judgment and decision making is likely to have a greater effect on non-colocated partners.

While this work is focused on VCs, who are central figures in the technology entrepreneurship ecosystem, it is worth considering the implications of our results for newer, de novo, forms of capital markets. An analogous question could be posed, for example, in the related context of crowdfunding, for example, do investors on such platforms exhibit clear preferences for colocated projects, and under what conditions are such preferences relaxed? Indeed, received research has already described the importance of colocation in such markets (Agrawal et al. 2010; Burtch et al. 2014; Kim and Hann 2015; Prpić et al. 2014), showing that crowdfunding platforms have far from obviated the need for colocation. Moreover, recent work investigating the ramifications of geographical dispersion among those who propose new projects, and their financial supporters, suggests that the benefits do not accrue uniformly (Burtch et al. 2014; Kim and Hann 2015). While several solutions have been proposed to further the democratization of capital, ranging from tapping the entrepreneur’s social network (Agrawal et al. 2010), to competition design changes (Prpić et al. 2014), to the mitigation of cultural differences (Burtch et al. 2014), the impact of broader, environmental, influences has yet to be considered. As media coverage and significant herding provide cues as to the viability of the entrepreneur, and insofar as investors are often information starved (Agrawal et al. 2010), it is plausible that such environmental factors may “nudge” an investor into investing in a non-colocated project (Agrawal et al. 2013; Thaler and Sunstein 2008). Of course, the source of media need not be relegated to traditional news media, but could also include social media platforms. However, the underlying mechanisms linking media discourse and peer investor behavior to the preference for colocation are likely to carry over into the new context. Indeed, the recent rise of equitybased crowdfunding only serves to underscore this point (Agrawal et al 2013). For the nascent entrepreneur, this media does potentially provide information on when and how to launch her crowdfunding campaign to maximize investor attention (Greenwood and Gopal 2015).<sup>12</sup>

Our work suggests two primary implications for IT entrepreneurs and policy makers. For IT entrepreneurs, location remains an important decision variable.<sup>13</sup> On the one hand, the entrepreneur benefits from her home environment, and the exploitation of resources that she has access to in that location (Dahl and Sorenson 2012). On the other, funding ties are easier established in a VC hub. By virtue of being an IT entrepreneur, relocation is possible, if not always desirable. Our work suggests that entrepreneurs should consider the implications of environmental influences on VCs as they make these tradeoffs. If the technology entrepreneur happens to benefit from the time-varying media “buzz” factor, there is likely greater marginal value in remaining within the home environment than in relocating to VC hubs.

For policy makers charged with increasing local entrepreneurship and job creation, our work suggests two implications. First, policy-makers should try to attract increased VC funding to their region while retaining entrepreneurs there. Samila and Sorenson (2011) show that regions in the United States that receive high levels of VC funding experience higher firm founding, innovation, and economic growth. The authors do not argue for the presence of colocated funding; rather, their arguments are based on only the reception of VC funding. How do policy makers attract greater VC funding to their regions? Our work suggests that industry sectors that experience media buzz are likely to be good candidates for attracting remote VCs. Policy makers should highlight such entrepreneurs, incentivize them further to stay local while helping to attract VCs. A second implication for policy makers emerges from the support and local funding that they provide to local entrepreneurs. Firms in industries not receiving media attention are less likely to garner institutional or VC support, and are therefore likely to need further assistance as they cross the chasm into maturity. New ventures that, for reasons of design or serendipity, do enjoy the benefits of media buzz are less likely to need institutional support from local governments and agencies. In the limit, policy makers may even find it worthwhile to encourage and financially subsidize certain entrepreneurial new ventures to relocate to VC hubs, if it is clear that the colocation constraint is too hard to overcome in certain technology sectors.

This study is subject to certain limitations, and hence opportunities for future research. First, while we conjecture about the right location decision for the entrepreneur, our dataset is limited in terms of visibility into the other processes that an entrepreneur may adopt to find funding. Our focus here is simply on the colocation constraint. Second, the structure of the VentureXpert database does not provide information about the entrepreneurs that were not funded by any VC, a limitation common to all such research (Sorenson and Stuart 2001). We deal with this limitation by considering multiple methods of matching and a series of robustness tests. Third, we operationalize media discourse using 11 mainstream periodicals in the United States, and arguably other sources of media could be influential as well. Given the time of our study (1985– 2006), print media appears to be the most prevalent form of easily available public discourse. This also raises the potential of further research in present times that extends the analysis to other forms of media, as Aggarwal et al. (2012) do with eWOM. Similarly, our measure of herding is an aggregate measure and does not include information on what specific firms a VC funded in a technology space and the amounts invested. Again, a richer empirical conceptualization would represent a next step in terms of future research. Fourth, our analysis considers colocation based on the headquarters of the VC firm. We are unable to account for the VC firm having multiple offices, one of which may be near the entrepreneur’s location. In most cases this is not a problem as most VCs have few branch offices. However, our binary measure of colocation is a limitation. Fifth, we note that at present we cannot cleanly distinguish between the mechanisms driving the observed effect (and cannot determine if colocation moderates media and herding or vice versa). We leave the identification of the dominant mechanism for future research. Finally, we repeat our cautionary note regarding the direct effects within the model.

## Alternate Matching Strategies

One potential cause for concern, and an inherent weakness with the naïve matching methodology described in the paper, is that the method considers only the year, industry, and funding reception on the part of the entrepreneur. To the extent that structural components of the VC syndicate (i.e., size and relative reputation), or observable characteristics of the entrepreneur, may correlate with the willingness of the VC to back the new venture, this undermines both the quality of the match and the extent to which the matched sample is balanced across the covariates with the original set of realized ties. To remedy this potential concern, we execute two additional matching strategies. The first, propensity score matching (PSM), is a well-established method in both the economics (Caliendo and Kopeinig 2008; Dehejia and Wahba 2002) and information systems (Brynjolfsson et al. 2003; Smith and Telang 2009) literatures. The second, coarsened exact matching (CEM), offer a more nuanced matching method for contexts where matching based on categorical variables may not provide adequate balance (Blackwell et al. 2009; Iacus et al. 2012). Specifically, while traditional matching methods increase homogeneity between the treatment and control groups in the aggregate, CEM offers the benefit of balancing each individual variable as well as the aggregate to ensure that heterogeneity in changes in the matching variables are consistent across the both the treated and control groups, thereby ensuring both multivariate and univariate balance. Unsurprisingly, this methodology is becoming increasingly popular in the social sciences (Azoulay et al. 2013; Greenwood and Wattal 2017).

To execute the PSM, the propensity score is calculated based on the predicted probability that the entrepreneur would have been funded by the focal VC. This was done by running a logit estimator on the full counterfactual set and then using the Stata predict command to determine y. The largest y for each realized tie was then selected to serve as the counterfactual. Duplicates were prohibited. Following Breslow and Day (1980) we enforce a 1:1 ratio of unrealized ties to realized ties. Econometrically, we replicate the effect from Equation 2 using both a conditional logit and a fixed effect LPM. Results are in Table A3.

To execute the CEM procedure (Blackwell et al. 2009, Iacus et al. 2012) between “treated” entrepreneurs (i.e., funded) and unfunded entrepreneurs by matching on the following entrepreneur and VC characteristics: year of funding, ISC2, evidence of funding by the focal VC to another entrepreneur in that year, size of the funding syndicate, and VC prestige as indicated through the VC reputation index (Lee et al. 2011). To the degree that the consideration of additional matching criteria will eliminate concerns of VC and VC syndicate heterogeneity, and the CEM balances both on the individual matching variables (note that year and technology sector must be exact matches) as well as the univariate imbalance between the two datasets, this should further eliminate unobserved heterogeneity from the dataset. Again, we maintain a 1:1 matching. Empirically, as with the PSM, we reestimate Equation 2, first using a conditional logit estimator and again using the LPM. Results are in Table A4.

Before we discuss the results of these estimations we inspect the matches manually. A comparison of the datasets created by the PSM and CEM are available in Tables A1 and A2, respectively. First, we note that the relative quality of the matching process increases dramatically as we move from the naïve match (shown in Table 2 of the main document), to the PSM, and then to the CEM. It should be noted that the treated set for the PSM and the naïve match are identical by construction (as a result of building the counterfactual set based on the predicted probability of funding). Second, we observe that the standard errors for the individual covariates within the treated and control groups are rather large, as is to be expected when using secondary data of this type. Moreover, it is not surprising that the standard errors for the treated and control groups are large since they are disregarding any time-based variation that exists in the source data (i.e., the method essentially crosssectionalizes matches made over time). While it is possible to estimate differences of means t-tests between the treatment and control groups for each covariate, the large sample sizes and the presence of temporal variation, that is unaccounted for, make such tests somewhat impractical. Instead, a model-free comparison of the mean values on the covariates between the two groups suggests that the CEM leads to a more defensible matched sample than the PSM or naïve matching. Finally, we note that to the degree that heterogeneity does persist between the treatment and control groups (as we see even in the CEM matches), it underscores the importance of including the many controls and fixed effects (i.e., time, location, industry, and VC) that we use in the estimation reported in the paper, so as to account for remaining heterogeneity in the groups which might be correlated with our independent variables of interest. We now turn to the results pertaining to the effects of media and herding on colocation using these matched samples

When considering the effect from the PSM we see across both the conditional logit, as well as the fixed effect LPM, a strong and significant correlation between Colocation, Media, Herding, and Funding reception. This increases our overall confidence in the a priori assumptions of the model. Furthermore, when considering the hypothesized effects, we see a negative and significant correlation between the interaction of Colocation and Media with first round funding reception (Columns 2, 4, 6, and 8 of Table A1). This same relationship is observed for the interaction of Herding and Colocation (Columns 3, 4, 7, and 8 of Table A3). Taken in sum, these correlations provide additional support fo our proposed hypotheses that media and herding will relax the colocation constraint, and that the effects will accumulate disproportionately to non-colocated entrepreneurs.

When considering our proposed hypotheses under the CEM sample, we see some discrepancies from the results discussed thus far (Table A4). Consistent with our prior estimations, there is a strong and significant positive relationship between colocation with the focal VC and first-round funding reception. Second, as indicated by the coefficient of the Media variable, there is a strong and significant correlation between increases in media coverage and first round funding, as well as a negative moderation of the colocation effect providing support for it. We note that even with a more balanced match, the results pertaining to the effect of media discourse on VC funding remains strongly influential. Third, and most strikingly, we see no evidence of the relationship between Herding and the likelihood of first-round funding, or an interaction between Herding and Colocation. This brings out a counter-intuitive result that we believe is worth exploring.

Why might the interaction of colocation and herding not appear when we use the CEM sample? One factor driving this result could be that in comparison to the naïve matching method and PSM, the CEM sample matches a given VC with a closer counterfactual VC. Since the naïve matching method, which is based on Sorenson and Stuart’s (2001) methodology, randomly chooses from all the available counterfactual VCs, it is plausible that the counterfactual is not appropriate. The CEM avoids this contingency. Following this logic, this would suggest that the results from herding’s interaction with colocation are overstated in the naïve matching sample and are therefore insignificant. In other words, the presence of considerable funding from peer VCs in the previous years does not provide a VC with the impetus to overcome the colocation constraint. Two significant factors may be at play here. First, while it is accepted that VCs do resort to herding (Khanna and Mathews 2011), this herding behavior may not extend to the point of funding nonlocal entrepreneurs because of the associated costs of ex post coordination and management (Gorman and Sahlman 1989). Second, the presence of herding could indicate to the focal VC that the appeal of investing in a specific IT subsector has reached a peak, thereby signaling possible market saturation. This contingency is explored further below. Recall that herding is, by definition, backward looking in that it builds on others’ observed investments, which are likely made after in-depth analysis of technology trends, technological maturity, and entrepreneur valuation (Gompers and Lerner 1999). If saturation is inferred, it is less likely that the VC will be willing to undergo the costs of nonlocal investment, even with a local syndicate partner (Sorenson and Stuart 2001). Thus, even though the direct effect of herding is positive, it will not substitute significantly for colocation.

## Correlation of Media

One potential issue that emerges with our measure of media is that we are sampling an individual newspaper as representative of the discourse within each given economic zone. This introduces the possibility of bias arising from the specific ideological or market-oriented attributes of the newspaper chosen, making the discourse in that newspaper nonrepresentative of media discourse in that region in general. To remedy this concern, and examine whether our results stand on changing the specific newspaper, we replicate our media collection efforts for seven of the nine local media outlets: The Philadelphia Inquirer for The New York Times, The Plain Dealer for The Chicago Tribune, The Omaha World Herald for The Star Tribune, The News Observer for The Washington Post, The Dallas Morning News for The Austin-American Statesman, The Arizona Republic for The Denver Post, and both The San Diego Union Tribune and The Los Angeles Times for The San Jose Mercury News. The Atlanta-Journal Constitution and The Boston Herald are omitted due to structural changes in the coding of the media vendor which did not allow our web scraper to function properly. We then compare the media counts across the used newspapers and the sister newspaper. Results can be found in Table A5. As can be seen from the result, significant correlation exists between each of the leveraged periodicals in the economic zone and the alternate major newspapers. Furthermore, as indicated in the note, all newspapers are significantly correlated (p < 0.001) with the sister newspaper when time and industry fixed effects are applied. From this, we conclude that the selection of periodical is not significantly biasing the results.

## Firm Specific Media Coverage

While our theorizing regarding media, as well as our subsequent empirical investigation, has focused on the technological sector (i.e., the ISC3), it is also plausible that the majority of discourse is actually firm-specific. As a result, the theoretical linkages between any media coverage and the decision to invest in a colocated versus remotely located firm would be undermined and the empirical correlation would likely be spurious or driven by the firm-specific media. To examine if this is potentially true, we randomly sample 100 firms from our sample and investigate the media coverage which these nascent ventures have received prior to their first-stage funding.

Three observations are gleaned from this investigation. First, only 22% of entrepreneurs have received any media coverage prior to first round funding, suggesting that media coverage before capital acquisition is rare. Recall that other investigations, such as Pollock et al. (2008), occur at the time of IPO (creating a significant selection bias in terms of longevity and quality over the marginal entrepreneur). Second, the maximum amount of media coverage received by a single entrepreneur is two articles in the sample (observed on 3% of the sample). Once again, this is not surprising. For example, Aggarwal and Singh (2013) find that the mean blog coverage of a single venture (arguably a much less stringent measure of coverage as blogs are not physically constrained by column inches and are not subject to editorial controls) after the first round of funding is 18 articles per round across the entire blogosphere (and only 6 articles in popular blogs (Aggarwal and Singh 2013)). Once again we note that this is conditional upon multiple rounds of funding. Third, only a single article was from one of the vendors chosen from our sample (The New York Times). Instead, most coverage came in the form of press releases or other self-promotion materials well after the venture had received funding. Given these observations, as well as the fact that our measure of media change is scaled to the thousands to increase interpretability, we believe that firm specific media coverage is not biasing the results of our investigation.

## Variation in Effects Across Time

Our next potential confound is the possibility that these effects are varying over time. Inasmuch as the speed and capabilities of varying ICT has increased exponentially since the advent of the dotcom boom, it is plausible that the importance of colocation is dwindling over time. While this possibility does not invalidate our theory, because a mitigation of the marginal willingness to extend beyond geographic boundaries is observed, it does sharply impact the implications of our research for the flat earth hypothesis (Friedman 2006; Gefen and Carmel 2008; Mithas and Whitaker 2007). To examine these potentially changing dynamics we take two approaches. In the first, we split our sample before and after 1995 (i.e., the Netscape IPO which is often heralded as the beginning of the dotcom boom; Goldfarb et al. 2007), and replicate equation 1. This strategy allows us to observe if the marginal effect in the hypothesized moderations is changing over time. Results are in Tables A6 and A7. Second, we interact our indicator of colocation with the time fixed effects and then replicate the estimations from equation 1. This strategy allows us to observe if the marginal importance of colocation is changing over time. Results are in Table A8.

Findings in Tables A6 and A7 provide several cogent insights into the changing dynamics of the effect of media and herding across time. First, we see that the relative number of VC dyads is heavily weighted toward the dotcom boom side of the sample (i.e., private equity investment exploded after the Netscape IPO, which is consistent with conventional knowledge on VC capital disbursements; Gompers 1994). This is further observed in the number of observable counterfactuals for the 1:5 match observed in Table A4. Second, we see across both samples a consistent correlation between Colocation and Herding with first round funding reception. Further, the effect of Media, both pre-boom consistent appears in the fully specified models and universally manifests in the post-boom models. Finally, we observe consistent negative and significant interactions between our hypothesized variables and colocation (with the exception of Herding in Columns 3 and 4 of Table A6). All else equal, this suggests that the effect of our moderating variables on the colocation constraint is likely increasing over time. Turning to Table A8, interesting findings emerge as well. In both models we see no consistent evidence of moderation of the colocation constraint in funding IT entrepreneurs over time. As a result, we conclude that time dynamics are not playing a significant role.

## Alternate Mechanisms and Controls

Our final set of robustness checks relate to other possible mechanisms by which the colocation constraint is mitigated. The first possibility is that syndicate size dominates syndicate colocation as the effective means by which information is channeled to the VC. Although Sorenson and Stuart (2001) original conceptualization implicated colocation by the syndicate as the primary method by which the focal VC would extract information about the entrepreneur from their social network, it is plausible that digital markets function in a strikingly different fashion. To the extent that the increased digitization of the firm and the entrepreneur might allow an IT entrepreneur, as compared with an entrepreneur in pharma or biotech, to increasingly digitize otherwise explicit pieces of information, it is plausible that the number of opportunities to overlap social networks dominates the number of colocated social network connections. If this were the case, then the effective proxy for the ability of the syndicate to collect information about the entrepreneur would be the size of the syndicate, as opposed to the syndicate colocation measure currently used. We therefore replicate our current analysis using the size of the syndicate. Results are in Table A9 and remain consistent.

The second possibility is that the local market is being sufficiently saturated by the direct effect of media and herding increases that the VC is forced to look elsewhere in order to fund an entrepreneur in the focal technology market. As certain technologies come into salience, and are seen as more legitimate to the VCs, local demand to fund such ventures will rise. If local markets for such investments are saturated, VCs who perceive value in these technology spaces may look elsewhere. Since the odds of non-colocated funding ties are small to begin with, for the reasons discussed above, the marginal effect of media discourse and the resulting demand for non-colocated ties will be larger. It is well accepted that VCs operate in socialized settings where they face pressure from their investors and peer VCs to invest in specific industries that are experiencing a time-dependent boom (Lerner 2002). If this is the case, then the influence of these stakeholders will nudge the VC toward funding a non-colocated entrepreneur in a technology sector experiencing positive media discourse, on the margin (Sunstein 2002). While yielding the same effect as our arguments, the net effect is by a singularly different mechanism (i.e., local market saturation as opposed to a conscious or unconscious uncoupling of the constraint).

With this in mind, measuring degree of local saturation is inherently difficult. To the degree that VentureXpert does not contain information on unfunded entrepreneurs, capturing precisely the extent of the unfunded market is impossible. To remedy this concern, we proxy the size of the local market (i.e., the number of local entrepreneurs) with the number of funded local entrepreneurs within the focal entrepreneur’s ISC2 that period. Inasmuch as the location fixed effects should account for the local propensity for entrepreneurs of a certain ilk to found the firm in the local area, this proxy should serve as a reasonable control. We then replicate our analysis from equation 1. Results are in Table A10 and remain consistent.
