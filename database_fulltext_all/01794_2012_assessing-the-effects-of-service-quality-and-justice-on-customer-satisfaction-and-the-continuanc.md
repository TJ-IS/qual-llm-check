# Assessing the effects of service quality and justice on customer satisfaction and the continuance intention of mobile value-added services: An empirical test of a multidimensional model

Ling Zhao <sup>a</sup>, Yaobin Lu <sup>a,</sup>⁎, Long Zhang <sup>a,</sup>⁎, Patrick Y.K. Chau

<sup>a</sup> School of Management, Huazhong University of Sci. and Tec. Wuhan 430074, China

<sup>b</sup> School of Business, The University of Hong Kong, Hong Kong, China

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 23 September 2010 Received in revised form 7 October 2011 Accepted 23 October 2011 Available online 29 October 2011

Keywords: Service quality Justice Transaction-specific satisfaction Cumulative satisfaction Continuance intention Mobile value-added services

## a b s t r a c t

Understanding the antecedents and consequences of customer satisfaction in the mobile communications market is important. This study explores the effects of service quality and justice on customer satisfaction, which, in turn, affects continuance intention of mobile services. Service quality, justice and customer satisfaction were measured by multiple dimensions. A research model was developed based on this multidimensional approach and was empirically examined with data collected from about one thousand users of mobile value-added services in China. Results show that all three dimensions of service quality (interaction quality, environment quality and outcome quality) have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and positive effects on cumulative satisfaction while only one dimension of service quality (interaction quality) has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and positive effect on transaction-speci<sup>fi</sup>c satisfaction. Besides procedural justice, the other two dimensions of justice (distributive justice and interactional justice) signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uence both transaction-speci<sup>fi</sup>c satisfaction and cumulative satisfaction. Furthermore, both types of customer satisfaction have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and positive effects on continuance intention. Implications for research and practice are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

With the rapid advancements of mobile network technologies, provision of various kinds of value-added services by mobile service providers is on the rise around the world. As the market becomes more and more mature, value-added services become more homogeneous and the competition for acquiring new customers and retaining existing customers becomes more intense. In this environment, customer satisfaction is a critical factor for mobile service providers to maintain or improve their market share and pro<sup>fi</sup>tability. Prior studies have found that customer satisfaction contributes to a <sup>fi</sup>rm's profitability and customer retention [33,35].

In a reorganization of the communications industry in China between 2008 and 2009, the original six mobile network operators were reduced to three. Meanwhile, the availability of thirdgeneration telecommunications technologies suggested that more mobile value-added services would be provided to the customers. A recent value-added services survey report on mobile communications conducted by Analysys in 2010 predicted that, the competition among existing mobile network operators would become <sup>fi</sup>erce after the reorganization of the industry and the introduction of third-generation services. Thus, for these mobile network operators, in order to retain customers, enhancing customer satisfaction is an urgent task to tackle with. Moreover, as new mobile value-added services are released, service providers need to focus on if these new services appeal to customers and on the willingness of customers to continue to use the services. Therefore, understanding the antecedents and consequences of customer satisfaction in regard to mobile value-added services is important. This study aims to investigate two factors that are closely related to customer satisfaction, service quality and justice. Although they are frequently investigated in service marketing and customer behavior research, these two factors are rarely examined at the same time in information technology (IT) use studies.

Many prior studies reported in the service marketing and consumer behavior literature have found evidence of the importance of service quality to consumer satisfaction [10,78]. Service quality is closely related to consumer satisfaction as it re<sup>fl</sup>ects the customers’ evaluation of the performance of service providers. Service quality is a multidimensional construct and different researchers have proposed different dimensions to capture the essence of this critical factor in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing customer satisfaction. Several researchers have explored speci<sup>fi</sup>c quality dimensions of this type of service in the mobile services environment [20,54,65,95]. We incorporate these speci<sup>fi</sup>c dimensions in our research model and examine how they in<sup>fl</sup>uence customer satisfaction.

Prior research also suggests that when customers evaluate their consumption experiences, they also consider justice since their consumption can be considered as an exchange between the service provider and the consumer [85]. Most users of mobile value-added services have an intrinsic motive to seek justice when engaging in an exchange with service providers because they have to pay for their subscriptions for these services. Justice involves a subjective fairness judgment of individuals if what they procured or how they were treated was fair under a speci<sup>fi</sup>c context, especially in a situation with uncertainty [91]. Recent marketing studies have examined this service fairness aspect as a “justice” concept [49,85], and found that being treated fairly was an important consideration to customers when they were evaluating their satisfaction with the services provided [67]. Similar to service quality, justice can be determined from several dimensions that are related to the outcome or the process of the service provision. We also incorporate these dimensions into our research model.

Customer satisfaction leads to positive outcomes, especially in predicting future intention to use a service. For example, research in service marketing and consumer behavior has found strong support for the positive impact of customer satisfaction on repurchase intention [12,22]. Customer satisfaction is a dynamic, complex and context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c construct [39] that has been divided into transaction-speci<sup>fi</sup>c satisfaction and cumulative satisfaction [10,21]. Though the two types of satisfaction are highly correlated, they are different conceptualization of satisfaction and serve different purpose [51,77]. In this study, we distinguish between these two types of customer satisfaction and investigate their antecedents and outcomes.

In summary, the contributions of this study are threefold. First, we incorporate two factors (service quality and justice) that have been rarely investigated together in technology adoption/acceptance studies into our research model and examine their possible in<sup>fl</sup>uence on customer satisfaction. Second, we examine both factors as multi-dimensional constructs rather than treating them as uni-dimensional constructs. Third, we distinguish between transaction-speci<sup>fi</sup>c satisfaction and cumulative satisfaction and examine how they might in-<sup>fl</sup>uence the adoption/acceptance behavior of mobile value-added services.

The paper is organized as follows. The theoretical background of the study is presented in the next section. The research model and corresponding hypotheses are then described, followed by a review of the study design and data collection procedures. The discussion section presents the results of the study. The paper concludes with a summary of the study's <sup>fi</sup>ndings, its limitations and its implications for technology adoption research and practice.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Customer satisfaction

The psychological processes that motivate customer satisfaction response are complex. The formation of satisfaction can be considered as evaluative (cognitive), emotion-based or both [75]. Research on the emotional bases of satisfaction mainly investigates how customers’ positive and negative emotions resulting from service encounters affect their satisfaction [74,86]. Research on the cognitive bases of satisfaction pays attention to the formation of cognition and how such cognition affects a user's emotional response and subsequent behavior [70]. In our study, we focused on the cognitive bases and regarded satisfaction itself as an affective state resulted from cognitive evaluation process. We adopt a de<sup>fi</sup>nition of satisfaction from McKinney et al. [70] who stated that satisfaction is “an effective state representing an emotional response” to the service encounter.

From a cognitive perspective, satisfaction resulted from the customers’ evaluation of their experience. For example, the expectation discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation paradigm is widely used to investigate the antecedents of satisfaction [73]. According to this paradigm, customers have expectations before consumption and evaluate the actual performance after consumption. Positive discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation between the expectation and evaluation leads to satisfaction and negative discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation results in dissatisfaction. Later, researchers found that performance evaluation also in<sup>fl</sup>uenced customer satisfaction directly [10]. Because of the complex features of service, researchers considered that satisfaction should be attribute based. That is, customer satisfaction results from the performance of attributes of the service [74]. Thus, in this study, scales to measure end-user satisfaction are developed from this angle [29,66,72,93]. In their information systems (IS) success model, DeLone and McLena [27] found that IS user satisfaction was determined by system quality and information quality. After extended research, they added service quality to the IS success model as an antecedent of user satisfaction [28]. Besides quality evaluation, researchers have also found that justice is another important cognitive theme in satisfaction research [74]. In the mobile services context, the cognitive determinants of satisfaction, especially the joint in<sup>fl</sup>uences of service quality and justice on satisfaction, have been scarcely studied.

An important perspective of looking at customer satisfaction focuses on the “dynamic” or temporal nature of customer satisfaction. From a “dynamic” perspective, customer satisfaction is conceptualized as transaction-speci<sup>fi</sup>c satisfaction and cumulative satisfaction [52]. Transaction-speci<sup>fi</sup>c satisfaction is an evaluative judgment of a speci<sup>fi</sup>c service encounter at a single point in time whereas cumulative satisfaction is the overall evaluation of all services encountered over time [50,75]. In other words, transaction-speci<sup>fi</sup>c satisfaction forms based on information about a service encounter and is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by speci<sup>fi</sup>c attributes of the service. Cumulative satisfaction, on the other hand, provides information about an organization's service performance over time, and thus is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by factors across service encounters. Therefore, cumulative satisfaction can be considered as an accumulation of transaction-speci<sup>fi</sup>c satisfaction at various points of time.

Johnson [51] pointed out that transaction-speci<sup>fi</sup>c satisfaction and cumulative satisfaction are more complementary than competing, and the purposes they serve are different. For example, transactionspeci<sup>fi</sup>c satisfaction is better to track the changes in performance and customers’ reaction to these changes, while cumulative satisfaction is better to understand customer-company relationship over time [77]. Olsen and Johnson [77] found that when taking into account equity, the ways that transaction-speci<sup>fi</sup>c satisfaction and cumulative satisfaction affected customers’ behavior intention were different. Thus, distinguishing these two types of satisfaction would help us better understand how they affect customers’ behavior and how to cultivate them in proper way. Many studies of mobile service acceptance/adoption have looked at factors affecting customer/ user satisfaction [5,55,57,59,94]. These studies, however, did not distinguish between cumulative satisfaction and transaction-speci<sup>fi</sup>c satisfaction.

## 2.2. Service quality

Service quality has been extensively investigated over the past three decades. In IS research, Pitt et al. [80] <sup>fi</sup>rst pointed out that IS researchers should include a measure of IS service quality in the assessment of IS effectiveness. DeLone and McLean [28] extended their IS success model by highlighting the importance of service quality in IS success. Service quality is a complex concept and there are two main methods to measure this variable. The <sup>fi</sup>rst method regards service quality as the discrepancy between customers’ expectations and perceptions of the service [45,78]. The second method conceptualizes service quality as “the consumers’ overall impression of the relative inferiority/superiority of the organization and its services” [9, p.77]. In this study, we use the second method to measure service quality as the former method may lead to poor validity resulting from the use of difference scores which are calculated by subtracting exceptions from perceptions [31].

A review of the literature also suggests that service quality is a multi-dimensional concept regardless of which measuring method is used. For example, Grönroos [45] proposed that service quality contained two dimensions—technical quality, which is related to what consumers get from the service encounter, and functional quality, which is related to how the service is delivered. Later on, Parasuraman et al. [78] proposed a <sup>fi</sup>ve-dimensional framework for service quality, comprising reliability, responsiveness, assurance, empathy, and tangibility. Based on these <sup>fi</sup>ve dimensions, they developed a 22-item instrument called SERVQUAL, which has become a widely used instrument to measure service quality in subsequent service marketing studies. Subsequent studies, however, suggested the limitations of the SERVQUAL instrument [31], thus leading to the emergence of several alternative instruments. Rust and Oliver [82] recommended a three-component service quality model and proposed that service quality should be measured by three dimensions, i.e., service product, service delivery and service environment. Dabholkar et al. [25] suggested a multilevel model in which service quality is assessed by <sup>fi</sup>ve primary dimensions (physical aspects, reliability, personal interactions, problem solving, and policy) and six sub-dimensions (appearance, convenience, promises, doing it right, inspiring con<sup>fi</sup>dence, and being courteous and helpful). They found support for the model by conducting empirical tests. Brady and Cronin [13] constructed a hierarchical model to measure service quality. In their model, service quality has three dimensions (i.e., interaction quality, physical environment quality and outcome quality) and each of these primary dimensions has three sub-dimensions, including attitude, behavior, and expertise for interaction quality; ambient conditions, design, and social factors for physical environment quality; waiting time, tangibles and valence for outcome quality. Through qualitative and empirical research, they obtained strong support that their multidimensional, hierarchical model measured service quality. Many subsequent studies on service quality, both in the physical and virtual environments, have used this threedimensional model and found strong supporting evidence [16,64,65]. All these studies indicate that the measurement of service quality varies across the type of service.

Recent studies of mobile service acceptance/adoption also adopt the multi-dimensional approach for measuring service quality. For instance, Kim et al. [55] conducted an empirical study and obtained three signi<sup>fi</sup>cant service measures that they called quality, valueadded services and customer support from an initial group of six measures. Lim [61] also looked at the mobile services quality issue and identi<sup>fi</sup>ed <sup>fi</sup>ve important dimensions of mobile services quality, i.e., pricing plans, network quality, mobile data services, billing systems, and customer services. Cho and Sung [20]introduced six dimensions to measure service quality of mobile Internet services, including responsiveness, assurance, empathy, convenience, usefulness, and diversity. Their <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that each dimension of service quality signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uences customer satisfaction [20].

More recently, based on Brady and Cronin's [13] model, Lu et al. [65] developed a multidimensional and hierarchical model to measure mobile service quality. They proposed that mobile service quality was composed of three primary dimensions, interaction quality, environment quality and outcome quality, and each primary dimension further included sub-dimensions. An instrument was developed and empirically tested using data collected from 438 mobile brokerage service users. Strong empirical support for the model and the instrument was obtained [65]. In our study, we use Lu et al.'s [65] multidimensional model of mobile service quality. We chose Lu et al.'s model because it was developed in a mobile services context and it was shown to be empirically valid.

According to Lu et al. [65], interaction quality re<sup>fl</sup>ects the “quality of a customer's interaction with the mobile service provider during the service delivery” [65, p. 232]. This dimension consisted of attitude, expertise, problem solving and information. Environment quality refers to the consumer's evaluation of the quality of equipment that is used, the extent to which the interface is well designed, and the extent to which the service is delivered under proper contexts. Outcome quality was de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “what the customer is left with when the production process is finished [45, p. 38]”. Many studies have shown that outcome quality signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affected the perceived service quality [15,37,45,82].

## 2.3. Justice

Stemming from social exchange theory and equity theories, justice provides a useful perspective for understanding customer satisfaction, customer loyalty, and customer complaint [85]. Many studies reported in the service marketing and organizational behavior literature have looked into justice and its relationship with other individual behavior variables [8,86]. Viewed as a multi-dimensional construct, justice is generally conceptualized in terms of three dimensions including interactional justice, procedural justice, and distributive justice [8]. These three dimensions address the whole exchange process, including the outcome, how the outcome is achieved and the manner in which the individuals are treated during the process [4]. Even though these three aspects of justice are correlated, they are viewed as distinct dimensions.

Interactional justice was de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the fairness of interpersonal treatment an individual received during the process of activity conducting [8,88]. It involves the manners that customer is treated by the service agents throughout the process [68]. Some speci<sup>fi</sup>c aspects suggested in the interactional justice literature are “courtesy”, “respect”, “interest”, “careful listening”, “effort”, “trust”, “explanation”, “empathy”, “apology”, and “communication” [87]. Procedural justice focuses on the fairness of the process [68]. Since the service outcome is generated by procedures, procedural justice is an important consideration in justice evaluation [85]. Three criteria for procedural justice in general situations, including consistency, neutrality, and accuracy of information, were outlined in the literature [60]. Furthermore, some more speci<sup>fi</sup>c indicators are also provided when assessing procedural justice, including “timeliness”, “promptness”, “approach”, “<sup>fl</sup>exibility”, “procedure control”, “outcome control”, “right policy and execution”, and “appropriate method” [68,87]. Distributive justice refers to the perceived equity of the outcome of the process [85], which is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the extent of customers’ feelings of fairness based on comparing their bene<sup>fi</sup>ts with costs [11,77]. Distributive justice is perceived when customers feel that their equity score, which is formed by comparing their input and output during an exchange, is proportional to the scores of a referent point or referent others [1,85].

In marketing research, justice is usually used to study events like service failure and recovery encounters, and ample empirical evidence shows that customers’ perceived justice is critical to their satisfaction with a <sup>fi</sup>rm's efforts [41,87,88]. Smith and Bolton [86] examined service failure in two service settings (i.e., restaurants and hotels). Their results indicated that justice is a cognitive antecedent of service encounter satisfaction and cumulative satisfaction. These studies imply that when events, such as service failure/recovery encounter, trigger more interactions between customers and <sup>fi</sup>rms, customers tend to expect to get just responses from <sup>fi</sup>rms [87]. This is why research focused on customer complaint behaviors found that justice contributes to enhancing customer satisfaction [68,88].

Different from the expectation con<sup>fi</sup>rmation/discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation paradigm of satisfaction formation, justice is more suitable for understanding long relationships maintaining between customers and service providers, as justice enhances trust and commitment to the organization [62]. Due to the uncertainty existing in the circumstances, justice is imperative for people as they need these justice judgments to manage the uncertainties [91]. Equity, or distributive justice, is the dimension that gets greatest attention from researchers. Au et al.[3] integrated equity theory with needs theory to provide three antecedents of end user IS satisfaction, including equitable relatedness ful<sup>fi</sup>llment, equitable work performance ful<sup>fi</sup>llment, and equitable development ful<sup>fi</sup>llment. Their <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggested that equitable relatedness ful<sup>fi</sup>llment and equitable work performance ful<sup>fi</sup>llment signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced end user IS satisfaction. Kuo et al. [58] conducted a study in the mobile service context and found that customers’ perceived value resulted from comparisons of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces, which represented distributive justice, and this perceived value signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced customer satisfaction and postpurchase intention. All these studies indicate that equity is an important cognitive judgment during the process of satisfaction formation [74]. Later, other researchers take all the justice dimension in their studies on IT/IS usage. For example, Chiu et al.[18] integrated IS success and fairness theory and examined the motivations behind Web-based learning continuance. The results indicate that distributive and interactional fairness have positive impacts on learners’ satisfaction. In another study of Chiu et al.[19], they investigated the antecedents of customer satisfaction and loyalty intention toward online shopping by integrating TAM and fairness theory. They found that fairness not only affected customer satisfaction directly, but also exerted indirect in<sup>fl</sup>uence on satisfaction through trust. Further, satisfaction was the strongest predictor of customer's loyalty intention. In short, the above literature review suggests that justice is a useful construct to help understand and explain customer satisfaction toward IT/IS. However, to our best knowledge, very few studies have been conducted that look into the explanatory power of justice in mobile service usage.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

Based on the above literature review, we developed a research model as depicted in Fig. 1. The main features of the model are that both service quality and justice are examined with a multidimensional approach and customer satisfaction is split into two types. The model also follows a cognition-affect-conative framework [6,42], which has been adopted in many prior research studies in service marketing [33,42]. This framework also re<sup>fl</sup>ects the selfregulatory processes of appraisal processes, emotional reactions and coping responses. Bagozzi [6] argued that there were limitations existing in current attitude theories, and introduced this selfregulatory mechanism to explain the relationships among attitude, intention, subjective norm and behavior. For the attitude-intention relationship, Bagozzi [6] proposed that individuals evaluate the outcomes <sup>fi</sup>rst, and then such appraisals of outcome-desire lead to speci<sup>fi</sup>c emotional reactions, which in turn arouse coping responses of intentions toward particular actions. This framework is also adapted to service context. For example, a customer orders some food in an exclusive restaurant that is recommended by his friends and he may expect delicious food and quality service before consumption. If such goals are achieved during the consumption, he would have an outcome-desire ful<sup>fi</sup>llment, and such positive experience (appraisal processes) will lead to positive affect, such as satisfaction, pleasure (emotional reactions). And then, he may form the intentions to maintain or increase the feelings (coping responses), like visiting this restaurant next time or recommend it to other friends. In this study, we also adapted this framework to a service context. In our study, service quality and justice re<sup>fl</sup>ect customers’ cognitive judgments with the consumption experience [76,86]. Customer satisfaction, no matter if it is transaction-speci<sup>fi</sup>c or cumulative, reveals customers’ affective responses to the consumption experience [39]. Continuance intention refers to the future continuation of satis<sup>fi</sup>ed customers. Therefore, the research model generally hypothesizes that cognitive judgments (i.e., service quality and justice) lead to customers’ affective responses (i.e., transaction-speci<sup>fi</sup>c and cumulative satisfaction), which, in turn, drive behavior (continuance intention). The hypotheses are presented below.

## 3.1. Relationship between service quality and customer satisfaction

The relationship between service quality and customer satisfaction has been studied extensively and ample positive evidence that “service quality significantly influences customer satisfaction” has been gathered [10,33,42,52,75]. In studies of mobile services, similar conclusions have been obtained [55,58,59,84,90,94,95].

The <sup>fi</sup>rst dimension of service quality is interaction quality, which re<sup>fl</sup>ects the “quality of a customer's interaction with the mobile service provider during service delivery” [65, p. 232]. This dimension captures the consumer-seller interaction of the service delivery process. This interaction is critical to the development of the relationship between the consumer and the service provider. Because of the intangibility of a service [13,97], much research has shown that the interaction quality is the most signi<sup>fi</sup>cant dimension of service quality [9,45,46].

Fig. 1. The research model.

In the traditional environment, Chiou and Drog [17] found that the quality of interactive service provided by employees played a more important role in impacting customer satisfaction than facility service quality. Though in the context of mobile services, consumers may not meet with the service provider face to face, the interaction between the two parties occurring through the mobile device is still an important component in mobile service delivery. For example, Wang et al. [96] found that assurance, which re<sup>fl</sup>ected the interactive factors in service quality, in<sup>fl</sup>uenced customer satisfaction in mobile services by China Telecom signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly. Applying the same framework of service quality to investigate usage of SMS (Short Message Service) in Singapore, Tung [89] also found a positive relationship between empathy, assurance and customer satisfaction. Thus, we may posit that a higher level of interaction quality will lead to a stronger sense of satisfaction toward the mobile value-added services. Moreover, this dimension could also contribute to the cumulative satisfaction. For example, in a B2B context, Caceres and Paparoidamis [14] found that communication with the advertising supplier exerted the strongest indirect effects on the consumer's relationship satisfaction. Here relationship satisfaction refers to a cumulative satisfaction as the relationship develops, rather than transaction-speci<sup>fi</sup>c satisfaction. Thus we posit that:

H1. Interaction quality positively affects (H1a) transaction-specific satisfaction and (H1b) cumulative satisfaction.

The second dimension of service quality is environment quality. Previous research on traditional services consider environment quality as the extent to which tangible features of the servicescape play a formative role in overall service quality perceptions [13]. In the mobile context, environment quality is viewed as how the consumer considers the overall environment of the service delivered by the service provider [65]. Environment quality of mobile services re<sup>fl</sup>ects the basic requirement of using such services, such as the equipment quality, the interface design and service delivery contexts[65]. It would be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for a consumer to achieve his goal without a supportive environment. For example, a consumer may get frustrated if the interface of a mobile service he used is complicated. Though he <sup>fi</sup>nally achieves the goal, negative feelings would still remain and cumulate to exert negative in<sup>fl</sup>uences on satisfaction.

Previous research on mobile service also found empirical evidence for the positive relationship between environment quality and customer satisfaction. By conducting an empirical study on mobile value-added services in Taiwan, Kuo et al. [58] found that navigation and visual design, system reliability and connection quality were important dimensions of mobile value-added service quality, which further in<sup>fl</sup>uenced customer value and satisfaction. In the research of Wang et al. [96], two dimensions of service quality (i.e., reliability and network quality) which represent the facilities and context aspects of service, have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impacts on the satisfaction of telecommunication users in China. Thus we hypothesize that:

H2. Environment quality positively affects (H2a) transaction-specific satisfaction and (H2b) cumulative satisfaction.

Outcome quality, as the third dimension of service quality, includes not only punctuality of the service delivery, but also tangible evidence and intangible valence[65]. Valence captures “the attributes that control whether customers believe the service outcome is good or bad, regardless of their evaluation of any other aspect of the experience” [13, p. 40]. Outcome quality directly relates to the consumers’ ful<sup>fi</sup>llment of desire and leads to a feeling about the experience. Thus some researchers considered outcome quality as the re<sup>fl</sup>ection of actual service, and also a determinant in the assessment of service quality [23]. Oliver [75] regarded customer satisfaction as a more global evaluation while service quality was attribute-based. Outcome quality is closely related to satisfaction as it also re<sup>fl</sup>ects a global assessment of the service quality based on customers’ experiences. Dabholkar and Overby [24] studied real estate agent services and found that outcomes were closely related to satisfaction, while process factors were closely related to service quality. Thus, outcome quality is positively related to customer satisfaction. In addition, as Brady and Cronin [13] pointed out, even if an individual had a positive assessment of other service quality dimensions, the negative valence of the outcome would result in an unpleasant experience. We posit that outcome quality is positively related to consumer satisfaction.

H3. Outcome quality positively affects (H3a) transaction-specific satisfaction and (H3b) cumulative satisfaction.

## 3.2. Relationship between justice and customer satisfaction

Interactional justice occurs when customers feel that they are treated with courtesy, politeness, respect, empathy, and that company takes effort in solving problems [87]. Tax et al. [88] summarized <sup>fi</sup>ve important elements of interactional justice based on previous research on organizational behavior and social psychology, including explanation, honesty, politeness, effort and empathy. In a mobile value-added services context, though service offerings are primarily provided to subscribers automatically through a computerized system and related software, interactions between customer and service provider are inevitable. Fair treatment in such interactions in<sup>fl</sup>uences the evaluation of customer satisfaction, especially when customers encounter service failure events. Therefore, we posit:

H4. Interactional justice positively affects (H4a) transaction-specific satisfaction and (H4b) cumulative satisfaction.

Procedural justice refers to the fairness of policies, procedures, and criteria during the process of service offerings. Since the process is an integral part of service offerings, it prescribes the service providers how to provide services. Procedural justice had been neglected for a long time by organizational researchers as they primarily focused on the outcome fairness, or equity [43]. While later on, more and more research have showed the importance of procedural justice. For example, Folger and Konovsky[32] found that procedural justice contributed to employees’ attitudes about the employing institution and its authorities, trust in supervisor and organizational commitment. Studies in marketing found that procedural justice is positively related to customer satisfaction [68,87,88]. Moreover, the perception of procedural justice may even diminish the perceived unfairness of outcomes and increase individuals’ satisfaction with the outcomes [63].

Maxham and Netemeyer [68] found that procedural justice had insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant effects on satisfaction with the recovery, but it was positively related to overall <sup>fi</sup>rm satisfaction. This con<sup>fi</sup>rms the idea that procedural justice is helpful to maintaining long-term overall satisfaction between parties [32]. Therefore, we posit the following hypotheses:

H5. Procedural justice positively affects (H5a) transaction-specific satisfaction and (H5b) cumulative satisfaction.

Distributive justice is related to evaluation of an outcome based on the comparison of inputs to outputs [44]. When customers perceive that their inputs are equal to outputs, they perceive high distributive justice. This dimension re<sup>fl</sup>ects the fundamental principle of Social Exchange Theory that individuals expect equity in interpersonal relations. Previous marketing research mainly focuses on distributive justice and <sup>fi</sup>nds that it signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affects customer satisfaction [76]. Meanwhile, research on organizational behavior has also found that distributive justice explained more variance in job satisfaction and pay satisfaction than procedure justice [32,69].

The inputs and outputs involved in the evaluation of equity could be tangible or intangible. For example, inputs may include monetary cost, learning cost, emotions, searching cost and time cost, while outputs may include enjoyment, convenience, and so on. In Chiu et al.'s [18] study of Web-based learning system continuance, distributive fairness described the extent to which the learners felt that they received the fair grade from instructors. Their empirical study also found that distributive fairness had the strongest effects on learners’ satisfaction with Web-based learning. In the mobile value-added service context, users invest time and money in learning and using the service in exchange for convenience or enjoyment. When such investment is proportional to the outcomes, users would perceive high distributive justice. We expect that they would be more satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the service when they perceive that there is distributive justice. There is considerable empirical evidence showing that distributive justice in<sup>fl</sup>uences the level of transaction-speci<sup>fi</sup>c satisfaction [85,87]. Though some others have shown that distributive justice positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences cumulative satisfaction [68], the relationship between these two variables needs more investigation. H9. Cumulative satisfaction positively affects continuance intention.

## 3.3. Relationship between two types of satisfaction

Previous researchers have reached a consensus that transactionspeci<sup>fi</sup>c satisfaction and cumulative satisfaction should be viewed as distinct types of satisfaction [10,21,33] and these two types of satisfaction are more complementary than competing due to their different functions [77]. To a large extent, the evaluation of cumulative satisfaction is based on previous experiences of transaction-speci<sup>fi</sup>c satisfaction. In other words, cumulative satisfaction is a function of all previous transaction-speci<sup>fi</sup>c satisfaction or is the aggregation of all previous transaction-speci<sup>fi</sup>c satisfaction [9,53,79]. It re<sup>fl</sup>ects customer stored evaluation in one's memory[40].

As transaction-speci<sup>fi</sup>c satisfaction may vary from experience to experience, its effect on cumulative satisfaction could be marginal because the most recent transaction-speci<sup>fi</sup>c satisfaction may in<sup>fl</sup>uence cumulative satisfaction either positively or negatively [36]. Therefore, the overall effect may be cancelled out. Nevertheless, empirical studies have obtained support on a positive relationship between transaction-speci<sup>fi</sup>c satisfaction and cumulative satisfaction [53,71,83]. Jones and Suh [53] found that transaction-speci<sup>fi</sup>c satisfaction is different from cumulative satisfaction, and transaction-speci<sup>fi</sup>c satisfaction has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uence on cumulative satisfaction. Similarly, Shankar et al.[83] found that service encounter satisfaction had positive effect on overall satisfaction in both online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine environments. As such, we posit the following hypothesis:

H7. Transaction-specific satisfaction positively affects cumulative satisfaction.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Measures

To measure the constructs in the research model, validated items by other researchers in the extant literature were adapted and multi-item scales were used. All items were modi<sup>fi</sup>ed to <sup>fi</sup>t the mobile value-added services context. The measurement scales of interaction quality, environment quality, and outcome quality were adapted from Lu et al. [65] and Brady and Cronin [13]. Each dimension of service quality was measured by two-item scales. The measurement scales for interactional justice, procedural justice, and distributive justice were adopted from the services marketing literature. To measure interactional justice, three items were adapted from Davidow [26]. Similarly, three items were used to measure procedural justice from Smith et al. [87]. Items for distributive justice were measured by four items adapted from Smith et al. [87] and Veloutsou et al.[92]. To measure customer satisfaction, four items primarily adapted from service marketing research were employed to measure transaction-speci<sup>fi</sup>c satisfaction [7,77,87] and seven items from mobile services and service marketing literature were adapted to measure cumulative satisfaction [47,74,77,86]. Lastly, the six items measuring continuance intention were taken from prior studies on repurchase intention in mobile services [48,56]. Thus, a total of thirty-three items were used to measure the constructs. For each item, a seven-point Likert scale was used with anchors from “1=strongly disagree” to “7=strongly agree”.

## 4.2. Pretest and pilot test

In order to ensure the reliability and validity of measures, a pretest and pilot test were conducted prior to the main study. Five professional marketing researchers and <sup>fi</sup>ve mobile carrier managers were asked to verify content validity and assess the suitability, readability, and ambiguity of the scales through personal interviews. Feedback received from the interviews was used to modify the questionnaire. Then, a convenience sample of one hundred undergraduate students who had more than two years of experience with mobile valueadded services was invited to participate in the pilot test. The results of the exploratory factor analysis identi<sup>fi</sup>ed nine factors as expected in the research model. The instrument showed good reliability and validity. The questionnaire was further revised following the comments received from these participants. The <sup>fi</sup>nal instrument is shown in Appendix A.

## 4.3. Sample

The main survey was conducted in a major university in China. University students at both undergraduate and graduate levels were selected as subjects for two reasons. First, according to a mobile value-added service survey report of 2007 released by Analysys, the age group of 16 to 30 was the primary group that used mobile value-added services, which is approximately 70.7% of the market share. If this age group was selected for sampling, the results could be representative to a certain degree of the population, thus enhancing the study's generalizability. Second, most researchers investigating customer behaviors with mobile services also selected this age group as their survey sample [20,58,90].

We chose students from several different schools in the same university as our sample. We then distributed the questionnaires to the students through classes that were randomly selected in each school. As the sample in our study was a convenience sample to some extent, we then compared major demographic characteristics and variable means of the samples from different schools to avoid potential sample bias. The results showed that there were no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences among the samples, indicating that we could conduct further analysis based on the combination of the full samples.

A total of 1500 questionnaires were distributed. After two weeks, 1342 questionnaires had been returned. Two hundred and sixtyseven of these were dropped owing to (1) usage experiences of less than six months, (2) age not in the selected age group (16–30), (3) the same answers to all questions or (4) more than 20% questions not answered. The <sup>fi</sup>nal sample consisted of 1075 valid respondents, resulting in a response rate of 71.7%.

## 6. Discussion

The main objective of this study was to assess the effects of service quality and justice on customer satisfaction, which in turn in<sup>fl</sup>uence continuance intention of mobile value-added services. Taking a multi-dimensional approach with reference concepts from studies on service marketing and consumer behavior, a research model was proposed and empirically tested against data collected from over 1000 mobile value-added services users. The results generally support the model and most of the hypotheses.

The results show that the in<sup>fl</sup>uences of transaction-speci<sup>fi</sup>c satisfaction and cumulative satisfaction seem to be different. Of the three dimensions of service quality, only one, interaction quality, was found to have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly positive effect on transactionspeci<sup>fi</sup>c satisfaction. Under the context of traditional services where simultaneous participation of customers and service providers is needed most of the time, Grönroos [45] found that the functional quality (which relates to how the service is delivered—the interaction process) was more important than the technical quality (which relates to what service is delivered) in the service encounter. In obtaining mobile services, customers might not interact with service providers directly all the time, but the customers interact with the service system frequently. Thus, customers would de<sup>fi</sup>nitely place importance on the interactional quality of each service encounter. Another possible reason might due to the nature of transaction-speci<sup>fi</sup>c satisfaction. Since transaction-speci<sup>fi</sup>c satisfaction emphasizes the experience of a speci<sup>fi</sup>c service encounter at the point of consumption, customers may pay more attention to the service implementation, rather than the environment and the outcome of the service.

However, when assessing cumulative satisfaction, in contrast to transaction-speci<sup>fi</sup>c satisfaction, customers emphasize outcome and environment quality. All three service quality dimensions were found to have positive in<sup>fl</sup>uences on cumulative satisfaction, with the most signi<sup>fi</sup>cant the outcome quality dimension followed by environment quality and then interactional quality. A possible explanation would be that with an increase in consumption experiences, customers become more familiar with the service process, thus making the interaction part more or less standard, and thus less important. In contrast, customers may now pay relatively more attention to the environment of the service provided by the service provider and the outcome of the service (what they actually get from the service).

Both interactional justice and distributive justice signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in-<sup>fl</sup>uence both types of satisfaction while this is not the case for procedural justice. However, the different effects of procedural justice and distributive justice on satisfaction accord with the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of Van den Bos and Lind's experiment [91]. They found that when people have more direct and explicit information about the authority's trustworthiness, they would be less concerned about procedural fairness. People also reacted more sensitively to the fairness of the outcome when the authority employed a fair procedure. In China, mobile valueadded services are provided by a few mobile network operators, and customers are quite familiar with these service providers. Meanwhile, different from traditional services, handling of applications for mobile value-added services is primarily performed automatically, thus making the procedures basically the same for every customer. Therefore, distributive justice exerts signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effects on customer satisfaction while procedural justice does not.

The insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant relationship between procedural justice and cumulative satisfaction is opposite the results of Maxham and Netemeyer [68] that procedural justice and interactional justice have greater in<sup>fl</sup>uence on overall <sup>fi</sup>rm satisfaction than does distributive justice. Among the three dimensions of justice, distributive justice exerts greatest effects on cumulative satisfaction. This might imply that when uncertainty in the service providers and procedures is small, customers tend to focus on the outcomes offered by the service providers. Equity plays a more important role in long-term relationship building. The positive relationships between interactional justice and two types of customer satisfaction accord with previous research [18,68], suggesting that if customers feel that they are treated respectfully during the service delivery process, they will be more satis<sup>fi</sup>ed.

Consistent with previous research, our results indicate a strong and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect of transaction-speci<sup>fi</sup>c satisfaction on cumulative satisfaction [68]. This provides empirical evidence that transaction-speci<sup>fi</sup>c satisfaction and cumulative satisfaction are two different constructs and the former is a good predictor of the latter, a result also found by Jones and Suh [53]. Last but not the least, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggests that both cumulative satisfaction and transactionspeci<sup>fi</sup>c satisfaction exert a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant positive effect on continuance intention. In particular, even though transaction-speci<sup>fi</sup>c satisfaction has both direct and indirect effects (through cumulative satisfaction) on continuance intention, its in<sup>fl</sup>uence is still much smaller than that of cumulative satisfaction on continuance intention. This also implies that one advantage of cumulative satisfaction is that it better predicts customers’ intentions and behaviors [77].

## 7. Conclusions

## 7.1. Limitations

Like all empirical studies, this study has several limitations. First, data were collected from students in a university. Although the 16 to 25 age group is the primary group using mobile value-added services, it is still only one single part of the user population. Moreover, the survey was performed in China. Although the research results may re<sup>fl</sup>ect the situation in China, owing to the differences of culture in different countries, the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance found for individual paths in the research model might be different in other countries. Thus, future research testing the research model and the corresponding hypotheses in other population groups and geographical contexts may be necessary in order to improve the generalizability of the results.

Second, as shown in prior literature, there exist many other factors in<sup>fl</sup>uencing customer satisfaction and continuance intention, such as value, expectation, performance, customer care and so on. Our model explains 72%, 85% and 56% of the variance in transactionspeci<sup>fi</sup>c satisfaction, cumulative satisfaction and continuance intention, respectively. Although the explanatory power is already quite good compared to other similar studies, examination of other factors might improve the model.

## 7.2. Implications

Findings from this study provide several suggestions for future research. First, service quality and justice, as two cognitive bases of customer satisfaction, are both incorporated into our research model. We examine their joint in<sup>fl</sup>uence on customer satisfaction. Though some research on IT/IS use indicates the importance of service quality or justice, their joint in<sup>fl</sup>uence on end-users’ satisfaction needs more investigation. Our study gives evidence that justice should be considered in a customer satisfaction model under the context of IT/IS use.

Second, we examine service quality and justice with their respective multiple dimensions, instead of treating them as uni-dimensional constructs. Doing so accounts for the complexity of human perceptions more fully [13,25]. Although the literature has a good consensus on the three dimensions of justice, the debate about the dimensions of service quality is still heated, with predominant views indicating that there are two dimensions [45], three dimensions [82], and <sup>fi</sup>ve dimensions [78]. This study chose the three-dimensional view and showed the validity of this view. It provides a reference for future research on service quality in the mobile context.

Third, we distinguish transaction-speci<sup>fi</sup>c satisfaction from cumulative satisfaction and examine what factors affect them and how they might in<sup>fl</sup>uence the adoption/acceptance behavior of mobile valueadded services. Our study indicates that the dimensions of service quality and justice exert different in<sup>fl</sup>uences on these two types of satisfaction, and transaction-speci<sup>fi</sup>c satisfaction and cumulative satisfaction should be viewed as two different types of satisfaction. Therefore, this study has extended our understanding of the dynamic nature of satisfaction and the antecedents of the two different types of satisfaction.

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings also provide implications for practitioners. The different natures of transaction-speci<sup>fi</sup>c satisfaction and cumulative satisfaction have different functions for practitioners. To enhance the probability of customers’ continuance intention, mobile value-added service providers should not only be concerned with transactionspeci<sup>fi</sup>c satisfaction, but also pay attention to the cumulative satisfaction of their customers. In fact, the much greater in<sup>fl</sup>uence of cumulative satisfaction on continuance intention than that from transactionspeci<sup>fi</sup>c satisfaction strongly suggests the importance of developing and implementing appropriate strategies to ensure suf<sup>fi</sup>cient levels of cumulative satisfaction among customers. Though cumulative satisfaction is better to predict behavior intention, transaction-speci<sup>fi</sup>c satisfaction is also important as it is the base of forming cumulative satisfaction. Mobile value-added service providers should also carry out measures to improve customer satisfaction in each service encounter.

In order to enhance these two types of satisfaction, practitioners could take corresponding measures according to the dimensions of service quality and justice to improve the service quality and justice perceptions of customers. For example, service providers should train the employees to better interact with customers, like how to deal with customers’ complaints, or how to understand customers’ requirements and provide effective solutions promptly, as interaction quality and interactional justice exert greater in<sup>fl</sup>uences on customers’ satisfaction toward a speci<sup>fi</sup>c transaction. For the long relationship building, it seems that almost all dimensions of service quality and justice become important to customers’ cumulative satisfaction. To be speci<sup>fi</sup>c, customers place more emphasis on environment quality, outcome quality and distributive justice. Thus, service providers should persistently improve the quality of infrastructure and mobile applications, and reduce the costs of accessing and using these applications. Such efforts would be helpful to cultivate cumulative satisfaction and customer loyalty over time.
