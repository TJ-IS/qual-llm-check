## A B S T R A C T

This paper reports on a design science research study that seeks to investigate how information service components affect consumers’ potential adoption of such services. More specifically, the paper develops a conceptual model that uses the theory of organizational information services (TOIS) and the unified theory of acceptance and use of technology (UTAUT) as a basis. The results indicate that individual constructs can be linked to service components. In turn, this result can potentially be instrumental in progress toward a deeper understanding of consumers’ adoption of information services and how this affects the development of such services and systems that support them.

## 1. Introduction

\- 2013 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

The development and use of information systems is changing dramatically. Instead of being developed for (and used by) organizational ‘‘users’’, information systems are increasingly more often being developed for consumers. When developing consumer information systems [50] (in contrast to developing information systems for organizational users), the over-riding focus shifts from efficiency and effectiveness to facilitating consumers’ service encounters and how they experience them. This shift is creating new types of information systems, which provide services to consumers, such as Internet Protocol Television (IPTV), or mobile services, such as Apple’s App Store for the iPhone. This new focus on the consumer therefore pushes services to the forefront of information systems research.

The growth of the Internet, globalization, and increasing automation have driven the reconfiguration of service value networks. New opportunities exist to develop innovative services, research and deliver new information services and business services [46]. A new dominant logic for marketing has been proposed that focuses on intangible resources and the co-creation of value and relationships, as opposed to tangible resources, embedded value, and transactions [53]. There is, however, a lack of formal representations of service systems that combine technology, business, and social aspects in the development of service innovation [46]. The current literature is also limited with regard to theories that explain how information services are different from information systems and how information services can be designed to support consumers.

The transition from products to services is a major driver in the current economy. This study focuses on understanding what this transition means from the point of view of developing information systems-based services, specifically for consumers. This focus differs from that in the current main stream of IS literature, which is frequently the development of systems for businesses or organizations [22]. Mathiassen and Sørensen [33] were among the first to suggest a service approach, and they have developed a theory of organizational information services (TOIS). They argued that we should view organizational information systems as services. Others have suggested a more scientific approach to the design and development of services [46].

Many of the studies concerning consumers’ adoption of services have used the technology acceptance model [54] as the basis for understanding consumer behavior. For example, the Decomposed Theory of Planned Behavior has been applied to aid in the explanation of the adoption behavior of early adopters of mobile services [40] and consumers’ intentions to use mobile services [37]. The unified theory of acceptance and use of technology has also been suggested for the investigation of the acceptance of mobile devices and services [5]. However, a limited number of studies have attempted to link consumer behavior to information systems development. Recently, this issue has been approached in terms of how consumers perceive and derive value from information systems they utilize [50]. More specifically, it has been argued that value co-creation must be recognized to comprehend differences between organizational users of information systems and consumers [50]. However, these studies do not provide insights into how to understand, e.g., how to measure, the perceived utility of developed information systems or how different types of services influence co-creation activities.

Our paper is motivated by the above gap in the literature. We base our research on the theory of organizational information services and the way in which this theory differentiates services in terms of peoples’ information processing capabilities, namely, how we address information uncertainty and equivocality. Second, we observe that the unified theory of acceptance and use of technology also provides a good starting point for understanding user behavior in the consumer context. Thus, our objective is to use these two theories to understand how users perceive value provided by different information service types. This objective leads us to our research question:

Do service components affect how consumers perceive the value of information services?

Our paper uses the design science research methodology (DSRM) [41] to conduct and present the research and findings. The DSRM was developed to provide a process and mental model for presenting and conducting design science research [19] in information systems. First, in Section 2, we review the literature to provide a basis for understanding the theoretical setting and a theoretical argument for linking UTAUT constructs to TOIS component types. Next, we design and develop an artifact to operationalize the research model. Section 3 provides the findings of our experiment. Finally, we discuss the implications of our findings and propose future directions for research.

## 2. Problem identification

Mathiassen and Sørensen [33] have provided a theoretical view of how we could begin to make the transition from a systems perspective, the artifact driven use of information technology, to a services-driven approach

The theory of organizational information services [33] draws upon the constructs of information processing and requirements to understand the design, consumption, and provision of information services. TOIS adopts Daft and Lengel’s [10] view that there are two fundamental information processing requirements: uncertainty and equivocality. Uncertainty relates to the availability and reliability of the information required to execute a task. Equivocality is the existence of multiple conflicting interpretations of a situation.

Mathiassen and Sørensen [33] have synthesized these concepts into a theory of organizational information services by applying contingency theory to describe the relationships between information services and work contexts in the form of four components of services: computational, adaptive, collaborative, and networking. Computational service components support users in transforming available and formalized information into organizational stimuli by following standardized and repeatable patterns of information processing. Adaptive service components interpret and transform available and emergent information into organizational stimuli by adapting patterns of information processing to specific work contexts. These components rely on relationships and allow involved actors to explore and debate interpretations during task execution. Networking service components aid users in producing information on phenomena in an organization and environment by following standardized and repeatable patterns of information processing. These components typically connect users to relevant information sources using IT artifacts, such as email systems, search engines, electronic libraries, mobile phones, and SMS messaging. Collaborative service components support users in producing information about phenomena in an organization and its environment through interpretation of the specific work context [33]. This theory, however, leaves service undefined in terms of information services for consumers.

In the following section, we present six propositions regarding how the theory of organizational information service components can be conceptually linked to the constructs of the UTAUT model. In a recent UTAUT study by Carlsson et al. [5], the hypotheses were simplified to contain only the relationships between constructs, excluding their moderators. We have taken a similar approach and used hypotheses similar to these hypotheses.

Proposition 1. Computational service and adaptive service components are tied to performance expectancy.

The first proposition states that computational service components support stable processing information requirements and competent actors who cope with equivocal tasks through the use of available information and the execution of adaptive services. Both service components fall under the category of the user’s need-todo-something and the use of information [33]. To do something, a task should be perceived as useful, which, using the definition from [11], can be defined as the degree to which a person believes that using a particular system would enhance his or her job performance. Usefulness is the key to allowing a user to do something, which implies a belief in the existence of a positive useperformance relationship [11].

With regard to performance expectancy, usefulness can be considered similar to the other constructs of which it is composed [54]: extrinsic motivation [13], job-fit [48], and relative advantage [35]. The usefulness of a computational or adaptive service component provides extrinsic motivation for a user to perform an activity to achieve a valued outcome [11,12]. This fact implies that if a service component were not useful, the user would not have any motivation to use that part of the service. The tasks a user may be required to undertake must be able to enhance the user’s job performance; hence, there is a similarity between job-fit and usefulness [48]. Using computational or adaptive service components should also provide some relative advantage over the precursor of the system for the user; hence, there is also a relationship between the usefulness of this system over other systems [12,35,42].

Research relating to performance expectancy for information services has indicated that the possibility of obtaining information while ‘on-the-go’ is considered useful. This potential is considered particularly useful if the information available can support ‘nomadic’ undertakings [27]. A nomadic undertaking implies an encounter relationship, and receiving information ‘on-the-go’ implies that the consumer needs to do something, which fits the requirements of a computational service and adaptive service.

Therefore, we suggest that performance expectancy (PE) has an influence on behavioral intention (BI) [Hypothesis 1].

Proposition 2. Computational service components are tied to effort expectancy.

Computational service components are characterized by low uncertainty and low equivocality; therefore, information is usually always available to the user and they are, therefore, not required to spend time interpreting the information available to them. Furthermore, computational service components have standardized inputs, processes, and outputs [33] and can be considered relatively easy for users to use. This definition alone leads us to propose that there is a link between effort expectancy and computational service components. Effort expectancy consists of three constructs, perceived ease of use, complexity, and ease of use [54], which has helped us to formulate our second proposition. Computational service components are free from uncertainty and equivocality, and therefore, by their very nature, should be perceived as easy to use [11,12,35] and not difficult to understand [48]. Computational service components are also characterized by their use of information, and hence, should require little effort to use.

Therefore, we suggest that effort expectancy (EE) has an influence on behavioral intention (BI) [Hypothesis 2].

Proposition 3. All four service components are tied to anxiety

Computer anxiety has been theorized in UTAUT to not have a direct influence on behavioral intention [54]. Anxiety can lead to reluctance in undertaking specific activities with a computer system and can impact an individual’s performance [39], his or her perceived ease of use [1], the usage of the computer, and selfefficacy [9] and generally leads to an outcome in which software is difficult to use [39]. We also observe that anxiety is linked to all four service components. We make this proposition based on the definition of anxiety: ‘‘an individual’s tendency to be uneasy, apprehensive, or fearful about the current or future use of a technology’’ [30]. Anxiety can impact an individual’s fear of computers, and hence, individuals would be less likely to use computers or would under use computers and their associated technologies and applications [28]. Therefore, anxiety relates to consumers’ general perceptions of technology use and can be characterized by a negative reaction toward technology. Because anxiety relates to the actual technology itself, it can be applied to all four service components, and hence, toward the use of the artifact.

Therefore, we suggest that anxiety (AX) has an influence on behavioral intention (BI) [Hypothesis 3].

Proposition 4. Computational service and adaptive service components are tied to self-efficacy.

Self-service technologies, especially IT-enabled service encounters, have attracted widespread interest in recent years [16]. Selfservice technologies can be defined as ‘‘technological interfaces that enable customers to produce a service independent of direct service employee involvement’’ [34]. Examples of such self-services include ticket machines, electronic check-in terminals at airports, and online-banking. The provision of self-service technologies is perceived as being a major contributor to a company’s overall success, particularly in terms of the ease of transaction processing in today’s fast-paced world [34]. Businesses are continuing to implement self-service technology components in their service offerings to save costs, open new channels for sales, and satisfy increasing demand [4]. Computational and adaptive service components are characterized by the need to do something; therefore, we have characterized these service components as containing self-service tasks.

To assess the general acceptance of a self-service technology, it is necessary to examine the different stages of the adoption process. An important pre-condition imposes the user’s awareness of the respective technology, and furthermore, is determined by their ability, i.e., the perceived capability (of the user) to perform the behavior [49]. Self-efficacy can be defined as the judgment of one’s ability to use a technology (e.g., a computer) to accomplish a particular job or task [54]. Therefore, we can link self-efficacy directly to the adaptive and computational service components of TOIS. Furthermore, for services for which the involved actor needs to do something, we can conclude that the service components must be self-efficient in that the actor can perform the task at hand without seeking help from others, such as the service provider.

Therefore, we suggest that self-efficacy (SE) has an influence on behavioral intention (BI) [Hypothesis 4].

Proposition 5. Collaboration service components are tied to social influences.

In recent studies of mobile technology usage, social factors were found to have a significant influence [14]. According to the literature, subjective norms are particularly important for young users in the adoption of mobile services [32]. This group is easily influenced by social norms, peers and their surroundings [14]. Social norms were also found to have an influence on the intention to use a mobile service [20,26]. Push-to-talk technologies are also affected by social norms and have had an influence on usage of the technology for communication purposes [14]. Mobile services should also enable users to express their personal and social identity [37].

Reid et al. [44] have investigated the psychology of SMS text messaging. It was found that text messages helped users to develop new and deeper relationships with ‘‘text mates’’ and altered the way they expressed themselves. Users were more comfortable saying things in a text message than they were in faceto-face conversations. Many text message users prefer to text their closest friends than speak to them on their mobiles, and a significant proportion prefer to text rather than meet face-to-face. The immediacy, mobility, and perpetual contact offered by mobile phones allows for near conversational levels of texting, so the mobile phone often resembles online chat. Reid et al. [44] also suggested that the combination of the spontaneous sociability of the chat room and the artful editability of email renders texting appealing to key user groups. The researchers also established the existence of text circles, which is a well-defined and close-knit groups of contacts with whom texters regularly, and often, continuously exchange messages [44].

This finding relates to the model of PC utilization in that Social Factors are individuals’ internalization of the reference group’s subjective culture and specific interpersonal agreements that the individual has come to with others in specific social situations [48] and to UTAUT in that social influence is defined as the degree to which an individual perceives that important others believe that he or she should use the new system [54]. Therefore, we propose that whenever users must collaborate, social factors affect the use of collaboration technologies.

Therefore, we suggest that social influence (SI) has an influence on behavioral intention (BI) [Hypothesis 5].

Proposition 6. Networking service components are tied to facilitating conditions.

Delivering relevant information at the right time to the right person and in the right place is always a key idea in mobile services. With mobile computing, mobile advertising can be highly personalized and can also easily be focused on a consumer’s current location [31]. Mobile advertising can take the form of a push (location-sensitive information is automatically sent to users based on their location) or pull (users request services based on their location) service. The most common form is mobile advertising, a service provider promoting its services by sending SMS text messages to users according to their location [31]. Attitudes toward location-based advertising among youth are often positive, but only if users have the ability to control what type of advertisements they receive and from whom they receive them [23]

Leung et al. [31] state that the informativeness of mobile advertising is a key determinant of the underlying economic benefits in that mobile computing can include consumer localization and personalization. Localization describes the ability to locate a mobile user using positioning technologies such as GPS. Personalization is also a key factor because the mobile device can act as a personal assistant and offer unique opportunities in the user’s environment [31]. An individual’s knowledge or experience is a central psychological determinant of consumer behavior. Existing knowledge can affect the cognitive process relating to a consumers decision and can also affect their ability to understand the features and usage of an innovation. The innovation is considered less complex if the consumer already possesses a certain amount of knowledge about the innovation. In this case, knowledge of mobile advertising is based on the consumer’s existing knowledge of mobile communications. Mobile communication is the technology behind the basis for mobile advertising. Therefore, the more experienced a consumer is with mobile communications, the less difficult the use of mobile advertising will appear to him or her [3]

Facilitating conditions are defined as the degree to which an individual believes that an organizational and technical infrastructure exists to support use of the system [54]. Therefore, we can place our link facilitating conditions into the networking service components of TOIS. Users must be aware of the conditions that are available to support their use. Users assume that services suiting their needs, as well as the technical infrastructure to support them, such as GPS technology and network infrastructure, are adequately available.

Therefore, we suggest that facilitating conditions (FC) have an influence on use behavior (UB) [Hypothesis 6]. In addition to the above six hypotheses, which are conceptually linked to TOIS constructs, we will also test how behavioral intention (BI) has an influence on use behavior (UB) [Hypothesis 7], as suggested by the original UTAUT model.

Finally, the research model has been constructed based on the six propositions and subsequent seven UTAUT hypotheses (see Fig. 1). However, we faced a challenge in determining how to study these relationships. In the following section, we propose a solution for this dilemma.

## 3. Solution: artifact

To study how TOIS components affect consumers’ perceptions of the value of information services, we decided to design and develop a software artifact. More specifically, we chose to develop a simulation of a mobile service targeted primarily to consumers. The argument was that this simulation enabled us to provide a means of ensuring that the depicted propositions of TOIS components were built in to the software artifact. In this way, it would be possible to perform an analysis of the UTAUT constructs to determine if the above propositions hold and to determine how TOIS components affects service behavior and intention to use information services.

Second, at the time of the study, it was difficult to find a suitable service that would encompass all of the TOIS components and that would be available for empirical testing. Therefore, we turned to an earlier study, with which we developed requirements for a suitable service: City Wanderer mobile service [51]. This study focused on how consumers perceive a new type of mobile service called Presence. For mobile service providers, handset manufacturers, mobile industry specialists, and mobile UI designers, mobile presence, particularly location-based presence, has become a hot topic. In Japan and South Korea, some forms of mobile presence have already been implemented in commercial mobile services and handset devices [21]. According to Nokia, presence allows the user to do the following:

\- Display to others your location, preferred method of communication, mood and availability.

\- Display different availability statuses to different groups of people.

\- Be in control of what information is displayed and to whom

\- Know where a person is and what he or she is doing before you call him or her [36].

## 4. Operationalization: artifact design and development

For this research, City Wanderer was designed as a desktop simulation of a mobile service rather than a real-world artifact. The artifact has two main parts. On the left side of the artifact is a map layout of a fictional city. The participants are located in the city, and their current location is indicated by a red dot. The user can ‘‘walk’’ around the city by using the directional arrows at the bottom of the screen. On the right hand side of the artifact is a mobile phone. Participants were asked to imagine that there were holding this phone as they walked around the city. The prototype works exactly as a mobile phone would operate, with the buttons performing the same actions as those on a real mobile phone.

To operationalize the research model (see Fig. 1), we needed to consider the tasks and information services enacted by the user. Table 1 summarizes this task level analysis. The first task involves making a ticket reservation for a movie at a nearby cinema. In this task, the location-based service displays a nearby cinema and the movies showing there for the user. This task involves the selection of movies and session times and may vary based on the availability of session times, depending on the movie and number of tickets requested, and it is suited to an adaptive service because the number of conflicting movies, session times, and availability causes the equivocality of the task to be high. The uncertainty of the task is low, as information on movie times and sessions is always available. This task involves the use of information on movie and session times and implies the use of a relationship service, as the user must already have an account with the service provider to make a payment for the movie.

A second task involves determining preferences for receiving information once arriving at a specific location. The user is provided with a list of possible service components from which to select. These components include services such as nearby restaurants, activities, local information, and events occurring in the area. Once the user has indicated their preferences, he or she is sent to the service provider. When the user arrives at a specific location and has opted to receive services, if that service is available at that location, the service provider will send the user a message stating that such service is available. The user can then decide to discard the message or make use of it by performing an act such as purchasing a ticket.

This task can be divided into two separate service types. The first task, selecting the services you want to receive, is suited to an adaptive service, as users must decide which service types are the most desirable to receive, offset by the receipt of messages from the service provider that they do not want. This task will involve the use of information, as the user must select from a list of predefined service components he or she wishes to receive and must have a relationship with the service provider to receive the services requested and implies that the user has a relationship with the service provider. The second task, receiving automatic messages when you approach a location, is suited to a networking service. The user may have elected to receive a certain type of service but are uncertain that the information will be available in a specific location. Networking service components will connect the user to relevant information sources by sending messages to the user. Information will be produced on the fly and sent to the user when he or she arrives at a specific location. Networking service components are encounter relationships, and this type of relationship is applicable to this situation, as there is no ongoing communication between the user and the service provider.

A third task requires the user to change his or her presence profile, find people nearby and view their presence profiles. This task requires the user to set a presence status, such as ‘looking for food’, and set his or her status to available. Any friends of the user who are nearby can find the user on their maps, view the user’s status, and then negotiate to meet. This task can also be divided into two separate service types. The first task, setting the presence profile, is suited to a computational service type. With this service type, the user selects a standardized set of presence statuses such as ‘available’ or ‘not available’ and writes a brief statement about what he or she is doing. This service allows the user’s friends to view the user’s current status and availability. The second task, negotiating with a friend to meet, is suitable for a collaborative service. This task requires a user to look for a friend, find one, view the friend’s presence status and availability, and send a message to that friend asking to meet. The two friends may negotiate via messages and arrange a time and place to meet. Information will be produced in this scenario, as the users will communicate with each other, and it is implied that there must be a pre-existing relationship between the two.

In a fourth task, the user wants to view his or her current location and receive local information. The user will be shown a map on the screen on which they can view his or her current location. The user can zoom in or out on the map to obtain a better idea of his or her location. If desired, the user can choose to view local information, which will be shown on the screen in textual format. This process involves a standardized and repeatable pattern of information that involves checking the user’s current location, processing it, and displaying a map for the user. This task is suited to a computational service, as there is no uncertainty in obtaining map information and current location cannot be misinterpreted. During this task, the user will make use of information only for the time for which it is needed.

In a fifth task, the user requests the location of a service and directions to get there. This task requires the user to select an item to find the location a service such as an ATM. The user’s current location will be determined, and the service provider will determine which ATM is closest to the user. The system will display a map showing the location of the ATM, on which the user can zoom in and out to view his or her own location as well. The map will also include a recommended route to the ATM. The user can also choose to view a textual description of the route. This task is also similar to the previous task in that the user’s current location must first be determined, processed, and displayed on the map, but in this task, the location of the requested item and a suggested route are also displayed. During this task, the user will make use of information only for the time for which it is needed.

Finally, in a sixth task, the user requests a list of attractions, festivals and events. This task requires the user to choose to find events near his or her current location or citywide events. The user will then be shown a list of events based on his or her selection and has the option to make payment for any event he or she wishes to attend. This task is suited to a computational service, as the user must select an attraction, which will then be displayed on the screen. The interpretation of information is not required, as the user is simply requesting and then receiving information. In this instance, the user will make use of information only for the time for which it is needed.

## 5. Evaluation: controlled experiment

We evaluated the artifact by presenting the City Wanderer artifact to potential users of location-based mobile services, i.e., consumers. According to Hevner et al. [19], a controlled experiment is a possible method of measuring the efficiency and utility of an artifact. Our experiment follows a scenario-based approach by first asking participants to complete a series of tasks in a laboratory session, as described in the previous section. According to Kjeldskov and Skov [25], it is possible to recreate and simulate significant elements of real-life use situations in a laboratory setting and thereby increase the level of realism, while maintaining a high level of control and avoiding the complexities of field experiments, such as physical user movement in an environment, over which researchers have little control [24].

In our experiment, the participants used the artifact, and afterwards, answered an online questionnaire about their use experiences. Initially, participants were shown a 2-min video that explained how to use the City Wanderer artifact and then asked to complete a series of tasks using the artifact. The questionnaire was based on the UTAUT constructs described in Fig. 1. The questions used a 7-point Likert scale, with 1 being ‘strongly disagree’ and 7 being ‘strongly agree’.

Data for the study were collected from undergraduate students in their second and third year and graduate classes at a university in New Zealand. Based on our research model, the minimum required sample size is 60 [8]. We obtained a final sample size of 100. The gender distribution of the sample was 68% male and 32% female. The majority of participants were 21–25 years old (60%), with the next largest group being 16–20 years old (25%). Potential participants were emailed a request to participate in the study and were provided with a link to download the City Wanderer artifact. Participants were asked to complete a series of tasks using City

Wanderer, and upon completion of the tasks, were asked to complete an online questionnaire based on their experiences using City Wanderer. All participants completed the same set of tasks in the same order. On average, it took the participants 20–30 min to complete the tasks and answer the questionnaire. The questionnaire used for this study is composed of items already used in the existing literature [5,54].

The partial least square technique was chosen as the analysis method for the evaluation. This use is consistent with UTAUT research [54]. This technique is also useful when the sample size is small and the number of indicators is high [8]. We used SmartPLS 2.0 (M3) Beta [45] to perform the PLS analysis. PLS was used to test the entire sample. Before we ran the PLS analysis, we tested the sample for common method bias. We performed a Harman’s onefactor test, including all items in a factor analysis [38,43]. The data did not indicate any evidence of common method bias.

Item reliability, internal consistency, and discriminant validity were used as criteria to ensure that the model has acceptable measurement properties. An examination of the loadings of the items assessed the individual item reliability. A minimum value of 0.7 is used as criterion for the reliability of individual items and as a cut off point for internal consistencies [2]; however, we used a slightly less conservative approach and included items with loadings close to 0.7. All assessment values are provided in Table 2. It is commonly recommended that all average variance extracted (AVE) values be higher than 0.5.

To test the discriminant validity, the square roots of the AVE values were compared to the correlations among latent variables, as shown in Table 3. The square roots of the AVEs are shown in the main diagonal. The off-diagonal elements are the correlations among latent variables. For adequate discriminant validity, the square root of the AVE should be greater than the off-diagonal elements in the corresponding rows and columns [2].

The structural model comprises the hypothesized relationships among latent variables. The PLS bootstrap procedure was then used to test the hypotheses. We used bootstrapping with 500 subsamples to test the statistical significance of each path coefficient using t-tests [7]. The test results are provided in Table 4. Fig. 2 illustrates the results of the PLS analysis and the completed research model.

The explanatory power of the model, or nomological validity, is assessed using the R<sup>2</sup> value of the endogenous construct in the structural model estimation. It is recommended that $R ^ { 2 }$ be at least 0.10 for the latent constructs to be judged adequate [15]. All of the $R ^ { 2 }$ values satisfy this recommendation, and as such, the nomological validity is satisfactory. Therefore, the model explains approximately 29% of the total variability of behavioral intention and approximately 46% of the total variability of usage behavior. Finally, the results of the power analysis for this research indicate that we achieved a power level of 0.76 and that this level can be considered sufficiently close to the recommended value of 0.8 [18]. Thus, we conclude that we have adequate power to support our research.

Based on the results presented, we can accept Proposition 4, which states that computational service and adaptive service components are tied to self-efficacy. We can also accept Proposition 5, which states that collaboration service components are tied to social influences, and finally, we can accept Proposition 6, which states that networking service components are tied to facilitating conditions. Furthermore, based on our analysis results, we also found that statistical support at least partly explains consumers’ behavioral intentions in terms of the usage of consumer information services. These intentions are further explained by social influence, self-efficacy and facilitating condition constructs from our research model (see Fig. 2).

Our results indicate that social influences are important to understanding collaborative service components (P5 and H5). This conclusion is consistent with the theory of organizational information services, as it calls for understanding and support of the relationship with the consumer and the service. Furthermore, self-efficacy (P4 and H4) appears to explain that consumers also view computational service components as information processing, which is also similar to the original TOIS. Self-efficacy also appears to have an effect on adaptive service components, but surprisingly, social influences appear not to have any. This result contradicts the original TOIS argument. The theory of organizational information services holds that collaborative but adaptive service components are relationship based. Our findings indicate that consumers may not share this perception. Furthermore, facilitating conditions (P6 and H6) have an influence on consumers’ perceptions of networking service components and how they want to encounter service situations and utilize them to produce information.

We summarize the findings as follows. When designing and developing collaborating service components for consumers, we should focus on social influences of the service, which support both the high uncertainty and high equivocality needs of information processing. Furthermore, when designing and developing adaptive or computational service components for consumers, we should focus on self-efficacy related features of services. In the case of adaptive service components, the high equivocality needs of consumer information processing should be emphasized. Finally, with networking service components, facilitating conditions should be the focal point of design and development efforts.

## 6. Implications

Our findings indicate that use of the theory of organizational information services [33] and the unified theory of acceptance and use of technology [5,54] can at least partly explain why consumers would like to use consumer information service components. This fact can potentially have several implications for research and may enable IS researchers to be at the forefront of designing and developing services for consumers and so advance the discussion of service sciences [46,53].

First, we found that using TOIS concepts enabled us to provide a theoretical rationale for the development of the artifact. It was challenging to separate the distinct service components within the developed artifact because of the heterogeneous nature of the features and because a task within one service type may in fact impact a task in another service component. For example, the adaptive task of setting consumer preferences to receive automatic messages impacted the networking service task of receiving those messages. This relationship implies that the boundaries between service components are blurred, which supports our argument for extending the view of IT-enabled services for consumers [50]. Service development appears to be inherently complex, and we do need to examine the use of the artifact more than merely the artifact itself [33].

Second, our findings indicate that when the theory of organizational information services is utilized within a consumer context, it can potentially enhance the usage of such services. For services for which the consumer needs to do something (computational and adaptive service components), the designers must ensure that these services are self-efficient, i.e., that consumers must be able to complete the tasks on their own. Collaborative service components are impacted by the social influences of the technologies utilized in the service, and networking service components are impacted by the conditions that facilitate their use, as well as the previous experience of the consumer.

Third, we have demonstrated how design science research methodology [41] and design science research [19] in general can be used for theory development. We have used the DSRM to conceptually justify the design and development of an artifact, which in turn, is utilized to theorize how consumers perceive services. This progress potentially opens avenues for other researchers’ use of the methodology for theory development within the design science research community, and perhaps, in the emerging service sciences field as well.

Finally, our findings may be of interest to researchers in service science. Our emerging theory indicates that consumers perceive collaborative services as social. This supposition is interesting when thinking about the co-creation of services [17,34]. As a discipline, service marketing has argued that we should engage consumers in co-creation activities to improve service quality. Our findings suggest that such engagement might not be essential for all consumer information service components. We recognize that co-creation would be important in situations in which uncertainty and equivocality are high, i.e., in collaborative service components, and social influence has a strong impact on services. However, other service components may not benefit as much from cocreation efforts.

## 7. Conclusions

Our paper presents a design science research study that aims to understand how consumers perceive and potentially adopt information technology-based services. We used design science research methodology [41] to guide our research and present our findings in this paper. We extended the theory of organizational information services and the unified theory of acceptance and use of technology to develop a software artifact. The artifact was an innovative mobile service called ‘‘City Wanderer’’, for which the consumer requirements were derived from an earlier study [51]. In turn, these consumer requirements were linked to organizational information service types [33], and later, during the evaluation phase, to UTAUT constructs [5,54] for a laboratory experiment.

Our study contributes in four ways. First, the research provides further insight to be used in understanding consumers’ perceptions and deriving of value from the information systems they utilize [50]. The results also promote a more heterogeneous view of information systems-enabled services for consumers, which is consistent with [33,46]. Second, our results indicate that distinct service components can be potentially linked to specific UTAUT constructs, which in turn, enables us to further explain how consumers perceive different components of information services and how these should be designed and developed. Third, our research has attempted to use design science research methodology [41] for theory development through the use of a software artifact. Finally, we note that our findings may be of interest to service science researchers. Our work suggests that not all service components may benefit equally from service co-creation efforts [17,34] and that these efforts should perhaps target primarily collaborative services.

This work has various limitations. One of the limitations relates to the scales we used to measure the core constructs in the UTAUT model [5,54]. Based on recommendations from Chin et al. (1996), each of the core constructs was operationalized by using the highest loading items from each of the respective scales. By eliminating low loading items, these items were no longer represented in some of the core constructs. Second, we recognize that the current operationalization of how the UTAUT constructs are mapped to the theory of organizational information services is a limitation. Because of the research design, we had to rely on a conceptual linkage between the theories. Nevertheless, the results are encouraging, but we should also consider alternative methods of further integrating the theories. Third, our artifact was a desktop simulation of a real mobile device. Although the artifact was designed to work exactly as a mobile device would work, in reality, participants used it on a desktop computer in a laboratory setting. This circumstance could remove some of the realism in the participants’ minds, and hence, could impact their impressions while using the artifact.

Finally, we recognize a bias in our population sample in that the genders were significantly skewed toward males. We therefore suggest that some of the hypotheses may be biased because of the sample limitations and that a more balanced population sample may produce different results. Furthermore, we also recognize that our sample contains primarily students, and we acknowledge that this is a limitation. However, there are several examples of studies in the IS literature that have presented results using students as the sample [29,47,52]. As noted by Carlsson et al. [6], mobile services are more actively used by younger people than older people; therefore, we may have captured the usage patterns one would expect from this type of analysis and the general direction of the effect. Therefore, we recognize that this weakness is not a major limitation in the current study. Nevertheless, we also recognize that in further research, we should seek to use a larger representative sample to further test the model.

We perceive that our research opens a rich avenue for future research. First, we want to encourage other researchers to use design science research methodology for theory development. Our attempt was one of the first of its kind, and we recognize that more work should be performed to address the challenges we found with this study. Second, although we were pleased that we were able to map the theory of organizational information services and the unified theory of acceptance and use of technology together, we were also the first to say that further research should be completed. Linking theory development to design a science research process is challenging, especially when using a software artifact to do so. Finally, our findings suggest that co-creation may be more efficient for collaborative services, and others may introduce very interesting possibilities for service science researchers.
