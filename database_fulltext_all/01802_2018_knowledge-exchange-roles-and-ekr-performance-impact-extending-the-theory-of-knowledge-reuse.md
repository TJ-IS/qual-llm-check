# Knowledge exchange roles and EKR performance impact: extending the theory of knowledge reuse

Nicole Forsgren<sup>a</sup>, Rajiv Sabherwal<sup>b</sup> and Alexandra Durcikova<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>DevOps Research and Assessment (DORA ), Portland, OR , USA; <sup>b</sup>Department of MIS, Sam M. Walton College of Business, University of Arkansas, Fayetteville, AR , USA; <sup>c</sup>Division of MIS, Price College of Business, The University of Oklahoma, Norman, OK, USA

## ABSTRACT

Organisations are spending considerable resources on electronic knowledge repositories (EKRs) that should have a positive efect on employees’ EKR performance impact. Seeking to build on and extend the theory of knowledge reuse, this paper focuses on the various roles individuals play in knowledge exchange, and how the knowledge exchange role mediates the efect of previously studied individual perceptual variables (perceived time pressure, extrinsic and intrinsic rewards, and knowledge contribution self-eficacy) on EKR performance impact. In order to identify the various knowledge exchange roles, we simultaneously consider both knowledge contribution process (embedded vs. separate activity) and knowledge reuse (internal EKR, external EKR, colleagues, manuals, knowledge self-reliance). When work is highly specialised and technical as in the context of system administrators, we observed four roles: Self-reliant Contributors, Detached Moderates, Involved Brokers, and Reluctant Limited Users. Results indicate that the efects of intrinsic rewards and knowledge contribution self-eficacy on EKR performance impact are fully mediated by knowledge exchange role. Perceived time pressure does not influence either knowledge exchange roles or EKR performance impact. Overall, the paper provides insights into roles individuals play in knowledge exchange, and extends the theory of knowledge reuse.

## KEYWORDS

Knowledge exchange roles; theory of knowledge reuse; knowledge contribution; knowledge seeking; electronic knowledge repositories; performance impact

## 1. Introduction

In the current knowledge economy, a key challenge facing organisations is the ability to both capture and reuse knowledge held by their employees (Hajro, Gibson, & Pudelko, 2015; Huber, 2001). Therefore, organisations spend considerable resources developing systems, called electronic knowledge repositories (EKRs), to capture knowledge so that it can be organised, stored, and reused by others (e.g., Argote, McEvily, & Reagans, 2003). However, the factors influencing EKR adoption are not well understood (Bock, Sabherwal, & Qian, 2008; Majchrzak, Rice, King, Malhotra, & Ba, 2000), and inconsistent results have been obtained regarding the efects of contextual factors on EKR performance impact (e.g., Hung, Durcikova, Lai, & Lin, 2011). These results may be inconsistent because prior studies on EKR have focused on only a part of the knowledge exchange process between employees, such as either knowledge seeking (e.g., Chen, Hsieh, Van de Vliert, & Huang, 2015; Kankanhalli, Tan, & Wei, 2005b), knowledge contribution (e.g., Cheng & Guo, 2015; Wasko & Faraj, 2005), or simply on overall EKR use (e.g., Kulkarni, Ravindran, & Freeze, 2006).

Recognising that “an organisation’s [EKR] must feature a dynamic balance of contribution and seeking behaviours” (He & Wei, 2009, p. 826), recent articles have started investigating knowledge contribution and seeking behaviours together (e.g., Wang, Gray, & Meister, 2014; Wang & Noe, 2010). However, knowledge exchange does not exclusively occur through organisational EKRs; rather an EKR functions as a complementary source of knowledge to online searches and colleagues (e.g., Gray & Durcikova, 2006). Thus, employees enact knowledge exchange roles that include knowledge contribution and seeking from various sources. Accordingly, this paper develops a classification of the alternative roles individuals adopt in knowledge exchange through an EKR, while considering their behaviours with respect to the EKR as well as other knowledge sources. Moreover, this paper examines how the knowledge exchange role adopted by an individual depends on the individual’s perceptions (e.g., about perceived time pressure and rewards), and how the role then influences the impact of EKR on the individual’s performance.

Roles are not unique to knowledge exchange and can be also seen in other contexts, such as the stock market. In the stock market, we can see that individual perceptions – such as what one believes about the economy, how sensitive one is to risk, or an individual’s knowledge of the stock market – influence the role one adopts, which in turn afects the financial outcomes. For example, individuals who believe they are good at selecting the right stocks, seek long-term gains, and have some time to research their stocks, may assume the role of long-term trader in the stock market. Individuals who believe they are extremely good at predicting the stock markets, seek regular returns, and can spend considerable time every day to trade, may become day traders, buying and selling stocks throughout the day to maximise their gains. Individuals who do not consider themselves good at selecting stocks may choose to pay a trader to select a portfolio for them or stay out of the stock market altogether, instead investing in other areas (e.g., a savings account or real estate). Thus, an individual’s financial gains from participating (or not participating) in the stock market may be mediated by the role the individual adopts in the stock market. This example illustrates that people assume roles based on their perceptions and abilities, which in turn influence their performance in the relevant context.

The existence of knowledge worker roles is not a new idea (Markus, 2001), but we have yet to identify and empirically examine the diferent knowledge exchange roles individuals assume in the knowledge management (KM) context. To address this gap in the literature, this paper adopts a more holistic view of knowledge exchange that includes a richer conceptualisation of the various knowledge contribution and seeking behaviours (Burton-Jones & Straub, 2006). This paper does this in two respects. First, it explicitly includes knowledge contribution and knowledge seeking. Second, it considers knowledge seeking from sources other than the EKR itself. In addition, building on the theory of knowledge reuse (Markus, 2001), this paper argues that the knowledge exchange role an employee assumes will mediate the influence of contextual factors (beliefs and incentives) on EKR performance impact.

The two research questions that this paper seeks to answer are:

(1) What are the alternative knowledge exchange roles that a user can adopt with respect to an EKR?

(2) To what degree do these knowledge exchange roles mediate the relationship between individual perceptions and the performance impact of EKR?

Prior literature is used to develop a theoretical model related to the above research questions. Overall, this paper proposes that an individual’s knowledge exchange role mediates the efect of the individual’s perceptions on EKR performance impact. This is shown in Figure 1, which depicts the broad research model for the study.

The detailed research model, which builds on the above broad model by including the efects of four specific individual perceptions and behaviours, is partially supported by an empirical study of system administrators (sysadmins) in a Fortune 100 company, with two of the four mediated efects being supported. Overall, the paper contributes to the literature by: (1) identifying the knowledge exchange roles employees assume while contributing and reusing knowledge via EKR and other means; and (2) providing empirical evidence that these knowledge exchange roles act as a mediator between individual perceptual variables and the performance impact of EKR. By doing so, this paper extends the theory of knowledge reuse and provides insights into how future research may view knowledge exchange behaviours.

## 2. Theoretical foundation: theory of knowledge reuse

The theory of knowledge reuse represents an initial step in conceptualising knowledge exchange rather than just knowledge contribution or reuse, with an emphasis on the use of technology in KM, and claims three things pertaining to knowledge workers: roles, the antecedents of roles, and the outcomes of roles (Markus, 2001). In the first claim, knowledge exchange roles are comprised of several behaviours. Specifically, knowledge workers can enact roles as knowledge producers (those who document knowledge), knowledge consumers (those who retrieve existing knowledge), or in a combination “knowledge exchange” role, which includes both knowledge producing and consuming behaviours.

Second, the theory identifies three broad categories of individual perceptual variables that are antecedents to knowledge exchange roles: costs of knowledge contribution and seeking, incentives to share and seek knowledge, and intermediary activities in seeking and contributing knowledge. Therefore, in investigations of knowledge workers, costs (such as perceived time pressure experienced at work), incentives (such as extrinsic and intrinsic rewards), and the ability to perform intermediary activities (such as cleaning and indexing knowledge) will influence the knowledge exchange behaviours enacted in the role they adopt.

Third, successful knowledge contribution or seeking (the dependent variable) is directly influenced by the knowledge exchange role that the knowledge worker assumes. Markus states the dependent variable in the theory is “successful knowledge transfer or reuse” (Markus, 2001, p.60). In studies on organisational EKR, this dependent variable can be conceptualised as performance impact attributed to the use of an EKR, such as efectiveness or eficiency in completing work (e.g., Gallivan, Eynon, & Rai, 2003). These performance impacts can include both objective and perceived impacts on performance (Goel, Johnson, Junglas, & Ives, 2013; Janz & Prasarnphanich, 2003; Seddon, 1997).

In summary, the theory of knowledge reuse proposes that knowledge workers assume a knowledge exchange role, which is comprised of knowledge contribution and seeking behaviours. The specific knowledge exchange role an individual assumes is influenced by costs, incentives, and intermediary activities. The adopted knowledge exchange role, in turn, impacts work outcomes resulting from EKR use.

## 2.1. Extension of theory of knowledge reuse

This paper extends the theory of knowledge reuse as introduced by Markus (2001) based on our understanding of KM today. First, this paper extends the knowledge exchange role itself. In the original theory, knowledge exchange roles are comprised of knowledge seeking and contribution exclusively via EKR. However, according to current literature, knowledge exchange is not limited to organisational EKRs, and other sources of knowledge are available to knowledge workers (e.g., Gray & Durcikova, 2006; Wang et al., 2014). Therefore, this paper extends these two behaviours to include sources outside of an organisational EKR, such as online searches and colleagues.

Second, this paper argues that the knowledge exchange role mediates the efects of individual perceptions on the impact of EKR on individual performance (or, simply, EKR performance impact). The original theory states that costs, incentives, and intermediary activities influence knowledge exchange roles and are required for successful contribution and seeking of knowledge, but does not require that they directly influence EKR performance impact (the dependent variable). That is, the theory allows for an individual’s knowledge exchange role to mediate the relationship between individual perceptual variables and EKR performance impact. Two studies of such a mediated relationship (Gray & Meister, 2004; Wang et al., 2014) found that knowledge seeking mediates the relationship between individual perceptual variables and outcome. Thus, this paper argues this mediating relationship holds when multiple knowledge exchange behaviours are studied simultaneously.

Since costs are a very narrow conceptualisation of one’s belief about the ability to contribute knowledge, we reconceptualise this aspect of the theory to include other beliefs regarding the ability to contribute knowledge. Costs, as suggested by Markus (2001), are generally viewed as a restrictive component. Therefore, there is a need to broaden the factor that describes one’s belief about the ability to contribute knowledge to include a contributing component, knowledge contribution self-eficacy (KCSE).

A small deviation from the theory of knowledge reuse was made by omitting the intermediary activities as an antecedent to knowledge exchange roles. Markus defines a knowledge intermediary as someone who “prepares knowledge for reuse by eliciting it, indexing it, summarising it, sanitising it, packaging it, and who performs various roles in dissemination and facilitation” (Markus, 2001, p. 61). The intermediary role was omitted because it was not pertinent in the context of this study: the work was highly specialised and technical, so that only experts would have been able to index and summarise. However, the organisation decided their expertise was needed in doing the work, not in curating the knowledge in the EKR. This absence of intermediary activities is also seen in other contemporary organisations, but the exclusion of intermediaries is noted as a limitation of the study. While Markus (2001) does state that IT can step in as an intermediary, with examples given in categorising and disseminating documents, these functionalities were quite basic in the technology studied and are now considered commonplace, and therefore not included in the research design. Thus, advanced AI or machine learning techniques were not used to summarise or abstract the knowledge in the EKR, thereby providing intermediary capabilities via technology.

This paper begins with theoretical development by focusing on the centre of our research model and examining knowledge contribution and seeking behaviours. In doing so, the focal individuals’ roles in knowledge exchange are identified. Then, this paper presents the development of the hypotheses for the mediated efect of individual perceptions on EKR performance impact through knowledge exchange roles.

## 3. Knowledge exchange roles

## 3.1. Knowledge contribution and seeking behaviours

This paper includes the role of the knowledge worker as central, and not exogenous, to the theory of knowledge reuse (Markus, 2001). This is an appropriate extension to the original theory for several reasons.

First, the roles of individuals have been highlighted in other processes. For example, in the difusion of innovations, individuals have been classified based on timing of their adoption into innovators, early adopters, early majority, etc. Similarly, individuals involved in the software development process, have been classified into senior managers, users, internal developers, vendors, etc. The underlying arguments in these streams of literature are that individuals with diferent roles perceive things diferently during the process. Similarly, this paper focuses on the various roles of individuals in the knowledge reuse process, and examines how an individual’s perceptions influence his or her role.

Second, prior literature has investigated behaviours such as knowledge seeking as a mediator (Gray & Meister, 2004; Wang et al., 2014), showing that a knowledge exchange behaviour can act as a mediator between individual perceptions and outcome variables. This paper broadens the view of behaviours as mediators by including the individual’s role which represents a combination of several knowledge exchange behaviours.

Third, the inclusion of a knowledge role as a mediator within knowledge reuse theory is consistent with Markus (2001). Her paper includes descriptions of performing combinations of knowledge exchange behaviours (as roles), as well as the individual perceptions that are likely to influence knowledge seeking and contribution behaviours. Therefore, this paper argues that while not explicitly presented as such, the original theory does allow for such an extension.

Finally, role theory suggests that individual perceptions influence a characteristic pattern of behaviours, which combine to form a role, and that role impacts outcomes (Biddle, 1979). We note that prior literature (e.g., Biddle, 1979; Burt, 1982) conceptualises role as a characteristic set of behaviour patterns; this aligns with the concept of role presented in this paper. The theoretical extension in this paper is based on this conceptualisation of a role.

The use of empirically derived knowledge exchange roles is consistent with prior literature in role theory. We utilise role theory as our theoretical lens because it enables us to study the characteristic behaviour patterns we wish to include in our study, called roles (Biddle, 1979), which emerge as individuals perform knowledge work. A role that an individual assumes can be prescribed by the organisation (e.g., accountant, help-desk analyst) or can emerge while engaging in a work-related activity (e.g., leader, follower). We adopt this latter definition because knowledge exchange roles emerge as individuals perform knowledge activities, rather than resulting from organisational assignment. For example, it is not uncommon to call a knowledge worker who only sources and never contributes knowledge a “free rider” (Fulk, Heino, Flanagin, & Monge, 2004); while it is unlikely that an organisation would assign this role to any individual, a knowledge worker may still adopt this role through their emergent behaviours. Thus, role theory would label this knowledge worker’s behaviour as the role of free rider. The relevance of role theory in information systems (IS) research was highlighted by Galletta and Heckman (1990) when they stated, “Role theory might assist in providing greater power for researchers and practitioners to describe, analyse, and understand the impacts of technology in a complex and ambiguous environment” (p. 182). Because no such study identifying the types of knowledge exchange roles has been conducted before, we argue that an empirically derived set of knowledge exchange roles is an important first step of discovery in this research area.

An individual’s knowledge exchange role is comprised of knowledge contribution and seeking behaviours (Markus, 2001; Wang & Noe, 2010). This study includes two contribution and five seeking behaviours. The first contribution behaviour, knowledge contribution through an embedded process, represents knowledge capture that occurs while completing work tasks, and includes information about the task. The second contribution behaviour, knowledge contribution through a separate process, is distinct from one’s work tasks and is “a deliberate, after-the-fact strategy of filtering, indexing, packaging, and sanitising knowledge for later reuse, as in the creation of … expert help files” (Markus, 2001, p.60). Such documentation requires structure, completeness, and elaborate details that carefully reconstruct and describe a task at hand (Schultze, 1999) while providing information to maximise understanding by diferent users (e.g., Bailey, Kandogan, Haber, & Maglio, 2007).

Recognising that even after EKR introduction in an organisation, individuals continue to source knowledge through other means (Gray & Durcikova, 2006), this study includes five knowledge seeking behaviours. The first, knowledge seeking from an internal EKR, includes seeking knowledge that has been shared through both embedded and separate processes through the organisational EKR (as described above). The second seeking behaviour, knowledge seeking from external EKRs, includes vendor-provided knowledge repositories and online searches and is prevalent among knowledge workers, such as sysadmins (Velasquez & Weisband, 2009). In addition to EKRs, technical workers often reference formal product documentation (e.g., 2006); accordingly, knowledge seeking from manuals is included. Fourth, knowledge workers have many opportunities to use colleagues as a convenient source of knowledge; therefore, knowledge seeking from colleagues (e.g., Alge, Wiethof, & Klein, 2003) is included. Finally, recognising that some individuals may maintain personal notes, this paper includes knowledge self-reliance as a seeking behaviour. This type of knowledge documentation is called “documenting for ourselves,” and may take the form of piles of papers or post-it notes (e.g., Malone, 1983).

Figure 2 summarises the behaviours that combine to make up one’s knowledge exchange role. Following the theory of knowledge reuse, this paper proposes that individuals can enact one, some, or all of the knowledge exchange behaviours to varying degrees, and can also contribute to EKRs in one or two ways. The extent to which an individual performs these behaviours determines the individual’s knowledge exchange role (Biddle, 1979; Markus, 2001).

In order to identify the various knowledge exchange roles, we used cluster analysis, as detailed in Appendix A. Four knowledge exchange roles emerged, which are summarised in Table 1 and discussed below.

## 3.1.1. Role 1: Self-reliant contributors

Adopted by 46 individuals, this knowledge exchange role represents the greatest reliance on both external and internal EKRs for knowledge seeking and contribution. These users seek moderate knowledge from colleagues, but consider themselves self-reliant in knowledge needed for their tasks, and seek minimal knowledge from manuals. They contribute considerable knowledge through both embedded and separate means, and are considered most valuable for a business (Jones, Herschel, & Moesel, 2003; Kach, Azadegan, & Wagner, 2015).

## 3.1.2. Role 2: Detached moderates

Adopted by 18 individuals, this knowledge exchange role is one where users seem detached from KM eforts. These users contribute the least knowledge through the embedded workflow and acquire the least knowledge from colleagues and manuals. They obtain moderate knowledge from EKRs, consider themselves moderately self-reliant in knowledge, contribute moderate knowledge through a separate process, and can be considered low level knowledge workers (Kach et al., 2015).

## 3.1.3. Role 3: Involved brokers

Adopted by 36 individuals, this knowledge exchange role is in sharp contrast to Detached Moderates. These users consider themselves reliant on outside sources of knowledge, source moderate knowledge via internal EKR, and source significant knowledge from external

EKRs, colleagues, and manuals. They contribute considerable knowledge through the embedded workflow, but provide the least amount of knowledge through separate processes. In the absence of knowledge contribution through embedded processes, these individuals may be knowledge “free riders,” using the knowledge contribu tions of their coworkers while providing very few of their own (Kankanhalli et al., 2005b).

## 3.1.4. Role 4: Reluctant limited users

This knowledge exchange role is in sharp contrast to self-reliant contributors in all aspects except knowledge seeking from colleagues. These users make negligible use of internal or external EKRs. Although they consider themselves more dependent on outside knowledge than two of the other roles, they primarily seek knowledge through manuals, supplemented by interactions with colleagues. They contribute moderate knowledge through the embedded workflow, but contribute very little knowledge through discrete processes. This role was adopted by 13 individuals. As in the case of Involved Brokers, Reluctant Limited Users are experts in contributing knowledge (Gibbert, Leibold, & Probst, 2002).

Having described the four emergent knowledge exchange roles, the development of the research hypoth eses follows the extended theory of knowledge reuse. Remember that the conceptualisation of role as a characteristic set of behaviour patterns presented in this paper aligns with that found in prior literature as argued by Biddle (1979) and Burt (1982). That is, these patterns of behaviour are typical of those in similar positions: Self-reliant Contributors are typified by a characteristic behaviour pattern that rarely sources knowledge; Involved Brokers are identified by a characteristic behaviour pattern that provides minimal knowledge through separate processes while also seeking considerable knowledge from colleagues, manuals, and EKRs; etc. In this way, the combination of characteristic behaviour patterns identifies each role seen in knowledge workers. Role theory also states that roles mediate individual perceptual measures (e.g., those experienced in one’s work environment) and outcome variables (Biddle, 1979), which provides support for our extension of the theory of knowledge reuse.

## 4. Hypotheses development

Our investigation of the theory of knowledge reuse (Markus, 2001) includes two types of individual perceptions as likely to influence EKR performance impact (Gallivan et al., 2003) through knowledge exchange behaviours (i.e., the set of knowledge seeking and contribution behaviours): beliefs about ability to contribute knowledge (hereafter ability) and incentives. Next, the mediated efects of individual perceptual determinants on EKR performance impact via knowledge exchange roles are hypothesised.

## 4.1. Perceived ability

Ability involves actual capabilities for action, while perceived ability refers to one’s judgement about their capabilities. For example, an individual’s computer competency is ability whereas computer self-eficacy is a perceived ability. Some studies focus on perceived ability, rather than actual ability, due to two reasons: (a) perceived ability, and not actual ability, may influence behaviour; and (b) measuring actual ability may not be possible. Due to the combination of both reasons – for example, an individual’s KCSE rather than actual knowledge contribution ability may afect his or her knowledge contribution, and the individual’s actual knowledge contribution ability may not be possible to measure – this study focuses on individuals’ perceived abilities associated with knowledge seeking and contribution (Markus, 2001).

As discussed earlier, this paper includes costs and KCSE as aspects of perceived ability. A specific cost identified by the theory of knowledge reuse (Markus, 2001) is time pressure, which limits the quantity and quality of knowledge contribution (e.g., Gallivan et al., 2003) as well as the sources of knowledge utilised (Gray & Durcikova, 2006). Time pressure is a perceived ability because it is usually viewed in terms of an individual’s perception rather than the actual number of minutes (s) he is spending on other tasks and the number of minutes (s)he therefore has available for the focal task. Thus, perceived time pressure is included in our study.

## 4.1.1. Perceived time pressure

Perceived time pressure is the stress perceived by knowledge workers because they feel there are too many work tasks to complete in regular work hours. In EKR research, time pressure has been shown to influence knowledge exchange and thus EKR performance impact in various ways. Knowledge workers sufer from perceived time pressure and therefore are less likely to share knowledge via EKR (Markus, 2001). In contrast, studies show that perceived time pressure is also likely to influence which knowledge source is selected. For example, under perceived time pressure, the Internet may be a preferred source of knowledge over colleagues (Prabha, Silipigni Connaway, Olszewski, & Jenkins, 2007), and printed documentation and colleagues may be preferred over internal EKRs (Gray & Durcikova, 2006). Let’s look at the following scenario on how perceived time pressure influences work outcomes through the behaviours enacted in one’s knowledge exchange role. Knowledge worker A, who feels a low level of perceived time pressure, may assume a role that includes an even mix of knowledge contribution behaviours and knowledge seeking behaviours. Contrast this with knowledge worker B, who feels a high level of perceived time pressure; worker B therefore occasionally contributes knowledge through embedded processes and makes full use of knowledge sources for seeking in an efort to save time, possibly preferring to ask colleagues for guidance. These two knowledge workers enact diferent knowledge seeking behaviours, thereby adopting diferent knowledge exchange roles of Detached Moderates and Involved Brokers, because of the perceived time pressure they face in their work (Markus, 2001). Because of the very diferent ways these workers interact with the internal EKR through their knowledge exchange role, the EKR will impact their work diferently. Worker A may perceive a moderate impact on her work efectiveness and eficiency because she uses the EKR for two types of knowledge contribution as well as knowledge seeking, while worker B may perceive no impact, especially if she rarely contributes knowledge and prefers to source all knowledge from colleagues or external sources.

H1: The knowledge exchange role assumed by an individual fully mediates the efect of perceived time pressure on internal EKR performance impact.

## 4.1.2. Knowledge contribution self-eficacy

The second type of belief about one’s ability to contrib ute knowledge is knowledge contribution self-eficacy (KCSE). Self-eficacy is a person’s evaluation of one’s own capabilities with respect to an intended behaviour (Bandura, 1992, 1993). Knowledge contribution self-ef ficacy (KCSE) is one’s belief of their ability to contribute knowledge and the efect this knowledge will have on others (Kankanhalli, Tan, & Wei, 2005a). In order to have this belief, a person needs to be confident they possess actual knowledge worth contributing, and have the necessary skills to provide such knowledge (Van Acker, Vermeulen, Kreijns, Lutgerink, & van Buuren, 2014). KCSE is expected to influence EKR performance impact through the behaviours enacted in one’s knowledge exchange role. An individual with greater KCSE might find an EKR to perform better because of her higher likelihood to contribute knowledge through both separate and embedded processes (Hsu, Ju, Yen, & Chang, 2007). In contrast, a knowledge worker with a low KCSE is less likely to contribute knowledge (Constant, Sproull, & Kiesler, 1996), especially through a separate process. Thus, these two knowledge workers will enact difer ent knowledge contribution and seeking behaviours: one with a mix of knowledge contribution and seeking behaviours (i.e., high KCSE) such as Self-Reliant Contributor or Involved Broker, and one in which knowledge is sought but rarely shared (i.e., low KCSE) such as Detached Moderate or Reluctant Limited User. Because these roles interact with the internal EKR in diferent ways due to the diferential impact that KCSE has on the behaviours seen in the role, they will perceive the impact of the EKR diferently.

H2: One’s knowledge exchange role fully mediates the efect of KCSE on internal EKR performance impact.

## 4.2. Incentives

Eliciting knowledge contributions from all individuals is challenging; when all knowledge is contributed and shared within a group, some EKR users may attempt to “free ride” on the knowledge contributions of others (Fulk et al., 2004). In fact, the costs of knowledge exchange disproportionately (and negatively) influence knowledge contribution; consequently, incentives are generally geared toward addressing this disparity by rewarding knowledge contribution behaviours (e.g., Bock et al., 2008; He & Wei, 2009). Therefore, as suggested by the theory of knowledge reuse, this study focuses on addressing knowledge contribution through positive reinforcements, namely extrinsic rewards and intrinsic rewards.

## 4.2.1. Extrinsic rewards

Organisations can use rewards to encourage employees to enact particular role behaviours (Biddle, 1979). One of the most frequently used rewards to encourage knowledge contribution behaviours is extrinsic rewards, such as monetary bonuses (Deci, 1971, 1972). Extrinsic rewards are expected to influence EKR performance impact, mediated by the knowledge exchange role (as their combination of knowledge exchange behaviours) an individual assumes. Bock et al. (2008) argue that there are three reasons for this efect. First, greater extrinsic rewards for contributing knowledge may cause indi viduals to focus more on contribution than on seeking knowledge (Tiwana & Bush, 2005), and therefore, view the EKR as less useful to their work (Sambamurthy & Subramani, 2005). This in turn reduces the potential positive impact of EKR on work outcomes. Second, if individuals receive greater extrinsic rewards for contrib uting knowledge, they may devalue the knowledge in the EKR, because their focus will be on quantity rather than quality of submissions, thus decreasing the overall quality of knowledge in the EKR (Bock et al., 2008). An increased number of lower quality entries in the interna EKR will likely lead to knowledge seeking behaviour from other sources. This type of role, which includes less knowledge exchange behaviours through the internal EKR, such in the case of Detached Moderate or Reluctant Limited Users, will again decrease the poten tial impact of an internal EKR on work outcomes. Third, extrinsic rewards might lead to a perception that the EKR contains irrelevant knowledge (Sambamurthy & Subramani, 2005), making users avoid it as a source of knowledge, such as Reluctant Limited Users. Therefore, knowledge exchange profiles that emerge when workers are sensitive to extrinsic rewards will exhibit more knowledge sharing behaviours via EKR while reducing knowledge seeking behaviours via EKR, while still making full use of other knowledge sources (e.g., Self-Reliant Contributors). Hence, if a knowledge worker adopts this type of knowledge exchange role (high knowledge contribution behaviour, low EKR seeking behaviour, high knowledge seeking behaviour from other sources) the impact of the EKR on performance outcomes will be significantly reduced (e.g., Detached Moderates). Alternately, knowledge workers who are not sensitive to extrinsic rewards may exhibit knowledge contribution behaviour and make moderate use of knowledge from an EKR; these knowledge workers will likely perceive moderate impact from the EKR on their work.

H3: One’s knowledge exchange role fully mediates the efect of extrinsic rewards on EKR performance impact.

## 4.2.2. Intrinsic rewards

Intrinsic rewards also counteract costs and increase knowledge contribution behaviours. Intrinsic rewards relate to personal role expectations that arise from within (Biddle, 1979). These rewards are feelings of satisfaction that come from performing a task and may include the joy of contributing knowledge because it is inherently rewarding or brings a sense of accomplishment (Deci, 1972; Murray, 1964), as well as the satisfaction that comes from behaving prosocially (Blau, 1967; Watson & Hewett, 2006). Research suggests an intrinsically motivated individual would depend on an EKR to a greater extent because she would use the EKR to seek knowledge for her own purposes and for oth ers (Szulansky & Cappetta, 2005), such as Self-Reliant Contributors or Detached Moderates. Thus, she would be more eficient in utilising the EKR and perceive it to have a positive impact on work outcomes (Bock et al., 2008). Furthermore, one’s intrinsic motivation to share knowledge will also increase their subsequent knowledge seeking behaviours via EKR; these users will believe the EKR contains truly useful knowledge based on the knowledge they have freely shared in the past (Watson & Hewett, 2006), and this access to useful knowledge will positively influence performance arising from EKR use. In contrast, an individual who is not intrinsically motivated to help others would only use the EKR for his own purposes, such as Involved Brokers who only source knowledge from an internal EKR but don’t contribute, thereby perceiving it to be less useful (Constant et al., 1996; Putnam, 1995) and thus reducing the potential impact the EKR can have on his work outcomes. Because intrinsic rewards influence the behaviours enacted in one’s knowledge exchange role, positive impact of the EKR on work outcomes come through an enactment of that role (Biddle, 1979).

H4: One’s knowledge exchange role fully mediates the efect of intrinsic rewards on internal EKR performance impact.

Figure 3 summarises the overall research model. One’s knowledge exchange role is seen in the combination of the knowledge contribution and seeking behaviours they adopt, as presented earlier. Knowledge exchange role mediates the efect of perceived abilities (perceived time pressure and knowledge contribution self-eficacy) and incentives (intrinsic motivation and extrinsic motivation) on EKR performance impact. Years of experience and coworker and supervisor support are included as control variables (e.g., Durcikova, Fadel, Butler, & Galletta, 2011; Kulkarni et al., 2006).

## 5. Analysis and results

## 5.1. Study context

This study focuses on sysadmins at a Fortune 100 firm (called ALPHA for simplicity), technical experts who are considered knowledge workers that belong to communities of practice (Velasquez & Weisband, 2009) and are often provided with internal EKRs to help them in their work (e.g., Lenchner et al., 2009). The primary work activity of our participants was the maintenance of computer systems and networks, which requires specialised skills and knowledge. To assist in this work, they were provided with an internal EKR that contained a searchable ticket system to track details of their work and allow summarisation of work completed (i.e., knowledge contribution through an embedded process) as well as functionality to contribute and search decision procedures and best practice documents (i.e., knowledge contribution through a separate process). These two processes are distinct because a sysadmin contributes to the embedded process while completing a ticket that takes no extra time to complete above and beyond their responsibilities. However, to contribute a decision procedure or best practice document, a sysadmin must take time of solving tickets, draft a document, and then submit it to the system, which is separate from the ticket platform. The sysadmins were encouraged to use the internal EKR (for example, they were informed in emails and meetings about this tool and about the benefits of using it), which had been developed at great expense. However, they were not required to use it through either process, and still had access to vendor documentation, external EKRs, personal notes, and colleagues.

## 5.2. Data collection methods and measures

An invitation to participate in an online survey was distributed to the 1,012 sysadmins authorised to access the internal EKR at ALPHA; analysis of system logs suggested that 431 used the system. We received 130 completed questionnaires, yielding a response rate of 30.2%. Of the respondents, 89.2% were male and 10.8% were female. This male dominant sample is similar to that found in the SAGE Salary Survey for 2008 (SAGE, 2008), considered the latest and most comprehensive survey of sysadmin personal and work demographics. Participants reported working in their current position for an average of 4.5 years (ranging from 0–25 years) and in their profession an average of 6.3 years (ranging from 0.25–30 years). Comparing early and late respondents in terms of all individual perceptual variables did not produce significant diferences (p < 0.05) in any cases.

A survey instrument was developed following Dillman (1978) and using previously validated items. Each question was measured on a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). The instrument was reviewed by researchers and practitioners at ALPHA familiar with the internal EKR. Measures of knowledge seeking and contribution were adapted from Gray and Durcikova (2006) and Durcikova and Brown (2007), respectively. Items measuring perceived EKR impact were adapted from Rai, Lang, and Welker (2002) and Muhammed, Doll, and Deng (2008). Items measuring intrinsic and extrinsic rewards and KCSE were taken from Kankanhalli et al. (2005a). Items measuring perceived time pressure were adapted from Gray and Durcikova (2006). Items measuring supervisor and coworker support were taken from Kulkarni et al. (2006).

The items used to measure the dependent and independent variables are shown in Appendices B and C, respectively. Principal components analysis was conducted with varimax rotation, with separate analyses for independent and dependent variables (Straub,

Boudreau, & Gefen, 2004). Items loading less than 0.60 on respective constructs were dropped. After excluding such items, all items load higher on their respective constructs than on other constructs, and all cross-loadings are below 0.50, as shown in Appendices B and C. Appendix D provides the average variance extracted (AVE) values and the correlations among the variables. As shown in Appendix D, all AVE values exceed 0.50, and all square root of AVE values exceed of-diagonal correlations, indicating adequate convergent and discriminant validity (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). The reliabilities of all measures are 0.70 or more (Nunnally, 1978). Thus, the measures demonstrate good psychometric properties.

We conducted two common-method bias tests. First, Harmon’s single-factor test (Podsakof & Dalton, 1987) revealed 10 factors explaining 87.2% of the variance, with no single factor featuring significant loading for all items. Second, the marker variable test (Lindell & Whitney, 2001) showed that after adjusting for the second-lowest positive correlation among the constructs, all originally significant correlations remain significant. Thus, common-method bias does not seem to be a problem with our sample.

## 6. Results

The research hypotheses concern the mediating efects of the knowledge exchange role on the relationship between individual perceptions and EKR performance impact (H1, H2, H3, H4). The three-step procedure recommended by Baron and Kenny (1986) and MacKinnon (2008) was used to test the hypotheses. While recent research (Zhao, Lynch, & Chen, 2010) has shown some limitations of this approach, it is most appropriate for our investigation, because our model includes multiple mediators and mediators that are categorical, and other approaches cannot handle both of these conditions. Table 2 provides results related to the tests of these hypotheses.

The first step in testing the mediating efects involves testing the efects of individual perceptions on roles. Because role is categorical, the efects of the individual perceptions related to incentives (extrinsic rewards, intrinsic rewards) and perceived ability (KCSE, perceived time pressure) on role were examined using multinomial logistic regression (Model 1 in Table 2). Multinomial logistic regression generalises logistic regression to multi-category problems, i.e., when there are more than two possible discrete outcomes (Greene 2012). It is used to predict the probabilities of the various possible outcomes of a categorically distributed dependent variable (i.e., the diferent roles in this study), given a set of independent variables (i.e., perceived time pressure, KCSE, extrinsic rewards, and intrinsic rewards in this study, although years of experience and coworker and supervisor support were included as control variables because they have been shown to influence knowledge contribution and seeking behaviours in prior research (e.g., Durcikova et al., 2011; Kulkarni et al., 2006). The overall model is significant, with a $\chi ^ { 2 }$ of 48.83 $( d . f . = 2 1 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ The model is able to accurately predict role with 57.3% likelihood, which is significantly better than the 38.3% accuracy expected due to chance alone, supporting the criteria for classification accuracy. Standard errors for all parameter estimates are below 2.0 as recommended (MacKinnon, 2008). Likelihood ratio tests (Model 1 in Table 2) indicate that perceived time pressure does not have a significant efect, while extrinsic rewards, intrinsic rewards, and KCSE significantly influence knowledge exchange role. Thereby, these efects should be investigated further to examine which knowledge exchange roles these factors influence. None of the control variables afects knowledge exchange role.

The second step in testing mediating efects involves testing the total efects model – i.e., the efects of the independent variables (including costs, incentives, intermediary, and the control variables) on the dependent variable (EKR performance impact) – using multiple regressions. A two-step regression analysis was conducted; the control variables were entered first (Model 2a in Table 2), followed by the independent variables (Model 2b). In this step, the hypothesised independent variables should influence the dependent variable; results show that intrinsic rewards and KCSE influence EKR performance impact but extrinsic rewards and perceived time pressure do not. Thus, knowledge exchange role may mediate the efects of intrinsic rewards and KCSE on EKR performance impact. H1 and H2, concerning the mediated efects of perceived time pressure and extrinsic rewards on EKR performance impact, respectively, are not supported, because perceived time pressure and extrinsic rewards do not influence EKR performance impact in Model 2b.

In the third step to test for mediation, significant independent variables from step one (Model 1 in Table 2) for which mediation is being tested (intrinsic rewards and KCSE) should significantly influence the mediator (knowledge exchange role). Results of this analysis are shown in Model 1 in Table 2, and finds that extrinsic rewards, intrinsic rewards, and KCSE significantly influence knowledge exchange role, while perceived time pressure and the control variables have no significant efect. Therefore, the second step in the test for mediation is supported for intrinsic rewards and KCSE. Since the mediator (knowledge exchange role) is a categorical variable, it was converted to three dummy variables. A three-step regression analysis was performed, first including only the control variables (Model 3a), followed by inclusion of the three dummy variables representing knowledge exchange role (Model 2b), and finally the four individual perceptual factors (Model 3c). In this test, we should find both: (a) a significant relationship between role and performance, which is supported, and (b) a non-significant relationship between the independent variables identified in step 1 (intrinsic rewards and KCSE), which is also supported (Baron & Kenny, 1986). Moreover, the change in R<sup>2</sup> when the independent variables are included (Model 2c) is not significant. Together, the results support H2 and H4, supporting KCSE and intrinsic rewards efects on EKR performance impact being fully mediated by knowledge exchange role. Also, all dummy variables for knowledge exchange role are significant in both Models 3b and 3c. Upon including the dummy variables for knowledge exchange role, the control variable supervisor support is significant, but control variables coworker support and years of experience are not, in both Models 3b and 3c. As discussed earlier, Self-Reliant Contributors seek moderate knowledge from colleagues, but contribute considerable knowledge through both embedded and separate means. This may be because they are highly motivated by extrinsic rewards, and to a lesser extent by intrinsic rewards. Thus, the key to encouraging such users to contribute knowledge via EKRs is the promise of explicit rewards such as promotions or bonuses (e.g., Kankanhalli et al., 2005a). These individuals seem to achieve the greatest EKR performance impact.

By contrast, Detached Moderates obtain some knowledge from EKRs, and contribute some knowledge through a separate process. This may be because they do not feel they are experts at contributing knowledge (lowest mean KCSE), and are least influenced by intrinsic rewards. These individuals seem to achieve moderate EKR performance impact.

Involved Brokers source moderate knowledge via internal EKR, but source significant knowledge from external EKRs, colleagues, and manuals. They contribute considerable knowledge through the embedded workflow, but provide the least amount of knowledge through separate processes. These individuals consider themselves experts in knowledge contribution and are motivated by intrinsic rewards, which may influence knowledge contribution through embedded processes. In organisations seeking knowledge contribution through discrete processes from this user group, messages tying their intrinsic motivators to formal knowledge submission should be used. Alternatively, if document submission from this group is more valued than document submission from others, no extrinsic rewards should be used at all, as extrinsic rewards may “cheapen” the knowledge contributed (Deci, 1971, 1972). That is, knowledge workers in this group may feel no sense of duty or joy in contribution their expertise if it can simply be “bought” for a low price. These individuals seem to achieve moderate EKR performance impact.

Finally, Reluctant Limited Users make minimal use of internal or external EKRs. Although they consider themselves more dependent on outside knowledge than two of the other roles, they primarily seek knowledge through manuals, supplemented by interactions with colleagues. They contribute moderate knowledge through the embedded workflow, but contribute very little knowledge through discrete processes. Similar to Involved Brokers, Reluctant Limited Users are also experts in contributing knowledge, but lack the motivation to do so. Because use of the EKR in this study was purely volitional, they are likely not willing to take time to fully adopt the new system when existing processes allow them to complete their work. Not surprisingly, these individuals seem to achieve the least EKR performance impact.

## 7. Discussion

This paper makes two significant contributions. First, it identifies four knowledge exchange roles employees can assume while engaging in knowledge contribution and knowledge seeking. Prior EKR literature has viewed individuals’ use of EKR for either seeking or contribution knowledge, or focused simply on EKR use with out distinguishing how it is used. Furthermore, prior EKR literature has focused on EKR as the only source of knowledge; this paper considered use of EKR as well as other knowledge sources. Adopting this more comprehensive view, and referencing the theory of knowledge reuse, we identified five knowledge seeking behaviours (from internal EKR, external EKR, colleagues, manuals, knowledge self-reliance) and two knowledge contribution behaviours (embedded process or separate activity). This paper argues that the knowledge exchange role should be conceptualised in terms of the extent to which users exhibit knowledge exchange behaviours. Using empirical data, a taxonomy of four distinct knowledge exchange roles was generated (Self-reliant Contributors, Detached Moderates, Involved Brokers, Reluctant Limited Users) that capture variations among knowledge exchange behaviours. While this set of roles may not be representative across all contexts, it is an important first attempt to identify such roles in knowledge exchange.

Second, prior EKR literature has examined the direct efects of individual perceptual variables on EKR performance impact on performance. This paper extends the theory of knowledge reuse to argue that knowledge exchange role mediates the efects of four individual perceptual variables related to perceived ability (KCSE and perceived time pressure) and incentives (extrinsic and intrinsic) on EKR performance impact. Empirical results indicate that the efects of intrinsic rewards and KCSE on EKR performance impact are fully mediated by knowledge exchange role. Extrinsic rewards influence knowledge roles but do not have a significant mediated efect on EKR impact. Finally, perceived time pressure influences neither knowledge exchange role nor EKR performance impact.

Before discussing implications of the above findings for research and practice, limitations of this research should be considered. First, the study sample may be limiting. This study focused on sysadmins, technical experts who work in complex, high-risk environments that are male dominated. While this reduced variations among our subjects, it may limit generalisability to other contexts. Other research has shown that the sysadmin context is distinct from other service work centre environments, such as call centres. The sysadmin context is particularly complex and high risk (Forsgren, Durcikova, Clay, & Wang, 2016), which may influence the efects of perceived abilities and incentives on knowledge exchange roles. The work tasks and environment of sysadmins, while similar to other technical professions that are male dominated, such as technology and engineering, may not capture EKR use behaviours of other professionals. The generalisability may also be limited by our relatively small sample size.

Second, this is based on a study of EKR use in one organisation, which may limit generalisability. However, this may not be a major limitation because the unit of analysis is the individual, and by focusing on one EKR in a single organisation, the presented model controlled for organisational factors, thereby enhancing the internal validity of our findings.

Third, the taxonomy is based on knowledge contribution and seeking behaviours that may not be present in all EKR implementations. In particular, not all organisations can or are willing to provide methods to contribute to knowledge through embedded processes. Furthermore, organisations may not have the tools necessary to make such knowledge easy to access and search. Also, although care was taken to select representative individual perceptual variables suggested by the theory of knowledge reuse, others may exist in diferent contexts. For example, other costs, such as the cost of knowledge acquisition and application or knowledge coding efort, should be investigated.

Fourth, the focus of the study is on how perceptions of EKR success may depend on the individuals’ use of both the EKR as well as alternative sources of knowledge. Therefore, we only examined the efects on EKR impacts. However, these roles may also impact perceptions of usefulness of other sources. Future research should include perceptions of the impact of other sources of knowledge.

Finally, this paper is based on a cross-sectional study. While this research design allowed us to focus on knowledge exchange roles at a point in time, it precluded examination of any longitudinal or progressive patterns of knowledge exchange roles, which are likely to change and adapt over time (Biddle, 1979); it also precluded us from examining if one’s knowledge exchange role is a fixed trait. Alternative future work could design an experimental study where a knowledge worker is exposed to diferent individual perceptual variables to examine if these changes afect the behaviours and roles they adopt. This cross-sectional design also precluded us from investigating the efects of EKR performance impact on knowledge exchange role, wherein anticipated impact could influence subsequent EKR use; future research should examine these efects. While this initial identification of roles among EKR users is an important first step, the evolution of roles and role adoption should be investigated in future studies. Finally, the collection of data from a single organisation at one point in time could lead to low variation in the individual perceptual variables. While this also reduced variability in our research design by controlling for organisational and technology factors, future research should investigate other contexts and consider longitudinal studies.

Despite the above limitations, this research has several implications for future research and practice. First, it provides a more detailed view of the knowledge exchange behaviours individuals exhibit when using EKRs, moving beyond simple level of use (Burton-Jones & Straub, 2006). This framework allows organisations to recognise that there is no single “right” role for users to adopt in using EKR. Instead, EKR users exhibit several combinations of knowledge exchange behaviours, reflected in the four roles in this paper, providing researchers with a richer conceptualisation of EKR use. While this research examined the types of knowledge sourcing and contribution behaviours that comprise a knowledge exchange role, future research should investigate factors that impact the selection of one knowledge source over another, such as quality, ease of use, expectations, or knowledge type (e.g., Watson & Hewett, 2006; Zimmer, Henry, & Butler, 2007). Longitudinal studies could be conducted to better understand how those who seek and share knowledge may change roles over time. Studies could also investigate knowledge contribution through outlets other than internal EKRs (Rulke, Zaheer, & Anderson, 2000).

Second, this research provides evidence that knowledge exchange role mediates the efect of intrinsic rewards and KCSE on EKR impact on performance. This may be because both intrinsic rewards and KCSE are factors that are internal to the individual and based on personal expectations (Biddle, 1979). Therefore, the influences of these factors are internalised and remain significant in one’s work long after any external rewards (Deci & Ryan, 2000). This distinction is important to industry, as interventions need to be evaluated in the light of their efects on the employees’ knowledge work. Further research is also needed to examine the ways in which intrinsic rewards and KCSE influence the various knowledge exchange roles in other kinds of organisations. Finally, this study focused on relative performance impact; future research could examine absolute performance impact and include objective data.

Third, this research shows that extrinsic rewards significantly influence one’s knowledge exchange behaviours, but may not impact performance in the context of this study. There may be several reasons for this. First, extrinsic rewards have been shown to succeed only at securing temporary compliance in some studies (Bock et al., 2008; Kelman, 1958). In the context of an EKR, these rewards may encourage users to share knowledge to secure the reward, but they may not improve performance. Second, Von Krogh (2005, p. 376) stated: “No material incentive can make people change their interest to codify and share their tacit knowledge.” Indeed, the sysadmins in the study were given performance evaluations based not on knowledge contribution, but on the eficiency with which they completed their work tasks. Finally, our results confirm Bock et al. (2008), who did not find extrinsic rewards to influence EKR performance impact. These findings are significant for industry, because managers may choose extrinsic rewards when knowledge contribution is critical and temporary, such as new EKR implementation when knowledge seeding is important (Gallivan et al., 2003), but they should be wary of extrinsic rewards when the EKR already has considerable knowledge and its primary goal is performance improvement. Similarly, these findings are significant as researchers identify the true goal of the EKR being studied: is the goal to change knowledge exchange behaviours or to improve the efectiveness and eficiency of its users (Ba, Stallaert, & Whinston, 2001)? Moreover, our results difer from those of Rowe, Marciniak, and Clergeau (2011), who found group-based extrinsic rewards to improve productivity in service work. Further research is needed to examine the efects of group-level extrinsic rewards, rather than individual-level extrinsic rewards examined in this study, and when EKRs are used in other contexts, such as service work.

Fourth, results related to the control variables indicate that supervisor support plays a significant role in EKR performance impact, but coworker support does not. This may be explained by the messages from these two groups. For example, supervisor support for EKR use is likely an organisational efort, such as a carefully composed or regular message. In contrast, support from coworkers reflects individual opinions on the performance benefits of EKR use. However, previous research shows that doing one’s work eficiently and efectively, especially in service work units like call centres, may also serve the group and thus the efect of coworker support is indirect (Rowe et al., 2011). Practitioners can benefit by recognising that eforts of the management team to encourage EKR use are influential. Moreover, future research should further examine: (a) the efects of coworker and supervisor support in other KM contexts; and (b) if the results are also found when examining knowledge exchange roles of individuals in social networks.

Finally, this paper finds that perceived time pressure does not impact knowledge exchange role or EKR performance impact through the knowledge exchange role. This is surprising, because we expected those who are under significant perceived time pressure to alter both knowledge seeking activities (increasing, to save time) and knowledge contribution activities (decreasing, to focus time on completing essential work tasks) (e.g., Gray & Durcikova, 2006). In addition, in call centre environments, perceived time pressure experienced through limiting call duration in order to boost productivity statistics have been shown to have counter-productive efects on performance (Rowe et al., 2011). However, our results suggest that sysadmins did not consider perceived time pressure to be significant when engaging in diferent knowledge exchange roles, which may be due to several reasons. First, sysadmins routinely report long work hours and high perceived time pressure in their work (SAGE, 2008), and they may be used to dealing with perceived time pressure. Second, perceived time pressure may only be a factor in early implementation phases of an EKR, but not have a significant efect in knowledge exchange role once an EKR is integrated into daily work routines (e.g., Gallivan et al., 2003). Future research should investigate the impact of perceived time pressure among other types of knowledge workers in less busy contexts, as well as during diferent phases of implementation. Furthermore, future research may also want to examine if other costs, such as knowledge coding efort, are significant through out adoption and assimilation of EKRs.

## Appendix A. Generation of empirical taxonomy of knowledge exchange roles

Using the seven knowledge contribution and seeking behaviours, a cluster analysis was utilised to develop an empirical taxonomy of knowledge exchange roles (Ketchen & Shook, 1996; Lorr, 1983). Cluster analysis was conducted using five methods: Ward’s (1963), between-groups linkage, within-groups linkage, centroid, and median. The results for cluster solutions with three to five clusters were compared in terms of: (a) change in fusion coeficients; (b) number of individuals in each cluster (solutions including clusters with five or fewer individuals were excluded); and (c) univariate F-statistics (Ulrich & McKelvey 1990). Based on these criteria, the solution with four clusters using Ward’s method performed best and was selected for the taxonomy. It included two large clusters and two smaller clusters. Seventeen individuals could not be classified due to a missing value of a classifying variable. Based on analysis of variance (see Table A1), these four clusters are significantly $\left( p < 0 . 0 0 1 \right)$ diferent from each other in terms of each of the seven knowledge behaviours.

For example, for knowledge seeking from the internal EKR, the test sorted the clusters into three groups, as seen by the designation of H, M, and L in Table 1. ## Appendix E. Further analyses of the knowledge exchange roles

## Diferences across knowledge exchange roles on EKR performance impact

To identify the efects of the three individual perceptual variables related to incentives (extrinsic rewards, intrinsic reward) and perceived ability (KCSE) that were found significant in multinomial regressions (Table E2), on the four knowledge exchange roles (represented by the four clusters), multiple discriminant analysis was conducted. In order to discriminate efectively among n clusters, n − 1 discriminant functions are needed (Sabherwal & King, 1995). Therefore, three discriminant functions were generated to discriminate among the four clusters in this study.

An estimate of the efectiveness of the discriminant functions in predicting knowledge exchange role is provided by the hit ratio, i.e., the percentage of cases correctly predicted by the discriminant functions. In this analysis, the hit ratio was 48.2%, which is significantly exceeds $( \chi ^ { 2 } = 2 7 . 8 8 , d . f . { \stackrel { \textstyle } { = } } 9 , p < 0 . 0 0 { \dot { 1 } } )$ the 30.9% accuracy expected by chance alone.

The nature of each rotated discriminant function was assessed using its significant correlations with the discriminating variables (Panel 1 in Table E2). The diferences across the clusters were then interpreted by examining the values of each discriminant function at the four cluster centroids (Panel 2 of Table E2), with special attention given to the two clusters at whose centroids each discriminant function has the highest and lowest values. Function 1 is correlated positively with intrinsic rewards and KCSE. It discriminates between Involved Brokers (for which Function 1 has the highest mean) and Detached Moderates (for which it has the lowest mean). Function 2 is correlated positively with extrinsic rewards, and discriminates between Self-reliant Contributors (for which it has the highest mean) and Involved Brokers (for which it has the lowest mean). Function 3 is positively correlated with intrinsic rewards and negatively correlated with KCSE, and discriminates between Self-reliant Contributors (for which it has the highest mean) and Reluctant Limited Users (for which it has the lowest mean). Together, these significant correlations of each function with the discriminating variables and the values of the rotated discriminant functions at cluster centroids help explain the conditions under which each knowledge exchange role is adopted.

## Diferences across knowledge exchange roles in EKR performance impact

Analysis of variance showed significant (p < 0.001) diferences among the four knowledge exchange roles in terms of EKR per formance impact. Post hoc comparisons were then conducted using Duncan’s Multiple Range test (Hair et al., 2006).

Results suggest that Reluctant Limited Users perceived the least performance impact from EKR use (mean = 2.29 on a scale from 1 to 5); Self-reliant Contributors perceived the highest performance impact from EKR use (mean = 3.56); and Detached Moderates and Involved Brokers perceived a moderate performance impact from EKR use (mean = 3.12 and 3.04, respectively), which was significantly higher than that reported by Reluctant Limited Users and significantly lower than that reported by Self-reliant Contributors, but not significantly diferent from each other. These results are generally consistent with our expectations. We had expected the Self-reliant Contributors to benefit the most from EKR use, followed by the Involved Brokers, and then the Detached Moderates, with the Reluctant Limited Users seeing the least benefit. The results show a similar pattern, except that the middle two groups – Involved Brokers and Detached Moderates – do not significantly difer in EKR performance impact.
