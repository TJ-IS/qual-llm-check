## Abstract

The last few decades has seen an ever-increasing growth in the way activities are productized and associated with a financial cost. This phenomenon, termed financialization, spans all areas including government, finance, health and manufacturing. Recent developments within finance over that past decade have radically altered the way trading occurs. This paper analyses high-frequency trading (HFT) as a necessary component of the infrastructure that makes financialization possible. Through interviews with HFT firms, a software vendor, regulators and banks, the effects of HFT on market efficiency, and its impact on costs to long-term investors are explored. This paper contributes to the literature by exploring the conflict that exists between HFT and traditional market makers in today’s fragmented markets. This paper argues that society should be unconcerned with this conflict and should instead focus on the effects these participants have on the long-term investors, for whom the markets ultimately exist. In order to facilitate the best outcomes, regulation should be simple, aimed at keeping participants’ behavior stable, and the interactions among them transparent and straightforward. Financialization and HFT are inextricably linked, and society is best served by ensuring that the creative energy of these market participants is directed on providing liquidity and removing inefficiencies.

## Introduction

Since the deregulation in the 1970s, the resulting push has been toward ‘‘productization,’’ the characterizing of all value in financial terms and the creation of markets for (virtually) everything (Epstein, 2005). Critics of this phenomenon argue that this impairs market efficiency. Deregulated markets may allow institutions to create and exploit information asymmetries and complexities in ways that increase costs to investors (Turbeville, 2013).

Significant literature exists on the subject of financialization. For example, Philips (1994) looks at how new types of instruments and indexes have boosted investment. Palley (2007) examines whether financialization as a process is sustainable in the long term. Krippner (2011) analyzes the growth and importance of profits due to financialization, and its effect on national economies. Hein (2012) examines the rise in the economic power of the rentier class, those people or institutions who own shares.

More recently, high-frequency trading (HFT) has become an increasingly prominent activity in the financial markets. Today, almost all of a company’s trading is done via a computer. The media describes these firms’ large expenditures on technology as an ‘‘arms race,’’ where each company competes on technological speed. New regulations aimed at controlling trading activities have caused the markets to become increasingly fragmented, benefiting those who are able to simultaneously trade at multiple venues. In the USA alone, over 40 such fragments, or automated ‘‘execution venues,’’ exist on which to trade stocks (TABB, 2015). HFT firms can generate revenues by extracting asymmetric information from among these fragments and solving the technological complexities necessary to exploit them on microsecond timeframes (Seddon and Currie, 2016). In this respect, HFT is the information-processing infrastructure that supports financialization. By employing the most advanced technologies, these firms attempt to capture opportunities present in market inefficiencies. What has not been analyzed clearly, however, is if the vast growth in this form of financial activity has increased or decreased the investors’ costs of share ownership. This paper explains the use of computers in financial trading and how they impact the evolution of financialization. The global interconnectedness MacKenzie (2014) calls the market ecology of algorithms poses new challenges, and this requires new theories (O’Hara, 2015).

This paper discusses this new form of financialization, using the term ‘‘algorithmization.’’ This represents those profit-seeking strategies characterized by sets of programmable steps or procedures, and the consequences that this has had on the markets and traditional market participants. The importance of this characterization is articulated by the role of HFT in financialization. HFTs keep the markets liquid and informationally efficient across execution venues. Just as the modern hypermarket requires a large multi-lane highway system to bring goods to the store, the modern financial market needs a broad band of information processing to provide liquidity and efficiency.

This paper depends upon two axioms. First is the assumption that markets provide better outcomes than central planning (i.e., the invisible hand works better than the visible one). Second, independent of any good that regulations may accomplish, is the assumption that regulatory compliance in financial markets incurs costs, which are passed on to investors.

Finally, as an aide to those outside the field, a brief description of the three major categories of market participants is presented below:

Investors are individuals and firms who are looking for longer-term profits from the price movements of stock (or other asset). They are interested in the fundamental prospects of the company. Generally, these investors are only concerned with market microstructure to the extent that they demand adequate liquidity at a ‘‘fair’’ price when they choose to buy and sell.

LFTs (low-frequency traders) are firms that are contractually obligated by the exchange to continuously make markets by placing both buy and sell limit orders. They transact against the incoming orders of investors and profit from the difference between the bid and offer prices (called the spread). They adjust the size of the spread in relation to their perception of risk and competitive pressures from the other market participants. Traditionally, they have been the main source of liquidity for investors.

HFTs (high-frequency traders) are firms with very fast computers and communication networks, who look for very short-term profit opportunities due to imbalances in supply and demand, or price discrepancies among different trading venues. Rather than providing continuous liquidity, HFTs are free to go into and out of markets rapidly. Nevertheless, they are currently the largest provider of liquidity, though this may sometimes be a by-product of a profit-seeking business model.

## A brief history

This section will discuss the evolution of markets and the rise of HFTs relative to traditional providers of liquidity. The foundations of the US financial system lie in the Securities Act of 1933, which provided structures and rules to make sure that a repeat of the 1929 crash could not occur. In the decades since, regulators have made numerous changes to this act as the market structure and products traded have evolved. As data processing and speed of execution have always been key sources of competitive advantage in financial markets, new information technologies have been the drivers of this evolution. HFT is an entirely new paradigm that has developed from within this setting.

Since markets began, what counts as high frequency is simply any strategy that uses the fastest available technology. Freedman (2006) provides an excellent history of technological advancement in markets. In 1815, this advancement was the use of homing pigeons. This, allegedly, is how the Rothschild’s learned quickly about the outcome of the Battle of Waterloo and sold French bonds before competitors. In 1865, the advancement was schooners, which were faster than mail ships at carrying news about the US Civil War. By 1870, telegraph lines transmitted stock prices to the ticker tape and traders used telephones to place orders. The integration of computers first began in 1964 when IBM installed their systems at the New York Stock Exchange to facilitate automated quotation of prices. In 1978, the Intermarket Trading System encouraged traders to use computers to speed up their activities. In 1990, Datek released a personal computer program called ‘‘The Watcher,’’ which could spot intermarket arbitrage opportunities more quickly than those using a workstation could.

The US Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) has reacted to keep pace with automation in trading. Sometimes, their regulation seems to have hampered innovation and other times this has encouraged growth. In 1999, the SEC began to regulate electronic communication networks through its Regulation Alternative Trading System (Reg. ATS). In 2005, Regulation National Market System (Reg. NMS), enacted to strengthen the structure of the equity markets, provided the impetus for the development of HFT-fully automated, black box systems placing orders on electronic exchanges and competing on microsecond timeframes. Since then, regulators have adopted additional rules aimed at increasing the quality and transparency of automated markets. In 2014, the SEC adopted Regulation Systems Compliance and Integrity (Reg. SCI), and in 2015, the US Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) adopted Regulation Automated Trading (Reg. AT).

Prior to Reg. NMS, two classes of market participants existed-LFTs who provided liquidity and long-term investors who took that liquidity. They met to transact on the exchange floor and later through electronic networks. The advent of information technologies, however, emphasized the market fragmentation inherent in the USA. With a multiplicity of exchanges, the same stock could potentially be traded at a different price. Reg. NMS defined rules for matching orders across exchanges, so that investors would always receive the best available price. This paradigm shift gave rise to HFT as a new class of market participant. Reg. NMS defined rules whose outcome would converge price on a common value, but they did not specify a specific mechanism, and HFTs filled that gap by providing rapid information flow from one market to the next, effectively ‘‘defragmenting’’ the market. The reward for HFTs was profit from impending price moves.

Today, HFTs have effectively replaced LFTs as the primary providers of liquidity and information processing to the market, transporting the private information of investors into the equilibrium price of a security (Jones, 2013). The activity of HFTs now accounts for roughly 70% of the daily trading volume in the USA and 40% on the London Stock Exchange (LSE) (see Brogaard, 2010, 2011; Hendershott and Riordan, 2013; Castura et al., 2010).

Although the behavior of LFTs has been modeled extensively (see for example Stoll, 1978; Ho and Stoll, 1983; Grossman and Miller, 1988; Bondarenko, 2001), understanding the behavior of HFTs is only beginning to take shape. Recent information systems work has researched thematic topics such as data analytics and business model transformation (Loebbecke and Picot, 2015), technological investment required to generate business value (Dehning et al., 2003), standardization of financial services (Markus et al., 2006) and disruptive technologies (Lyytinen and Rose, 2003; Carlo et al., 2011). Where LFTs traditionally employed market savvy professionals, HFT companies employ computer scientists and mathematicians who specialize in probability and statistics, machine learning, game theory, and developing models around the market microstructure. These models typically ignore the fundamentals of shares they buy and sell. Rather, they attempt to extract profitable, asymmetric information largely from messages in the exchange multicast, which transmits data about the activity of other market participants in the limit order books for individual stocks. Examples of such information could be opportunities to earn the bid–ask spread, engage in intermarket or index arbitrage, or predict future price movements (readers wishing a brief introduction to market terminology should consult ‘‘Appendix 1: Liquidity and the bid–ask spread’’). These opportunities may arise from the ‘‘market footprint,’’ or recognizable pattern of trading by competitors or other participants. Whatever the source, this private information drives the advantageous placement of buy and sell orders back in the exchange limit order books, removing the inefficiency.

HFT only affects investors when those investors open and close their long-term positions. Proponents of HFT point to the decreased execution time and the reduced trading costs to investors as benefits of their activity. Detractors argue the HFTs frequent placement and canceling of limit orders represents only ‘‘phantom liquidity,’’ which actually increases costs to investors. In a paper published by the Financial Services Authority, Martin Wheatley, then chief executive of the UK finance regulator, commented that the costs or benefits associated with HFT were not yet clearly understood and that more research was needed to guide policy formation (Brogaard et al., 2014). Over the remainder of the paper, the business model used by HFT firms will be unpacked.

The next section discusses the changing structure of the market. Four research questions emerge from this discussion, and these are answered through modeling the insights gained in a series of primary interviews with HFT professionals and secondary source data. Recommendations made will then support the efficient operation of financial markets.

## Change to the market structure

The single function, or role, of the stock market is to provide a place for people to sell their ownership in companies. The collective interplay between buyers and sellers of those shares results in the discovery of the ‘‘fair’’ or equilibrium price for each stock. This role is essential since the ability to sell ownership is a primary factor in deciding to invest in the first place. The ability of a company to raise capital (in the primary market) is a function of the ability of their investors to sell at a fair price without undue delay (in the secondary market).

In finance, the idea of a fair price derives from informational efficiency (Fama, 1965), where the price reflects all known information-past prices, company earnings, future prospects, and the like. If all information is known, then any price changes should be a ‘‘random walk,’’ preventing any prediction on future values (Malkiel and Fama, 1970). This is the foundation of the efficient market hypothesis. Buyers and sellers in an efficient market will feel they have received a fair price, since neither they nor their counterparty (i.e., the market participant on the other side of their transaction) has any advantage. Neither knows which direction the price will move after the transaction.

A securities price can be described as a never-ending search for fundamental value. However, the role of the market is not to discover the fundamental or true price of a stock (Alan and Schwartz, 2013), only the equilibrium price, which can be thought of as the market’s best guess for this. The ideas of fundamental value comes from investors’ forecasts, which are drawn from the underlying fundamentals of the company that drive the price of its stock over the long term-its financial statements, the prospects for its products or services, the trends of its industry, or economic factors that may have a direct affect. However, over the very short time intervals of interest to HFTs, prices have been shown to not be random walks (Easley et al., 2010), and this nonrandomness is what HFTs aim to profit from.

Informational efficiency is not a fixed property of the market. This is a by-product of market participants adding their private information through their decisions to buy and sell (Cao et al., 2009). Furthermore, efficiency is not perfect. Markets only tend to move toward efficiency through price discovery. Investors seek profits by extracting private information from long-term data. HFTs seek profits by extracting private information from short-term data. The only real difference between the two is the type of data and the analytical techniques by which they derive their private information. Market efficiency arises from the competitive interaction among HFTs and investors, whose strategies and counter-strategies seek to remove the nonrandomness present in different types of data.

The idea of undue delay goes hand-in-hand with that of market liquidity. Essentially, liquidity describes the ability of a market participant to trade without adversely moving the price. Typically, a larger number of market participants enhance liquidity. As an analogy, Katz and Shapiro (1985) explain the economic term ‘‘network externalities,’’ which accurately describes liquidity when ‘‘the utility that a user derives from consumption of the good increases with the number of other agents consuming the good.’’ When liquidity disappears, prices are thought to be less fair and may deviate more from true value.

The role of providing liquidity has traditionally been the obligation of broker-dealers who act as market makers. Many articles have been written about them and how they improve market quality (Anand et al., 2009), enhance stability (Easley et al., 2011), and facilitate price efficiency (Benos and Wetherilt, 2012). Additionally, research has shown the above average returns that LFTs can generate (Anand et al., 2009; Menkveld and Wang, 2013). In return for satisfying their obligation to continuously provide liquidity by posting both limit orders to buy and sell, exchanges incentivized LFT in three ways:

1. Not charging them trading fees, or providing a rebate.

2. Provide them with a privileged physical location on the exchange floor and privileged access to the limit order book.

3. Limit the number of LFTs in each stock, effectively offering a monopoly.

However, as technology and regulation have changed the way markets operate, LFTs are not always able to compete with HFTs to provide liquidity. The sizes of bid–ask spreads, the price of liquidity, have decreased by 50% (Angel, et al., 2011). However, unlike LFTs, HFTs are under no contractual obligation to continuously provide liquidity. They are able to connect directly to the exchange servers and place limit orders at their whim. They are free to provide and remove their liquidity whenever this makes strategic sense.

Some studies criticize HFT in this regard, suggesting that this process is harmful to a functioning market at times of stress (Boehmer et al., 2014), and unlike an LFTs, which nominally have an obligation to stay in the market continuously, they simply withdraw when the market price is no longer favorable (Pannayides, 2007). Such findings are in contrast to those that find that HFT aids in market liquidity, lowers spreads and reduces latency (Hendershott et al., 2011; Hasbrouck and Saar, 2013).

It is not surprising to find friction between LFTs and HFTs. Existing contracts with LFT have had to change to ensure these providers can continue to operate profitably against those without formal obligations. Both the London Stock Exchange and the New York Stock Exchange no longer expect their LFTs to post bid and ask quotes continuously throughout the day (LSE, 2015; NYSE, 2014). LFTs seem to cast ethical aspersions on their liquidity-providing competitors. Four questions shaped this papers discussion:

1. What is the nature of the conflict between HFT and LFT firms?

2. Does the conflict negatively affect investors?

3. With HFTs now replacing LFTs in providing liquidity, will any unfulfilled functions exist in the market? If so, how long might they last?

4. What role should regulators play, if any, in the conflict?

## Data sources

One of the problems in researching HFT is obtaining data. This is true for both structured market data (which is expensive and often not made publically available) and unstructured data from interviews (due to the highly secretive nature of this process). The four research questions presented are investigated by interviews and telephone calls with industry professionals. This was made possible because two of the authors are based in Chicago, the largest center for HFT trading, and the third has extensive contacts having worked for two decades in financial services in the city of London. Additionally, two of the authors have each been employed for over twenty years in financial services (one at an HFT company, while the other heavily involved with US regulatory bodies). This provided excellent access to market participants and regulators. This study was conducted between June and December 2015.

Primary data sources consist of discussions with 28 individuals (informants) from 19 different organizations. Eight different HFT firms were contacted, located on the west coast, Chicago, and New York. All eight firms have been engaged in HFT for several years though not all have invested heavily in their information technology infrastructure. Six people were interviewed from an independent software vendor (ISV) involved with setting up a dark pool that competes against the NYSE and other execution venues. Four interviews were arranged with regulators based in either the USA or the UK to discuss HFT regulation. In addition, eight people from six large banks were interviewed in London. Head traders at these banks discussed how they built and executed orders.

Secondary data source consists of additional insights into HFT drawn from the literature, industry white papers and reports, the specialist media, regulatory publications, and presentations at industry conferences. The authors have attended many academic and industry conferences relating to HFT, in particular the 2015 Conference on High Frequency and Algorithmic Trading in Chicago during the data collection period. This provided further opportunity to meet with industry participants and capture views from the regulators. Table 1 provides a summary of the informants.

The selection of the above informants supports a functional rather than institutional approach when analyzing the activities of HFT, the methodology promoted by Gomber et al. (2011). The HFT firms traded different strategies over various instruments. The ISV and one of the banks operated their own dark pool. By having a mixture of informants, a detailed picture of the evolution of HFT was built, to gain insights on how the industry works, and to study the flow of information through order execution from the perspective of the investor.

Due to the very different activity types happening within each of these organizations, a semi-structured questionnaire was used. Traders were asked questions including: (1) What is the process used to execute your trades? (2) Have your trading strategies changed over the past few years? (3) Are you faced with any significant operational challenges? (4) Do you trade at more than one venue, and if so, which ones? (5) What changes to your trading approach have you made over the past few years? (6) Has trading become more expensive? (7) How have regulatory changes shaped the way you operate? (8) Are regulatory changes restrictive or necessary? (9) What regulatory changes do you think should be introduced?

The theories developed are based on these exploratory questions and can be seen as appropriate for inter-disciplinary research in management, information technology, and finance (see Silverman, 2011). The goal is to contribute to the development of an extensive case-study literature on financialization and HFT by drawing upon research perspectives from various disciplines (see Eisenhardt, 1989). Interviews ought to reveal how the markets have changed and shed light on emerging issues. The conflict between HFT and LFT arises from such changes in the structure of the markets, which are driven by changes in information technology and regulation. These interviews contribute to a better understanding of ‘‘algorithmization,’’ and how HFT operates within the market ecology of algorithms.

## HFT in financial markets

HFT strategy differs from other market participants (Woerner and Wixom, 2015). HFTs look for profitable opportunities in the small mispricing that arise from very short-term imbalances in supply and demand across multiple instruments, exchanges, or short-term price forecasts. Profits may be only a penny or two per trade. They necessarily rely on speed, since the entire duration of those opportunities and their position holding times may only be several seconds. This need for speed means that HFTs invest heavily in low latency information technologies that are able to respond to market data faster than any rival firm.

Technological speed is a way to make money and avoid losses. Thus, speed is an inherent part of the expected value equation for the HFT firm. In order to profit from shortterm opportunities, the firm must be able to gain advantageous position in the limit order queue, ahead of competitors. Their algorithms add limit orders to the exchange limit order book when an opportunity appears and often cancel them the instant they disappear. This can occur many times per second. This process of adding and removing liquidity tends to push the price in the correct direction when new information arrives, toward true (though unknown) value.

However, this speed is not without side effects. Since HFT firms can profit from smaller opportunities, and can leave the market quickly, they need a smaller cushion for adverse events compared with LFT firms. This cushion takes the form of a difference in the best price available to buy a stock (the bid price) and the best price available to sell a stock (the ask price). Traditionally, LFT firms use the spread as a main form of risk control. Because they cannot cancel their limit orders quickly when new information arrives and the price moves against them, they prefer larger spreads. In the finance literature, this form of insurance is called the ‘‘adverse selection premium’’ (see Glosten, 1987; Bessembinder, 2003). However, HFTs are better able to avoid adverse selection, enabling them to tolerate a smaller adverse selection premium (‘‘Appendix 2: An example of adverse selection offers a brief discussion on adverse selection). This has the effect of ‘‘pinching’’ in the spread compared to when the market is dominated by LFT.

A great deal of empirical research demonstrates that this pinching actually occurs. Spreads are on average much lower now than before the rise of HFT (Angel et al., 2011). In aggregate, this has saved long-term investors a significant amount of money in terms of trading costs. However, these costs have arisen at the expense of reduced profitability of LFTs (Menkveld, 2013). This has caused the same conflict that would occur in any industry where the purveyors of one technology seek to replace the purveyors of an older technology. Market participants or classes of market participants do not have any inherent right to earn profits in the market. Nevertheless, conflict and controversy exist. To understand the nature of this controversy, the first step is to examine the inner workings of liquidity and price discovery in the markets.

## HFT, LFT, and market liquidity

In this section, the physical structure of the market and the roles of both HFT and LFT are outlined. Figure 1 presents a simplified model of informational flows within the financial market’s ecology of algorithms. LFTs participate in the market as either market making liquidity providers or as brokers, algorithmically taking liquidity on behalf of their clients, who are long-term investors. Liquidity takers are those market participants whose main need is transactional immediacy, and they are willing to pay a fee (i.e., the bid–ask spread) for that immediacy by entering a marketable order. A liquidity providing order to (say) buy is placed as a bid in the limit order book. Whenever a liquidity taking order to sell at the market price arrives, the two orders are matched and a trade, or execution, occurs. This method of placing passive limit orders and waiting for active orders from investors is how all liquidity providers generate revenue by earning the spread.

By co-locating their servers at the exchange, as close as is physically possible to the matching engines, HFTs are able to quickly respond to market data. Looking in more detail at these information flows, Figures 2 and 3 present an overview of the differences in the HFT and LFT liquidity provision and information processing models.

The primary difference is that LFTs (depicted in Figure 2) derive their business decisions from the private information of investors. This information causes them to decide on the number of limit orders, number of market orders, and what bid–ask spread is necessary to control their adverse selection risk. Comments made by some interviewees argued that the size of the bid–ask spread is based on how large this can be and still attract investor orders.

In Figure 3, HFTs largely derive their trading decisions from the public information broadcast by the exchange about the state of the limit order book. In this sense, HFTs reprocess information left behind by the LFT traders to seize opportunities, as HFTs do not currently take outside investor orders. This also means that HFTs generally need to step inside the spreads established by the LFTs, and for this reason, they tend to improve the market for investors as will be explained below.

## The language of price discovery

Price discovery occurs through the interaction of market and limit orders. Several studies show that the choices of market participants between limit and market orders are essential to understanding how new information is incorporated into prices (e.g., Anand et al., 2005; Bloomfield et al., 2005). If a market order comes in and consumes all the liquidity at a certain price (i.e., ‘‘clears the level’’), people will either place limit orders at the same level and re-establish the price or change their limit orders on the other side of the book in the direction of cleared price level. The speed of this interaction is important for the level of efficiency in the market. The faster the traders can assert themselves, the less likely a trade from an investor (i.e., someone with a very long-term desire to buy or sell) will execute at the wrong price.

These market and limit orders, as well as adds and cancels, convey price information to the market (see Hasbrouck, 1995; Cao et al., 2009). This is important because HFTs focus primarily on exploiting the secondary information contained in the order book to enhance market efficiency. In fact, this secondary information processing can mean that price discovery does not need executions at the ‘‘wrong’’ prices to occur in order to find an equilibrium. Instead, this can be established as orders are placed then canceled as HFTs compete with each other to be in the correct place at the correct time (see Blocher et al., 2016).

## The HFT–LFT conflict

All liquidity providing firms, both HFT and LFT, have the same basic profit equation, which is depicted in Eq. (1):

$$
\begin{array}{c} \text {Profit = Captured Opportunity - Trading Costs} \\ \text {-Infrastructure Costs} \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

In Eq. (1), the Captured Opportunity represents the amount of informational opportunity present times the ability of the trading firm to profit from opportunity (i.e., being in a position to have their orders executed first so as to capture this opportunity). Trading Costs include the bid–ask spread and the rebates offered by the exchange. Infrastructure Costs include information technology costs, compliance costs, rent, salaries, and other expenses related to running the trading business. When a company looks to minimize latency, not only are charges made by the exchange for order data and co-location, but the infrastructure costs for the latest and fastest servers, fast-executing, proprietary software, microwave and laser towers, and fiber optic cables are high. All this speed enables the algorithm-versusalgorithm competition for advantageous position in the limit order queue.

The nature of each of these terms in Eq. (1) is different for HFT and LFT firms. This is what causes the conflict. HFT trading firms both capture opportunity and provide liquidity, earning the spread. LFT firms also rely on these two methods of making money. However, LFTs can also make money by charging commissions to their investor clients to execute their trades and promise to give them ‘‘best execution.’’ Their infrastructure costs are fairly low. Some retail LFT brokerage sites allow direct access to trades of common exchange traded funds for free, and many LFT brokerage firms allow clients to trade as many times as they want per day in a stock for a flat fee. The collapse of both the bid–ask spread and the commission schedule of LFT trading services has made their business less profitable.

HFT activity disrupts the LFT business in two ways. The willingness of the HFT to squeeze the spread changes the risk-return calculation for LFT market makers. Moreover, the increased liquidity, and rapid movement to true price based on information make the process of adding value by the LFT even harder. An investor can directly trade in the market and reduce any costs. This is easier also because of the general access to markets that HFTs have brought, at least in part, to the industry. These forces lead to a natural enmity between HFT and LFT firms.

If an LFT is trading against informed investors, they will effectively be paying more than they can sell for (negative spread). Further, unlike the LFT, the HFT does not hold any inventory at the end of the day, reducing the risk associated with any positions. Thus, in implementing their business model HFTs make the market unfriendly to the business model of LFTs. This comes at a time when LFT firms feel pressure on many fronts. The general advance of information technology means that investors are no longer as willing to pay for other services the LFT firm may provide, such as stock research. LFTs themselves compete more strongly on commissions charged to clients, but the ease with which clients can switch their broker has increased.

For all these reasons, LFT firms may view HFTs as representing an existential threat. Some interviewees commented that LFT firms are fighting back by casting ethical aspersions on HFTs. Some LFTs, for example, refer to HFT activity as ‘‘front-running,’’ now that their own privileged access to market data cannot generate excess returns. This is an illegal activity, which involves trading for yourself in front of a customer, or other market participant, to whom you owe a fiduciary responsibility. HFTs have no such obligation legal or ethical, so use of the term is wrong and purely derogatory. The regulators have picked up such comments, and the SEC recently broke new ground in claiming that merely placing orders in the book in a certain pattern may be ‘‘deceptive’’ and therefore, market manipulation (Cooper et al., 2016).

One other important criticism heaped on HFT stems from the flash crash of May 6, 2010, when the stock market plummeted roughly 1000 points and then rallied back within minutes. This event is widely cited as the embodiment of what is wrong with HFT-liquidity is only provided whenever this is convenient, but withdraws that liquidity whenever the market is unstable, thereby exacerbating illiquidity exactly when the market has the greatest need. This causes disruptions in otherwise orderly price discovery and most of all may cause a lack of confidence in the fairness of prices and the ability of the market to do its job.

## HFT as a business

HFT firms are above all businesses. They seek to compete by operating strategies that consistently make profits. Recent papers (Kumiega et al., 2014; Cooper et al., 2015) establish the criterion for HFT firms to remain viable and called this ‘multi-scale capability.’’ In essence, a trading firm that is multi-scale capable can ‘‘almost always’’ cover its average costs with average profits, including infrastructure costs, over an acceptable period. Although the statistics can be compli cated, the basic idea is not. The HFT firms aims to combine its trading strategies in ways that they achieve a consistent state of profitability after paying all their costs, while limiting or controlling the risk of extreme loss, or even technological failure, that may overwhelm them.

In order to achieve this state of multi-scale capability, HFT firms use competencies that fall into three groups:

• Identifying more trading opportunities and capturing more of the ones discovered;

• The efficiency of the firm’s internal development cycle, cost management, and quality control;

• The firm’s ability to both respond to and manipulate its competitive environment.

The trading community in general (both HFT and LFT) has an interest in making as many profitable trades a day as possible. However, they do not (necessarily) have an incentive to make all their money from providing efficiency services. If any trading firm can enhance their capability by manipulating the regulatory environment, they will. Therefore, a quick answer to the role of government in markets is to say they should ensure that the profit incentives for all trading firms are aligned with efficient price discovery, and not market manipulation.

Another important point is that the HFT business model does not include continuous liquidity provision to the market to keep trading orderly. For HFT, this is a side effect of their business. Does the competition among HFTs to be the first in the limit order queue result in greater liquidity than having the markets controlled by LFTs whose role is solely to maintain a continuous market? The answer is certainly yes, at least on average. Critics, however, think this liquidity provided by the HFT firms is not real, because this can be withdrawn at a moment’s notice. In this kind of reasoning the larger spreads and slower market response times provided by LFT-only markets is a necessary insurance premium charged by LFT firms for the maintenance of continuous markets. An orderliness necessary to maintain confidence in the markets so that people will continue to trade (Cooper et al., 2016).

The arguments against this are several. The flash crash of 2010 (WSJ, 2010) lasted 36 min and has not been repeated. Although many people predicted doom and gloom and a lack of confidence in markets as a result of this debacle, the fact is most investors never actually noticed the crash and return of the market. They were in a meeting, or at lunch, or only looked at closing prices. The competitive pressures exist to rapidly establish the correct new price. The fact that a second flash crash has not occurred is evidence of this. HFT algorithms have evolved from single purpose models that can allow only one mode of trading to multi-stage models that can switch modes with market conditions. The specter of flash crashes seems real only in the dreams of regulators and academics.

The question that does need to be addressed, however, is: what is the correct role for regulation in the marketplace?

## Regulation and responsible trading

Automation may enable unethical behavior by obscuring its source by burying this in technological complexity (Ramaswamy and Joshi, 2009). In the broader sense of the term, the ethics of automation is still evolving. This begins, however, with Asimov (1942) and his laws that automatons may not harm humans and must obey orders. The fear is that, ‘‘(t)he modular design of systems can mean that no single person or group can fully grasp the manner in which the system will interact or respond to a complex flow of new inputs…This kind of blamelessness provides insufficient protection for those who might be harmed (Allen et al., 2006).’’ Harm in the sense of electronic markets is the financial loss caused by some other participant’s trading algorithm. The fear of robots in the markets (rational or irrational) is often used as the justification for new regulation.

HFT provides two challenges to regulation. First, the ability of co-located equipment to place orders faster than a human can intervene means that an out-of-control HFT strategy can disrupt a market, and bankrupt at least one company. Knight Capital lost \$440 million in firm value in roughly half an hour due to software problems in its trading systems (WSJ, 2012). Second, since provision of continuous liquidity and servicing of investor orders is not explicitly a part of the business goals of an HFT firm, they could find ways to make profit that are detrimental to investors or market stability. Algorithms running amok, trading wildly, and crashing the entire financial system are a real concern. Actual experience, however, is not nearly so dramatic. Knight Trading accidently left one trading server with old software, while several others were trading with new software. Unable to communicate properly with each other the server with the old software proceeded to put out incredibly large numbers of orders leading to ruinous losses. The loss bankrupted the firm. However, trading did not stop. The market was not disrupted, and in a sense, exactly the correct party bore the consequences of the error. Moreover, large trading debacle seem to have become a rarity (see Kumiega et al., 2016). Nevertheless, safety is a desirable goal.

Cooper et al. (2015) argue that operating in control is part of being prudent in HFT. In the trading context, the first steps toward control mean building expectations about the relevant control variables defining a strategy. The second step involves monitoring activity continuously, to determine the likelihood that the system running is currently performing as expected. Such a system is outlined in Cooper and Van Vliet (2012), but a sophisticated methodology may not even be necessary. The point is to have a sense of the important control variables: profit and loss, number of messages (add, cancels, executes) expected per minute, etc., and a sense of whether the current activity is likely consistent with those expectations.

Along with a monitoring system, some procedure would be necessary for stopping the accumulation of further risk with a mechanism to wind down an offending strategy. Although these precautions seem obvious, the lack of such a system allowed the Knight debacle to unfold. The fact that such a debacle happened has caused clamor for regulatory oversight in many parts of government. Some interviewees commented that further regulation should not really be necessary to limit such events, but sadly, this seems to be vital.

In lieu of prescriptive regulation, some have argued that self-regulation, through industry-defined ISO 9000-style standards would be more flexible and better attuned to the needs of the industry (Van Vliet et al., 2013). This is the approach of many other industries such as health care, aerospace, food processing and chemicals (Davis et al., 2013). What would seem appropriate is that all HFT firms are required to have a demonstrable capability for setting expectations for their trades, monitoring their performance in real time, and unwinding their positions if a violation occurs. Thus, the consequences of failure fall on the participant, whereas the consequences of regulatory failure would fall on taxpayers as a whole. Furthermore, a marketplace of prudent strategies is essential for an informationally efficient market. Such a marketplace would have strategies eliminating market inefficiencies without the fear of out-ofcontrol activity. More complicated regulation than this could simply lead firms to regulatory manipulation. Cooper et al. (2016) argue that ethical automated trading strategies:

1. Operate in statistical control, or some equivalent

2. Should be multi-scale capable

3. Place all orders in plain view of the public and leave them their long enough for another firm with the fastest common technology to respond to them

If these three criteria are met, the algorithmic trading strategy is likely to satisfy Asimov’s rules. The strategy will be unlikely to harm other market participants and generate returns that are acceptable to the firm. The first point could easily be handled by an industry-specific management standard. The second point is a self-evident condition to stay in business. The third, however, involves following several controversial principles:

1. No pattern of orders that can be responded to can be called deceptive or manipulative

2. No order is devoid of information. Therefore, all orders should be freely observable

3. No one has a right to profit in the marketplace. All orders should be entered on an equal footing

Undoubtedly, dark pool operators, exchanges with many different order types, and LFTs would strongly contest these points, though transparency is a hard principle to argue against from first principles.

## Regulatory manipulation

As alluded to earlier, regulatory manipulation is a distinct possibility in a dynamic industry. Regulations always lag innovation. Highly agile HFT firms are in a position to exploit any opportunity in the regulatory topography. Then, the larger LFT firms must react as a matter of survival. Thus, the incentives exist for LFTs to manipulate the regulatory response to innovation. Of course, the market does not exist for the benefit of either of these groups.

Nevertheless, having no regulation is bad. Participants can do bad things, such as quote stuffing, wash trading, even cornering markets, all of which are designed to prevent or inhibit the flow of information. Civil remedies to such acts (i.e., lawsuits) would be slow and cumbersome. On the other hand, excessive regulation has two problems. One, this would be expensive to comply with. Two, because the regulated are faster and more agile than the regulators, a new form of profit opportunity from regulatory manipulation naturally arises. This undermines the reason that markets exist. Therefore, the solution is to the amount of sensible regulation that maximizes information throughput. Those rules should be simple, in order to minimize the chance for manipulation, putting the focus on the needs of investors.

## Conclusion

Financialization is a trend that has rapidly increased the need for information processing in all markets. In the financial markets, this need has been filled by HFT firms. This trend began in the 1990s and accelerated in the USA with Reg. NMS, and in Europe with MiFID. Today, the rapid flow of large volumes of capital at efficient prices is highly dependent on the work of HFT firms and simply turning them off is not a viable option without sending the market itself back in time.

However, this rapid development in the markets has come with controversy. LFT firms, adhering to a more traditional role as market makers and intermediaries, find their profits inevitably squeezed. The conflict between the business models of HFT and LFT lead invariably to name-calling and calls for more regulation. However, the problem with regulation is that complicated rules lead to manipulation when applied to highly adaptable and rapidly evolving industries.

The better approach to regulation is to focus on the longterm investor’s ability to execute in a liquid market with efficient and effective price discovery. This means making sure that the infrastructure of LFT and HFT firm’s is reliable, and all their market actions are visible and easily interpreted. Specifically, regulators should not worry about the individual winners and losers among the liquidity providers, nor slow the market down as this will not change the basic business model.

This does not mean that HFT should be left unattended and unobserved. Some informants speculated that HFT will continue to evolve to provide liquidity in all market environments and at all times, since a profit incentive to do this exists, and HFT is a competitive and rapidly evolving industry. However, this must be tested and observed in the future. The lack of a second flash crash does not necessarily rule out another one, but is indicative of HFT’s evolution to accommodate many market environments.

The future of the LFT-HFT conflict is uncertain but is best left to the participants to sort out. Perhaps mergers will once again restore the market to having one class of intermediaries providing liquidity to investors. Though that is unclear, what is clear is that only a climate that permits methodological pluralism will promote efficiency and robust markets. The market has changed as financialization has shaped its new structure. Transparency is essential if the market is to be regarded as robust and fair, but further regulation alone is not the only solution to resolving this conflict. Responsible trading will only happen through improved standards within the industry itself. The main concern from a societal point of view is whether the prices for investors, the true purchasers of goods, are being set as quickly and effectively as possible. The main goal of all regulation should be to facilitate price discovery. Individual players are only useful to the extent they aide in this process.

Our analysis opens up new research questions. What constitutes responsible trading relative to algorithmization is still evolving, especially in light of greater autonomy granted to these automatons by new techniques, namely machine learning and artificial intelligence. Might some previously ethical HFT strategy ‘‘learn’’ its way across some ethical boundary? Who bears the liability? How do regulators monitor for such occurrences? The disruptive effect of information systems on industries and organizations (Straub and Wetherbe, 1989) continues to raise new concerns about future development as innovation continues-new financial instruments, new quantitative techniques and algorithms, and more disruptive innovations, such as Bitcoin-how will their impact be assessed? How and how fast can regulators react? How will conflicts between the more agile and the more entrenched market participants be resolved? These are all open questions at the intersection of financialization-both productization and algorimization-and information technology, and the academic community must contribute to their resolution.

## Appendix 1: Liquidity and the bid–ask spread

Liquidity describes how easily investors can either buy or sell their shares without changing the price. The more shares that are available, the more liquid the market is said to be. The traditional exchange model (up until approximately the turn of the century) was built on market makers and specialists in most trading venues. Market makers are stock brokers who (by contractual obligation with the exchange) continuously offer to both buy and sell shares, so that the market has continuous liquidity (they add), and people can smoothly transact business (they take). The idea is that continuous liquidity provides for orderly price discovery. Similarly, a specialist on the floor of an exchange holds the entire order book (a list of prices at which people have stated they will buy and sell) and matches orders of buyers and sellers, acting like a market maker and providing additional liquidity when necessary. Figure A1 is a simplified picture of an exchange’s order book. Different prices represent different ‘‘levels’’ of the book. Liquidity may be shallow or deep depending on how many levels have significant volume in them.

When an order is sent to a stock exchange that is intended to provide liquidity, one of the most common order types will be an Add limit order. This is an instruction to buy a security at no more than a specified price (the bid), or to sell a security at no less than a specified price (the ask). This limit order will ‘‘rest’’ in the book until executed or canceled. Limit orders may be canceled by issuing a Cancel order. The quantity that is offered at each price level is usually made up by one or more limit orders. The time recorded when a limit order was received is used to prioritise how orders are matched, with the earliest received being executed first. A Market order is often sent by a fund manager when they want to either buy or sell a security at the current market price. This will take liquidity away from the market, by being matched at the exchange against standing limit orders.

Looking at Figure A2, market makers are offering liquidity at the ‘bid’ (they are prepared to ‘‘buy’’ 1,000 shares at a price of \$98) and ‘‘ask’’ (they are prepared to ‘‘sell’’ 1,000 shares at a price of \$104) price.

Table A1 illustrates how a fund manager will receive \$9,800 if they sell or pay \$10,400 if they buy 1,000 shares. The market maker will trade in the opposite direction, buying low and selling high.

Market makers and specialists (collectively DMMs) do not provide liquidity out of a sense of altruism. Like any business, they seek to earn a profit, and the profit they earn comes through the bid–ask spread, the difference between the (lower) price at which they are willing to buy and the (higher) price at which they are willing to sell. The greater the spread, the more profit is potentially available to the market maker. This value represents the premium an investor must pay on each share to compensate the market maker for any risk that he could incur. An investor will sell a share for many reasons, most often because he believes their value will fall. By how much and over what time period is unknown, but trading against such informed traders is clearly a risky strategy.

Around the world, HFT has squeezed the BAS to its lowest ever values, which in the example shown in Figure A2 is \$1. This reduced spread will lower any potential adverse selection that is obtained by trading against informed traders. It will also benefit the investor because now his cost to buy will fall, while the returns from a sale will increase. Looking at Table A2, the cost to buy 1,000 shares will fall from \$10,400 to \$10,100, while his revenue from a sell will increase from \$9800 to \$10,000.

## Appendix 2: An example of adverse selection

Consider the following scenario. Investors come to the market with their private information about a stock and wish to sell their shares. This transaction will (in general) move the stock price down. This is certainly not in the best interest of the liquidity providing LFT firm that just bought the stock. That is if an investor decides to sell their IBM shares to raise cash instead of selling Apple shares, the investor often thinks this is because IBM has worse prospects. The LFT firm taking the other side of this trade now has the risk of holding in inventory shares of IBM, the price of which may very well go down (this is the adverse selection risk). In order to offset this risk, the LFT firm must charge the bid–ask spread. The actual direction the market is going is not that important to the LFT trader as long as the spread is wide enough to cover the risk. If the best, but incomplete information available to the LFT traders is that the market price should be \$50 for a stock, they may collectively allow the buy and sell prices to be \$49.90 and \$50.10 to cover any adverse selection risk to which they are exposed. Moreover, if LFT firms see a large order imbalance coming in the spread may be adjusted wider accordingly.
