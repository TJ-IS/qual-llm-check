# A middleware framework for managing transactions in group-oriented mobile commerce services

Upkar Varshney

Department of Computer Information Systems, Georgia State University, Atlanta, Georgia 30302-4015, United State

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 8 March 2007 Received in revised form 1 July 2008 Accepted 6 July 2008 Available online 17 July 2008

Keywords: Mobile commerce Group-oriented services Transactions Network protocols

## a b s t r a c t

The emerging group-oriented mobile commerce services are receiving signi<sup>fi</sup>cant interest among researchers, developers, wireless service providers, and users. Some of these services, including mobile auctions, mobile <sup>fi</sup>nancial services, and multi-party interactive games, are transaction-oriented and will require the network and protocol support for managing transactions. In this paper, we focus on technical challenges of managing transactions in group-oriented mobile commerce services by presenting a framework, which includes requirements, membership-management and support for dependable transactions. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we present several group-oriented mobile services, characterize transaction requirements of group-oriented m-commerce services, present protocols for membership management to support both distributed and centralized processing, and present multi-network access and agent-based system for dependable transactions.

## 1. Introduction

The emerging mobile services, including mobile commerce services, are receiving signi<sup>fi</sup>cant attention in terms of identifying new applications, designing frameworks, and engineering networking solutions [8]. A more recent review of these services can be found in [3] and personalization for mobile commerce service is presented in [13]. Many m-commerce services including mobile auctions, multiparty games, mobile <sup>fi</sup>nancial applications, mobile advertising, and mobile entertainment services will require group communications, where several users have to simultaneously interact for an outcome or the same information is sent to multiple users [2]. These grouporiented m-commerce services are likely to be transaction-oriented with signi<sup>fi</sup>cant monetary value of some transactions. These also have the potential to generate billions of dollars in revenue due to their business, entertainment, and monetary values. Before group-oriented m-commerce services can be implemented and widely deployed, many challenges, including transaction support, must be addressed. The support for transactions is inherently complex due to the need to co-ordinate group membership, manage user inputs, and implement application synchronization. The support for transaction may become even more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult challenge due to brief dis-connectivity or intermittent connectivity in the underlying infrastructure and adhoc wireless networks, user mobility, and the varying performance of wireless links. These limitations will lead to poor transaction performance in terms of increased delays and lower transaction completion probability.

We <sup>fi</sup>rst overview some of the related work in this area. Although routing and related issues for wireless group-oriented services have received considerable attention [2], very little work exists for transactions in mobile services. So far, the transaction support primarily deals with database access under connectivity problems. This includes using synchronization [1], performing recovery for transactions [4], and using recovery protocol [7]. A preliminary simulation-based performance evaluation of group-oriented mobile services using basic time-out mechanisms has been presented in [9].

It can be observed that most of the work done so far primarily deals with database access and recovery for mobile transactions after an occurrence of wireless connectivity problem or failure. The following issues have not been addressed in the literature so far: (a) detailed requirements of group and transactions-oriented mobile commerce services, (b) support for group membership for mobile transactions under user mobility and wireless connectivity problems, and, (c) support for dependable transactions. Therefore, there is a need to address many of the above challenges in group and transactionsoriented mobile commerce services. The research questions we attempt to address are:

(i) What are the major requirements of group and transactionsoriented mobile services?

(ii) How to provide networking and protocol support for managing continued membership in group-oriented mobile services under user mobility and wireless connectivity problems?

(iii) How to design networking support for ensuring the reliability of important and highly-valued transactions?

Although each of the above questions may take a separate paper to provide necessary details, we attempt to address these issues here in the form of a framework [11]. This can be considered as the part of future decision support systems [5]. The challenges in the group and transactions-oriented mobile commerce services include (i) how to provide group management for mobile users who may even experience connectivity problems, (ii) how to support transaction performance such as reliability, (iii) how to charge for transactions and other business model and strategies issues for wireless service providers, and, (iv) user issues including adoption, training and trust issues. Although all of these challenges are very important, we have decided to focus on requirements and technical challenges as shown in Fig. 1.

Fig. 1. The components of the proposed framework.

The group management deals with how to maintain group membership for mobile users even when they experience brief disconnectivity and/or intermittent connectivity. The group management process can be implemented using network protocols, which can perform pre-processing, manage user inputs, check for failures, and offer support for multi-stage transactions. The group management process can be implemented by either distributed or centralized processing. For each of these scenarios, we present a protocol for group membership management.

The support for dependable transaction requires that mobile users are allowed to complete their transactions even when not able to access a certain wireless network. For this requirement, we propose that mobile users can access one of several different wireless networks to complete their transactions. To support such multinetwork access, we present network architecture and protocols for group members and coordinator.

The major contribution of this paper is a framework for managing transactions that includes requirements of group-oriented m-commerce services, protocols for membership management, and network support for multi-step dependable transactions. The paper is formatted as follows: in section 2, we discuss group-oriented mobile commerce services, and the details of the proposed framework are presented in section 3. A discussion of current research and conclusion is presented in section 4.

## 2. Group-oriented m-commerce services

Some examples of group-oriented m-commerce services are mobile auctions, mobile multi-party interactive games, and mobile <sup>fi</sup>nancial services [8]. Next, we present an overview of mobile auctions, mobile multi-party games, and mobile payment/<sup>fi</sup>nancial services.

## 2.1. Overview of group-oriented m-commerce services

Mobile auctions will involve users with hand-held devices joining one or more groups. A demanding service due to a number of users in several locations, auctions will require co-ordination of user inputs, continued connectivity, and symmetric real-time multicast. The transactions are likely to occur few times in a week lasting from seconds to minutes. Using mobile agents, a user could become part of several auctions and mobile agents can also reduce the increased bidding traf<sup>fi</sup>c as the auction progresses.

Mobile auctions can be modeled using multiple stages and participants with signi<sup>fi</sup>cant interdependency between stages (Fig. 2). Pre-processing involves service advertisement and discovery, joining and veri<sup>fi</sup>cation of members, and setting up of auction rules and regulations, while post-processing will involve wrapping up the transaction including any veri<sup>fi</sup>cation. In between pre- and postprocessing, a typical stage involves single request and/or multiple responses (one from each participant) and requires a subset of mobile users to provide inputs/actions in reaching a consensus or outcome.

The duration of mobile auction transactions could vary signi<sup>fi</sup>- cantly depending on number of users involved, type of auction, and number of bids per user. Since the lack of input from a certain user in a stage could result in a different outcome of the auction, safeguards must be put in place to avoid such problems. There are auctions with speci<sup>fi</sup>c rules, which could be included to avoid unintended outcomes. However, we instead rely on network and protocols to provide support for group-oriented applications including mobile auctions. In some cases, it is possible to identify both the number and actual “critical” users whose response will be suf<sup>fi</sup>cient for the correctness of application. Other potential safeguards could include (a) use of timeout and acknowledgement based inputs and response, where each input is timed and acknowledged, (b) use of multi-step protocols where “intention” is indicated and responded before actual input can be transmitted, and (c) where all or none inputs are considered in moving forward or backward on individual stages. Our future work will address correctness of group-oriented transactions using modi<sup>fi</sup>cations in auction rules and protocols.

Multi-party interactive games include variety of games to mobile users in a user- or application-de<sup>fi</sup>ned group. The games could involve millions of users worldwide and utilize location-based information in forming user groups. These will require continued connectivity, very low delays for producing real-time or near real-time performance for mobile participants, and symmetric real-time multicast with active participation from multiple users. The transactions are likely to occur few times in a week lasting from seconds to minutes. Some games could last longer to several hours and may only be limited by the energy level of players, device battery power and/or continued connectivity of wireless networks.

Transactions involved in multi-party games could also be payments for playing games or even credits for winning certain steps. It has been suggested that the games could drive deployment and adoption of mobile commerce services, especially the group-oriented. Many wireless carriers and content providers and developers are in the process of designing and developing suitable multi-party games for mobile users.

Mobile Financial/Payment Services will allow mobile users to conduct multi-party <sup>fi</sup>nancial transactions including mobile payments. Financial services are likely to have the most economic impact, as mobile transactions could involve billion of dollars. These will require low delays, continued connectivity, and symmetric multicast and active participation from users. The transactions are likely to occur few times a week from seconds to minutes.

As shown in Fig. 3, a mobile user could order products and services from one or more service providers or businesses, who will then contact either a trusted third party, wireless service provider, or a <sup>fi</sup>nancial institution for veri<sup>fi</sup>cation involving the user and amount of purchase. The payments can be made to the business or service providers by a bank, wireless service provider, or another payment party. These can then be subtracted from user's bank account, added to his/her wireless phone bill, or withdrawn from his/her m-wallet. It is also possible that user could pay for products and services using “mobile money” that has been provided to it by another user or 3rd party mobile money provider using pre-paid or post-paid service. Notice that this scenario could be used for P2P, B2C, and B2B mobile commerce involving both micro and macro payments. A <sup>fi</sup>nancial transaction is shown in Fig. 4.

The macro payments may involve as low as \$10 to as high as several million dollars depending on whether the transaction is P2P (person-to-person), B2C or B2B. Unlike micro payments, there are major incentives for both <sup>fi</sup>nancial institutions as well as wireless service providers to get involved as the stakes are much higher. The security is a bigger issue due to the amount of money involved and few non-payments could lead to signi<sup>fi</sup>cant losses for retailers and vendors. Also unlike micro-payments, it will be much harder for users to pre-pay or allow to be added to phone bills for wireless services. It is likely that <sup>fi</sup>nancial institutions will lead the macropayment market due to their traditional expertise in <sup>fi</sup>nancial transactions. However, regulatory and market forces combined with the level of economic activities could in<sup>fl</sup>uence such decisions in different countries. In any case, risk management and co-operation among multiple players would be a critical issue in supporting macropayments.

## 2.2. Connectivity problems

A transaction will involve several mobile users, some of which may experience brief dis-connectivity and/or intermittent connectivity [10]. Although, instances of such connectivity problems are likely to be independent, however, co-related dis-connectivity or intermittent connectivity for users may exist due to the sharing of common environments. It is known that failures of base stations, switches, and database in infrastructure-oriented wireless networks are not very common [6], but the channel or radio-frequency (RF) links between base stations and mobile users can experience a range of problems affecting user's connectivity. There must be some differentiation between the transaction failures/dis-connectivity due to RF problems and the ones due to core network problems involving base station, switches and databases. This is because RF problems are short-lived and transactions can be re-tried soon, while failures may take longer time to be repaired before the transaction can be re-tried.

![](/api/attachments/6UH4PJT3/fulltext/images/33923d98fbc33399f628c038c472a240068d8aac07aa18b4e61c0ed21e295469.jpg)  
Fig. 3. Several mobile payment scenarios.

Based on the duration and frequency of on–off periods, we propose to divide these into three classes: short-term off periods lasting from few to few hundred milliseconds and resulting due to channel behavior, handoff-problems; medium-term off periods lasting from few hundred milliseconds to few seconds due to lack of resources, obstacles, and user mobility; long-term off-periods lasting from few seconds to several minutes due to failure of devices, base stations, or other components. In this paper, we address brief dis-connectivity and/or intermittent connectivity for group-oriented mobile commerce applications by designing protocols that can provide a highlevel of transaction completion under short- and medium-term connectivity problems.

## 3. Details of the transaction framework

We describe the framework for transactions management in terms of requirements of group-oriented m-commerce services, membership management protocols, and protocols for dependable transactions.

## 3.1. Requirements of group-oriented mobile commerce services

Many of the group-oriented mobile commerce services are likely to be transactions-oriented. A transaction can be de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a multi-step task where all steps must be executed in an ordered sequence. The outcome of transaction could be quite different if one or more steps are not properly executed or a certain sequence of execution is not followed. To avoid this, all or some steps of a transaction can be undone. Also a transaction should be isolated from other transactions that may affect the common values/parameters. M-commerce services present complex transaction requirements due to the diversity of applications, users, and the usage model. The diversity can be shown in terms of group communications (symmetric vs. asymmetric), number of stages (single vs. multiple), performance (real-time vs. non real-time), connectivity and reliability requirements. One extreme is mobile advertising requiring asymmetric, single stage, non-real time support where reliability and connectivity are not critical. On the other side, mobile auctions and multi-party interactive games require symmetric, multi-stage, real-time support where reliability and connectivity are critical.

We focus on supporting several different group-oriented mobile services by utilizing a more general approach. The group-oriented mcommerce services can be modeled using one or more stages and participants. The number and the inter-dependency of stages will vary signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly among applications (one simple stage with a large number of participants for mobile advertising to multiple stages with fewer users for mobile auctions). Each step requires a subset of mobile users to provide inputs/actions in reaching a consensus or outcome. The duration of transactions vary signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly, such as longer transactions for mobile multi-party interactive games and fairly short transactions for mobile advertising. For a general case, we assume a three-stage sequence: pre-processing, inputs from users, and post processing. The pre-processing stage could involve service advertisement and discovery, joining of members and setting up of rules and regulations, while post-processing will involve wrapping up the transaction including any veri<sup>fi</sup>cation. The lack of input from a certain user in a stage could affect one or more inputs in the next stage, thus possibly resulting in a different outcome of the application. In some cases, it is possible to identify both the number and actual “critical” users whose response will be suf<sup>fi</sup>cient for the correctness of application. The number of stages could be chosen by the total number of users, minimum and maximum number of users per stage, delay tolerance per stage, processing delay per stage, and the maximum tolerable transaction delay. Using these and inter-dependency constraints, a transaction can be broken into a certain number of stages, each with some number of users to achieve a certain delay. The current connectivity of mobile users could also be used in determining the number of stages.

Since there has been little work in group-oriented m-commerce services, it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to characterize the requirements of grouporiented mobile commerce services. A preliminary set of preliminary metrics exists in [9] and we will use ETR (Entity to Transaction Ratio) and AFI (Application Flexibility Index). ETR describes number of users in a group transaction while AFI signi<sup>fi</sup>es the ratio of minimum number of users required for a transaction to the total number of users. Additionally, we include Transaction Frequency and Transaction Duration as additional metrics in characterizing the requirements of group-oriented m-commerce services (Table 1). These are few metrics to characterize group and transactions-oriented mobile commerce services. In future, it may be possible to use social and managementoriented metrics to characterize group and transaction-oriented mobile commerce services. These could include factors such as the level of user training, quality of service, level of trust, pricing of transactions-oriented mobile commerce services. These are harder to quantify at this time, but can be considered in future for group and transactions-oriented mobile commerce services.

## 3.2. Group and membership management protocols

Membership protocols are important in transactions support as these allow mobile users to remain the group members even under brief periods of wireless dis-connectivity. The work on membership in group-oriented mobile services includes basic requirements [10] and virtual guide function for a tourist group [12]. In Virtual guide, metropolitan services are envisioned to support groups of mobile users who have some kind of logical association with each other, but who are distributed throughout the city. The authors have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed voice group communication and localization of group members as the two main requirements. The authors support membership management in mobile group application to allow members of a tourist group which can move independently but still keep in touch. A preliminary performance evaluation of group-oriented mobile services using basic time-out mechanisms has been presented in [9].

In this section, we address how to support group-oriented mobile commerce services by designing protocols for transaction management. We have used the following factors in the design of protocols:

• Support for both real-time as well as non real-time transactions

A transaction stage can involve reliable transmission of information on application/transaction to member users, reliable reception of user inputs (response) using one or more time-outs and retransmissions [10]. All inputs must be processed and critical users for next stage are also determined based on current state of transaction, user polling, and current list of group-members. In a stage multiple nonoverlapping requests and responses could exist. The number of stages and time-out for different users in a stage are important and challenging issues. To ensure transaction completion, applications could allow certain range of time. Also, if failure of a critical user occurs, then transaction must be aborted. Also, if non-critical stages exist, then transaction should not be aborted after a time-out during those stages. These requirements are included in the design of two protocols as shown in Figs. 6 and 7. It is possible to have more choices of protocols, but we decided to use one protocol for distributed and one for centralized implementation.

Protocol A involves deriving dynamic time-outs for different stages of a transaction, and, can be used for symmetric or asymmetric group communications and reliable transactions. It manages group memberships, co-ordinates user inputs, and supports distributed processing of group application by treating all users equal. Protocol B can offer all of the functionality of Protocol A, but it works in a more centralized applications environment as users are treated with different status (priority) in its operation. To overcome failure of single source, it has the additional ability to assign protocol functions to other user(s) if the source crashes, which may overcome the effects of single-point failure and result in a more dependable support for transactions. Both Protocols A and B derive dynamic time-outs for individual stages of a transaction using transaction response time, number of stages, time left, and predicted time interval for a user input. The protocols can coordinate multi-stage inputs from users, and communicate with the applications on certain users whose participation is necessary for a transaction. The protocols can operate deadlock free even if users crash or do not provide inputs and the intelligence to support diverse set of transactions. Protocol A and Protocol B can be used for mobile auctions, interactive games, mobile <sup>fi</sup>nancial services, patient monitoring services, mobile entertainment services, and more sophisticated mcommerce services. The decision model for such evaluation is shown in Fig. 8, where several decisions are made on number of sessions, users per session, mobility, and connectivity levels. Certainly more work is needed in evaluating the correct operation and performance issues before implementing these protocols on hand-held devices.

## 3.3. Protocols for dependable transactions

The support for dependable transactions is important as many group-oriented transactions may carry signi<sup>fi</sup>cant monetary value. The work towards improving the reliability of transactions includes performing recovery for transactions [4] and using recovery protocol [7].

In this section, we address how to provide support for dependable transactions for group-oriented mobile commerce services. These services can be modeled using multiple stages and participants. The number and inter-dependency of stages will vary signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly among applications. In general, dependable support for mobile transactions can be provided by wireless infrastructure by overcoming network failures and brief intermittent connectivity problems [10]. Here, we address transaction management, which includes transaction stages, user inputs co-ordination, and consensus functions.

The architecture for dependable support of group-oriented mobile transactions (Fig. 9) with multi-network access will allow users to overcome time varying and/or location-sensitive dis-connectivity or intermittent connectivity problems and failures in one network by switching to another wireless network. We assume that local characteristics of all wireless networks are available and updated as connectivity levels to different networks change in time and in location. This information will allow a device to decide which network to switch to based on signal strength, quality of channel, and network load. Additional factors can also be added in the decision making process. This multi-network decision for switching will allow users to remain part of the group transactions and can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the outcome, not possible before with a single wireless network access where connectivity problems or failures would have resulted in transaction getting aborted. As shown in Fig. 9, user A provides input for ith stage using network Y (Net-Y), but as the access quality worsens, it switches to network X (Net-X) for jth stage and after some time signal strength worsens there, so it switches back to Net-Y for kth stage. Similar switching occurs for user B. These handoffs are performed to overcome connectivity problems in networks X and Y at different times.

The information on users current ability to access wireless networks will be kept and updated as needed in a matrix with number of rows equal to number of users in the transaction and number of columns equal to the number of different wireless networks that can be accessed. This matrix, called “User Network” matrix can be updated as necessary. The current access to a wireless network may be based on the network coverage, intermittent and brief dis-connectivity, and various failures in the network. There are many issues that must be addressed before matrix can be deployed to support dependable transactions. The matrix is needed to allow users to know their own access to wireless networks and also to determine which wireless networks the next inputs from other users will come from. It can be assumed that a user-input for an individual step will involve single wireless network, while most or all of the wireless networks can be used by different users before a multi-step transaction is completed. Depending on the implementation of group applications, a user may need the whole matrix (distributed implementation) or just its own row along with the row for group coordinator (centralized implementation). In any case, group coordinator in both implementations would require the entire matrix. Although individual wireless networks could offer some support for transactions, such as retransmission or time-out in ad hoc wireless networks, however, we do not assume such functionality.

The protocols for dependable transactions are shown in Fig. 10. These protocols are designed to work with any application that requires execution of multiple stages in some order. The protocols can facilitate group-oriented mobile services such as mobile auctions by collecting inputs from multiple users, who are now able to access one or more wireless networks, and processing all inputs for a stage. The protocols also ensure that all users (members) are also sent acknowledgements for their inputs. As each stage is processed and executed, mobile auctions can move to the next stage and so on based on the pre-speci<sup>fi</sup>ed auction rules.

Different users can be prioritized where prioritized access to a network is based on the number of networks that can be currently accessed. For example, if user A can access only 1 network and user B can access three networks then user A has higher priority for accessing the network. The composite priority of a user also depends on its role in a multi-step transaction. Many of the above issues need to be addressed in greater details to allow development and implementation of User-Network matrix to support dependable transactions.

In addition to multi-network access, the dependability of transactions can also be enhanced by adding fault-tolerance. This can be introduced in individual wireless networks and for source or group coordinator of transactions and critical members. In distributed implementation, the loss (failure) of group coordinator can be tolerated by designating the transactions functions to other users. In centralized implementation, one or more back-up sources can be employed. To support a group transactions, protocols must be designed that can process multiple stages of a transaction, co-ordinate user inputs for individual stage and can come up with a consensus in each stage.

The improvement in performance by above enhancements should be studied in future research.

## 3.4. Using agents and protocols

In this section, we address how to support mobile commerce transactions in a limited and variable bandwidth environment where both user mobility and location could create intermittent connectivity and/or brief dis-connectivity combined with connectivity of varying bandwidth. Such on–off phases of connectivity could exist in remote areas with poor wireless coverage, urban areas with obstacles (tunnel, overarching buildings), and indoor coverage by satellites. These will create dif<sup>fi</sup>culty for users who want to remain part of a group transaction while being mobile. One way to support transactions involving users experiencing such problems is to use software programs, agents, in helping to provide the necessary inputs. The key idea is to use a network-based agent that either manages a detailed user pro<sup>fi</sup>le (preferences, <sup>fi</sup>nancial and history of transactions), accesses the user pro<sup>fi</sup>le from one or more databases, or a combination of these two. We propose an end-to-end network architecture (Fig. 11) that would reduce the amount of information user has to enter and wireless network has to carry. The amount of information that still has to transmitted would depend on the type of transactions, such as real time or non real-time. The reduced amount of information over a network would also alleviate the impact of intermittent connectivity as users could still send limited amount of information to an agent who executes the transactions on behalf of a user (with all the user information).

In this architecture, a user sends information to his/her agent, which in turn sends a more detailed input to a transaction server. The agent has access to detailed user pro<sup>fi</sup>le and updates it as and when necessary. This architecture supports m-commerce transactions even when a user has access to limited wireless bandwidth or is going through phases of intermittent connectivity. As stated earlier, the architecture reduces the amount of information that has to be transmitted by users and network. It is possible that the delay requirements (such as in a group m-commerce transaction requiring highly interactive response from a user) for some transactions could require signi<sup>fi</sup>cant transaction-level QoS and faster processing. In some cases, transaction server may also request inputs directly to agents, if no inputs for a given user have been submitted in certain time.

The agent can be assigned a level of “trust”, thus matching this with the “criticality” or level of importance of a transaction. So an individual user could assign a level of “trust” to an agent based on his/ her level of comfort, agent's capabilities, details and complexity of stored user pro<sup>fi</sup>le among other factors. If the degree of criticality of a certain input to transaction is higher than the level of trust, the transaction server will have to wait to acquire input from the user as the agent is not designated that trust worthy. The series of decision making steps are shown in Fig. 12, where different scenarios are included to create more comprehensive support for transactions in connectivity-limited environment. Major steps include determining if a certain user is needed to give inputs in a transaction stage, user's current access to bandwidth and network resources, and criticality of user inputs at the current stage of transaction.

The proposed architecture can provide network designers a tool that would allow a desired level of tradeoff between the amount of information that travels over wireless networks and the amount of storage and processing required at the agent side. The proposed architecture and related protocols could lead to optimal solutions for multiple different environments. For example, in wireless bandwidth limited case, the optimal solution could involve minimum amount of information over wireless networks with a reasonable processing and storage overhead on the agent side. In case, wireless bandwidth and user access issues are not major restrictions, then the optimal solution could involve a balance of bandwidth and processing requirements. To compare multiple solutions, we could de<sup>fi</sup>ne a new metric “overall transaction support cost”, as a sum of unequal weighted wireless bandwidth and processing overhead. The future work could include detailed design of network architecture for m-commerce transaction support, design of protocols to support dependable operation of mcommerce transactions, design methodology for achieving an optimal solution under bandwidth or processing limited environment, and performance evaluation of the proposed architecture and related protocols. More work can also be done in resource allocation schemes for wireless networks, protocols for processing, ef<sup>fi</sup>cient storage, and updating of user pro<sup>fi</sup>les, and the evaluation of reduction in the endto-end overhead (or resource requirements). A trade-off involving bandwidth vs. processing and storage can also be done. Transaction blocking due to agent failure and/or network overload can also be derived along with a scalability of the proposed architecture. It should be noted that the architecture offers a degree of fault-tolerance where network failure may still be overcome, if the input from agent can be received by the transaction server.

## 4. Conclusions and future research

Before group-oriented m-commerce services can be implemented and widely deployed, many challenges, including transaction support, must be addressed. The support for transactions is inherently complex due to the need to co-ordinate group membership, manage user inputs, and implement application synchronization. The support for transaction may become even more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult challenge due to brief dis-connectivity or intermittent connectivity in the underlying infrastructure and ad-hoc wireless networks, user mobility, and the varying performance of wireless links. There are several challenges in the group and transactions-oriented mobile commerce services such as how to provide group management for mobile users who may even experience connectivity problems, how to support transaction performance such as reliability, how to charge for transactions and other business model and strategies issues for wireless service providers, and, user issues including adoption, training and trust issues. Our framework focused on the requirements and technical challenges. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we presented several grouporiented mobile services, characterized transaction requirements of group-oriented m-commerce services, presented the design of two protocols for membership management to support both distributed and centralized processing, and presented a multi-network access system for dependable transactions. The protocols are designed to perform a set of decisions based on transactions' requirements and connectivity and resources of wireless networks.

The future work includes optimal combination of response time and transaction completion probability for diverse m-commerce services, combined effect of connectivity problems and failures on protocols' performance, cross-layer optimization of protocols, and transaction performance among users in multiple heterogeneous wireless networks with diverse connectivity patterns. Certainly much more work is also needed towards addressing social and management issues in group and transactions-oriented mobile commerce services, especially the ones dealing with user trust and business models of wireless service providers.
