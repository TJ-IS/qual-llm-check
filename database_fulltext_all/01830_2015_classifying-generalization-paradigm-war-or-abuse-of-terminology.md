## Abstract

Lee and Baskerville (2003) attempted to clarify the concept of generalization and classify it into four types. In Tsang and Williams (2012) we objected to their account of generalization as well as their classi<sup>fi</sup>cation and offered repairs. Then we proposed a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of induction, within which we distinguished <sup>fi</sup>ve types of generalization. In their (2012) rejoinder, they argue that their classi<sup>fi</sup>cation is compatible with ours, claiming that theirs offers a ‘new language.’ Insofar as we resist this ‘new language’ and insofar as they think that our position commits us to positivism and the rejection of interpretivism, they conclude both that our classi<sup>fi</sup>cation is more restrictive than theirs and also that we embrace ‘paradigmatic domination.’ Lee and Baskerville’s classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of generalization is based on a distinction between theoretical and empirical statements. Accordingly we will <sup>fi</sup>rst clarify the terms ‘theoretical statement’ and ‘empirical statement.’ We note that they <sup>fi</sup>nd no fault with our classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of induction, we re-state our main objections to their classi<sup>fi</sup>cation that remain unanswered and we show that their classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of generalizing is in fact incompatible with ours. We argue that their account of generalization retains fatal <sup>fl</sup>aws, which means it should not be relied upon. We demonstrate that our classi<sup>fi</sup>cation is not committed to any paradigm and so we do not embrace ‘paradigmatic domination.

## Introduction

nduction, namely the use of inferences from observed matters of fact to unobserved matters of fact (Cambridge Dictionary of Philosophy 1999: 745), is essential to science. One form of induction, among others, is what is known in logic as ‘inductive generalization’ or in the literature on information systems as ‘statistical generalization.’ This occurs in studies in which a researcher observes a characteristic of a sample of a population and then infers that the population itself has that characteristic, within a margin of error. Since such generalizations offer new knowledge by drawing conclusions that are broader than the speci<sup>fi</sup>c circumstances of the study, it is important for them to be justi<sup>fi</sup>ed in a way that scientists, including those in information systems, will accept. Lee and Baskerville (2003) provide the major treatment of generalization in the information systems literature. They warn us that uncritical application of statistical generalization as the norm for all forms of generalizations may result in inappropriate evaluations of many studies. They also invoke Hume’s problem of induction, namely his argument that induction is unjusti<sup>fi</sup>able. Lee and Baskerville are committed to endorsing this and so must deny that an increase in sample size increases the probability that the population has the characteristic found in the sample. This is because any statistical generalization, however large the sample, is still as much an inductive inference as the case in which the sample consists of just one member of the population, and so is equally unjusti<sup>fi</sup>ed. Although they are committed to the insolubility of the problem, they think that it imposes no prohibition of the conclusion that an increase in sample size leads to an increase in the generalizability of one sample to other samples that the same sampling procedure would produce (We will call this ‘Hume’s prohibition’). They next attempt to clarify the concept of generalization and to classify it into four types. Their classi<sup>fi</sup>cation is based on a distinction between empirical and theoretical statements, these types being empirical statement to empirical statement (EE), empirical statement to theoretical statement (ET), theoretical statement to empirical statement (TE) and theoretical statement to theoretical statement (TT).

In Tsang and Williams (2012) we argued that they have not escaped Hume’s prohibition, and that the insolubility of Hume’s problem spells the end of science, something that Lee and Baskerville seem to have not appreciated. We gave a balance-of-evidence argument that Hume’s problem of induction has a solution, even if we do not know what it is. We objected to their account of generalization, arguing that their de<sup>fi</sup>nition of it is not the sense used by researchers in natural or social sciences, and is contradicted by their own characterization of it. We showed that their de<sup>fi</sup>nition of ‘empirical statement’ is circular and that their de<sup>fi</sup>nition of ‘theoretical statement’ is too narrow. We argued that EE generalization in fact refers to deduction and validating measurement instruments; ET generalization misuses the term ‘theory’; their de<sup>fi</sup>nition of TE generalization contradicts their own de<sup>fi</sup>nition of generalization and TE generalization refers to empiri cal testing; their discussion of TT generalization is not coherent, is internally inconsistent and is irrelevant to generalization as most usually understood. We offered repairs to all of the above problems.

Then we proposed a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of induction, within which we distinguished <sup>fi</sup>ve types of generalization. We proposed that for any two statements P and Q, Q is more general than P if and only if the class of entities that P is about is a subset of the class of entities that Q is about. Then we may say that for any two statements P and Q, Q is a generalization of P if and only if (1) Q is inferred from P, and (2) Q is more general than P. In line with this, we classi<sup>fi</sup>ed <sup>fi</sup>ve types of generalizations. Theoretical generalization consists of generalizing from research <sup>fi</sup>ndings to theories. Within-population generalization, which is equivalent to statistical generalization, consists of generalizing from research <sup>fi</sup>ndings about the sample to those same characteristics in the corresponding population. Cross-population generalization consists in generalizing from a sample in one population to members of another population, with both populations existing in a similar context and a similar period of time. In contextual generalization, researchers generalize from a sample in one population to members of another population, with both populations existing in signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different contexts but within a similar period of time. Finally, temporal generalization refers to generalizing from a sample in one population at one point in time to members of the same or a different population at another point in time, with the context remaining more or less the same.

In their (2012) rejoinder Lee and Baskerville argue that their classi<sup>fi</sup>cation is compatible with ours because theirs supposedly subsumes ours, claiming that theirs offers a ‘new language.’ Insofar as we resist this ‘new language’ and insofar as they think that our position commits us to positivism and the rejection of interpretivism, they conclude that we are engaged in ‘paradigmatic domination.’ Nor do they mention our argument for Hume’s prohibition. Instead they give mathematical reasons against the prohibition. They object to our balance-of-evidence argument that Hume’s problem has a solution. Claiming that they are in the ‘good company’ of Kuhn (1962), they sketch a solution to it that they think is consistent with Hume’s own position.

A careful reader of the debate so far would notice that since Lee and Baskerville offer no rebuttal to nearly all of our objections to their account of generalization and its classi<sup>fi</sup>cation and offer no objection to our own classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of induction (within which we distinguished <sup>fi</sup>ve types of generalization) it would be reasonable to conclude that their account remains problematic while ours does not. Nonetheless a less careful reader might be tempted to conclude that all remains well with Lee and Baskerville’s (2003) account of generalization and its classi<sup>fi</sup>cation. We will show that this is not so and that one is wiser to accept our (2012) account.

We will <sup>fi</sup>rst clarify the terms ‘theoretical statement’ and ‘empirical statement.’ We will use this clari<sup>fi</sup>cation to show the following. Their classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of generalizing is incompatible with ours. Unlike our account of generalization, theirs remains <sup>fl</sup>awed and should not be relied upon. Our classi<sup>fi</sup>cation is not committed to any paradigm and so we do not embrace ‘paradigmatic domination.’ In Appendix A we argue that their mathematical reasons against Hume’s prohibition fail to de<sup>fl</sup>ect it. Their objection to our balance-of-evidence argument is fallacious, as is their solution to Hume’s problem of induction. In Appendix B, we contrast Lee and Baskerville’s use of basic logical terms with standard usage.

## A clari<sup>fi</sup>cation of the terms ‘theoretical statement’ and ‘empirical statement’

Lee and Baskerville (2003) classify generalization into four types, namely EE, ET, TE and TT. This classi<sup>fi</sup>cation is based on a distinction between theoretical and empirical statements. We will now clarify this distinction. This will enable us to see that problems remain with their classi<sup>fi</sup>cation.

Those who use the term ‘empirical’ usually recognize that it means ‘based on what is experienced.’ This is corroborated by the Cambridge International Dictionary of English. Since dictionaries are a helpful reminder of the standard meanings of words in common usage, and since part of our objection to Lee and Baskerville relates to the extent to which their appropriation of standard terms is liable to mislead the unwary, we will sometimes appeal to their authority. It follows that a statement is empirical if and only if it is capable of being evaluated as true or false by direct or indirect observation. This coheres with ‘empirical statements’ de<sup>fi</sup>ned by the Merriam-Webster Dictionary as those ‘capable of being con-<sup>fi</sup>rmed, veri<sup>fi</sup>ed, or disproved by observation or experiment,’ since both observation and experiment involve sense-experience. Phenomena are directly observed if and only if they are observed by the senses. They are indirectly observed if and only if observation of other phenomena is made from which their nature may be derived. Thus ‘This piece of metal expanded when heated’ is an empirical statement because it may be evaluated as true or false by direct observation. For example, an experiment in which the metal is observed to expand while being heated con<sup>fi</sup>rms it, while one in which the metal is observed to not expand while being heated falsi<sup>fi</sup>es it. In contrast one may indirectly observe the path of an electron by observing trails of condensation of supersaturated water vapor along its path in a cloud chamber, so a statement reporting its path is also empirical. On the other hand ‘5 is larger than 3’ is a non-empirical statement, because observa tion is irrelevant to deciding whether it is true, as opposed to re<sup>fl</sup>ection upon the relationship between the concept of the number 5 and that of the number 3.

Merriam-Webster de<sup>fi</sup>nes ‘theory’ as ‘the general or abstract principles of a body of fact.’ Relatedly, the Oxford English Dictionary (OED) de<sup>fi</sup>nes it as ‘a supposition or a system of ideas intended to explain something, especially one based on general principles independent of the thing to be explained’ while the Macmillan Dictionary has ‘the set of general principles that a particular subject is based on.’ Based on this, one may say sensibly that a theory is a general statement or conjunction of general statements that purports to predict and explain the phenomena to which it refers. The proper contrast with ‘general’ is ‘speci<sup>fi</sup>c’ rather than ‘empirical.’ Accordingly Merriam-Webster de<sup>fi</sup>nes ‘general’ as ‘involving, relating to, or applicable to every member of a class, kind, or group,’ while de<sup>fi</sup>ning ‘speci<sup>fi</sup>c’ as ‘restricted to a particular individual, situation, relation, or effect.’ Thus we propose that a general statement is one that attributes a property to all members of a class of entities and a speci<sup>fi</sup>c statement is one that attributes a property only to some members of a class of entities. It follows that what is general is not speci<sup>fi</sup>c and conversely. Thus ‘All dogs bark’ is a general statement and ‘Fido barks’ is speci<sup>fi</sup>c.

As mentioned, a theory is a general statement or conjunction of general statements that purports to explain and predict the phenomena to which it refers. A statement is theoretical if and only if it constitutes a theory or is one of its conjuncts. So ‘All metals expand when heated’ is a theoretical statement because it is general and purports to predict the behavior of metals when heated (they will expand) and to explain the behavior of metals (they expanded because they were heated). So is ‘All even numbers are the sum of two primes’ because it is general and purports to predict that any even number we care to think about will turn out to be the sum of two primes and to explain why it is even (once again because it is the sum of two primes – which are necessarily odd). In contrast ‘All the coins in my pocket are silver’ is a general statement but not a theoretical one, because it does not purport to explain anything to do with the coins. One cannot say on its basis that a coin is silver because it is in my pocket.<sup>1</sup> Another way in which a statement may be non-theoretical is by being non-general. Thus ‘This piece of metal expanded when heated’ is non-theoretical.

One theory may subsume another. For example, ‘All metals expand when heated’ is subsumed by ‘Heating any metal raises the kinetic energy of its molecules and any metal that undergoes such increase expands’ from which it is deducible. The latter is more general because it has greater predictive and explanatory power than the former (Tsang and Williams, 2012). Like the former, it explains why metals expand (because they were heated) and unlike the former also explains why metals expand when heated.

There are theories about what is empirical and theories about what is non-empirical. In other words there are general statements that purport to predict and explain phenomena that are based on sense-experience and general statements that purport to predict and explain phenomena that are not based on sense-experience. ‘All metals expand when heated’ is a theory about what is empirical. ‘All even numbers are the sum of two primes’ is a theory about what is not empirical.

Theoretical statements about the empirical are themselves empirical since they are capable of being evaluated as true or false by direct or indirect observation. Even general statements like ‘All metals expand when heated’ are capable of being corroborated as true, although not proven, by repeated observations of heated metals expanding. Theoretical statements about the non-empirical are themselves non-empirical since they are not thus capable. Accordingly ‘All metals expand when heated’ is a theoretical and empirical statement, ‘All even numbers are the sum of two primes’ is theoretical and non-empirical, ‘This piece of metal expanded when heated’ is non-theoretical and empirical, and ‘5 is greater than 3’ is nontheoretical and non-empirical.

One moral of all this is that it is a mistake to think that just because a statement is empirical it is non-theoretical or that just because it is non-empirical it is theoretical. This undermines Lee and Baskerville’s basis for their classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of generalizations, because it mistakenly assumes that the class of empirical statements and the class of theoretical statements are mutually exclusive, as we elaborate in the next section.

## Why Lee and Baskerville’s account of induction and of generalization is incompatible with ours

We made many objections to Lee and Baskerville’s account of generalization and its classi<sup>fi</sup>cation in our (2012) commentary. We will now brie<sup>fl</sup>y rehearse some of these.

## De<sup>fi</sup>nitions of key terms

We objected that their de<sup>fi</sup>nition of induction is too narrow. They de<sup>fi</sup>ne it as ‘a reasoning process that begins with statements of particulars and ends in a general statement (2003: 224). This de<sup>fi</sup>nition is too narrow, a point well made by logicians, because there are at least two forms of inductive inference that do not proceed from particular premises to a general conclusion, both of which may be usefully employed in scienti<sup>fi</sup>c contexts. One of these is what logicians call statistical syllogism (Gensler, 2001)<sup>2</sup> and another is what logicians call inductive analogy (Copi and Cohen, 1998; Hurley, 2003).<sup>3</sup> Both of these forms of inference are inductive because they go from observed matters of fact to unobserved matters of fact. But in both cases the inference leads to a particular conclusion and so are not generalizations.

We also showed that that their characterization of generalization contradicts their own de<sup>fi</sup>nition of it (2012: 734). They cite the OED (1989) when de<sup>fi</sup>ning generalize as ‘to form general notions by abstraction from particular instances’ (2003: 221). Yet they asserted that ‘the generalizability of an IS theory to different settings is important’ (2003: 221). Their idea of generalizing from theory (i.e., general notions) to settings (i.e., particular instances) simply contradicts their own adopted de<sup>fi</sup>nition of generalization, which refers to forming general notions by abstraction from particular instances.

We showed that their distinction between empirical and theoretical statements faces two objections. They said that

Empirical statements can refer to data, measurements, observations, or descriptions about empirical or real-world phenomena, while theoretical statements posit the existence of entities and relationships that cannot be directly observed, and hence can only be theorized.

(2003: 232)

But this cannot serve as a de<sup>fi</sup>nition because it is circular. To understand what makes a statement empirical we must <sup>fi</sup>rst understand what makes real-world phenomena empirical (2012: 735). Second, theoretical statements need not posit the existence of entities and relationships that cannot be directly observed. They may or may not posit them. Newton’s laws of motion certainly constitute a theory, but they are about observable relations between perfectly ordinary, observable objects. (2012: 735). In other words, as we explained above, the class of theoretical statements and the class of empirical statements are not mutually exclusive, but Lee and Baskerville assume that they are.

Even based on their own distinction between empirical and theoretical statements, they misuse the terms. For instance, in their reply to our commentary they discuss Gefen and Straub’s (1997) study and state that Figure 2 of their article has ‘a total of seven theoretical statements’ (footnote 3). One of the socalled theoretical statements is ‘H4: Women’s use of e-mail will be greater than that of men’ (Gefen and Straub, 1997: 395). But Gefen and Straub conceived of the greater use of email in terms of ‘the number of sent and received messages’ (1997: 395). One may directly observe how many sent and received messages there are, just as one may directly observe how many apples are on a table. So according to Lee and Baskerville’s own de<sup>fi</sup>nition, the statement is not theoretical (and should be empirical), contrary to what they say.

These are faults with Lee and Baskerville’s basis for their classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of generalizations. Since our classi<sup>fi</sup>cation does not suffer from this basic fault, theirs is incompatible with ours and ours is not subsumed by theirs.

## Their classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of generalization

We next showed that Lee and Baskerville mischaracterize each of the so-called four types of generalization, even within their own terms. In particular, only what they call ‘ET generalizability’ is generalization in any commonly accepted sense (2012: 735).

In their discussion of EE generalizability, they use the example of calculating the sample mean from sample points (2003: 233–234). But such a calculation is deduced and not induced from sample points. Moreover they state that ‘where the measurement instrument has not been validated, the data collected from a research subject would lack generalizability to any valid measurement for that individual (2003: 234). But this does not involve any sense of ‘generalize’ that characterizes inference. (2012: 735). So this is not generalization.

In their discussion of TE generalizability, their de<sup>fi</sup>nition of it contradicts their own de<sup>fi</sup>nition of generalization (2012: 736). They tell us that ‘TE generalizability … involves generalizing from theoretical statements … to empirical statements (here, descriptions of what the practitioner can expect to observe in his speci<sup>fi</sup>c organization if he were to apply the theory)’ (2003: 237). But although empirical statements may be general, theoretical statements are necessarily general. Therefore Lee and Baskerville’s own de<sup>fi</sup>nition of general ization, which has it that generalization consists in ‘general izing from particular instances to general notions’ (2003: 232, our italics), rules out TE inferences as cases of generalization. They go on to say that ‘Type TE generalizability happens to be closely related to empirical testing’ (2003: 237). But Type TE generalizability just is empirical testing. It is related to generalization in the sense that one deduces an empirical prediction from a theory in an attempt to falsify it. So this is not generalization either.

TT generalizability is supposed to take place when ‘a researcher generalizes from theoretical propositions in the form of concepts … to the theoretical propositions that make up a theory’ (2003: 238). But this characterization of TT generalizability is incoherent because concepts are not propositions. Second, their own de<sup>fi</sup>nition of generalization rules out generalizing from concepts to theories, because concepts are not particular instances. An electron is an instance of the concept of an electron, but it is not a concept, only an electron. Thus Lee and Baskerville’s account is internally inconsistent. Third, much of their discussion of TT generalizability is about constructing a theory from concepts extracted from the literature, but this is not even remotely related to inference, let alone generalization. Once again, this is not generalization.

In their (2012) rejoinder they fail to address any of these objections to their account of generalization. Our own account of generalization is not vulnerable to these. So the balance of evidence is that their account remains problematic in ways in which ours is not. Moreover we explicitly argued that only ET generalization is really generalization. So our classi<sup>fi</sup>cation excludes EE, TT and TE generalizations, while theirs includes these.<sup>4</sup> It follows that theirs is incompatible with ours and does not subsume ours.

Instead of responding to our objections, their rejoinder perpetuates some of the errors to which we originally objected. As they admit in their reply, ‘theoretical statements are, by de<sup>fi</sup>nition, general’ (p. 752). On the other hand, empirical statements may refer to particular phenomena, such as ‘Amazon is the largest online book seller in the world.’ So by ‘generalizability’ they denote inference from something that may be particular to something that is necessarily general (ET generalizability) and at the same time inference from something that is necessarily general to something that may be particular (TE generalizability). Surely the same term ‘generalizability’ should not denote inferences that go in opposite directions, as we elaborate in the next section.

Our commentary explains the difference between deduction and induction using the commonly understood terminology of logic (p. 731) and points out that contrary to Lee and Baskerville’s (2003) claim, ‘a sample estimate is deduced and not induced from sample points’ (p. 735). Their rejoinder perpetuates this misconception by talking of ‘Generalizing from sample points to a sample statistic’ when illustrating EE generalization (p. 756).

## Differences between our classi<sup>fi</sup>cation and theirs

Finally, there are two further reasons why our classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of generalization is incompatible with theirs. First, our withinpopulation generalization is not, contrary to their claim, ‘a special case of L&B’s EE generalizing’ (Lee and Baskerville, 2012: 758)<sup>5</sup> because, as stated above, the latter includes generalizing from sample points to a sample statistic and thus con<sup>fl</sup>ates induction with deduction. Deductive inferences do not go from what is less general to what is more general and so cannot be properly called ‘generalization.

Second, contrary to their claim, our cross-population generalization, contextual generalization and temporal generalization are not compatible with TE generalization (Lee and

Baskerville, 2012: 758), because unlike TE generalization they are not inferences from theoretical statements. We de<sup>fi</sup>ned cross-population generalization as ‘generalizing from a sample in one population to members of another population, with both populations existing in a similar context and a similar period of time’ (Tsang and Williams, 2012: 741). To illustrate this with Gefen and Straub’s (1997) study, it ‘might consist in generalizing the results of the American airline (in which Gefen and Straub collected data) to another American airline that existed at the time of data collection’ (Tsang and Williams, 2012: 741). Inferring, say, ‘About 80% of all employees of Southwest Airlines are frequent users of e-mails’ from ‘Of a sample of 10% of employees of Delta Airlines, 80% were found to be frequent users of e-mails does not generalize from a theoretical statement because the latter does not have the generality or explanatory power required by one. Moreover, to adopt Lee and Baskerville’s terminology, the inference generalizes from one empirical statement to another empirical statement, and so is EE generalization. The same points hold mutatis mutandis for our other two types of generalization (i.e., contextual general ization and temporal generalization).<sup>6</sup>

Lee and Baskerville (2012: 758) give the following argument that our classi<sup>fi</sup>cation allows for TT generalization:

Gefen and Straub, whose work T&W use as an illustration, performed TT generalizing in their derivation of new theoretical statements (their extension of TAM) from other theoretical statements (the prior literature on TAM and other research literature, such as literature on gender and culture). To the extent that T&W use Gefen and Straub’s theory, T&W’s generalization framework allows for TT generalizing.

In other words, Gefen and Straub performed TT generalizing in their study and we use parts of their study as an illustration of how our classi<sup>fi</sup>cation might be used, so our classi<sup>fi</sup>cation allows for TT generalizing. This does not follow. Their argument has the form

A did X in study S.

B used parts of S as an illustration of how Y might be done. So doing Y allows for doing X.

This form of argument is deductively invalid as shown by the following counterexample: Early scientists who did studies on mushrooms classi<sup>fi</sup>ed them as plants because they only recognized two kingdoms, plants and animals. Later scientists who recognize the <sup>fi</sup>ve kingdoms (i.e., bacteria, protozoans, animals, fungi and plants) used parts of these early studies as an illustration of how mushrooms might be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as fungi. But classifying mushrooms as fungi does not allow for classifying them as plants, because fungi are not plants (Margulis and Schwartz, 1998).

## Why Lee and Baskerville’s use of the term ‘generalize’ is seriously <sup>fl</sup>awed

We have argued that only what Lee and Baskerville call ‘ET generalizability’ is generalization in any commonly accepted sense. We will now build on this point by showing that they use the term ‘generalization’ in a way that is at odds with its commonly understood meaning within logic, science or commonsense and in a way that offers no gain. This is for three reasons. First, their usage is internally inconsistent and thus a backward step. Second, they use the term ‘generalize’ in various unusual ways none of which are related to its commonly understood meaning and which are liable to sow confusion. Third, they use the term in a way that does not denote a unidirectional inference and hence in a way that impoverishes its semantic content.

First, we have shown that their de<sup>fi</sup>nition of ‘generalization contradicts both their own characterization of it and their de<sup>fi</sup>nition of TE generalization and that their discussion of TT generalization is both incoherent and internally inconsistent. Adopting this way of conceiving of generalization is a backward step because it is internally inconsistent.

Second, they use the term ‘generalize’ in various unusual ways none of which are related to its commonly understood meaning and which are liable to sow confusion. This may be illustrated by the following account of a <sup>fi</sup>ctitious IS researcher, Dr. Smith.

After receiving a handsome research grant, Dr. Smith started his research on user perception of the usefulness of enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems. Having conducted a thorough literature review, he generalized from concepts discussed in the literature to a theory of perceived usefulness of ERP systems. Then he successfully negotiated access to a large company, which had recently implemented an ERP system, and conducted a questionnaire survey of a random sample of 100 managers drawn from the population of managers in the company. With full support from top management, the response rate was 100%. In addition to other variables, he obtained a score of perceived usefulness from each respondent. He generalized from these 100 scores to a mean score of the sample, from which he further generalized to the population mean score. Since all of his propositions, which had been generalized from related concepts in the literature, were supported by his data, Dr. Smith concluded that his theory could be generalized to this company. Unfortunately, he later realized that he had forgotten to validate his questionnaire before carrying out the survey. In other words, ‘the data collected from a research subject would lack generalizability to any valid measurement for that individual’ (Lee and Baskerville, 2003: 234, our italics). As a remedy, Dr. Smith conducted an ethnographic study in the company, interviewing and observing managers who used the ERP system. He tried to assess whether the answers given by his respondents and his observations of their behaviors could be ‘generalized into a valid, empirical statement’ (Lee and Baskerville, 2003: 235, our italics). His decision was that if the respondents’ replies were believable, the data he collected ‘would be generalizable to a valid descriptive statement’ (Lee and Baskerville, 2003: 235, our italics). Otherwise, the data would lack such generalizability.

As we argued (2012), with the exception of its underlined occurrence, the term ‘generalize’ is not used in a way that is related to its commonly understood meaning. Moreover the reader of such a passage would be bewildered. She would wonder how the term can mean all of these different things at once. If she is an IS researcher who adopts this terminology she would <sup>fi</sup>nd it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to communicate not only with other IS researchers but also with those outside the discipline.

We observed (2012: 733) that Lee and Baskerville’s (2003: 221) de<sup>fi</sup>nition of generalization as ‘to form general notions by abstraction from particular instances,’ which they extract from the OED, is not the sense used by researchers in natural or social sciences. They now claim that ‘Contrary to T&W’s assertion, L&B did not adopt, but reported the OED’s (2003) de<sup>fi</sup>nition of generalization’ (2012: A6). But what they said was:

Generalizing, according to the de<sup>fi</sup>nition we cited at the beginning of this essay, refers to generalizing from particular instances to general notions. … By joining the two building blocks, we recognize that generalizing can occur in four ways: From empirical statements to other empirical statements, from empirical statements to theoretical statements, from theoretical statements to empirical statements, and from theoretical statements to other theoretical statements. Given the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of generalize (‘to form general notions by abstraction from particular instances’), the four different ways of generalizing indicate that the outputs of general izing (the ‘general notions’) can be either theoretical statements or empirical statements, and the inputs to generalizing (the ‘particular instances’) can also be either theoretical statements or empirical statements.

(2003: 232)

‘The de<sup>fi</sup>nition we cited at the beginning of this essay’ is the OED de<sup>fi</sup>nition cited on the <sup>fi</sup>rst page of Lee and Baskerville (2003). This clearly shows not only that they both reported and adopted the OED de<sup>fi</sup>nition but also that it is the basis of their classi<sup>fi</sup>cation.

Further evidence of this is their own claim that ‘Induction refers to a process of reasoning and can be a synonym for generalizing. It refers to a reasoning process that begins with statements of particulars and ends in a general statement (2003: 224). Since they think that induction and generalization are the same thing (in fact they are not, since statistical syllogism and inductive analogy are forms of induction but do not involve generalization), they must say that generalization is a reasoning process that begins with statements of particulars and ends in a general statement, which is very similar to the OED de<sup>fi</sup>nition of generalizing.

Even if we accept their claim that they did not adopt the OED de<sup>fi</sup>nition, that leaves them with another problem, namely, that they have de<sup>fi</sup>ned the four types of generalization without de<sup>fi</sup>ning what they are types of, namely, generalization.

A third and more central problem for Lee and Baskerville is that however else researchers and laymen have used the term ‘generalize,’ it denotes something unidirectional. In generalizing we necessarily go from what is less general to what is more general and not vice versa. Otherwise we would use a different term such as ‘particularize.’ Therefore what they mean by ‘generalize’ is not what we ordinarily mean by it. Those sympathetic to the later Wittgenstein (2001) should endorse this conclusion. The Wittgenstein of the Philosophical Investigations held that the meaning of a word is its ordinary use within a linguistic community, but we know of no logician or philosopher who uses ‘generalize’ as ‘particularize’ or ‘deduce.’ Nor can we think of anyone else except Lee and Baskerville who ordinarily uses the term this way.

To elaborate this point, let us consider an analogy. Suppose that someone departs from standard usage in using the term ‘rose’ to mean exactly what ‘changed’ means. She tells us that the Dow Jones Industrial Average Index rose from 13,300 to 13,400 yesterday and also that it rose from 13,400 to 13,150 today. First, from our perspective she has contradicted herself. Second, what she means by ‘rose’ says less than what we mean by it, since she means ‘changed’ while we mean ‘changed by increasing.’ It follows from these two points that it means something quite different from what English speakers ordinarily mean by ‘rose.’

## Why we are innocent of semantic ‘paradigmatic domination

Lee and Baskerville (2012) respond to our point that they misuse the term ‘generalize’ by claiming that they ‘originally generalized generalizability by offering new language’ (p. 749). Insofar as we reject this ‘broad language’ (p. 759) they conclude that we embrace ‘paradigmatic domination’ (p. 759).

They did indeed offer new language. But so did Humpty Dumpty in a famous passage from that venerable logician, Lewis Carroll (1872: 72):

‘I don’t know what you mean by “glory,” ’ Alice said. Humpty Dumpty smiled contemptuously. ‘Of course you don’t – till I tell you. I meant “there’s a nice knock-down argument for you!” ’ ‘But “glory” doesn't mean “a nice knock-down argument,” ’ Alice objected. ‘When I use a word,’ Humpty Dumpty said, in rather a scornful tone, ‘it means just what I choose it to mean – neither more nor less. ‘The question is,’ said Alice, ‘whether you can make words mean so many different things.’ ‘The question is,’ said Humpty Dumpty, ‘which is to be master – that’s all.

This is not to say that one may not usefully single out a more central meaning in an equivocal term or stipulate new terms. One may stipulate an entirely new term such as ‘brunch. Or one may stipulate that a univocal term already in common use such as ‘fork’ has a new meaning such as ‘implement used to pick one’s nose.’ But then one has to be clear that this is a stipulation and specify that the term that has the new meaning is denoted in a way that disambiguates it from the old one, for example by calling it ‘fork ’ as opposed to ‘fork .’ Or one may take a term that has multiple senses (because it has multiple uses in different contexts) such as ‘gay’ and then single out the salient or most central sense given the relevant context or conversation. This is what we did for ‘generalize’ (2012: 734). Or one may take a vague term (namely one for which there are borderline cases where there is no principled way of deciding whether these fall within the extension of the term) such as ‘bright light’ as in ‘A lighthouse is a structure that displays a bright light as a navigational aid to shipping’ and stipulate what is known in logic as a ‘precising de<sup>fi</sup>nition’ in order to make the vague term more precise, such as ‘A bright light is a light that is visible to an observer with normal eyesight on a clear night from a distance of 20 nautical miles’ (see Farber et al., 2011, Chapter 11).

Humpty Dumpty does none of these things, and nor do Lee and Baskerville. They single out a term that is already in common usage in the English language and then give it a meaning that is incompatible with that usage. It follows that what they mean by the term is not what the rest of the world means by it. If they had said this then there would be less risk that discussants will talk past each other or contradict themselves. They do not. They seem to think that they are elucidating the meaning of a term as it is commonly used. They are not. If they are stipulating a new meaning for the term ‘generalize,’ one that already has wide currency, then it behoves them to explain the gain to the community of researchers and speci<sup>fi</sup>cally the community of IS researchers of championing a change that reduces the semantic content of the term. Lacking such an explanation it is reasonable to conclude that their usage is a misuse compared to most normal understandings of the term. On the other hand if they wish to argue that a whole new research language is needed then they would be better off developing one rather than using terms that already have central and speci<sup>fi</sup>c meanings for scientists.

In saying this are we advocating a ‘paradigm’? That of course depends upon what a paradigm is supposed to amount to. In the Kuhnean sense of the word it is hard to say, since Kuhn (1962) himself failed to clearly de<sup>fi</sup>ne the term (Masterman, 1970). In its ordinary and less grandiose sense it just means ‘model or exemplar.’ This coheres with Merriam-Webster, which gives ‘typical example’ and Collins, which gives ‘pattern or model.’ In this sense some paradigms are good and others are bad. Thanking one’s host is a good paradigm of polite behavior while spitting in her face is not. Likewise insisting that a term not be misused is a good paradigm of clarity while insisting that ‘generalize’ may mean ‘particularize’ or ‘deduce’ is not.

## Why we are innocent of epistemological ‘paradigmatic domination

The second way in which we supposedly commit ‘paradigmatic domination’ is that ‘Compared to T&W’s ideas on generalizability, the ideas presented earlier by L&B and now by us in this essay are more general, as it were, and are compatible with qualitative research, interpretive research, and design research, not just statistical and positivist research (Lee and Baskerville, 2012: 759) and that their four types of generalization ‘are not restricted to positivist, statistical, and quantitative forms of research’ (Lee and Baskerville, 2012: 758). In other words they give the following argument:

Tsang and Williams’ classi<sup>fi</sup>cation, but not theirs, embraces positivism and excludes interpretivism.

Both epistemologies are ‘paradigms.’

So Tsang and Williams’ classi<sup>fi</sup>cation represents an epistemological ‘paradigmatic domination’ of positivism over interpretivism.

This argument is deductively unsound because its <sup>fi</sup>rst premise is false. Our classi<sup>fi</sup>cation does not embrace positivism, nor does it exclude interpretivism. Lee and Baskerville do not tell us what they think positivism amounts to. A radical positivist is presumably someone who thinks that ‘knowledge is derived only from experimental investigation and observation’ (Collins). This coheres with Merriam-Webster, which de<sup>fi</sup>nes positivism as ‘a theory that positive knowledge is based on natural phenomena and their properties and relations as veri<sup>fi</sup>ed by the empirical sciences.’ But although our <sup>fi</sup>ve types of generalization start from observations of samples of populations and end with conclusions that might be the objects of knowledge, it does not follow that they are the only ways in which knowledge may be attained, nor that we are committed to saying so.

Nor do Lee and Baskerville tell us what they think interpretivism amounts to. Presumably one of the central claims of interpretivism is that the social realm may not be subject to the same research methods as the natural world. Nothing we have said opposes this claim. In fact it is consistent with the less radical positivist claim that social scientists may require different methods of investigation, thus combining interpretivism with moderate positivism, a position somewhat similar to one earlier advocated by Lee (1991) himself. For example one may inductively generalize from the properties of a sample of a population to those of the population itself to conclude that about 90% of Singaporean IT users care more about expense than quality. One might then develop a political, sociological, anthropological or cultural theory to explain this phenomenon by conducting an interpretivist case study on money-conscious Singaporean IT users to understand why they hold such an attitude based on their own descriptions of the rationale. Nothing we have said precludes this kind of integrative research.

An example of interpretivist research is Ignatiadis and Nandhakumar’s (2007) investigation of the impact of ERP systems on organizational resilience through processes of embedding and disembedding. Adopting an interpretivist approach and based on one case company in the UK they conclude, ‘Although the focus of this study was an ERP system, the results can similarly be generalized for other enterprise-wide information systems’ (2007: 42). Instead of excluding the study from our consideration, our classi<sup>fi</sup>cation provides guidance on how these results may be generalized. For example, a key <sup>fi</sup>nding of the study was that the introduction of the ERP system created power differentials, which served to increase control in the case company. This in turn resulted in increased rigidity and a decrease in organizational <sup>fl</sup>exibility and resilience. The perception of power differentials was an important factor as it ‘caused different people to see different ways in which to solve a problem’ (Ignatiadis and Nandhakumar, 2007: 41). As such, while this <sup>fi</sup>nding may be readily generalizable within the population of British companies that have implemented an ERP system, its across-population generalizability to, say, countries that have cultures of much higher power distance than that of the UK is doubtful.

Lee and Baskerville may have been misled into asserting that our classi<sup>fi</sup>cation is positivist because they think that it is quantitatively based. This does not follow. As Phillips (1987: 96) observes, ‘A positivist, qua positivist, is not committed to any particular research design. There is nothing in the doctrines of positivism that necessitates a love of statistics or distaste for case studies.

In any case our classi<sup>fi</sup>cation allows for both qualitative and quantitative research. In fact, as the passage below shows, we explicitly argued against the view that results of qualitative research (such as case studies) are necessarily less generalizable than those of quantitative research (such as surveys):

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of a case study are surely less generalizable to the population on which it is based, than the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of a large-scale random sample survey. But even in this case, the generalizability of survey results depends a great deal on whether a probability sampling method is employed to construct a representative sample … . For the other four types of generalization, there is simply no reason why survey results should be inherently more generalizable than case study results.

(2012: 743–744)

As long as an epistemological perspective recognizes that induction is legitimate, our classi<sup>fi</sup>cation will be applicable. While it may be true that researchers following the interpretivist tradition ‘largely reject generalization as a goal’ (Compeau et al., 2012: 5), the mere fact that we have elucidated the nature of generalization and classi<sup>fi</sup>ed it into types does not entail that we embrace it as a goal, let alone as the only goal. To think otherwise is like arguing that just because criminologists elucidate the nature of crime and classify it into types they are ipso facto committed to embracing crime as a goal!

Finally, the accusation that it is to our discredit that we engage in ‘paradigmatic domination’ is not one that Lee and Baskerville may make consistently. They see themselves in the ‘good company’ of Kuhn in sketching a solution to Hume’s problem of induction, namely that induction is a social process (2012: 755). But as they themselves recognize, Kuhn (1962) regards the social process of overthrowing old paradigms by new ones as scienti<sup>fi</sup>c progress. That certainly counts as paradigmatic domination.

## Concluding remarks

We remain entirely unpersuaded by Lee and Baskerville’s (2012) rejoinder to our previous commentary. We are disappointed that they chose to ignore virtually all of our objections. We have clearly shown that their classi<sup>fi</sup>cation is incompatible with ours and does not subsume ours. Unlike our account of generalization, theirs remains <sup>fl</sup>awed and should not be relied upon. We are also innocent of ‘paradigmatic domination.’ We have also shown that their usage of ‘generalization’ is a misuse compared to most normal understandings of the term. In this respect we are reminded of the old joke of the soldier’s proud mother commenting on her son’s <sup>fi</sup>ne marching style on the parade ground with the remark ‘What a shame that the rest of the regiment is out of step!’. In terms of inviting confusion and miscommunication, the cost of trying to follow Lee and Baskerville’s lead could be high.

In the two appendices we show the following. Their mathematical reasons against Hume’s prohibition fail to de<sup>fl</sup>ect it. Their objection to our balance-of-evidence argument is fallacious, as is their solution to Hume’s problem of induction. They have misused basic logical terms.

## Appendix A

## Misunderstanding Hume’s problem of induction

Lee and Baskerville (2003) were largely concerned with Hume’s problem of induction, which they invoked in order to support their commitment to the claim that statistical generalization is unjusti<sup>fi</sup>able (2003: 226). They were committed to the insolubility of the problem, although they argued that it imposes no prohibition of the conclusion that an increase in sample size leads to an increase in the generalizability of one sample to other samples that the same sampling procedure would produce (we call this ‘Hume’s prohibition’).

In our (2012) commentary we argued that this claim is false and that Lee and Baskerville failed to appreciate that the insolubility of the problem of induction means that science is a pointless activity. We offered an original balance-of-evidence argument that it is reasonable to assume that there is some solution to Hume’s problem, even if we do not know what the solution is.

In their (2012) rejoinder Lee and Baskerville do not mention our argument for Hume’s prohibition. Instead they give mathematical reasons against the prohibition. They object to our balance-of-evidence argument that Hume’s problem has a solution and sketch a supposedly Kuhnean solution to it that they think is consistent with Hume’s own position. A reader of the debate so far might well think that all is well with their position on the problem of induction. We show below that this is not so.

## Lee and Baskerville’s commitment to the insolubility of Hume’s problem of induction

An inference is deductive if and only if it is supposed to be valid; in other words, if its premises are true, then its conclusion must be true (Copi and Cohen, 1998; Hurley, 2003). Otherwise it is invalid. It is inductive if and only if it is an inference from observed matters of fact to unobserved matters of fact. If its conclusion is probably true relative to the information in its premises then it is strong (Farber et al., 2011). Hume argues that the use of induction cannot be justi<sup>fi</sup>ed deductively. Nor can it be justi<sup>fi</sup>ed inductively, since that would assume what is supposed to be justi<sup>fi</sup>ed. Given that justi<sup>fi</sup>cation is either deductive or inductive, induction is unjusti<sup>fi</sup>able. This is known as Hume’s problem of induction. Given this dismaying result, it is pointless to use any inductive inference in scienti<sup>fi</sup>c research. It is equally pointless to use induction to formulate a general theory on the basis of a set of particular observations. The upshot is that science is a pointless activity.

We noted that Lee and Baskerville are committed to claiming that Hume’s problem is insoluble and so are unwittingly committed to the pointlessness of science (2012: 732). They reply that ‘The position adopted by L&B was not that “ induction is not justi<sup>fi</sup>ed or warranted in any sense,” but instead, that “ induction or generalization is never fully justified logically,” as stated by Campbell and Stanley (1963: 17) and as quoted by L&B’ (2012: 754). There are at least two problems with this reply.

First, there is no evidence that this was Lee and Baskerville’s (2003) position. To the contrary, there is clear evidence that they quoted the phrase to describe Campbell and Stanley’s position: ‘The enormous signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of Hume’s truism leads Campbell and Stanley (1963) to take the positions that “induction or generalization is never fully justified logically” ’ (2003: 225).

Second, we did not say that their explicit position is that induction is not justi<sup>fi</sup>ed in any sense. Instead we argued that they are committed to this position (2012: 732). Our argument was that they say that they will ‘focus on Hume’s truism, which calls attention to an irremediable problem in induction’ (2003: 224), and they endorse Rosenberg’s ‘succinct description of Hume’s truism’ (2003: 225), which is that induction may be justi<sup>fi</sup>ed neither deductively nor inductively, and that ‘accordingly, claims that transcend available data, in particular predictions and general laws, remain unwarranted’ (Rosenberg, 1993: 75, our italics). As such, we are justi<sup>fi</sup>ed in arguing that Lee and Baskerville (2003) were committed to the position that ‘induction is not justi<sup>fi</sup>ed or warranted in any sense’ (2012: 732). This is because had Lee and Baskerville (2003) deemed that induction were justi<sup>fi</sup>ed in some sense, they would not have used the term ‘Hume’s truism’ over 20 times in their paper and would not have described the problem of induction as ‘irremediable (2003: 224).

Lee and Baskerville’s attitude toward Hume’s problem of induction is self-contradictory. Since they regarded Hume’s argument as a truism and ‘generalization,’ as they claimed (2003: 224), can be a synonym of induction, why did they propose a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of generalization, which would go against the truism, in the <sup>fi</sup>rst place? They argued that ‘as a consequence of Hume’s truism, a theory may never be generalized to a setting where it has not yet been empirically tested and con<sup>fi</sup>rmed’ (2003: 241, their italics), but included Type TE generalizability in their classi<sup>fi</sup>cation. Even more baf<sup>fl</sup>ing is their claim that ‘Type TE generalizability … is actually deduction, not induction’ (2003: 241). If Type TE generalizability is deduction and not induction, why is it considered one type of generalization (which can be a synonym of induction)?

Lee and Baskerville have not escaped Hume’s problem of induction In their original paper Lee and Baskerville claimed that Hume’s problem imposes ‘no prohibition of the conclusion that an increase in sample size leads to an increase in the generalizability of one sample to other samples that the same sampling procedure would produce’ (2003: 228). We argued that this claim is false (2012: 732). In their reply they fail to address our argument. Instead they offer a mathematical explanation, extracted from a standard statistics textbook (Neter et al., 1988), of why their claim is supposed to be true (2012: Appendix B). The mathematical extract is simply irrelevant and does nothing to de<sup>fl</sup>ect our original argument.

Their explanation basically consists of the following two elements:

(a) Inference is made from the characteristics of a sample of a certain size to other samples of the same size.

(b) The larger the sample size, the more accurate the inference will be.

As we argued in our commentary (2012: 732), (a) involves an inference from what we have observed to what we have not observed, and thus is induction. Even if we adopt the overly narrow de<sup>fi</sup>nition of induction in their original article – ‘a reasoning process that begins with statements of particulars and ends in a general statement’ (2003: 224) – the inference is still inductive because it is an inference from the characteristics of one sample (a particular instance) to all the members of the set that consists of other samples of the same size (something more general). Suppose that in a school of 1000 students, we take a random sample of 10 students, measure their heights and get a mean height of 5 feet. Then we infer that any random sample of 10 drawn from the school’s students will have a mean height of 5 feet, with a certain degree of error. (The degree of error will be smaller if the sample size is increased, as (b) states.) This is a general statement about what we have not yet observed. So Lee and Baskerville are wrong to say that the inference in question ‘does not involve induction’ (2012: A4).

Moreover, if induction is unjusti<sup>fi</sup>able then we are unjusti-<sup>fi</sup>ed in reporting the result of any observation of a sample, once we have made it. This is because we need a reason to think that once we have <sup>fi</sup>nished observing a phenomenon and started reporting, the phenomenon still resembles what we have just observed. For example, once we have <sup>fi</sup>nished observing that litmus paper has turned red, we need a reason to assume that it remains red once we have shifted our attention away from it. This reason must be inductive. This means that unless Hume’s problem is soluble then there is no point inferring from the characteristics of a sample of a certain size to other samples of the same size, because one is unjusti<sup>fi</sup>ed in thinking that the sample still has the characteristics that were observed. Science cannot even begin to get off the ground.

## Lee and Baskerville’s <sup>fl</sup>awed solution to Hume’s problem

Lee and Baskerville now offer a solution to Hume’s problem. They challenge the assumption in Hume’s argument that any justi<sup>fi</sup>cation of induction must be deductive or inductive. Supposedly following Kuhn, what justi<sup>fi</sup>es the practice of induction is that it is a social process. They claim that this position is consistent with Hume’s as he does not suggest that we eschew inductive reasoning. Rather, if we have observed that <sup>fl</sup>ame and heat ‘have always been conjoined together,’ our expectation of heat is ‘a species of natural instincts’ (2012: 755).

This attempted solution fails. That induction is part of a social process will not justify it because forecasting weather by divination of bones is part of a social process in some tribes. So is purging the body of vital spirits or telling horoscopes. This is bad science. Superstitions are part of social processes but remain unjusti<sup>fi</sup>ed ways of reasoning. Hume’s appeal to natural instincts likewise fails. As we pointed out (2012: footnote 3). Hume certainly thought that our use of induction is a psychologically engrained habit, or as he puts it a ‘custom, that we cannot shed. We cannot refrain from causal inference any more than we can refrain from breathing. But a skeptic could reply that this means that human nature dooms us to irrationality, because human psychology constrains us to use an unjusti<sup>fi</sup>able form of reasoning.

## Straw man fallacy

We gave a balance-of-evidence argument that we should adopt the position that Hume’s problem of induction has a solution even if we do not know what it is. Lee and Baskerville now make three objections to it (2012, Appendix C). Their <sup>fi</sup>rst objection is that we have given two arguments, both of which fail. The <sup>fi</sup>rst is supposed to be that ‘all past observations of attempted solutions to Hume’s Truism have been observations of attempts that failed; therefore the next observation will be an observation of failure; however, there is no uniformity of nature; therefore because all past observations were observations of failures, the next observation will be an observation of success (or the one after that, or after that, etc.)’ (2012: A5). They observe correctly that this relies on higher order induction and hence begs the question.

Our second argument is supposed to appear when we ‘recite’ an argument based on Strawson (1952) that ‘draws forward the notion that evidence can mount probabilistically to provide justi<sup>fi</sup>cation for acting on beliefs about the future (2012: A5). They then attack this argument in two ways. First they object that ‘belief is not the same as proof. Likewise, many observations may provide support, but support is also not the same as proof’ (2012: A5). Second they object that ‘there is still the current consensus in the philosophy of science that Hume’s problem of induction remains unsolved’ (2012: A5).

This objection commits the straw man fallacy, that of distorting the argument of one’s opponent and then attacking the distorted, <sup>fl</sup>imsier version to give the impression that the opponent’s argument has been demolished (Farber et al., 2011). We gave one argument, not two. We <sup>fi</sup>rst showed that there can be no evidence that there is no solution because it is impossible to list in advance all putative solutions (2012: 746). Next we argued that at a minimum, there is one solution – and perhaps more – that has some degree of plausibility. It follows that there is more reason to think that there is some successful solution than there is reason to think otherwise, even if we do not know what solution it is (2012: 747). This is nothing like the two silly arguments that Lee and Baskerville attribute to us.

As part of the second stage of our single argument we defended the claim that at a minimum, there is one solution – and perhaps more – that has some degree of plausibility. We did this, not by ‘reciting’ Strawson’s solution but by giving an original defence of it and by showing that Reichenbach’s (1938, 1949) solution defends it against objection (2012: 747). Moreover the argument that Lee and Baskerville attribute to Strawson before attacking it has no resemblance to the argument Strawson actually gives. This was that to ask whether we should form beliefs on the basis of inductive evidence is tantamount to asking the trivial question, ‘Are we justi<sup>fi</sup>ed in being justi<sup>fi</sup>ed?’ Trivially, the answer is yes. Once this is understood, Hume’s problem is no problem at all (2012: 747). Thus Lee and Baskerville have committed the straw man fallacy against Strawson as well.

Their second objection is that our balance-of-evidence argument ‘is not a widely held position’ (2012: A5). This is the fallacy of argumentum ad populum. The position that the world is not <sup>fl</sup>at was once not widely accepted, but that was not good reason against it. Besides, our argument cannot now be widely accepted because it is original and newly published. Who is to say that it will not eventually <sup>fi</sup>nd wide acceptance?

Their third objection is that ‘even if a solution to Hume’s problem of induction were to be found someday, the philosophy of science has already prepared, as the next problem, Goodman’s new riddle of induction, for which a solution has also proved to be elusive’ (2012: 759). We reply that Goodman’s (1955) riddle is no problem for generalization since unlike Hume’s problem it is a problem for description and explanation, not for the justi<sup>fi</sup>cation of induction (Rheinwald, 1993: 55). In any case Rheinwald (1993) has a good solution to the riddle. Applying the same logic of our balance-of-evidence argument, there can be no evidence that there is no solution to the riddle, and a defense of some solutions shows that there is at least one that enjoys some degree of plausibility. So the balance of evidence is that there is a solution to the riddle, even if we do not know what it is.

## Misusing basic logical terms

In addition to the term ‘generalize’ Lee and Baskerville have misused other basic logical terms in both their original paper and their rejoinder. This is worth explaining, because a reader of the debate might be encouraged to follow them in misusing these terms. Reminding the IS research community of their proper use does it a valuable service, not only because it protects the community from the disdain of logicians and philosophers in general, but also because it prevents the spread of confusion by putting everyone on the same page.

For example, Lee and Baskerville often use ‘valid’ as a synonym of ‘true,’ and ‘validity’ as a synonym of ‘truth value or ‘truth.’ Two examples are: ‘the conclusion in any syllogism can be valid only if its major premise is valid’ (2003: 225) and ‘to establish the validity of the uniformity of nature premise (2003: 225). Any textbook on logic will explain that validity is a property of the relationship between the premises of an inference and its conclusion, namely that if all its premises are true, then its conclusion must be true. Truth, on the other hand, is a property of a statement (such as premises and conclusion) and is commonly taken to consist in a correspondence between that statement and the real world. Accordingly, many philosophers hold that a statement ‘p’ is true just in case p is a fact (Russell, 1906; Moore, 1959). So it makes no sense to say that an inference is true (or false) or that its premises or conclusion are valid (or invalid) (see Copi and Cohen, 1998).<sup>7</sup>

Lee and Baskerville also betray confusion about what a syllogism is. They discuss the following statistical syllogism (2012: A6):

P1 75% of the women working at Golden Triangle Corporation are secretaries.

P2 Carol is a woman working at Golden Triangle Corporation.

C Carol is a secretary.

They claim that ‘to the extent that “Carol is a secretary” does not necessarily follow from the two premises (P1 and P2), one may argue that the logic leading to this “conclusion” (C) is not well described as the logic of the syllogism’ (2012: A6)

This does not follow, because although the argument is inductive and so aims to be strong rather than valid, the fact remains that, as we originally pointed out (2012: footnote 7), it ‘has two premises and so is ipso facto a syllogism, albeit not a categorical syllogism.’ A syllogism is just a two-premise argument. Some syllogisms are deductive, such as categorical syllogisms, while others are inductive, as is statistical syllogism.

They add that ‘the logic of the syllogism does apply in statistics’ (2012: A7) and give the following example. ‘The major premise (P1) is ${ \mathrm { ~ \ddot { i } f ~ \mathrm { \ddot { H } } _ { 0 } ~ } }$ is true, then P-value>α”, the minor premise (P2) is ${ } ^ { \mathfrak { a } } P .$ -value<α”, and the conclusion (C) is “therefore, reject H as true” ’ (2012: A7). This characterization is incoherent because these premises cannot be intelligibly called ‘major’ or ‘minor.’ These are terms used to describe the premises of a categorical syllogism. A categorical syllogism is a two-premise argument in which the premises and the conclusion are one of the standard categorical forms ‘All S are P,’ ‘No S are $\mathrm { ~ P ~ } _ { \ast } ^ { \ast }$ ‘Some S are $\mathrm { { \bf P } } ^ { \mathrm { * } }$ and ‘Some S are not $\mathrm { { \bf P } } ^ { \mathrm { * } }$ and which contains three terms, each of which appears exactly twice. The major premise is the premise that contains the predicate term of the conclusion. The minor premise is the premise that contains the subject term of the conclusion (Farber et al., 2011). For example, the following is a categorical syllogism:

Major premise: All IT managers are people that have college degrees.

Minor premise: All the people in this room are IT managers.

Conclusion: All the people in this room are people that have college degrees.

The major premise is so-called because it contains the term ‘people that have college degrees,’ which is the predicate term of the conclusion. The minor premise is so-called because it contains the term ‘the people in this room,’ which is the subject term of the conclusion.
