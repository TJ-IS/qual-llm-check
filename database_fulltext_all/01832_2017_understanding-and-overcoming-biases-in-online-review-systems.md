# Understanding and Overcoming Biases in Online Review Systems

Georgios Askalidis; Su Jung Kim; Edward C. Malthouse

## Abstract

This study addresses the issues of social influence and selection biases in the context of online review systems. We propose that one way to reduce these biases is to send email invitations to write a review to a random sample of buyers, and not exposing them to existing reviews while they write their reviews. We provide empirical evidence showing how such a simple i ervention from the retailer mitigates the biases by analyzing data from four diverse online retailers multiple years. The data include both self-motivated reviews, where the reviewer sees othe eviews at the time of writing, and retailerprompted reviews generated by an email invitation to verified buyers, where the reviewer does not see existing reviews. Consistent with previous research on the social influence bias, we find that the star ratings of self-motivated reviews decrease over time (i.e., downward trend), while the star ratings of retailer-prompted reviews remain constant. As predicted by theories on motivation, the self-motivated reviews are shown to be more negative (lower valence), longer, and more helpful, which suggests that the nature of self-motivated and retailer-prompted reviews is distinctively different and the influx of retailer-prompted reviews would enhance diversity in the overall review system. Regarding the selection bias, we found that email invitations can improve the representativeness of reviews by adding a new segment of verified buyers. In sum, implementing appropriate design and policy in online review systems will improve the quality and validity of online reviews and help practitioners provide more credible and representative ratings to their customers.

Keywords: online customer reviews, electronic word-of-mouth (eWOM), social influence bias, selection bias, intrinsic motivation, extrinsic motivation

## I. Introduction

“Word of Mouth (WOM),” defined as an informal communication between private parties concerning the evaluation of goods and services (Dichter, 1966; Singh, 1988; Westbrook, 1987), has been part of human behavior for a long time. With the rise of the Internet, scholars and industry professionals alike have observed the evolution and growth of electronic-WOM (e-WOM) and paid particular attention to its influence on consumer decision-making (Duan, Gu, & Whinston, 2008; Zhang, Zhao, Cheung, & Lee, 2014). Even though ‘traditional’ WOM will not be eliminated anytime soon (e.g., friends and family telling us about the latest shows they have watched on TV or asking for recommendations for a vacation destination), e-WOM offers the significant advantage that users are no longer forced to rely on scattered signals from their immediate social network to be informed about the quality of a product, but instead can access reviews from all over the orld in an organized and ondemand way (Hennig-Thurau, Gwinner, Walsh, & Gremler, 2004).

Indeed, reviews are being collected, aggregated and displayed to consumers in an easy-to-digest format in all types of settings: all of the top 10 U.S. online retailers, as well as most of the biggest retailers in the rest of the world (e.g., Alibaba), collect and display user reviews for their products. The same is true for all the major digital stores. Furthermore, companies like Yelp, Facebook, Google, IMDb and Rotten Tomatoes provide platforms for users to submit reviews that are, in-turn, aggregated and displayed to other users. User reviews are also being used to build trust between customers in decentralized marketplaces like eBay, Airbnb and Uber. The most recent report on global trust in advertising shows that two-thirds (66%) of global respondents said they trust consumer opinions posted online (The Nielsen Company, 2015). This trust between consumers is a cornerstone for the success of any marketplace, where customers interact and make financial transactions with brands and other consumers.

For online shoppers, reviews are not just an option anymore, but an expectation. According to a recent survey from PowerReviews, 30% of shoppers under the age of 45 consult reviews for every purchase they make, while 86% say that reviews are essential in making purchase decisions (O'Neil,

2015). The same survey also found that reviews are the second most important factor for making purchase decisions, following price. Apart from the widespread adoption of online reviews, a large amount of literature has showcased the economic importance of positive ratings. Luca (2011) found that a 1-star increase in the Yelp ratings of a restaurant can cause a 5-9% increase in revenue, and Anderson and Magruder (2012) found that an extra half-star is associated with a restaurant selling out its reservations 50% more frequently. Positive correlations between ratings and sales have been found for products on Amazon.com (Chevalier & Mayzlin, 2006), consumer package goods (Askalidis & Malthouse, 2016; Maslowska, Malthouse, & Bernritter, 2017), new products (Cui, Lui, & Guo, 2012), movies (Dellarocas & Zhang, 2005), and mobile applications (apps) in Google’s mobile app store (Engstrom & Forsell, 2015). In two-sided marketplaces such as eBay, previous research has found that positive user feedback leads to economic benefits (Cabral & Hortaçsu, 2010; Houser & Wooders, 2006; Resnick, Zeckhauser, Swanson, & Lockwood, 2006). Besides user ratings, Duan et al. (2008) found a significant association between a movie’s box office reven ue and the volume of online user reviews.

Aside from research on the economic effects of online user reviews, a recent body of research started to examine the biases that govern them and how these biases may damage the quality of reviews influence bias refers to a tendency that one’s opinion is influenced by the opinions expressed in other reviews (Muchnik, Aral, & Taylor, 2013). Selection bias, roughly speaking, happens when the set of users that submit a review is not representative of the entire purchasing population (De Langhe et al., 2015). With the omnipresence of reviews in the current two-way marketplace, such biases may result in factors other than the true quality of products or services deciding their success. Hence there is a need to understand these biases and find ways to fix them.

Although a few recent studies have provided theoretical understandings and practical suggestions regarding how to reduce the aforementioned biases (e.g., De Langhe et al., 2015; Krishnan, Patel, Franklin, & Goldberg, 2014; Muchnik et al., 2013), to the best of our knowledge, this study is one of the few studies that demonstrates how one simple intervention from online retailers can help reduce these biases by using datasets from multiple online review sites. In this study, we argue that inviting users who have purchased a product to contribute their review to the review site, without exposing them to existing reviews on the site, would mitigate the social influence and selection biases. It would significantly reduce the selection bias because the exclusion of social signals during the process of review submission would remove the risk of users reading other people’s reviews and adjusting their opinions closer to a group norm. It would help increase the representativeness of existing reviewer population by introducing a new segment of “verified” buyers who would have not been motivated to write their review in the online review system.

Thus, we examine how the introduction of email invitations from the retailers influences the online review eco-system with the following questions: (1) How do different populations (i.e., those who wrote reviews with vs. without email invitations) differ when they write reviews for the same product? (2) How do the differences change over time when part of population can see other users’ reviews, while the other part cannot (i.e., social influence bias)? (3) How does the contribution of users invited to write a review influence the overall representativeness of the population of the online review systems (i.e., selection bias)? We address these questions in two separate studies. Study 1 focuses on the differences between reviews written with and without email invitations and the social influence bias (the first and second questions above). Study 2 investigates the effect of the introduction of email invitations on the selection bias (the third question). Throughout the manuscript, we will refer to reviews written by those invited via email invitations as retailer-prompted reviews, indicating the fact that there was an email invitation from the online retailer. Accordingly, we will refer to reviews written without email prompts as self-motivated reviews, since they were written by consumers who share their opinions without prompting from the retailer.

## 2. Literature review

2.1 Intrinsic vs. extrinsic motivation

Introducing email invitations should bring a new subset of consumers who would have not written a review had it not been for the action initiated by the retailer. The next questions become (1) whether and how the nature of the review content that this new, retailer-prompted population generates would differ from that of the existing, self-motivated population, and (2) whether and how the existing population would be influenced by the influx of the new population (i.e., social influence bias)? We now turn to the literature on motivation and its effects on performance in order to predict how retailerprompted reviews will be different from self-motivated reviews and whether there will be any temporal changes in the way existing (i.e., self-motivated) reviewers produce their reviews.

Motivation has been extensively studied in psychology, with a particular focus on its effects on performance in academic settings (Hidi & Harackiewicz, 2000) and the workplace (Steers, Mowday, & Shapiro, 2004). Broadly speaking, there are two types of motivation: intrinsic and extrinsic. Intrinsic motivation refers to the “inherent tendency to seek out novelty and challenges, to extend and exercise one’s capacities, to explore, and to learn,” whereas extrinsic motivation refers to the “performance of an activity in order to attain some separable outcome” (Ryan & Deci, 2000, pp. 70-71). In other words, extrinsically motivated behaviors are conducted with the desire to gain an incentive such as money, awards, or praise, while intrinsically motivated behaviors are driven by the enjoyment of the very action.

Another extensive line of research has examined the effects of these two motivations on performance behaviors. Cerasoli et al. (2014) conducted a meta-analysis of extant research to understand the impact of intrinsic motivation and extrinsic incentives on performance. One of their findings is that intrinsic motivation is positively associated with performance and more strongly associated with qualitytype tasks than quantity-type tasks since intrinsically motivated people are more likely to engage in activities with a higher level of intensity. In the context of online reviewers, those who are selfmotivated to write a review can be considered intrinsically motivated. In contrast, consumers who are invited to write a review can be regarded as extrinsically motivated with some incentives (e.g., a word of appreciation, a chance to win a raffle, or the goodwill of sharing their honest opinions with other

consumers). Then, how do the reviews from intrinsically and extrinsically motivated reviewers differ? In other words, how is the difference reflected in the nature of review content?

First, previous research on e-WOM demonstrated that those involved in e-WOM activities exhibit either extremely positive or extremely negative attitudes toward the firm and their consumption experience, creating a J-shaped distribution of star ratings (Hu, Zhang, & Pavlou, 2009). Those contributing positive e-WOM do so to help the company or other consumers, whereas those who spread negative e-WOM are driven by the feeling of vengeance and anxiety reduction (Sundaram, Mitra, & Webster, 1998). Anderson (1998) also found that dissatisfied customers are more likely to be vocal about their dissatisfaction than satisfied customers about their satisfaction. Thus, we expect to see more negative valence in self-motivated reviews than retailer-prompted reviews.

H1: Self-motivated reviews will be more negative than retailer-prompted reviews.

Second, a large body of work has demonstrated that intrinsic motivation is a strong predictor of high-quality work. When people are intrinsically motivated, they are more willing to engage in the task intensely, resulting in high quality work. In this paper we focus on the review text length as an approximation to quality following previous research (Cheng & Ho, 2015; Huang, Chen, Yen, & Tran, 2015), and hence expect to see self-motivated reviews to be more elaborate (i.e., high quality), which in turn makes them longer.

H2: Self-motivated reviews will be longer than retailer-prompted reviews.

Third, past work has found that negative reviews are seen as more helpful (Bakhshi, Kanuparthy, & Shamma, 2014). Previous research has also shown that longer messages provide in-depth comments on products or services, which consumers find useful (Chen & Tseng, 2011). From H1 and H2, we expect that self-motivated reviews will be more negative and longer, which will cause them to be perceived as more helpful.

## H3: Self-motivated reviews will be perceived as more helpful than retailer-prompted reviews.

## 2.2. Difference in review submission process and the social influence bias

The previous section hypothesized how self-motivated reviews and retailer-prompted reviews may differ in terms of their valence, information quality, and perceived helpfulness. Another major difference between these two types of reviews is the process by which a review is submitted to the system, and the potential risk of a social influence bias for self-motivated reviews in particular. As mentioned in the introduction, one of the major concerns about online product reviews is whether the aggregated information about a product truly reflects the unbiased opinions of reviewers. Over the past years, scholars have voiced concerns about the social influence bias in online user ratings. Research by Li & Hitt (2008) and Godes & Silva (2012) showed that online reviews exhibit temporal trends, indicating that users who submit a review later in a product's life cycle are generally different than users who review earlier. In a similar vein, Nagle & Riedl (2014) found that the propensity of a user to review can be a function not only of their opinion about the product, but also of the current state of the reviews. Previous studies using randomized experiments support the existence of a social influence bias as well. Muchnik et al. (2013) demonstrated that an arbitrary positive vote on a comment submitted to a news aggregator website created accumulating positive herding that increased the final ratings by 25% on average. Salganik et al. (2006) created an artificial music market where participants downloaded previously unknown songs, either with or without information about the previous participants' actions, and found that the display of social signals increases the inequality and unpredictability of success.

We argue that one way to prevent reviewers from being influenced by previous reviews is to isolate the review submission process so that reviewers are not exposed to existing reviews. The mechanisms by which a retailer-prompted review is submitted often follow this process: customers who receive an email invitation to write a review are taken to an isolated page where no information about the current state of the reviews is displaced. In other words, there are no social signals on the email

invitation sent (e.g., current average star ratings). In contrast, self-motivated reviewers observe social signals about the current state of the reviews throughout the reviewing process. Hence, not only the review that a self-motivated reviewer submits can be influenced by the existing reviews, but even the decision of a user to submit a review can be influenced by the current state of the reviews, increasing the level of social influence bias.

Our next hypothesis addresses the social influence bias. Godes and Silva (2012) observed a downward slope for reviews on Amazon.com and concluded that this trend is partially explained by the fact that consumers face a challenge of finding reviews relevant to them when volume is large, which, in turn, makes later reviews more negative. We expect to see a similar temporal trend from self-motivated reviews in our dataset, but not from retailer-prompted reviews because of the absence of social signals during the review submission process for retailer-prompted reviewers. In case of helpfulness votes, we expect that the longer a review has been posted on the platform, the more helpful votes it will receive because older reviews have a higher chance of being read by consumers. It is also possible that older reviews that had already received helpfulness votes before recent reviews were posted have a higher chance of being found by the sorting function (i.e., when helpful reviews are displayed first).

H4: Self-motivated reviews will exhibit more temporal trends than retailer-prompted reviews in their (a) star ratings and (b) review length. (c) Perceived helpfulness decreases with arrival rank for both self-motivated and retailer-prompted reviews.

## 2.3. Issues of selection bias in online review system

If the social influence bias damages the quality of reviews due to people’s tendency to conform to a majority opinion rather than reveal their objective evaluation, the selection bias does so due to the lack of representativeness of the review-contributing population. De Langhe et al. (2015) argued that user ratings may not reflect the true quality of products being reviewed. The first issues they raised were “statistical,” where reviews for a product or service do not have a sample size large enough to conduct a statistical test with sufficient statistical power. The second issue, which is pertinent to this paper, was “sampling,” which suggests that online users who write a product review are not a random sample from the consumers who have purchased the product. If consumers interpret valence (average stars across all reviews) as being the average star rating of all buyers, then a company displaying reviews should compute valence using the reviews that best represent the population of all buyers.

In the language of statistics, the average star rating for all buyers is a parameter estimated by valence, the average of the ratings available. The available reviews, however, constitute a convenience sample from all buyers for at least three reasons. First, non-verified buyers may not have purchased the product and are thus not part of population (Mayzlin, Dover, & Chevalier, 2014). Second, companies and competitors write fake reviews to either promote or damage the firms’ reputation (Anderson & Simester, 2014; Luca & Zervas, 2016). Third, buyers self-select into writing a review. For instance, Hu et al. (2009) demonstrated that review distributions on online platforms tend to be bimodal, suggesting that extremely satisfied and extremely dissatisfied customers are more likely to submit a review, whereas consumers with a moderate level of satisfaction may not leave their comments. This means that the average of the available reviews is likely biased, in that its expected value does not equal the parameter.

The sampling bias can be avoided by obtaining reviews from a random sample of buyers. For firms that currently do not solicit reviews from buyers, a simple intervention would be to send email invitations to a random sample of buyers. We predict that this would reduce the selection bias by attracting a more representative sample of verified buyers. Thus, we pose the following questions to examine the impact of introducing email invitations.

RQ1: What is the effect of the email invitations on the online review platform of the retailers in terms of the number of reviews and the distribution of average star ratings?

RQ2: What is the effect of email invitations on existing reviewing population of the retailers (i.e., self-motivated reviewers)?

Our dataset is comprised of the entire review history of four major online retailers in different product categories. Collectively, the four sell a wide variety of electronics, appliances, bedding, kitchen, jewelry, personal-care items, and health products. ## 3.1.2. Estimation model

To test the different nature of review content by reviews who are intrinsically motivated (i.e., self-motivated reviewers) and extrinsically motivated (i.e., retailer-prompted reviewers) and the temporal changes of the nature of the reviews, we specify our model as follows:

$$
y = \alpha_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \text {selfmotivated} + \beta_ {2} \text {rank} + \beta_ {3} \text {selfmotivated} * \text {rank} + e,\tag{1}
$$

where “selfmotivated” is a binary variable indicating a self-motivated review, and rank is an integer indicating the arrival rank of the review. The coefficient of the rank variable will detect any temporal trends. The interaction variable selfmotivated\*rank tests differences in the temporal trends for the two types of reviews. The error term is e. To understand the model better, when the review is not selfmotivated (selfmotivated=0) the equation is $y = \alpha _ { 0 } + \beta _ { 2 }$ rank. When the review is self-motived the equation is $y = \left( \alpha _ { 0 } + \beta _ { 1 } \right) + \left( \beta _ { 2 } + \beta _ { 3 } \right)$ rank. Thus, $\beta _ { 3 }$ measures the change in slope for rank (trend) between retailer-prompted and self-motivated reviews. We expect the slope for retailer-prompted reviews $( \beta _ { 1 } )$ to be 0, since retailer-prompted reviewers do not see existing reviews. We expect the slope for self-motivated reviews to be negative (i.e., $\beta _ { 1 } + \beta _ { 3 } < 0 )$ . The term $\beta _ { 1 }$ gives the difference in intercept between retailer-prompted and self-motivated reviews.

We start by estimating Model (1) with review rating as the dependent variable. This shows if the differences we observe in the average rating and temporal trends between self-motivated and retailerprompted reviews are statistically significant. We also estimate Model (1) with review length as the dependent variable to explore differences between the text of the reviews submitted by self-motivated and retailer-prompted reviews. Finally, we seek to understand if there are differences between how many helpful votes these two types of reviews receive, and if one set of reviews is generally perceived, by the users, as more helpful. Hence, we also estimate Model (1) with the number of helpful votes.

This shows if the differences in the average rating and temporal trends between selfmotivated and retailer-prompted reviews are statistically significant. ## 3.2 Method for Study 2 (RQ1 – RQ2)

## 3.2.1. Data collection for Study 2

We examine Companies A and B in Table 1, which introduced email invitations during our study period. These companies began with only self-motivated reviews and began sending out email invitations at some point during our study period, creating a natural experiment where we can study characteristics of the reviews before and after the intervention. To approximate the date when a retailer started sending out email invitations to their customers, we can observe the first email review that was submitted in that retailer's platform. We then keep all the reviews that were submitted in the three months before the email review introduction and the three months after. We consider this set of reviews to be the ‘treatment’ year, in contrast to another set of reviews we use as a control. Our control set of reviews, which allows us to adjust for temporal and other trends, consists of those submitted in the same six-month period exactly one year before the treatment. For example, if the retailer-prompted reviews were introduced for a retailer on June 1, 2012 our treatment group of reviews is comprised of those submitted in the period March 1 - September 1, 2012 while our control group consists of those submitted in the period March 1 - September 1, 2011. We use a difference-in-differences approach to understand any effect the email introduction had. Overall, we had 6,078 reviews for 3,599 products from Company A and 1,649 reviews for 597 products from Company B. Email invitations were introduced during August 2008 by Company A and December 2011 by Company B.

## 3.2.2. Model estimation

The model to test the effect of the introduction of email invitations is:

$$
y = \alpha_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \mathrm{treat} + \beta_ {2} \mathrm{post} + \beta_ {3} \mathrm{treat} * \mathrm{post} + e,\tag{2}
$$

where the treat variable indicates if the review is in the treatment or control year and the post variable indicates if the review was submitted after the treatment, i.e., in the last three months of the study period. The treat\*post variable represents the interaction of the treat and post variables and estimates the variation in the treatment group that is not explained by the control group. We are interested in two

questions: 1) What was the effect of the email invitations on the entire review platform of the retailer, and, in particular, 2) What was the effect of the email invitations on the existing reviewing population of the retailer, i.e., the self-motivated reviews? Model (2) can be used to answer both questions.

## 3.2.3. Results for Study 2

Table 3 summarizes the results when we estimate Model (2) on the entire treatment and control datasets and Table 4 summarizes the results when we estimate Model (2) restricting our attention to selfmotivated reviews (treatment and control). The first row of Table 3 displays the results with volume as the dependent variable using the entire set of reviews (i.e., both self-motivated and retailer-prompted reviews). The highly significant and positive coefficient for the treat\*post variable confirms that the overall increase in volume is statistically significant. When we estimate Model (2) restricting the data only to the self-motivated reviews, we see that there is no observable effect on the volume of selfmotivated reviews from the introduction of retailer-prompted reviews (first row of Table 4), suggesting that the introduction of email invitations increased the volume without influencing the volume of existing reviews. Figure 2 shows the volume of reviews before, during, and after the treatment. We see the increase in all reviews. Indeed, we found that in the 90 days following the introduction of email invitations, the volume of reviews increased from about 30 reviews per day to 38 reviews per day. However, we also observed a decrease in the volume of self-motivated reviews from 30 reviews per day to 19 reviews per day. An additional analysis with the control dataset (i.e., reviews posted during the same time in the previous year) revealed that the decrease in the volume of reviews in the treatment year is due to a temporal trend, thus excluding the possibility that the introduction of retailer-prompted reviews influenced the existing reviewer population.

Having established that the volume of self-motivated reviews is unaffected by the introduction of the retailer-prompted reviews, it is interesting to see if there is any change in the valence of reviews from self-motivated reviewers after the influx of retailer-prompted reviews. The second row of Tables 3 and Table 4 show that the coefficient for treat\*post is highly significant and positive when Model (2) is estimated on the entire dataset, which indicates an overall increase in star rating. In contrast, the coefficient for the same variable is not significantly different from zero when we estimate Model (2) only on self-motivated reviews, which indicates no change in the star rating. Hence, by sending email invitations, a retailer can access a population that submits, on average, higher ratings without affecting the ratings of the existing reviewers.

In addition to review rating, we want to see the effect that retailer-prompted reviews had on the review length overall and on the review length of self-motivated reviews in particular. The negative and statistically significant coefficient for the treat\*post variable, shown in the third row of Table 3, confirms that there is a decrease on the average review text length. In contrast, the coefficient for the same variable in the model estimation based only on self-motivated reviews, shown in Table 4, is not statistically different from zero, indicating that even though the retailer-prompted reviews had a negative effect on the length of reviews overall, they had no effect on the length of self-motivated reviews. This again shows that introducing retailer-prompted reviewers did not influence self-motivated reviewers.

Overall, these findings suggest that by sending email invitations, a retailer can attract an entirely new segment of their purchasing customer base to submit a review, without having any effect on the volume of existing reviewers or the characteristics of their submitted reviews. This means that the new overall set of reviews can be more representative, reducing the selection bias.

## 4. Discussion and conclusion

## 4.1 Theoretical implications

This study addresses the social influence and selection biases that are prevalent in online review systems and how an intervention from the retailers can help reduce them. Our work is the first to compare self-motivated reviews and retailer-prompted reviews for the same products during the same

time periods using four different online retailers encompassing a wide range of product categories. Much existing work has focused on product reviews from platforms like Amazon and Yelp, which are populated mostly by self-motivated reviews. Our dataset enables us to compare the reviews from the two populations – intrinsically motivated and extrinsically motivated – and how the isolated mechanism of retailer-prompted reviews may help reduce people’s tendency to be affected by previously posted reviews. It also allows us to see whether introducing email invitations brings in an entirely new segment of the population and makes the reviewing population more representative.

With growing concerns about the quality and representativeness of online user reviews, this study sheds light on how to mitigate the social influence and selection biases embedded in review systems. It also provides insights to the literature on motivation research. First, it extends our knowledge of the role of motivation in predicting one’s performance in the context of online review creation. We found that there are major differences between intrinsically motivated (i.e., self-motivated) and motivated reviews are more negative, longer, and perceived as more helpful. This is in line with the findings from previous research by Cerasoli et al. (2014) in that intrinsic motivation leads to higher quality work. The finding is also consistent with Anderson’s (1998) study that shows dissatisfied consumers are more actively engaged in WOM behavior. A portion of those who are intrinsically motivated to submit a review may be a group of consumers who are not satisfied with the product, and thus want to vent about their negative consumption experience on a public online forum, whereas those who are invited to review are more likely to contribute with the intention to help the company and other consumers by sharing their opinions (Sundaram et al., 1998).

Second, this study confirms the existence of a social influence bias when reviewers can see the social signals from other reviewers by observing a downward trend in stars (Figure 2). Reviews written by of reviewers who received email invitations and could not see these social signals gave consistently higher ratings than self-motivated reviews and did not exhibit any temporal trends. Furthermore, we also found evidence supporting that a slight change to the review submission mechanism can reduce the

social influence bias. Our results suggest that introducing email invitations does not disturb the existing reviewing population. The volume of reviews and valence distribution of self-motivated reviews remained stable even after retailer-prompted reviews were introduced. Previous research showed that a social influence bias results in less diversity in aggregated reviews because people modify their evaluation to make it similar to a group norm (i.e., average). Our findings in Study 1 suggest that retailer-prompted reviewers produce reviews that contain distinctively different characteristics from selfmotivated reviews, which would help overcome the group herding effect in online review systems.

Third, this study also provides evidence that introducing email invitations contributes to improving the overall representativeness of the reviewing population by directing a new segment of the population to submit a review. In Study 2, we found that the reviews overall become more representative with a larger segment of the population reviewing for products and more credible with the new segment being verified buyers identified by the retailers. Recent work by Zhang et al. (2014) showed that source credibility and the quantity of reviews can have positive effects on review-readers’ purchase intention. Since the introduction of email invitations will increase the number of submitted reviews and a new segment of reviews will come from a “Verified Buyer,” we can expect that such an intervention from the retailers can increase the overall quality of online review systems, which will eventually improve economic benefits.

## 4.2. Practical implications

The results of this study provide practical guidelines for online retailers that run their own review system. We agree with Aral (2014) that site design and policy can help prevent biases and manipulation. As shown in this paper, a small intervention such as sending out an email to verified buyers to submit a review can motivate a new segment of reviewers and reduce the biases that govern the online review system. By offering different types of incentives (e.g., coupons, a raffle to win a big prize, or a badge for frequent contributors), retailers can invite a larger segment of retailer-prompted reviews. Also, the design of the review submission process can curb the social influence bias by

isolating the reviewers from existing reviews. This may seem counter to the industry practice, but our findings suggest that doing so will result in increasing the quality and validity of submitted reviews.

Beyond review collection, online retailers must make decisions about how they display reviews. Currently, most retailers provide the ability for users to sort reviews by date, star rating, or helpful score. We expect that the default options each retailer implements can have substantial effects on the purchasing decisions of users. Here, we propose a new policy: give customers the option to see the valence of only retailer-prompted reviews, only self-motivated reviews, or both, with the default being the valence from retailer-prompted reviews. A justification for this is that self-motivated reviews can be written by people who did not buy the product, and are thus not part of the population of buyers. It is possible that self-motivated reviewers have indeed purchased a product elsewhere, just not from the t of view, not having a “Verified Purchase” badge may harm the perceived credibility of the reviewer and the review message when these reviews are displayed with those written by verified buyers. Verified, retailer-prompted reviews that are less bimodal and more stable over time. Hence, they can be a more accurate and credible representation of the true underlying quality of the reviewed product. Furthermore, this approach can de-incentivize fake reviews, a problem that is becoming increasingly prominent (Luca & Zervas, 2016). A limitation of this prescription is that the text of self-motivated reviews is often longer and more helpful, but this could be solved by showing all reviews to those who navigate to the review page and adding a “verified buyer” badge to retailer-prompted reviews, as some retailers currently do.

It is important to note that we are not suggesting the retailer manipulate reviews or mislead consumers in any way. This would be a myopic strategy because a consumer who is misled and ends up having a dissatisfying experience will not return to make future purchases. It is in the retailer’s interest to have satisfied customers, and an unbiased set of reviews should help them achieve this goal. The interventions we have suggested are designed to attract a representative set of buyers who are not influenced by existing reviews.

## 5. Limitations and suggestions for future research

In this section, we address a few limitations of this study, which also guide suggestions for future research. First, although one of our practical solutions to mitigate biases is to send out email invitations to review, one may ask how aggressively a retailer should pursue such email responses. For example, a retailer may send multiple email prompts or offer monetary incentives. A potential negative outcome would be those who are more prone to incentives will only contribute their reviews, which presumably are low in quality. We believe that more research is needed to understand the biases that such strategies can introduce in the selection of the reviewing population and the reviews they attract.

Second, the current study does not have a direct measure of consumers’ motivation. Future research can investigate what types of users will be more likely to respond to monetary or social incentives, and the characteristics of reviews that they submit. Future studies should also examine how tolerable consumers are of getting multiple email invitations from the retailer that ask to submit a review. Annoyed consumers may submit a lower rating than they would have otherwise. Another research question is whether and how monetary incentives (and the disclosure of such information) would influence other consumers’ perception of credibility on products, reviews, and retailers. Insights towards this direction can help practitioners optimally design their review solicitation process.

Third, this study could only focus on two types of biases, but there certainly are other concerns about online review systems. For example, De Langhe et al. (2015) found a discrepancy between online reviews by ordinary consumers and those by experts by comparing average user ratings and Consumer Report scores. Moreover, they found that average user ratings are bad at predicting resale values for products. Future research should also address the issue of evaluation expertise in online user ratings. Would the performance of the average rating (towards the goal of predicting Consumer Report scores and resale values) be improved if retailer-prompted reviews were taken into account? Could retailerprompted reviews perform even better if considered alone? Insights here can help us understand which set of reviews (self-motivated or retailer-prompted) are a more accurate representation of a product's true quality.

Answers to the aforementioned questions can help retailers understand what the best practices of soliciting and displaying reviews will be. Ultimately, they will help customers make better decisions, which will earn their loyalty and increase their value to the retailer.

## Research Highlights

 We examine the effects of the introduction of email invitations to write a review by retailers on social influence bias and selection bias in online review systems.

 By using online review data sets obtained from four online retailers in a wide variety of product categories, we provide quantified evidence of the effects of the introduction of email invitations.

 Self-motivated reviews are more negative, longer, and perceived as more helpful possibly due to the different type of motivation among self-motivated reviewers compared to retailer-prompted reviewers.

Retailer-prompted reviews, which are written without social signals, do not show a downward trend (i.e., no social influence bias), whereas self-motivated reviews that are written with social signals during the review submission process exhibit a downward trend (i.e., social influence bias).

 The introduction of email invitations brings in a new segment of reviewing population which does not influence the nature of reviews from the existing reviewing population, which makes the online review system being more representative and providing higher quality information.
