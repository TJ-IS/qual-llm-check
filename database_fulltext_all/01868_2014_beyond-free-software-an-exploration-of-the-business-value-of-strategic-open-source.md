# Beyond free software: An exploration of the business value of strategic open source

Keywords: Open innovation Open source software Value creation Value capture Value network

## a b s t r a c t

The phenomenon of open source software (OSS) has been well studied from the software development perspective, but it has received much less attention from the perspective of business value. Nevertheless, OSS, when viewed as a value creation process rather than ‘free’ software, provides businesses with value through access to knowledge and innovation capacity resident in online communities. This conceptualisation, which we label ‘strategic open source’ requires firms to rethink their strategy and processes as there is a shift in focus from ownership to openness and collaboration with external parties. Nonetheless, the emergence of OSS poses a puzzle for conceptions of organisational theory. Therefore, a theorising process is needed in order to develop a deeper understanding of how value is created and captured with OSS. Using a field study of eleven European firms, this paper explores the creation and capture of business value from strategic open source. The findings reveal that while decision makers look to open innovation initiatives like OSS for value creation and capture, there is still a desire to remain self reliant, resulting in collaborative design (of external innovations) rather than collaborative decision making with value network partners in relation to how such innovations would help create and capture value within firms.

## Introduction

The use of Web-based communities to develop open source software (OSS) for business environments is widespread (Garriga et al., 2011; Spaeth et al., 2010; West and Lakhani, 2008; Fitzgerald, 2006). The economic and organisational benefits of leveraging such communities to access knowledge and innovation capacity have been well articulated (see Chesbrough, 2012, 2003, 2006). Open source software (OSS) is viewed as one of the most well-established examples of both open innovation (Morgan et al., 2013; West, 2007; Gassmann and Enkel, 2004) and commons-based peer production (Feller et al., 2008; Benkler, 2002); resulting in changing views of the OSS phenomenon from a low cost software artefact, ‘i.e. operational open source’ to a driver of strategic business value. We define the use of open source software and processes in a manner that leverages both the peer-produced and open innovation qualities of such software and processes in order to create business value as ‘strategic open source’.

The benefits of open source alternatives for significant operational software are cited widely in the existing literature. These include high quality, in terms of reliability and stability, (Forge, 2006; Varian and Shapiro, 2003); lower costs, as the software is often made available free or at a low purchase cost (Shaikh and Cornford, 2011; Morgan and Finnegan, 2007; Fanini, 2005); escape from vendor lock-in (Shaikh and Cornford, 2011; IDA, 2004; Johnson, 2003); user support from experts in the online community (Williams et al., 2005; Krishnamurthy, 2003); and flexibility of use in that the software can be customized or modified to specific needs (Varian and Shapiro, 2003, Krishnamurthy, 2003). Nonetheless, how these benefits translate into strategic business value – in particular how they impact a firm’s value creation and capture processes – are less well known.

Traditional business strategy has led firms to develop defensible positions against the forces of competition and power in the value chain, rather than promote openness (Chesbrough and Appleyard, 2007). However, the concept of open innovation challenges traditional business strategy, specifically its dominant view that experts ‘within’ a company design innovative new products to meet customer needs (i.e. closed innovation). Recently, shorter innovation cycles, the rising costs of research and development, and a lack of resources have motivated a change in innovation strategies, inciting some organizations to adopt a more open approach (Van de Vrande et al., 2010; Gassmann and Enkel, 2004). It has been argued that ‘‘open strategy balances the tenets of traditional business strategy with the promise of open innovation’’ as ‘‘it embraces the benefits of openness as a means of expanding value creation and capture for organisations’’ (Chesbrough and Appleyard, 2007). In formulating an open strategy, Gassmann and Enkel (2004) identify three core open innovation processes: (1) the outside-in process, where a company’s innovativeness can increase through the integration of suppliers, customers, and external knowledge sourcing, (2) the inside-out process, where companies can earn profits by bringing ideas to market and transferring ideas to the outside environment, and (3) the coupled process where companies can combine the outside-in and inside-out processes by working in alliance with complimentary partners. Gassmann and Enkel found that while companies choose one primary process, they often integrate some elements of others.

The OSS phenomenon demonstrates two key elements of the open innovation concept – namely the collaborative development of the technology and shared rights to its use (West and Gallagher, 2006). Indeed, it has been suggested that open source processes are the most prominent example of the revolution of traditional innovation processes (Gassmann and Enkel, 2004). As an open innovation phenomenon, however, strategic open source raises concerns in commercial settings due to worries about the quality and suitability of external ideas, perceived competitive necessities, and issues relating to organisational control (Chesbrough, 2004). In addition, strategic open source challenges business to exploit global networks not currently known to the firm (West et al., 2006; Pereira, 2007), as businesses must not only ‘procure’ innovation resources (skills and solutions) as they would operational products and services, but must also do so outside their existing supply and distribution chains in order to see the desired impact on innovativeness and competitiveness (Feller et al., 2009).

Peer production, a key aspect of the strategic exploitation of OSS, is a model for organizing production without reliance on markets, managerial hierarchies, property, and contracts. It is characterized by the decentralized accumulation and exchange of information, and is seen as potentially superior to hierarchy and market governance models as a mechanism for discovering/applying human skill and knowledge to the creation of information resources (Benkler, 2002, 2006). In its emergent form, OSS represented a commons-based peer production model where geographically dispersed programmers collaborated to produce software (West and O’Mahony, 2005). While OSS has transitioned into the realm of mainstream business, playing an important role in the business models of firms in technology and other industries (Rajala and Westerlund 2008; Fitzgerald, 2006; Overby et al., 2006), it is still generally peer produced. As a peer produced phenomenon, strategic open source challenges businesses from the perspective of how formal hierarchical processes can seamlessly engage with the community-based processes that characterise OSS development communities (Feller et al., 2008)

We argue that strategic open source raises questions about how businesses can effectively create and capture value not only from using the software artefact, but also from engaging with the open communities and commons-based peer production processes from which the software emerges. Specifically, the emergence of OSS as a form of peer-produced open innovation poses a puzzle for conceptions of organisational theory due to its non-reliance on markets or traditional managerial hierarchies to organise production. We therefore argue that an exploratory study is needed in order to develop a better understanding of how value is created and captured with OSS. As part of this process, this paper draws on a field study of eleven European firms and examines how they create and capture value through the strategic application of open source software and processes. The next section presents the theoretical grounding for the study; critiquing established conceptualizations of value creation and capture as being overly reliant on hierarchies and markets as forms of production. This is followed by a description of the research method and a presentation of the findings. The findings examine the characteristics of OSS that facilitate and impede value creation and capture, and reveal the importance of networks in enabling such value creation and capture. The paper concludes by using the study findings to delineate further the business value of strategic and operational open source

## Theoretical grounding

Traditionally our understanding of an organisation has been that individuals organise their productive activities in two ways; either as employees in firms, following directions of managers, or as individuals in markets responding to market signals (Coase, 1937). Open source software, however, does not rely on markets or traditional managerial hierarchies to organise production (Benkler, 2002). Like other forms of open innovation, OSS involves collaboration between firms, suppliers, customers and makers of related products to pool software R&D (Morgan et al., 2013; West and Gallagher, 2006); this is

However, little research has directly focused on questions related to value creation and capture in open peer production environments.

The concepts of value creation and value capture are at the core of strategic management since superior value creation and the ability to capture value in the form of profits are prerequisites for building competitive advantage (Priem, 2007; Enders et al., 2004). However, the process of value creation is often confused with the process of value capture. In addition, it has been argued that both value creation and capture should be viewed as distinct processes, since the source that creates value may or may not be able to capture that value in the long-term (Lepak et al., 2007). Recent work considers value creation a key element of a business model (Amit and Zott, 2012; Chesbrough, 2010). Within the business model context, value creation is defined as the ability of the firm to identify a value proposition for a relevant customer segment, and establish the ways in which the business model will provide that value (Chesbrough and Rosenbloom, 2002; Morris et al., 2005; Shafer et al., 2005). However, this particular view of value creation contains some shortcomings in that it focuses largely on value created for the customer only. Indeed, it has been argued that it would be remiss to believe that the customer is the sole target of value creation as there are many other potential targets for value creation, e.g. employees, shareholders, and the wider society (Lepak et al., 2007). In a similar argument, Moran and Ghoshal (1996) propose that ‘‘the more value a firm creates, the more likely it is to benefit from some of that value in the form of appropriable, if transient, rents. And what the innovator does not get in rents, society gets in progress’’ (p. 465). Value capture or value appropriation is also viewed as another important element of a business model and refers to the ability of a firm to capture value from its value creation in order to sustain the business model (West, 2007). Some of the key steps involved in this process include: defining a revenue model: ensuring the cost structure is consistent with the customer's perception of value (Amit and Zott., 2001) and establishing resilient external relationships for the firm with customers and third parties (West, 2007; Chesbrough and Rosenbloom, 2002). However, strategy research has paid less attention to value creation than to value appropriation/capture (Nickerson et al., 2007). As a result, the task of examining how firms create and capture value with OSS is made more difficult. As Chesbrough and Appleyard (2007) suggest, the very concept of OSS requires firms to rethink their strategy. In particular, the shift of focus from ownership to one of openness requires a reconsideration of the processes that facilitate value creation and value capture. In addition, it has been argued that while OSS shares the focus on value creation throughout an industry value chain, its proponents usually downplay the importance of value capture (Chesbrough et al., 2006).

The emergence of OSS poses a puzzle for conceptions of value creation in organisational theory. It has been argued that the notion of value creation and capture necessitates consideration at the level of the business model (Chesbrough and Appleyard, 2007). Alternative approaches to studying value creation and capture include neoclassical theory (Barnard, 1968), industrial economics (Bain, 1950, 1956), value chain analysis (Porter, 1985), transaction cost economics (Williamson, 1981), dynamic capabilities (Teece et al., 1997), the knowledge-based view of the firm (Grant, 1997; Kapler, 2007), Schumpeterian innovation (Schumpeter, 1934) and the resource-based view of the firm (Barney, 1991). However, most of these theories focus largely on value capture in the form of monopoly rents (Pitelis, 2002) with the objective being one of ‘‘gaining and maintaining market power to appropriate as much of the value that accrues from these economic rent sustaining barriers as possible’’ (Moran and Ghoshal, 1996, p. 464). More importantly, such approaches to organisational the ory are based upon ownership and control as the key levers in achieving strategic success. Consequently, most theories focus on those resources/capabilities housed within the firm, and by doing so take little notice of the potential value of external resources or capabilities that are not owned by the firm in question (Chesbrough and Appleyard, 2007). They, thus, do not prove to be useful lenses for examining the open peer produced nature of value creation and capture associated with strategic open source.

Firms need a business model to profit from innovation as a successful business model may offer more value to the customer or completely replace the old way of doing things (Teece, 2009; Chesbrough, 2003; Magretta, 2002). According to Osterwalder and Pigneur (2010) a business model can be defined as “a description of the value a company offers to one or several segments of customers and the architecture of the firm and its network of partners for creating, marketing and delivering this value and relationship capital, in order to generate profitable and sustainable revenue streams’’. Findings from studies carried out by West and Gallagher (2006) and West (2007) suggest that companies exploring new or disruptive technologies (such as strategic open source) must often identify a new or adapted business model. However, these findings also reveal that the use of open source generally begins in ways (i.e. operational open source) that do not disrupt a firm’s fundamental business model, or comes at a time when their existing business model is so threatened that they are forced to make radical changes. On the whole, however, comprehension of issues surrounding the impact of open source on business models appears inadequate. While researchers such as Rajala and Westerlund (2008), Onetti and Capobianco (2005), Krishnamurthy (2005), Koenig (2004) and Hecker (2000) have undertaken much research to refine and elaborate business models for OSS, revenue generation has been the main focus of this research; neglecting other aspects of the business model such as the value component.

Open innovation explicitly incorporates the business model as the source of both value creation and value capture. In West's (2007) study of value creation in OSS business models of IT vendor firms, business buvers were identified as the relevant customer segment, and lower costs and avoidance of vendor lock-in were identified as the key value propositions. In addition, as business buyers expect a richer ‘‘whole product’’ solution including integration, customization, support, and other services, OSS vendors had the opportunity to combine priced and unpriced complementary assets to create value (West, 2007). In relation to value capture, West (2007) found that for firms utilizing OSS, ‘‘the business model depends on selling complementary goods or services to capture value, or leveraging tacit knowledge or other intangible sources of

## Research objective and method

The objective of this study is to explore value creation and capture with strategic open source. Due to the relatively novel phenomena being examined and the scarcity of empirical work in the area, the study was categorised as exploratory. In such circumstances, Marshall and Rossman (1989) suggest that either a case study or field study research methodology can be used. The researchers decided that a field study would be appropriate as it would facilitate the collection of rich data from a larger number of organisations and would form the basis for more focused research at a later stage. Field studies are fieldoriented, cross-sectional case studies that focus on gathering qualitative, anecdotal observations (McGrath, 1979) in order to measure dependent variables without any attempt to control independent variables (Buckley et al., 1976).

Purposeful selection was used for site selection and twenty potential study sites were initially contacted. Preliminary analysis revealed that the approach to open source was more operational than strategic in nine of the companies, so they were eliminated from further consideration. The choice of study sites was based on the company’s history of engagement with OSS. For example, we opted for focal firms engaged in activities such as embedding OSS in products/services, or in suites of applications, those that sourced the software from communities, and also those that were actively involved in OSS communities. Firms that simply used OSS products for cost saving purposes were omitted from the study. Two decisive factors for selecting interviewees were set. First, the respondents should occupy roles that make them knowledgeable about the issues being researched; and second, most of the interviewees should hold a managerial position in the company and preferably be involved in IT strategy. Data collection was carried out using semi-structured interviewing based on a common protocol in 11 companies (see Table 1). The interviews began with some general open-ended questions around the company’s usage of OSS in addition to questions about the company and interviewee. Subsequently, interviewees were asked more specific and direct questions such as (i) what value the company see themselves as creating for the customer with OSS, (ii) how the company benefits from its customer-facing value creation activities and (iii) what external stakeholders, if any, are involved in creating/capturing value. The researchers also utilised the building blocks from the business model ontology by Osterwalder and Pigneur (2010) to further probe study participants on aspects pertinent to the study. These included the value proposition offered to customers, the target customer segment, the core competencies necessary to deliver value, and financial aspects such as the revenue model.

Interviews were carried out in person or by telephone and were recorded. Interviews lasted between 45 min and 2 h. When required, follow up questions were asked by phone or email. A copy of the transcript was sent to each of the interviewees for verification. Interviews were complemented by a review of public documents including firms’ websites, white papers, and brochures.

Content analysis was undertaken using coding techniques proposed by Strauss and Corbin (1990). This approach recognises that social phenomena are complex, and seeks to develop theory systematically in an intimate relationship with the data. This form of analysis facilitates the development of substantive theory without prior hypotheses (Baskerville and Pres-Heje, 2001) and can be utilised in the absence of, or in conjunction with, existing theory (Strauss and Corbin, 1990). In the initial phase, ‘open coding’ was used to determine the main ideas in each transcript. The open coding phase was informed by the literature surrounding the value of open source. These ideas were then grouped by significant headings to reveal categories and sub-categories. The next step involved ‘axial coding’, which is the process of relating categories to their sub-categories. As a list of codes began to emerge, the analysis moved to a higher or more abstract level; looking for a relationship between the codes. Once a relationship had been determined, the focus returned to the data to question the validity of these relationships. ## Analysis

Value creation with OSS

The study revealed that OSS was seen to have particular characteristics that facilitated value creation for the relevant companies and their customers (see Table 2). It is evident from this table that the value creation benefits of OSS are evident beyond cost savings that result from the free availability of software. It was found that OSS has presented the majority of the companies with many significant and unique opportunities for value creation. For example, the low cost of OSS lowers entry barriers and thus there is no market exclusivity. Open source software also facilitates more investment in employee development, while the escape from vendor lock-in drives down exit costs. Additionally, the quality of OSS has allowed most of the companies to meet and exceed customer expectations in terms of the product offering, delivery and the provision of complementary services. Embracing open source has also resulted in improved internal efficiencies, faster time-to-market, improved customer satisfaction, as well as encouraging collaborative behaviour and openness in all of the organisations, e.g. information and knowledge sharing with communities and customers, as well as joint ventures with external innovators. By networking with various complementors such as communities, customers and other firms, it was found that the majority of the companies gain access to unique competences and resources of others, thus enabling further value creation to their customers. For example, over half of the interviewees mentioned that participation in networks means they engage in co-development activities, and attract complements and improvements to their existing technologies, which in turn facilitate superior value creation in the form of improved product offerings to customers and faster time to market. The Open Source Community Expert at AV Networks explained that they have a lot of customer demand for reliable Linux products and therefore, it is imperative for the company to work with other partners to facilitate this. Nonetheless, for many of the companies in the study, the value creation opportunities that arise from the peer produced open nature of OSS are either emergent, rather than envisaged as part of the original OSS adoption decision, or part of a radical transformation of the business model of particular companies.

Prior to implementing OSS at Avison, booming sales meant their proprietary IT systems were overloaded, with customer service suffering as a result. Microsoft, at the time, were bringing out a volume licensing programme which meant that the company was starting to feel the push towards being moved on to this upgrade path. As the Technical Director explained ‘‘we were looking really at quite a significant budget, something in the region of about £15,000. This was quite an expense but we were also struggling with scalability and reliability issues and they were probably contracting more value from the business than just facing the licensing costs’’. This finding tends to illustrate the research of West and Gallagher (2006) who found that the use of OSS by vertically integrated producers comes at a time when their existing business strategy is so threatened that they are forced to make radical changes and admit they can no longer ‘do it alone’. This finding is in line with Chesbrough’s (2006) suggestion that one business model that makes good business sense is to be very open with technologies that are not inputs to the core activities of the firm but still are complementary. As this Technical Specialist revealed, ‘‘they (the customers) wanted this product, they wanted to be able to have Linux running on the machine’’. However, it can also be suggested that the use of OSS by this firm began in such a way that would not interfere with their core business model, thus supporting similar findings by West and Gallagher (2006). This Technical Specialist further explained that the Linux kit was not really made as a significant revenue source for the firm but rather, ‘‘it was made with a sort of business plan that we would break even on it. What it would do is give people a chance to get familiar with our platform. . . So it was more of an intellectual game than a financial one’’. This manager further revealed that the value creation in utilising OSS was ‘‘the fact that we can do some of the research using existing OSS tools, create some of the products based on OSS systems and run our support sites using OSS is an extra saving that we would lose otherwise’’ (Technical Specialist, CEES). By leveraging external peer production, the company could better afford an in-house team of website developers who were able to create the custom development that the company needed to increase the value of their services to developers. Therefore the quality of the product was better, it was produced in less time, and required fewer resources than it would otherwise.

According to West (2007) customers expect a richer whole product solution, e.g. integration, customization and support. One mechanism for creating some value is the use of complementary assets (Teece, 1986). The Technical Specialist at CEES pointed out that the lower costs associated with OSS facilitated the creation of an infrastructure that encourages participation and collaboration (i.e. peer production) between the company and their customers. The company provides support websites (all running on OSS) that give support to their customers for their various products. Similarly, the R&D Manager at Compu explained that initially value creation for the customer “was really a question of cost. Not so much the cost of licenses but the cost of the whole package, service and software and so on". However he revealed that after a few vears, customers became pleased with the flexibility they had to modify everything with little overhead (i.e. leverage open peer production benefits). According to the Head of Software and Technology at RNC, there are two major efforts where the company has embraced OSS. One concerns the telecommunications infrastructure and products where Linux is utilised as one of the primary platforms and the other is Linux embedded applications. The Head of Software and Technology in this company pointed out that one of the main reasons for embracing OSS was its peer produced nature as the company favoured components that were developed by active communities and used by many. When selecting open source components at the beginning of their product creation, the communities developing the components are analysed in addition to assessing the quality and maturity of the software component. This company integrates the knowledge and ideas of an active and vibrant community with their own internal knowledge to ensure that the selected components develop at a faster rate. Thus, as well as leveraging the benefits of peer production, an outside-in innovation process as suggested by Gassmann and Enkel (2004) is evident here. Incorporating this external knowledge involved face-to-face meetings, email discussions, participation in community mailing lists and other forums.

The analysis also revealed that several of the companies, i.e. the RNC, Compu, CEES, AV Networks and Avison have adopted elements of a coupled innovation process (i.e. a combination of an outside-in and inside-out processes), which has been a key success factor in creating value with OSS. However, the governance mechanisms that promote collaborative innovation and knowledge-sharing as part of a coupled innovation process are rather formal in that they involve contractual agreements with legal departments, steering committees and boards of directors that oversee firm participation in OSS pro jects and networks. The Head of Software and Technology at RNC acknowledged the collaborative development of OSS infrastructures; explaining that RNC ‘‘and other companies have done quite a lot in trying to make joint ventures or joint exercises around open source. . . we made a joint effort to enhance Linux with some features that are important for the telecom infrastructure’’. This manager also explained that the company finds it beneficial to use OSS in some of their consumer products because, in addition to saving time and money, it allows the company to shape their products according to market needs. The use of OSS in their product creates value for customers as it offers them upgraded software, enhanced features, as well as improved performance. For some of its products, RNC releases the code to the open source community, which enables both knowledge sharing and collaboration, in addition to creating value that is seen as beneficial to the community. Additionally, CEES had built up quite a significant customer base of Linux users for one of their games and in this way benefitted from extensive suggestions and feedback that not only increased the value of their existing product, but resulted in trans parent and detailed roadmaps for future product innovation. ## Value capture with OSS

Various attributes of OSS also enabled the companies to capture value from their value-creating activities (see Table 4). For several of the companies, much of the value captured had a direct impact on their revenue streams. However, the majority of the respondents referred to non-financial benefits captured with OSS such as access to code, knowledge and human resources outside the firm. Access to knowledge and ideas in various networks enable value capture as innovation and organisational learning improve as a result. For example, the majority of interviewees agreed that such networks have been extremely beneficial in terms of the high levels of knowledge being exchanged amongst all participants, as well as the open and transparent process that facilitates interaction. The view of the R&D Manager at Compu is that OSS makes it easier to cooperate with others, as there are no secrets to be guarded. As a result. knowledge can be exchanged freely: thereby increasing innovation and learning. Similar to the analysis of characteristics that facilitate value creation, it is also evident that leveraging the peer-produced and open nature of OSS for value capture tended to emerge post adoption rather than being envisaged as part of the original adoption decision. Most of those interviewed believed that OSS encourages more innovation in their respective companies, with several of them contending that OSS had taken them in directions they would not have initially imagined. ## Discussion and conclusion

In this paper we have used the terms ‘operational open source’ and ‘strategic open source’ to differentiate between adopting open source software as a low cost alternative to proprietary software (operational), and the use of open source software and processes in a manner that leverages both the peer-produced and open innovation qualities of such software and processes in order to create business value (strategic). We have demonstrated that strategic open source embraces networkbased activities for successful value creation and capture. For example, powerful value creation and capture drivers can be found in the open innovation activities that take place in communities, as well as with customers, and third-party complementors in value networks. Strategic open source also helps create and capture value through the promotion of open business models. With an open business model firms engage in co-development activities, as well as utilize, and build upon, complements and improvements that provide a ‘whole product’ solution for customers. These activities alone provide a solid basis for innovation and commercialisation. The transition to open business models in networks enables firms to continue innovating their core technologies whilst adding value to other technologies through co-operation with external parties. As a result, they can capture value in the form of profits and new customers. Moreover, the governance process for strategic open source is very much a top town process. Involvement in open innovation activities in value networks can involve contractual agreements with legal departments, steering committees and boards of directors that administer firm participation. However, it was obvi ous from the study that the use of open source by several of the firms either began in a way that would not disrupt their core business model or came at a time when their existing business model was so under threat, they had to make radical changes. Nevertheless, it was apparent that open source software provides new paths for firms in their quest for value creation and capture that goes beyond just leveraging the free nature of the software. This has allowed us to further differentiate between the value of operational and strategic approaches to leveraging open source software, as shown in Table 6.

As Table 6 reveals, strategic open source value creation is centred on opening up internal innovation processes, enhancing product development, improving internal efficiencies, promoting open business models and acquiring new customers. Moving to an outside-in process has proved successful for many of those studied from the perspective of value creation and capture. For example, beyond just saving time and money, non-financial gains such as the knowledge and ideas acquired externally proved extremely beneficial in terms of value capture for the companies in this study. The notion of non-monetary benefits is important for strategic OSS, as money is evidently not the prime motivator for formulating and implementing an outside-in innovation strategy. Instead, companies were fuelled by non-monetary value such as access to high quality software, superior technical knowledge and human resources outside the firm. Thus, while participants in this study referred to the value captured with OSS as intangible (e.g. knowledge acquisition, and skills) not easily reflected in company financial statements or balance statements, these were still viewed as significant in maximizing value and accomplishing business goals. The difficulty with quantifying returns or measuring intangibles such as external knowledge capture may perhaps explain why OSS proponents downplay the importance of value capture. Nonetheless, the work complements the literature around OSS business models (e.g. Rajala et al., 2006; Krishnamurthy, 2005; Koenig, 2004; Weber, 2004; and Hecker, 2000) in that it provides a better understanding of value creation and capture.

Given the openness of the network, the ability to innovate collectively and the ability to keep costs to a minimum, participants have the potential to present a very attractive value proposition to the market. The study demonstrates the importance of a value network for effective value creation and capture, and therefore is of interest to both research and practice.

## Implications for practice

In developing business models around OSS, practitioners need to be aware that access to a network of complementors is imperative for value creation and capture. For many organisations, the OSS community as a network is only incidental to the availability of the software. However, with strategic open source, the network is the ‘key’ building block that enables both value creation and capture. Managers also need to recognise that OSS business models are also driven by intangibles such as knowledge assets that may serve as an indirect revenue base. This has implications for organisations in that they need to put structures and incentives in place to convert intangible asset capture (e.g. the knowledge grounded in the expertise and experience of individual network members) into something tangible for the firm (e.g. value-added products or services that not only provide value to their customers but eventually contribute to the organisation's future cash-flows) To gain access to networks of complementors and form new collaboration models around OSS, practitioners need to encourage employees to both actively seek new ideas/knowledge outside the firm and cultivate external relationships. Additionally, practitioners should promote and encourage employee participation in open source communities, research projects and consortia. To stimulate this type of interaction, senior managers should introduce incentives that reward employees for establishing these external linkages/relationships and incorporating ideas/knowledge that leverage value creation/capture for the firm.

There is also need for practitioners to re-examine the governance processes that currently regulate network participation. We found that the majority of firms in the study have formal mechanisms and procedures that govern involvement in OSS communities. Nonetheless, managers need to recognise the opportunities possible for value creation and capture that arise from their employees’ embeddedness in the informal networks that arise from involvement in OSS communities. Firms may wish to consider investing time and resources in developing these informal networks, and their associated social/informal processes, rather than just relying on formal mechanisms.

## Implications for research

While this study explores value creation and capture with strategic open source, others will need to build theory and subsequently test it. The importance of the network raises questions that need to be addressed in future research. In particular, what are the prerequisites for successful firm participation in OSS networks? How do firms choose which stakeholders to collaborate with in the network? The ability to capture and exploit external knowledge is a critical component of strategic open source. Thus, firms need to (1) develop the absorptive capacity to make use of this external knowledge and (2) measure this absorptive capacity to better explain the effect on value capture. Furthermore, the effectiveness of participation in a network may be dependent on a firm’s knowledge transfer capabilities as well as the motivation of external stakeholders to engage and cooperate. This leads to questions regarding how networks are organised and sustained. Thus, it is crucial that future research seeks to understand the nature of effective governance in open source networks.

The findings reported here are based on a small purposeful sample of firms and, as such, are not generalizable. We also acknowledge that the study did not take into consideration variables such as organisational size, structure, or culture. Thus future research is needed to address these limitations. While the field study proved sufficiently rich in detail, the unit of analysis in the context of this study was the firm. Given that the network emerged as an important enabler of value creation and capture, future research should take the network as the appropriate unit of analysis rather than the firm. This way, other researchers could build upon the work presented to gain a better understanding of how multiple stakeholders in the network create and capture value with OSS.
