## 1. Introduction

Generally as part of an open innovation strategy, <sup>fi</sup>rms are increasingly treating open source software (OSS) communities as a complementary asset to be leveraged and combined with the <sup>fi</sup>rm's own internal assets to deliver competitive solutions (Dahlander & Wallin, 2006). For example, Nokia cooperated with an open source software (OSS) community of more than 450 volunteer developers to create mapping software, games, GPS and other applications for the company's N800 Internet Tablet device (Wallin & Von Krogh, 2010). IBM has also enjoyed considerable success through its participation in the Apache project (Capek, Frank, Gerdt, & Shields, 2005; Fielding, 1999; West & Gallagher, 2006). In both instances, the <sup>fi</sup>rms were able to turn community outputs into new service offerings. Argued to be the “best of both worlds” for innovation and knowledge creation, OSS communities are a form of private-collective communities and as such are characterized by two logics: 1) the private model focusing on the distribution of returns and the delegation of value creation solely to an organization and 2) the collective model focusing on the openness and free distribution of intellectual ideas for the common or public good (Von Hippel & Von Krogh, 2003, Von Krogh & Von Hippel, 2006).

Realizing the innovation potential of these communities, <sup>fi</sup>rms now create or sponsor their own OSS communities (Dahlander & Magnusson, 2005; West & Gallagher, 2006; West & O'Mahony, 2008). However, these firm-sponsored communities differ from their autonomous counterparts in that one or more firms control the community's short and long-term activities (O'Mahony, 2007; West & O'Mahony, 2008). Thus, the potential for goal con<sup>fl</sup>ict between the <sup>fi</sup>rm and the community is greater than with autonomous communities (West & O'Mahony, 2008). Although both the <sup>fi</sup>rm and the community seek the widespread adoption of the software they are developing, the <sup>fi</sup>rm's primary goal is to pro<sup>fi</sup>t from its investments while the community's goal is to improve the capabilities of the shared technology (West & O'Mahony, 2008).

Initial research suggests that due to this potential goal con<sup>fl</sup>ict, <sup>fi</sup>rm-sponsored communities generally tend to have a lower ability to challenge and transform actions in a goal-driven context (i.e., generative capacity (van Osch & Avital, 2010)) than autonomous ones (Jarvenpaa & Lang, 2011; West & O'Mahony, 2008). In contrast to traditional forms of organizing, OSS communities are characterized by highly permeable and dynamic boundaries across which ideas, resources, and individuals volunteering their time <sup>fl</sup>ow in and out (Dahlander & Wallin, 2006; Jarvenpaa & Lang, 2011; Laursen & Salter, 2006). This organizational form contrasts with traditional firms for which boundaries are relatively stable, demarcating what is inside and what is outside (Jarvenpaa & Lang, 2011; West & O'Mahony, 2008). As a result, <sup>fi</sup>rm management cannot rely on traditional hierarchical authority to control community members (West & O'Mahony, 2008), yet the successful boundary management of a <sup>fi</sup>rm-sponsored community is necessary to maximize the community's innovation capacity (Dahlander & Magnusson, 2005; Jarvenpaa & Lang, 2011), or the process of continuous improvement of resources and capabilities to meet market needs (Szeto, 2000).

Furthermore, for a <sup>fi</sup>rm to reap the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of its <sup>fi</sup>rm-sponsored community, it must have an appropriate level of absorptive capacity, or “the ability of a <sup>fi</sup>rm to recognize the value of new, external information, assimilate it, and apply it to commercial ends” (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990: 128). Without this absorptive capacity, the assimilation of new innovations from outside will be costly to the <sup>fi</sup>rm (Di Gangi & Wasko, 2009; Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995). Therefore, a <sup>fi</sup>rm's efforts to in<sup>fl</sup>uence its sponsored community's innovation capacity will also have rami<sup>fi</sup>cations for the <sup>fi</sup>rm's ability to effectively leverage the knowledge resulting from the sponsored community's software development process (Fosfuri, Giarratana, & Luzzi, 2008; Stam, 2009). For example, high levels of innovation capacity within the community may outpace the <sup>fi</sup>rm's absorptive capacity and result in a waste of resources expended by the <sup>fi</sup>rm on the community (Di Gangi & Wasko, 2009). The OSS community, as a complementary asset, must be properly managed not only to in<sup>fl</sup>uence its innovation capacity but also to ensure that the fruits of these efforts can be effectively leveraged and combined with the <sup>fi</sup>rm's own assets to deliver competitive solutions.

The objective of this paper is thus to investigate how the boundary management of a <sup>fi</sup>rm-sponsored OSS community impacts the community's innovation capacity as well as the <sup>fi</sup>rm's absorptive capacity.

The current study presents eZ Systems (eZ) and its <sup>fi</sup>rm-sponsored OSS community (eZ Publish) that eZ has successfully sustained since 1999. Through our analyses of data collected through questionnaires, interviews, and archival data, we investigate two research questions: 1) How does the boundary management of a firm-sponsored OSS community impact the community's innovation capacity? and 2) How does the boundary management of a firm-sponsored OSS community impact the firm's absorptive capacity? Our analysis offers insight into the role of boundary management of <sup>fi</sup>rm-sponsored communities as it shows the importance of managing boundary logics that facilitate the development of both the community's innovation capacity and the <sup>fi</sup>rm's absorptive capacity. Our results also highlight the underlying and central importance of an integrative IT platform to act as a non-intrusive participant to facilitate boundary management activities as well as to enhance the <sup>fi</sup>rm's absorptive capacity.

In the next section we provide an overview of the relevant research on boundary management and absorptive capacity in the context of OSS communities. This is followed by a description of our research methodology. We then present a case study of eZ and the eZ Publish community followed by an analysis of the data collected in our study. After a discussion of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings and their implications, we conclude with limitations and suggestions for future research.

## 2. Boundary management

Firm sponsors of OSS communities face a fundamental tension. On the one hand, the sponsoring <sup>fi</sup>rm has to offer opportunities for outside participation to gain the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of contributions and growth from sponsoring an external community while on the other hand, the <sup>fi</sup>rm desires to retain control over the technologies fundamental to its business success (West & O'Mahony, 2008). Moreover, individuals and organizations attracted by the <sup>fi</sup>rm to participate in and contribute to the community are wholly or partially autonomous individuals and organizations (Markus, 2007), and there is an inherent tension between ful<sup>fi</sup>lling their numerous divergent individual goals and integrating them with a common objective (March & Simon, 1958; O'Mahony & Ferraro, 2007; Ouchi, 1980). Unlike individuals under outsourcing agreements, members of OSS communities cannot be governed by the traditional means of hierarchical reinforcements like employment or contracts. Indeed, managing a community has been likened to herding cats in that developers do things out of self-interest and when the <sup>fi</sup>rm attempts to exercise direct control over them, they scatter (Dahlander & Magnusson, 2008).

Online community research discusses boundary management as a means to in<sup>fl</sup>uence a <sup>fi</sup>rm's sponsored community (Jarvenpaa & Lang, 2011). Boundary management is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the set of activities involved in de<sup>fi</sup>ning, negotiating, and protecting organizational resources and domains of action as well as managing external stakeholder relationships to achieve organizational goals (Ancona & Caldwell, 1990; Burger-Helmchen & Cohendet, 2011; Reagans & Zuckerman, 2001). Boundaries both enable the community to grow, but they also protect the community from external threats (Jarvenpaa & Lang, 2011). In the case of <sup>fi</sup>rm-sponsored communities, the objective of boundary management is to balance the tensions within the inherent trade-offs: 1) between openness (attracting external participation, stimulating innovation, creativity and organizational growth) and control (over platform activities, content production, and intellectual property right appropriation) or 2) between standardization of production processes and the availability of diverse resources (Engeström 2007; Jarvenpaa & Lang, 2011; Tiwana, Konsynski, & Bush, 2010; West & O'Mahony, 2008). In a study of one autonomous and one sponsored music remixing community, four boundary management logics have been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed: power, identity, competence, and transactional ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency (Jarvenpaa & Lang, 2011). We present these four logics below and discuss them within the context of <sup>fi</sup>rm-sponsored OSS communities.

## 2.1. Boundary of power

Power is an instrumental force in the development of a community's innovation capacity and in the coordination of tasks to ensure the useful production of resources (Jarvenpaa & Lang, 2011; van Osch & Avital, 2010). Power concerns the exerting of control over key resources, determining the appropriate domain of activity and in<sup>fl</sup>uence in the community, and managing relationships with external groups (Jarvenpaa & Lang, 2011; Murray & O'Mahony, 2007; O'Mahony & Bechky, 2008).

When designing OSS communities, research has found three areas related to power that <sup>fi</sup>rms must consider: 1) organization of production: the degree to which community members can read code, observe the production process, and change the code directly; 2) intellectual property rights: the degree to which community members can use and access the source code as well as reuse and recombine in the creation of derivative code, and 3) community governance: the degree to which community members can observe and participate in the community's governance (West & O'Mahony, 2008). A <sup>fi</sup>nal aspect of power is its dynamicity, that is, the degree to which the boundaries change over time in response to internal and external changes (Jarvenpaa & Lang, 2011).

## 2.2. Boundary of identity

The boundary of identity concerns the degree to which the <sup>fi</sup>rm enables and supports the development of a shared identity within the community. As members develop a sense of shared identity, they share common norms and values, and even obligations and expectations related to pursuing the community's goals (Jarvenpaa & Lang, 2011; Ren, Kraut, & Kiesler, 2007). A shared identity facilitates the development of trust and the development of bonds with others in the community leading to a willingness to share, contribute, and help community members. OSS developers already have a certain degree of shared identity as the OSS movement carries certain values and norms related to the use of the code (O'Mahony, 2002, 2003), and to become an OSS developer means to acquire an identity related to a social practice (Von Krogh, Hae<sup>fl</sup>iger, Spaeth, & Wallin, 2012). OSS norms and values serve as the basis for social mechanisms governing exchanges in OSS networks, or collaboration networks of <sup>fi</sup>rms, who share goals and norms related to servicing customer software needs based on open source solutions (Feller, Finnegan, Lundell, & Nilsson, 2008). Identi<sup>fi</sup>cation with the social practice instills a sense of moral obligation to support and reinforce OSS norms and practices to ensure sustainability of the OSS movement (Von Krogh et al., 2012). Firm-sponsored OSS communities may have more pronounced identity-building mechanisms than autonomous communities as members identify themselves as active developers and users of the <sup>fi</sup>rm's software through their participation. However, community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation has been found to be distinguishable from identi<sup>fi</sup>cation with the <sup>fi</sup>rm (Nambisan & Baron, 2007).

## 2.3. Boundary of competence

The third boundary area relates to the development of community member competence. In OSS communities, the main driver for user contributions is the ability of members to develop and master technical and leadership skills and knowledge (Fleming & Waguespack, 2007; Hertel, Niedner, & Herrmann, 2003; Jarvenpaa & Lang, 2011; Jeppesen & Frederiksen, 2006; Shah, 2006; von Krogh, Spaeth, & Lakhani, 2003). The motivation of community members to contribute arises from both intrinsic motivations: to explore, learn and improve their capabilities, and by extrinsic motivations: to build reputation and advance their career opportunities (Jarvenpaa & Lang, 2011). Firm-sponsored communities may encourage competence development through continuously offering complex and challenging development tasks as well as mechanisms for developers to create and maintain their reputations (Dahlander & Magnusson, 2005).

## 2.4. Boundary of transactional efficiency

The fourth area of boundary management is transactional ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, which is fundamental to the development of economically ef<sup>fi</sup>cient social production models of communities (Benkler, 2002, 2005; Butler, 2001). Firms can provide communication and computational resources necessary for the community to more ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently interact and develop the software individually or jointly (Jarvenpaa & Lang, 2011). User interfaces, social media and other tools for online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine communication among members, bug reporting systems, and documentation are among the tools that OSS <sup>fi</sup>rms may use to manage transactional ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency.

## 2.5. Integrative boundary management

Boundaries cannot be investigated in isolation as it is their integrative management that in<sup>fl</sup>uences the community's innovation capacity (Jarvenpaa & Lang, 2011). Jarvenpaa and Lang's (2011) study of two music communities illustrate that the boundary logics interact in different ways to positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence the community's generative capacity over time. For example, while the power boundary restricted the generative capacity of the <sup>fi</sup>rm-sponsored community through hierarchical control, it also enhanced it as it reinforced the identity boundary by focusing on collaborative activities around the <sup>fi</sup>rm's brand and ensuring consistency with the community's goals. However, the autonomous community through its management of the power and identity boundaries supported creation and collaboration around highly diverse content without hierarchical control. One key <sup>fi</sup>nding of this study is that integrative boundary management is highly context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c such that even when community sites share key characteristics, such as size (in terms of active members) and domain (in this study popular music), how the boundaries are managed and their interactions create signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences across communities.

Noting the context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c nature of this phenomenon, <sup>fi</sup>rm-sponsored OSS communities are distinct from the music remixing community identi<sup>fi</sup>ed above on a number of dimensions. In particular and as noted above, <sup>fi</sup>rm-sponsored OSS communities are viewed as a complementary asset to be leveraged and combined with the <sup>fi</sup>rm's own internal assets to deliver competitive solutions (Dahlander & Wallin, 2006) and enhance the <sup>fi</sup>rm's innovation capacity. While the preceding sections examine the four conceptual boundary management logics a <sup>fi</sup>rm must manage, much less is known about how <sup>fi</sup>rms manage the four boundary logics of a sponsored OSS community and how these logics interact to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the community's innovation capacity. Thus the focus of our research is on the actual implementation and practice of boundary management in the context of <sup>fi</sup>rm-sponsored OSS communities. The above leads us to our <sup>fi</sup>rst research question:

RQ1. How does the boundary management of a firm-sponsored OSS community impact the community's innovation capacity?

## 3. Absorptive capacity

One of the primary drivers for <sup>fi</sup>rms to sponsor an OSS community is to enhance the <sup>fi</sup>rm's own innovation capacity through access to external knowledge. For the <sup>fi</sup>rm to bene<sup>fi</sup>t from this external knowledge, it must have an appropriate level of absorptive capacity. Thus, while a <sup>fi</sup>rm's participation in OSS communities facilitates access to external complementary assets and technological know-how, the community is not to be used as a substitute, rather it is to be used as a complement to the <sup>fi</sup>rm's innovation practices (Dahlander & Gann, 2010). This argument builds on Cohen and Levinthal's (1989) suggestion that R&D has a dual role: to develop new products and services internally as well as to create the absorptive capacity to track and evaluate developments outside the <sup>fi</sup>rm's boundaries (Huizingh, 2011). Firms that develop a strong internal knowledge base are better suited to generate unique con<sup>fi</sup>gurations of internal and external resources, thus supporting their capacity to generate and appropriate returns stemming from innovations (Dahlander & Wallin, 2006; Grand, Von Krogh, Leonard, & Swap, 2004; Stam, 2009). The presence of such “complementarities" (Veugelers. 1997) suggests that the value of this external collaboration is contingent upon the firm's absorptive capacity (Chesbrough & Crowther, 2006; Cohen & Levinthal, 1990; Stam, 2009; Veugelers, 1997).

As OSS communities can mobilize a volunteer workforce of thousands of individuals to participate in a <sup>fi</sup>rm's innovation activities, the risk that the level of the <sup>fi</sup>rm's absorptive capacity is exceeded is high. The <sup>fi</sup>rm has little control over the sheer volume of innovation activities in the community and is likely to have dif<sup>fi</sup>culty <sup>fi</sup>ltering out relevant, high quality knowledge (Di Gangi & Wasko, 2009). Furthermore, innovations and knowledge from the community can even potentially contradict or disrupt a <sup>fi</sup>rm's current innovation processes (Di Gangi & Wasko, 2009), implying that a <sup>fi</sup>rm must align these external innovation efforts with the <sup>fi</sup>rm's absorptive capacity.

To effectively leverage the valuable knowledge resulting from the <sup>fi</sup>rm-sponsored community's software development process, <sup>fi</sup>rms must invest considerable resources in building their own knowledge base to develop their absorptive capacity (Fosfuri et al., 2008; Stam, 2009). Sponsored communities require further signi<sup>fi</sup>cant investments – valuable resources that may be limited in the context of software development – and these efforts may even detract the firm from managing its core business activities (Stam. 20o9). The potential result is that too much focus by the <sup>fi</sup>rm on in<sup>fl</sup>uencing innovation capacity may lead to a situation in which the <sup>fi</sup>rm's own absorptive capacity is impacted and may not be suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to absorb the knowledge generated externally.

Research on the absorptive capacity of software <sup>fi</sup>rms suggests that there are three dimensions of absorptive capacity that impact internal knowledge creation: 1) the relationship to the external environment

2) a collective dimension, and 3) an individual dimension (Matusik & Heeley, 2005). The relationship with the external environment is de<sup>fi</sup>ned by the level and intensity of contact that the <sup>fi</sup>rm has with the external environment. Research investigating the means with which <sup>fi</sup>rms build relationships with OSS communities in general has noted two primary channels: technical participation and social participation in the community (Dahlander & Magnusson, 2005; Stam, 2009). Technical participation includes activities directly or indirectly related to the development of the software, such as <sup>fi</sup>rm members participating in the community discussions, contributing to developing the code, and writing documentation (Henkel, 2006; Stam, 2009). In some cases, <sup>fi</sup>rms may even devote personnel, or a “man on the inside”, to work in the community (Dahlander & Wallin, 2006). Social participation includes activities initiated by <sup>fi</sup>rms to facilitate knowledge sharing and interactions among developers, <sup>fi</sup>rms, end users, and other community members with the purpose to promote the wider acceptance and adoption of OSS (Osterloh & Rota, 2007). Examples of social participation range from organizing user workshops and conferences to participating in political and marketing campaigns intended to further the interests of the OSS movement (Stam, 2009). The face-to-face interactions between <sup>fi</sup>rm employees and community members facilitate the transfer of tacit knowledge, which is argued to be more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult in the context of computer-mediated relationships (Fowler, Lawrence, & Morse, 2004; Stam, 2009).

The collective dimension of absorptive capacity comprises two facets: 1) relevant public industry knowledge and 2) routines and structures for knowledge transfers. The <sup>fi</sup>rst facet of relevant public industry knowledge concerns the degree to which the knowledge bases are similar between the <sup>fi</sup>rm and the external knowledge source. In the case of firm-sponsored OSS communities. the two knowledge bases are more than likely to be highly similar due to the openness and common technical knowledge characterizing both the <sup>fi</sup>rm and the community. What is more relevant is the second facet of routines and structures for knowledge transfers, which is primarily of a structural nature. For internal knowledge creation to increase, routines and structures must exist to not only identify and access valuable external knowledge from the community but also to route this knowledge to the appropriate people and areas within the <sup>fi</sup>rm who may then transform and exploit it for the <sup>fi</sup>rm's bene<sup>fi</sup>t (Matusik & Heeley, 2005).

The third dimension of absorptive capacity is a function of the absorptive abilities of the individual employees within the <sup>fi</sup>rm. These abilities depend on the knowledge and skills of the related technical practices of these individuals, their common communication styles, and their shared understandings of goals (Matusik & Heeley, 2005). Previous research on <sup>fi</sup>rms that open their software development to OSS communities has found that a tension is created at the individual employee programmer level (Matusik & Heeley, 2005). In working with OSS communities, <sup>fi</sup>rm employees become exposed to OSS practices, values. and norms that are contradictory to the commercial and proprietary models of firm organizing, As a result, <sup>fi</sup>rm employees develop their own individual strategies as to how to handle this tension (Rolandsson, Bergquist, & Ljungberg, 2011).

Similar to the above discussion on boundary management, our review of the literature reveals that research on the impact of the <sup>fi</sup>rm's boundary management of its OSS community on the <sup>fi</sup>rm's own absorptive capacity is scant. Thus, our second research question is the following:

RQ2. How does the boundary management of a firm-sponsored OSS community impact the firm's absorptive capacity?

## 4. Methodology

To address our research questions, we used an abductive, thematic analysis approach using a case study of the firm. eZ Systems. and its firm-sponsored OSS community. eZ Publish. The abductive approach is appropriate to use when there is a high level of novelty in the research area and the researcher is investigating a new phenomenon and the underlying variables and their relationships (Coffey & Atkinson, 1996; Dubois & Gadde, 2002). As the phenomenon in focus – <sup>fi</sup>rm-sponsored OSS communities – has a high degree of novelty and thus still suffers a paucity of research, this approach is appropriate. The abductive approach entails starting by identifying a particular phenomenon and then relating that phenomenon to broader concepts through an iterative process between the literature and the empirical data (Coffey & Atkinson, 1996; Dubois & Gadde, 2002). A case study is appropriate when there is a need to focus on contemporary events in their natural settings (Benbasat, Goldstein, & Mead, 1987; Yin, 1994).

## 4.1. Research site

eZ was established in 1999 in Skien, Norway and has grown to the world's largest open source content management software company in terms of downloads (2.5+ million) and site installations (250,000) in more than 170 countries with customers such as NASA, the United Nations, MIT, and T-Mobile. eZ is based on the OSS philosophy of openness, sharing, and innovation and prides itself on its development of the eZ ecosystem that includes the eZ Publish community at the core of eZ's business model. While eZ Publish includes some eZ employees, the vast majority of the more than 42,000 community members are 1) formal third party agents (i.e., someone working at one of eZ's customers), 2) informal third party agents (i.e., someone working at an organization that has installed eZ but that does not have a formal customer agreement with eZ) and 3) independent agents, or private individuals such as entrepreneurs, OSS enthusiasts/hobbyists, and others who have a special interest in the eZ software.

Within the ecosystem, two versions of eZ's primary software, eZ Publish, are developed: the Community version and the Enterprise version, and as such the software is distributed under a dual-license model: GPL (General Public License) and commercial license. The Community version, under GPL, allows eZ Publish members to contribute to its development and share back with the community. This software can be downloaded for free by anyone and is primarily developed by the community and eZ engineers. Its primary bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are the possibility to freely modify the code and no cost to users who are interested in using the software for both personal and commercial purposes. However, this version carries with it no of<sup>fi</sup>cial support by eZ and the risk to its users of lower reliability. The Enterprise version originates from the Community version and is a comprehensive content management system that is developed, tested, and fully supported by eZ and its in-house engineers. This version carries a commercial license, enabling eZ customers and partners that have a contractual agreement with eZ to develop and sell tailor-made solutions in turn for their clients. Depending on the agreement, eZ provides various levels of support to these customers and partners.

Under this ecosystem model, eZ's management claims that they have the innovation power of a huge company yet without the overhead, lack of speed, and high cost of a traditional large corporation. Moreover, eZ's paying customers receive considerable bene<sup>fi</sup>ts compared to those in a pure OSS model since eZ delivers the Enterprise version with total responsibility. This makes eZ and its eZ Publish community a suitable case for investigating a <sup>fi</sup>rm-sponsored model of innovation since they combine the OSS collective model with the traditional <sup>fi</sup>rm-centric approach aiming at generating revenues through the sale of products and services.

## 4.2. Methods

We employed a literature-driven thematic analysis as prescribed by Boyatzis (1998) using data collected from semi-structured interviews, direct community observation, and archival data from the eZ Publish discussion boards and eZ website. Thematic analysis is a form of qualitative analysis in which a codebook is used to analyze raw information obtained from various methods (e.g., interview data) and to identify themes based on existing literature. Theoretical relationships can then be derived among themes based on the coding of the raw information and from observation and interpretation by the research team, and a reliability assessment is conducted to ensure the consistent application of the codebook to the raw information.

Prior research within the <sup>fi</sup>eld of information systems has detailed a general framework for the thematic analysis operationalization, which set the initial steps in this study (Gallagher, James, & Mason, 2012). In this study we applied this to the focal phenomenon under investigation, eZ and the eZ Publish community, and the impact of eZ's boundary management of its community on the community's innovation capacity and the <sup>fi</sup>rm's absorptive capacity. The following sections illustrate the key steps undertaken in the thematic analysis.

## 4.2.1. Step one: identification of interviewees

The existing literature streams relevant to our focal phenomenon were those discussing community boundary management and <sup>fi</sup>rm absorptive capacity. In this study, the relevant sample was bounded by those individuals who directly in<sup>fl</sup>uence the eZ Publish community and its innovation capacity as well as the <sup>fi</sup>rm's absorptive capacity. To identify interviewees, we collaborated with the eZ Publish community moderator and organizational representative. In total we conducted 19 semi-structured interviews, lasting 45–120 minutes each. Nineteen interviews were deemed appropriate in terms of thematic saturation as no new themes emerged from the <sup>fi</sup>nal two interviews and the summarized results of the interviews were presented to both the interviewees and eZ Publish community to solicit feedback and observation. No feedback indicated missing themes or perspectives. A copy of the interview guide is provided in Appendix 1.

Five interviewees were eZ employees with direct responsibility for developing the eZ Publish software or managing the eZ Publish community, and the remaining interviewees were af<sup>fi</sup>liated partners, entrepreneurs, or OSS hobbyists who participated in the eZ Publish community. On average, interviewees were 34.9 years old and held a minimum of an undergraduate degree (typically Computer Science). We attempted to conduct an interview with a female community member; however, the moderator indicated that eZ Publish is overwhelmingly male and no females responded to our interview requests. Interviewees represented ten distinct countries, including Brazil, Croatia, France, Germany, Italy, The Netherlands, Norway, Spain, United Kingdom, and the United States.

All interviews were recorded and transcribed to distil the comments to the rawest form while removing extraneous comments (e.g., off topic conversation) to ensure coding was conducted solely on the information pertinent to the focus of the study (Boyatzis, 1998; Gallagher et al., 2012). This distilling also provided the researchers with the ability to code small segments of the interviewee responses for multiple codes present within the same question response.

## 4.2.2. Step two: codebook development

The second step developed an initial codebook to examine the raw information obtained in terms of theme identification and refinement. As no existing codebook sufficiently addressed the themes under consideration, we developed the relevant themes and codes based on the existing literature reviewed during the development of our research questions. Two authors independently coded two interview transcripts to re<sup>fi</sup>ne the codebook to the focal phenomenon (i.e., revise language for clarity and domain speci<sup>fi</sup>city, gain appreciation for interview syntax, and identify cultural norms within the community). Once completed, a reliability score was calculated based on the percentage agreement formula where a general calculation was conducted for the total number of codes in agreement divided by the total number of codes by the primary coding author. Revisions to the codebook were made based on initial assessments and a <sup>fi</sup>nal inter-rater reliability of 94% was achieved suggesting agreement with the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed themes in the raw information as well as a viable codebook.

## 4.2.3. Step three: code application

Once inter-rater reliability was achieved, the <sup>fi</sup>rst author coded the remaining interviews with an additional author providing a random reliability check to ensure consistent codebook application. The consistency in the reliability check suggests limited bias in coder behavior and reduced the likelihood of the coder projecting perceptions of the research on to the raw information as it was coded.

## 4.2.4. Step four: results interpretation

The <sup>fi</sup>nal step identi<sup>fi</sup>es relationships among the themes and draws conclusions about the relevance of individual themes. We took both a qualitative, descriptive approach and a quantitative approach. The qualitative approach assessed inter-relationships among themes and sub-themes by providing contextual information about how the theme was interpreted and its impact on innovation capacity and absorptive capacity. Each transcript was coded using a basic count approach for presence and frequency of a theme being identi<sup>fi</sup>ed. This approach enables a descriptive quantitative analysis of the coded data and the derivation of observational <sup>fi</sup>ndings concerning the strength and/or presence of themes within the focal phenomenon.

## 4.2.5. Step five: secondary validation via data triangulation

In addition, we analyzed data gathered through secondary resources, e.g., company documents, forums, an eZ LinkedIn group, and Twitter feeds of employees and community members, to verify our analysis.

## 5.2. Community boundary management logics & community innovation capacity

Below we <sup>fi</sup>rst discuss eZ's boundary management before looking at the impact of eZ's boundary management on innovation capacity.

## 5.2.1. Community boundary management logics

All interviewees were in agreement that the primary goal of the eZ Publish community is to provide additional innovation capacity for eZ and to generate a mutually valuable software application for both personal and shared interests. Consistent with prior research, we found that it is the interactions among the boundary management logics that greatly in<sup>fl</sup>uence the community's innovation capacity. As noted above, our frequency analysis indicates power to be the primary boundary management logic at play within the eZ Publish community, primarily since it had the highest degree of interaction with the other logics.

Due to its overarching goal, eZ continuously strived to balance its control over the community with openness towards the community to allow for software innovation. Originally only one version of the software existed, which was freely available for download and development by the eZ Publish community. Community members had the ability to suggest and develop extensions and to write code to the kernel. However, as eZ struggled with developing a pro<sup>fi</sup>table business model as an open source company, it came to the realization that this arrangement did not create suf<sup>fi</sup>cient value for its shareholders nor for its highly demanding customers paying for support from eZ. Thus, in 2011 eZ took one of its most strategic decisions since the company's inception — to split its primary software into two versions: the Enterprise and the Community version.

In terms of intellectual property and the organization of production of the Community version under the new setup, community members were still free to access and use the source code, to create derivative code, and to further develop the source code under the GPL. However, when it came to the Enterprise version, this software was to be richer in features and more stable than the Community version such that eZ could quickly respond to requests for <sup>fi</sup>xes. The Enterprise version was available only to eZ's paying customers and not to the eZ Publish community in general. As a result, eZ employees were primarily responsible for the development of the Enterprise version. Although community members were encouraged by eZ to contribute to the Enterprise version, they had to comply with eZ's Roadmap and Guidelines for contributing, which had been fully determined by eZ and its employees. eZ then reserved the right to adopt or deny any modi<sup>fi</sup>cations suggested by the Community to its Enterprise version.

By creating two versions of the software, eZ balanced the community's desire to innovate with eZ's commercial interests to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll the demands for robustness by the <sup>fi</sup>rm's paying customers. One eZ interviewee commented, “We are continuously working on how we can keep up our innovation pace at the same time that we have enterprise robustness…if it is not so business critical, then you can be on the bleeding edge and … contribute through the Community version, but if you are an organization and this i business critical, then you have the Enterprise version.”

The decision to create two software versions and other decisions made by eZ regarding the future direction of the software uncovered in our interviews led to considerable con<sup>fl</sup>ict between eZ management and the community on several occasions, con<sup>fl</sup>icts which were characterized as “<sup>fi</sup>erce discussions”. This con<sup>fl</sup>ict was the result of a high degree of engagement with the software stemming from a high level of identi<sup>fi</sup>cation by community members with the eZ Publish community, and identity was the third most discussed logic in our interviews. eZ manages the identity boundary through a number of face-to-face events and internet-based solutions designed to encourage interaction and the development of shared values, language, and trust within the community. The face-to-face events comprised several global annual conferences as well as numerous regional meetings bringing together community members for not only professional activities but also social events to promote relationship building and strengthening of community norms. Regarding internet-based solutions to promote identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, the community moderator introduced the use of multiple social media technologies, e.g., Twitter, to foster a sense of openness within the community as well as to encourage collaboration among eZ Publish community members.

While these identity management efforts served to build the identity of members with the eZ Publish community, these efforts simultaneously led to con<sup>fl</sup>ict when eZ exercised its power. These identity efforts reinforced the community's OSS principles of open and free access, yet they did little to create an understanding of eZ and its underlying pro<sup>fi</sup>t-making goals. Thus, eZ's decision to create the commercial Enterprise version of the software con<sup>fl</sup>icted with eZ Publish community members' identity as being part of an open source community. One eZ employee commented, “As a community… if you give them the little <sup>fi</sup>nger, they take the whole hand — they want more when you give them something, and if they don't get something, then they get irritated. We are a company and not a charity, so we have to make money — but this is a challenge and you have to differentiate between what you give away for free and what you sell.”

To resolve con<sup>fl</sup>ict and better manage the eZ Publish community, eZ implemented a Community Project Board in 2010, further indicating a dynamicity of the power boundary management. This new governance mechanism was a co-created, shared ownership model for the community and the Community version of the software, and it comprised three eZ employees, of which one was the community moderator, and three community members. The Community Project Board had the authority to make development decisions for the Community version's kernel, effectively bridging the gap between the eZ Publish community and eZ and its Enterprise version. One eZ employee explained, “Because on the one hand we have these very structured ways at eZ Systems, with strong quality constraints, and on the other hand, a quite unstructured community… the Board is the mandatory bridge to make it possible for both communities to be connected.” The three external members were, however, selected by eZ denoting that while more democratic, the authoritative structure still displays bias towards eZ.

In general, the community accepted the manner with which eZ managed the power boundary of the community. For instance, one community member stated, “I don't think the community has a will of its own. I think that members of the community who are not eZ … don't always like the way eZ Systems manages the community, but they allow eZ Systems to have complete responsibility.”

This acceptance was in large part due to the fact that eZ not only provided the software for free, but it also made a variety of other competence development resources freely available to the community through its IT platform, the second most discussed boundary management logic. This is not surprising given the strong technical focus of OSS development projects and the interest of eZ Systems to leverage the community through enabling members to build their engineering development capacity. eZ provided some training kits, manuals, published books, and online tutorials to facilitate the technical development of the community. However, community members indicated that eZ provided little support for helping transition new, inexperienced users into medium skilled users and then to experienced developers. Rather the community was of the opinion that eZ primarily focused its development efforts on the community's experienced developers, such as through organizing face-to-face development workshops across the globe.

In terms of the ability of eZ Publish community members to build their reputation and enhance their career opportunities, eZ offered several IT platforms (e.g., Jira, GitHub) where members could post their code contributions and extensions. It also organized an annual award ceremony and party to reward the most signi<sup>fi</sup>cant community members during the previous year. One interviewee referred to the annual award as the “Grammy Awards for eZ users”. However, eZ did exercise its power over the community related to the degree to which individuals could promote themselves or their products, and it did not allow community members to market their products or themselves to obtain employment and consulting opportunities within the community. One example mentioned is that when several members advertised their extensions and services within the community, eZ responded by quickly erasing their messages despite there not being any rules about this, which resulted in some highly competent members reducing their engagement in the community. However, in connection with the creation of the Commercial and Enterprise licenses in 2011, eZ softened its approach. It launched the R&D Partnership to manage commercialization and support initiatives for community members working as freelancers or with small consultancy <sup>fi</sup>rms to sell their eZ Publish extensions to the market, yet another example of the dynamicity of boundary management.

The <sup>fi</sup>nal boundary management logic, transactional ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, represented the least referenced logic. Transactional ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency focuses on the infrastructure enabling ef<sup>fi</sup>cient and effective communication and collaboration among community members and with the organization. IT platforms (e.g., social media channels, forum discussion boards, issue tracking and extension code repository databases) were the most vital component to ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently promoting communication within the community. Community members used the forum boards to socialize and support one another while uploading extensions and bug issues enabling eZ to track technical contributions and bugs in the software.

eZ was continuously monitoring the use of the IT platforms and feedback from the community on eZ. At one point the code contribution and extension platform was hosted on a separate website to the eZ Publish community website and was a source of major problems (due to little systems integration and availability) and “a little buried out in the sand” (ease of accessibility), according to one interviewee. In early 2012, eZ modi<sup>fi</sup>ed its IT platform by unifying the eZ Publish community website and the contribution platform. The goal was to make the entire process more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient from when a community member submitted an idea for a new software feature for the Enterprise version to the successful incorporation of the idea in the software. The moderator commented, “The great advantage is that it will also allow us all to work together much more easily on larger product features. From A to Z — from the idea being submitted in eZ Roadmap to the <sup>fi</sup>nal QA, testing and documentation, through functional speci<sup>fi</sup>cations, technical specs, and development.”

We found that it is not just the existence of the IT platforms that facilitates transactional ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, but it is also the <sup>fi</sup>rm's ability to communicate with the community that is of signi<sup>fi</sup>cance for the community to function effectively. In response to signi<sup>fi</sup>cant frustration in the community resulting from the way in which eZ had managed the community, eZ implemented the role of Community Director in 2009. The Community Director's responsibilities included fostering dialog and engagement within the community, introducing new tools to cooperate and share, and lastly, by organizing initiatives to physically bring together community members, e.g., conferences. The Community Director was also responsible for keeping the community informed about changes in the IT platform as well as other relevant decisions made by eZ. The Community Director was praised by interviewees both within the community as well as within eZ for his ability to successfully execute his role due to his personal qualities, behaviors, and/or approaches.

## 5.2.2. Impact of community boundary management logics on community innovation capacity

eZ provides strategic direction and exercises power over its innovation outputs in an effort to balance control with openness within the community. While part of the intention behind the decision to create two versions of eZ software was to promote the community's innovation capacity, our analysis reveals that innovation by the community tends to be rather limited (i.e., developing lower quality extensions and add-on modifications). One eZ developer described each party's roles. “Most of the code was written by us (eZ), and extensions and add-on modi<sup>fi</sup>cations were done by the community — for example we have done this with rest APIs and we have had several rounds with the community and have gotten lots of feedback on that.” From this perspective, the eZ developer con<sup>fi</sup>rms eZ's general strategy of seeing the eZ Publish community as a complementary asset that can be leveraged to extend its development efforts while maintaining its <sup>fi</sup>rm interests.

Our interviews revealed that the majority of community members were not capable of making signi<sup>fi</sup>cant contributions as the code was dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to write and complex in structure. As noted above, however, despite offering some initial training materials, eZ viewed the community as being responsible for facilitating the development of newbies and medium-skilled members into experienced developers capable of writing high-level code. eZ focused instead on developing the skills and knowledge of the more experienced and skilled community members — high performers who demonstrated their “loyalty” through their long-term commitment to gaining expertise with eZ software. While limiting the company's exposure to investing in new “riskier” resources that may not be reciprocated, the expectation by eZ was that their competence and identity boundary management activities would encourage these high performers to stay with the eZ Publish community and hopefully to deter them from switching to other communities, such as the Joomla or Drupal communities.

Despite these competence and identity boundary management efforts, the community's innovation capacity still was of a more incremental nature. One explanation for this unexpected <sup>fi</sup>nding is that while eZ did give considerable freedom to the community in terms of the degree to which members could develop and contribute code and participate in community governance (power boundary management), only a fraction of the community took advantage of these opportunities in practice. The members with a suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently high level of competence were typically professionals working for another company basing its products and services on eZ software. Several mentioned that their employer did not allow them to contribute to the community. Reasons offered were lack of time or competitive considerations. One interviewee noted, “Sometimes maybe people do want to share results of their work, but their boss does not want them to do so because the results of their work would then be given to the competition — so you always feel there is this tension of sharing some things but not everything … because of competitors.”

In spite of limited innovative contributions by the community, the high degree of competence of some community members does play a critical role in ensuring that eZ's software development efforts are progressing in the right direction. One eZ interviewee noted, “We say we are going to do this and then the community comes and says hey, hey what's going on. Then we may redirect our course, so we are much earlier in the market with ideas … we get help from the community to make sure we are making the right choices on where to go. If there is a new technology popping up, then it usually does not take long for some community member to have implemented that… so our innovation lab suddenly is a lot larger.”

As for the strategic direction of the software, as noted, any community member had the possibility to be elected into the Community Project Board. However, we found that similar to the above, interviewees stated that while they wanted to participate, community members felt it was dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to do so. This was because they either felt distanced from the community due to working in different time zones than eZ of<sup>fi</sup>ces or because their employer would not allow them to participate.

With regard to identity, the interviews revealed that the management of the identity boundary logic involved both eZ's promotion of a shared identity within the community as well as efforts by eZ to align the community's identity with that of the <sup>fi</sup>rm eZ. At times, these efforts led to people within the community being more motivated to contribute. One interviewee noted, “One of the founders used to jump in there and I always thought he had good insights …. When the higher ups are interested in the community, then I think the community maybe responds better…To me, it is like a Steve Jobs moment — he cares enough to pop in and say hi and it kind of motivates you.”

One eZ interviewee raised the concern, however, that the community's willingness to contribute resulting from OSS norms led to con<sup>fl</sup>ict with eZ when it came to deciding which contributions to accept and integrate. One eZ interviewee stated, “It is sometimes very hard to moderate what the community wants to contribute…. So that's one of the con<sup>fl</sup>icts — the different rhythms, the different work constraints When you watch from the outside as a community user, you just see your contribution. You focus on what you want to contribute and not that much on what others are doing. And you usually wonder why they can't just integrate what I have spent so much time doing.”

We found in our interviews that according to eZ's management, the alignment of the community's identity with that of eZ's goals was crucial to managing the expectations of the community. One interviewee noted, “Some people are starting to understand that we cannot integrate everything that is being done. Even though we appreciate the effort, there are quality constraints we have to live with. We try to educate them on this.” Another noted, “Why we are bitching so much about quality and testing and so on is because it is not very obvious for the community that everything must be of very high quality.”

When eZ lapsed in managing this alignment by failing to adequately provide status updates on contributions or explanations why certain community contributions were not absorbed into the software, some community members perceived this as inaction by eZ, which had a negative impact on innovation capacity. This was evident in one community member's comment, “And then eZ systems says yes, no, you know, well, we could do this, and we could do that, and bla-bla-bla, but at the end of the day, nothing is done. And that generates a negative point of view from the community towards eZ Systems.” Another community member noted, “The trust thing is very important for a company trying to build and grow a community around itself — this is one of the biggest problems of eZ in the past. eZ said, ‘Yeah, we will take care of your contribution and nothing happened, and we will take care of integrating zeta components into the core and nothing happened, and we will build a new version number and nothing happened.’ This can make you lose your community… it can drift away from your company and this is not a good thing for eZ.”

One of the reasons for this misalignment was that eZ overestimated its ability to communicate messages effectively to the community. On several occasions, many community members were not aware of signi<sup>fi</sup>cant changes made in support of the eZ Publish community. For example, while eZ thought that the Community Project Board was one of the most important things that it had recently implemented, many community members, when asked, did not even know of its existence or of any key details of its strategic purpose. A second example is when during one of the annual gatherings, eZ presented a major project named “Project V” aiming to redesign and re-code the eZ Publish software. The magnitude of the planned changes led many community participants to wonder about the details and schedule of the project and with a feeling of uneasiness and anxiety about the future since eZ was mostly silent about its plans, which led to a period of relatively low activity within the community.

In summary, community boundary management logics appear to play a crucial role towards in<sup>fl</sup>uencing community innovation capacity. In particular, the interaction among the boundary logics and the dynamic nature of the community as it matured required eZ to make managerial modi<sup>fi</sup>cations to ensure the success of the community. Such modi<sup>fi</sup>cations highlight the complex dynamics of managing a <sup>fi</sup>rm-sponsored OSS community. While these boundary logics enable the ability to innovate, understanding how a <sup>fi</sup>rm effectively absorbs ideas from the community into the <sup>fi</sup>rm remains unresolved, the subject of the next section.

## 5.3. Community boundary management logics & firm absorptive capacity

Below we discuss how boundary management impacts the three dimensions of absorptive capacity: relationship with the external environment, the collective dimension, and the individual dimension.

## 5.3.1. Relationship with external environment

As noted above, the relationship with the external environment was the absorptive capacity dimension most commonly referred to in the interviews. Through the <sup>fi</sup>rm's technical participation with the community, eZ was able to access technological advances made by community members. eZ's decision to create the two software versions facilitated the ability of eZ to scan the advances made by the community to the Community version and absorb those that eZ deemed most relevant to its Enterprise version. One interviewee noted, “We can look at say the 10 extensions built on a new technology, and then we can bring them back in and maybe we build them into the product. This is de<sup>fi</sup>nitely a win–win because we get innovation from the community and then we get the possibility to reengineer it and then can make it stable.”

On several occasions, eZ hired developers from the community after seeing the results of their technical work in order to bring their competence in-house and to enhance the <sup>fi</sup>rm's absorptive capacity. For example, eZ hired a community member who created a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant patch for PHP5 when eZ employees decided not to take on this effort as they thought it would be too much work to do internally.

In addition to software developments, technical participation with the community also provided strategic direction for the development of eZ's software. One eZ emplovee noted. “We went the way that the community suggested …. and this is exactly where we got very big value from community — if we had executed our internal plans, then we would have probably distanced ourselves from our customers and partners.” However, this decision to follow the community's suggestion over eZ's own internal plans created a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant level of dissatisfaction among a core group of eZ engineers. These engineers were of the opinion that eZ's management decided to side with the community over them in this important decision regarding the software's future development. As a result, they became disgruntled and left eZ, taking with them some of eZ's core engineering expertise and thus reducing not only eZ's internal innovation capacity but also its ability to leverage innovation coming from the community and thus its absorptive capacity.

Looking at social participation, as noted above eZ was very active in organizing annual conferences and local workshops during which eZ employees could interact with community members. However, very few mentioned in the interviews any type of direct or indirect impact on the <sup>fi</sup>rm's absorptive capacity resulting from these events. Rather the emphasis was more on these events being of a very professional manner and more for the bene<sup>fi</sup>t of the community than for eZ.

The above indicates that how the <sup>fi</sup>rm manages the power boundary, primarily in terms of control and organization of production, does indeed impact the <sup>fi</sup>rm's absorptive capacity. While technical participation in the community can positively impact the <sup>fi</sup>rm's absorptive capacity, the way in which the <sup>fi</sup>rm balances this technical participation with its own internal innovation activities can also negatively impact its absorptive capacity.

## 5.3.2. Collective dimension

The second dimension of absorptive capacity was the collective dimension in which routines and structures established by the organization are used to transfer knowledge into the <sup>fi</sup>rm. In addition to numerous routines that management had implemented in order to communicate strategic information relating to the community to its employees, one of eZ's key structures was its IT platform, which enabled the community to post, discuss, and develop extensions together with eZ employees as noted above. A second use of the platform was when eZ launched new product releases, the community was very active in using the platform to post and discuss any bugs or issues that were potentially overlooked by eZ. The IT platform was open to all eZ employees so that they could be passive observers, participate in the discussions and activity on the platform, and identify and access the relevant knowledge developed by the eZ Publish community.

Similar to previous research of embedding employees within communities, the Community Director position was created to improve the transactional ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of the community and played a central, bridging role between the community and eZ while ensuring alignment between the knowledge developed by the community and that of eZ. One eZ employee noted, “He is formalizing the unspoken roles we have had for a while. And he is trying to see how we can … bring people to do something we can use immediately through the community project.” The Community Director focused on using the proprietary technical forums and channels (e.g., eZ Publish discussion boards, email), non-proprietary social media applications (e.g., Twitter, Google+, LinkedIn), and social engagements via private conversations with key developers and during eZ events to ensure that information was shared and feedback was properly channeled to eZ. In this way, the Community Director was the primary mechanism for creating routines and establishing communication structures for managing absorptive capacity issues. However, while the Community Director could become a bottleneck, if removed it could also lead to potentially negative consequences for both eZ and the community.

The Community Director was effective at bringing in knowledge from the community and routing it to the appropriate people within eZ. Firm employees commented that they could speak directly with the Community Director to learn what the community was working on, and that this direct link was much more bene<sup>fi</sup>cial than when eZ marketing employees had been involved prior to the implementation of the Community Director role.

An unexpected <sup>fi</sup>nding related to the collective dimension was that the physical organizational structure of the eZ of<sup>fi</sup>ce played a role in the <sup>fi</sup>rm's ability to absorb knowledge from the community. We found that while the Community Director was located at one of the primary eZ of<sup>fi</sup>ces, what was important was that he sat physically right next to one of the chief software engineers at eZ. This employee noted, “His role has been huge for the last vear. I've been fortunate enough to be sitting right in front of him all last vear so we have already communicated a lot.”

The decision to create the Community Project Board as a community governance mechanism further facilitated the absorptive capacity of the <sup>fi</sup>rm as this structure and its accompanying routines helped eZ determine which advances made by the eZ Publish community should be absorbed into the Enterprise version. One eZ employee commented, “We introduced the Community Project Board of people from eZ and from the community, and they decide a roadmap for the Community version, which may be different from the Enterprise version. Then we from the eZ product management side, we decide what to add to the Enterprise version from the Community version. If something is not stable, then that means we do not add it just yet, or it could be something that we do not want to have in the Enterprise version. But usually it is more on the stability side — and often if there is something we decide not to have, then we build it as a community extension.”

In summary, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the <sup>fi</sup>rm's management of the transactional ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency boundary and the power boundary, primarily in terms of community governance, also impact the <sup>fi</sup>rm's collective dimension of absorptive capacity.

## 5.3.3. Individual dimension

Lastly, the individual dimension of absorptive capacity plays a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in eZ's ability to effectively absorb new knowledge through the activities of its individual employees. We found that while the IT platform facilitates individual eZ employees to identify and access knowledge in the community, it is the decision of each individual employee to actively participate in the community that impacts the <sup>fi</sup>rm's absorptive capacity. One eZ engineer commented, “I have always tried to … act as a bridge, trying to make sure that the engineering department remembers the community, and that the community doesn't feel like engineering is too far away from the community.” Some employees were quite active in soliciting the community for advice on their development work. One employee noted, “I sometimes ask for feedback on new features. My <sup>fi</sup>rst request for feedback was actually to the community through a blog post. And we got some reactions and comments from people and ideas for future development.”

While some eZ employees were actively engaged in the community, our interviews revealed that the vast majority of eZ emplovees did not participate in the community despite eZ management's interest and encouragement to do so. One eZ executive stated, “I would like to see more of our employees contributing in the community....to really see the power of working together.” This sentiment was con<sup>fi</sup>rmed in interviews with community members who suggested that dialog between eZ and the community was limited.

One reason provided by eZ employees was that they thought that community participation detracted from their ability to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll their duties of being an eZ employee. Some explained that time constraints and a priority to deliver on their internal tasks restricted them from participating. Additionally, if they started to engage with the community, then community members expected them to always respond to questions and participate in discussions. This led to information overload due to too many questions coming from the community, especially as the questions tended to be of a more basic nature due to the low competence level of the majority of community members. However, other employees were also of the opinion that community participation was the Community Director's responsibility — that the Community Director was “quite effective, works out well”, and there was no need for them to participate in the community.

A second reason for low community participation was the inherent tension resulting from being both an eZ employee and an open source community participant. eZ employees were often exposed through their eZ work to information that was considered con<sup>fi</sup>dential to eZ. While they may have wanted to engage with the community, they were unsure as to what should be shared and what should not be shared. This tension was further exacerbated as eZ did not provide any clear guidelines on this issue. One eZ employee stated, “It is a challenge to know what should be kept within the company and what can be provided to the community.”

This often resulted in eZ employees experiencing an identity con<sup>fl</sup>ict between their employee obligation and the OSS philosophy. In fact, eZ employees stated that a major reason for joining eZ was due to their enthusiasm for OSS and the opportunity to pursue this interest as a full-time organizational employee. As a result, eZ employees hesitated in responding to and providing opinions about issues, features, or development activities to the community since they “don't want to spill internal stuff into the community”. This reluctance to participate and share with the community makes eZ appear distant, uninterested, or to be holding back from the community. One eZ employee noted that this is quite problematic especially when eZ does not communicate with its employees on strategic issues and what can or cannot be discussed with the community. He commented, “It is not very simple for employees to act in the community — everything is taken as a statement by the company even if this was on personal level… But it becomes problematic because within the company there is not too much communication. So when roadmaps and priorities change and then an employee says something, this might not be true anymore.”

Furthermore, this uneven participation by employees in the community has led to misunderstandings and knowledge gaps within eZ as those employees who are active participants within the eZ Publish community may have a deeper level of understanding of the issues the community is experiencing. One employee commented, “Employees who are active in the community will know more than those who are not active so we have a knowledge gap. Perhaps we are in a meeting and one person says, ‘This is an issue we are already discussing in the community.’ Then the other person says, ‘I have never heard of that.´ So we can have a knowledge gap.”

Relating to the individual dimension, we found that eZ employees tend to shy away from directly engaging with the community to reduce tension and identity con<sup>fl</sup>ict. This identity con<sup>fl</sup>ict results primarily due to eZ's management of the identity boundary of the community — that of promoting an OSS community combined with a low level of community education regarding eZ's pro<sup>fi</sup>t-making goals. This lack of community engagement negatively impacts the ability of individual employees to identify relevant community knowledge and assimilate it into the <sup>fi</sup>rm as well as their own knowledge base. Furthermore, when eZ management pushed too far in enforcing <sup>fi</sup>rm identity, e.g., asking employees in one reorganization effort to relocate from of<sup>fi</sup>ces across the world to eZ headquarters in Norway, this balkanized the shared identity with the community, leading some individuals to leave eZ. The end result was a negative impact on the <sup>fi</sup>rm's absorptive capacity despite the <sup>fi</sup>rm's efforts to improve the community's transaction ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency through the IT platform that supported the development efforts of the eZ Publish community and Community version.

## 5.4. Importance of an integrative IT platform

In addition to our <sup>fi</sup>ndings related to our two research questions, our analysis also revealed the importance of an integrative IT platform in supporting the development of community innovation capacity and <sup>fi</sup>rm absorptive capacity. As discussed above, eZ exercised some control (power) over how technical contributions from community members (competence) were integrated into the software application and enabled the development of common interests (identity). All of this interaction occurred via channels such as the community portals, user forums, and code repository that were set up and maintained by eZ (transaction ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency). Noticeable is how the transaction ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency logic underlies the interactions but not socially present in the eZ engineers' mind. Rather, the platform acted as a non-intrusive participant to facilitate boundary management activities and their impact on community innovation capacity as well as to enhance the <sup>fi</sup>rm's absorptive capacity. In particular, the IT platform supported eZ in these areas: 1) community problem solving, 2) communication between eZ and the community, and 3) recruiting for competence and sustainable innovation capacity.

## 5.4.1. Community problem solving

When a member had a problem to solve or wanted to support the community, he could “just go to the forums and answer some questions, create some projects, and for eZ Publish extensions, go to Twitter and write some messages.” Other members joined projects to engage in problem solving with the original member posting and the technology served as the central point to coordinate the work of the newly created distributed team. Furthermore, the IT platform acted as a safety net to ensure information was not lost when eZ transitioned between processes. For instance, the issue tracker was designed to be backwards compatible so that community members tracking an old issue could use links that were bookmarked before the new issue tracker went online and could continue to use these links to locate an issue even afterwards.

## 5.4.2. Communication between eZ and the community

Various social media channels and the Community Project Board served as additional communication points bevond the traditional community forum to foster a dialog with community members. potential customers, and eZ. For example, the minutes of the Community Project Board meetings were stored in a central repository and posted on the forum to ensure that all community members had transparent access to the decision making process of eZ concerning the eZ Publish software and the ability to directly engage the board members to ask questions or comment. One board member stipulated that broad communication was not the only requirement of a good community. Occasionally <sup>fi</sup>ne-grained communication channels are necessary to coordinate processes that may not be ready for full community participation. This member highlighted how the board could “use the blog to post anything to the community, but also the internal mailing lists for both the board and the share team, while we also use Twitter.”

In this way, community members could use the IT platform to communicate with the intended recipients while also reaching the broader community when needed, indicating that the <sup>fl</sup>exibility of the platform was essential to communicating successfully. The platform itself was also useful to the community to maintain an awareness of the issues, problems, and ongoing community development activities. Members could “keep track of forums after releases for periods of two to three weeks when bugs show up and people experience issues.” This allowed eZ employees to quickly identify issues, develop solutions, implement and test solutions, and lastly to rapidly notify the community of the solutions using a central contact point. For issues requiring several days, a bug tracker maintained records on the status of the issue, its current progress, and a responsible developer to clearly signal that eZ was addressing the community's concerns. Additionally and as noted above, the Community Director played a large role in the success of the community, and the Community Director's ability to leverage the technical infrastructure was important in these efforts.

## 5.4.3. Recruiting for competence and sustainable innovation capacity

Of course, any community faces natural attrition and must continually recruit new members. By utilizing both 1) internal channels to develop new, inexperienced members into active, experts of the eZ software and community and 2) external channels (e.g., Twitter, Google+, LinkedIn) to attract new members and gain publicity for eZ from users, eZ ensured that it had a continuous supply of new developers willing to contribute to the community as older members retired or withdrew over time. In addition to traditional recruiting, the Community Project Board required new members to ascend to positions of volunteer responsibility; therefore, the Community Director used the forums and social media channels to identify potential replacements as board members retired or resigned.

## 6. Discussion

This paper sheds light on the role of boundary management of <sup>fi</sup>rm-sponsored OSS communities as it highlights the importance of integrative boundary management in facilitating community innovation capacity and the development of <sup>fi</sup>rm absorptive capacity. Furthermore, the results offer insights on the importance of an integrative IT platform in these activities as both an information retention point and enabler of boundary management activities. This section will discuss the <sup>fi</sup>ndings by highlighting the contributions. The results are used to suggest a theoretical model derived from the thematic analysis and interviews conducted in this study.

The results of this study contribute to the understanding of the <sup>fi</sup>rm-sponsored OSS community phenomenon and the literature in three ways. First, our understanding of <sup>fi</sup>rm-sponsored communities is enriched by an in-depth study of a single, successful <sup>fi</sup>rm-sponsored OSS community that plays a central role in the <sup>fi</sup>rm's business model. The results of our analysis suggest that our existing understanding of the boundary management of online communities was too limited to apply to the context of <sup>fi</sup>rm-sponsored OSS communities. This suggests the need for a typology of <sup>fi</sup>rm-sponsored communities to identify when speci<sup>fi</sup>c boundary management logics are more central than others. For instance, a single study of a music remixing community, which perhaps lacked a similar level of direct interaction between the <sup>fi</sup>rm and its community, focused on identifying these boundary logics and suggested that the dynamicity of the community is relatively stable in a <sup>fi</sup>rm-sponsored community (Jarvenpaa & Lang, 2011). The results of our study appear to contradict this observation in that several instances of dynamicity were found to play a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in the boundary management of the eZ Publish community and the impact boundary management had on innovation capacity and absorptive capacity.

Second, our study extends the existing literature examining boundary management of online communities in the speci<sup>fi</sup>c context of <sup>fi</sup>rm-sponsored OSS communities. Prior research has focused on how the four boundary management logics interact within the community to affect the generative capacity of the community itself. However, our study goes beyond this to examine the impact of boundary management of the community on the <sup>fi</sup>rm's absorptive capacity. For <sup>fi</sup>rms interested in practicing inbound open innovation, an understanding of how the community affects the <sup>fi</sup>rm's internal operations is critical in order to successfully leverage the community as a complementary asset. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study demonstrate the potential negative and positive impacts that the boundary management of the <sup>fi</sup>rm-sponsored community may have on the <sup>fi</sup>rm's absorptive capacity to generate new product and/or service offerings. Future research should investigate how <sup>fi</sup>rms that want to sponsor and engage communities can adapt their internal organization to better <sup>fi</sup>t with community-intensive innovation activities.

Lastly, our qualitative analysis suggests new theoretical insights into the role of integrative boundary management as it shows that a <sup>fi</sup>rm needs to balance the tradeoffs made when sponsoring an OSS community based on the effects that such tradeoffs will have on both the community's innovation capacity and the absorptive capacity of the <sup>fi</sup>rm. Fig. 1 depicts the theoretical model derived from the thematic analysis and interviews conducted in this study.

Our results suggest that boundary management plays a direct role on the community's innovation capacity as well as an indirect and direct role on the <sup>fi</sup>rm's absorptive capacity. First, boundary management in<sup>fl</sup>uences the community to use the resources provided by the <sup>fi</sup>rm in a strategically aligned manner to ensure that community innovation efforts are not wasted on areas that the <sup>fi</sup>rm cannot or is not interested in absorbing while also ensuring that the community's interests are met. Second, boundary management enables the community to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the <sup>fi</sup>rm's absorptive capacity as it may enable the community to develop a central role in the <sup>fi</sup>rm's innovation efforts. Additionally, the community's innovation capacity directly impacts the <sup>fi</sup>rm's absorptive capacity by in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the degree to which the <sup>fi</sup>rm can easily absorb and combine community knowledge with internal knowledge in the development of new products and services.

Third, depending on how a sponsoring <sup>fi</sup>rm's own internal organization is managed, boundary management may also directly impact the <sup>fi</sup>rm's absorptive capacity. For example, in response to boundary management actions. a firm's own emplovees may take an interest in the community's innovation outcomes or decide to actively participate in the community. However, boundary management may just as easily lead employees to reduce their interest or participation in the community or even to leave the <sup>fi</sup>rm, thus negatively impacting the <sup>fi</sup>rm's absorptive capacity. Consistent with previous <sup>fi</sup>ndings that <sup>fi</sup>rm employees may experience tension or role con<sup>fl</sup>ict due to the co-existence of the private and collective modes within their work (Rolandsson et al., 2011), we found that some eZ employees chose instead to more or less ignore the community and focus instead on interacting only with other developers within the <sup>fi</sup>rm to perform their work. If too many <sup>fi</sup>rm employees decide to behave in this manner, then knowledge silos may be created, leading to poor knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ows between the community and the <sup>fi</sup>rm, thus impacting the <sup>fi</sup>rm's absorptive capacity. Moreover, the <sup>fi</sup>rm's absorptive capacity may be further negatively impacted as employees are not able to stay abreast of technical developments in the industry or develop the necessary skills to engage in these developments due to their lack of community interaction. Therefore, integrative boundary management needs not only to take into account the positive and negative effects of salient boundaries and their interactive effects on the community's innovation capacity but also on their interactive effects on the <sup>fi</sup>rm's absorptive capacity.

Furthermore, the results also suggest the presence of a dynamic relationship between boundary management and the <sup>fi</sup>rm's absorptive capacity as both evolve over time. While longitudinal study is required to verify this statement, eZ repeatedly attempted to learn from its efforts within the eZ Publish community to ensure the success and continued sustainability of the community over time. For instance, the introduction of the Community Project Board was meant to facilitate dialog between the community and eZ to ensure that ideas and strategic goals were clearly and effectively shaped and discussed. The creation of the Community Project Board shifted the boundary management logic of power as it increased the level of shared governance that the community had to in<sup>fl</sup>uence eZ. Future research should then examine using a longitudinal study whether a <sup>fi</sup>rm's absorptive capacity impacts future boundary management activities of the community over time.

Consequently, this study contributes to theory through the development of middle range theory that extends our existing understanding of boundary management into the <sup>fi</sup>rm-sponsored OSS community phenomenon (Avgerou, 2013). This study also contributes to theory building within the boundary management area by expanding upon how boundary management logics interact and in<sup>fl</sup>uence both the community's innovation capacity and the sponsoring <sup>fi</sup>rm's absorptive capacity as opposed to merely focusing on the innovation capacity of the community. Lastly, this study contributes to the literature on inbound open innovation as it reaf<sup>fi</sup>rms previous research on the importance of a <sup>fi</sup>rm's absorptive capacity and provides guidance on how a <sup>fi</sup>rm's absorptive capacity can be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by both boundary management logics and the community's innovation capacity to produce ideas and innovations that can be used to further advance the OSS community.

## 7. Limitations and conclusion

All research is not void of limitations, and the limitations of this study are twofold. First, this study is limited to one <sup>fi</sup>rm-sponsored OSS community. Therefore, caution should be taken in terms of the generalizability of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study to other <sup>fi</sup>rms and industries. Future research should empirically examine the theoretical model presented in the previous section to replicate its <sup>fi</sup>ndings across different types of <sup>fi</sup>rm-sponsored communities, including music remixing communities, to determine a baseline model. Additionally, a longitudinal study is needed to determine the effects that a <sup>fi</sup>rm's absorptive capacity may have on future boundary management activities as the community evolves over time. Second, our study used a literature driven thematic analysis that, as a matter of design, possesses potential biases that must be mitigated. Projection bias by the researcher can occur since over interpretation of interview statements can potentially project themes where not present. Additionally, coder mood and style can lead to inconsistent application of the code if done in long sessions or at sub-optimal times. To mitigate these potential biases, the research team validated the coding through reliability analysis and randomly inspected remaining codes by the primary coding researcher to ensure consistent coding application. Furthermore, the coding researcher limited sessions to ensure that mood did not affect code application. Future research should consider empirically testing the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of these themes through replication of the thematic analysis and quantitative approaches such as survey or experiment manipulation.

In concluding, the private-collective community model aligns with the notion that the future of competition lies in new approaches to value creation (Chesbrough, 2003, 2006; Prahalad & Ramaswamy, 2004a,b,c), and this paper demonstrates how a <sup>fi</sup>rm's boundary management of its sponsored OSS community impacts not only the community's innovation capacity but also the <sup>fi</sup>rm's absorptive capacity. Using our results, we developed a model depicting the direct in<sup>fl</sup>uence of boundary management on the community's innovation capacity as well as the direct and indirect in<sup>fl</sup>uence of boundary management on the <sup>fi</sup>rm's absorptive capacity. Moreover, the results suggest that an integrative IT community platform enables boundary management activities as well as enhances the <sup>fi</sup>rm's absorptive capacity.

Our main <sup>fi</sup>nding supports what is expected to be the purpose of leveraging a <sup>fi</sup>rm-sponsored community, namely providing additional innovation capacity to the <sup>fi</sup>rm. However, the results of this study suggest a <sup>fi</sup>rm interested in pursuing such an approach must balance on a tightrope with both precision and due caution. With <sup>fi</sup>rm pro<sup>fi</sup>t interests and community interests each exerting in<sup>fl</sup>uence over the strategic direction of the community and its innovation capacity, it takes a highly skilled aerialist to balance these interests, translating the actions of both the community and the <sup>fi</sup>rm, while being supported by an integrative IT platform that may be latent to the audience's focused attention but critical to the success of the high-wire performance.
