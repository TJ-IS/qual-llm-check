# Community engagement and online word of mouth: An empirical investigation

Ji Wu<sup>a,⁎</sup>, Shaokun Fan<sup>b</sup>, J. Leon Zhao<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Business School, Sun Yat-sen University, 135 West Xingang Road, Guangzhou, China

<sup>c</sup> Department of Information Systems, City University of Hong Kong, 83 Tat Chee Avenue, Kowloon, Hong Kong, China

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Online brand community Online product review Community engagement Word of mouth Customer tenure

## A B S T R A C T

How customer engagement in a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s social media marketing platform − online brand community − might a<sup>f</sup>ect word-of-mouth behavior is an open research issue as well as a vital business problem in e-commerce. To this end, we collected a set of longitudinal data that include community engagement behavior, e-commerce transactions, and customers’ post-purchase reviews. Using a control function approach, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that consumer engagement in a brand community not only increases the likelihood of generating post-purchase reviews but also increases the likelihood of posting positive online reviews. Furthermore, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that customer tenure has a positive moderating e<sup>f</sup>ect.

## 1. Introduction

Facilitated by the development of online technologies, e-commerce companies currently implement a variety of online platforms to leverage product sales and customer value. Two such prominent platforms are online review platforms (i.e., retail-hosted (internal) review sources [26]) and online brand communities (OBCs). Online review platforms can e<sup>f</sup>ectively aggregate positive, negative, and neutral opinions into readily accessible online word of mouth (WOM), which has become a critical source of information that is reshaping the behavior of online consumers by reducing product uncertainties. Indeed, extant literature has shown that the public has become increasingly reliant on online product reviews to make purchase decisions [52,63]. Hence, most <sup>fi</sup>rms try to encourage customers to post online reviews in their e-commerce platforms [37].

An online brand community<sup>1</sup> is a type of social media platform, usually hosted by a company, which helps consumers of that company build relationships with each other through social interactions. It is believed that online brand communities can a<sup>f</sup>ect customers’ perceptions and actions, as well as provide substantial marketing value to <sup>fi</sup>rms. For example, studies have shown that brand community participants are more likely to visit the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s website and purchase products [51], are motivated to help other customers [44], and are very loyal [54]. Therefore, there has also been a groundswell of interest among marketers in organizing and facilitating OBCs.

While OBCs and online WOM channels are two separate platforms, customers often engage in both simultaneously. Given the frequent coexistence of these two online platforms, a question arises: how will customer engagement in one platform − OBC − a<sup>f</sup>ect his/her product review behavior in the other platform? Literature supports the connections between the two platforms. On one hand, the literature on online product reviews has shown that individuals’ decisions about whether to generate online reviews after purchase are in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the social context of online platforms [41], and online review ratings are subject to bias as a result of various environmental and behavioral factors [22,35]. OBCs, as <sup>fi</sup>rms’ social media marketing platforms, provide an in<sup>fl</sup>uential environment for guiding customer decisions [54].

On the other hand, the literature on OBCs has shown that commu nity engagement can not only e<sup>f</sup>ectively improve customer purchase value [51,62] but also enhance customer WOM intentions [2,48]. For instance, both Algesheimer et al. [2] and Ray et al. [48] found a positive relationship between community engagement and WOM intentions. However, little is known about how community engagement affects customers’ WOM in terms of the volume and valence of online product reviews (i.e., how community engagement will a<sup>f</sup>ect customers’ decisions about whether and what to post after purchase in online review platforms).

Further, community engagement behavior seems to be dynamic [31] and change during the course of customers’ membership in the community [47]. For instance, Hollebeek et al. [31] developed and validated a community engagement scale in speci<sup>fi</sup>c media settings and emphasized that the concept of community engagement should be dynamic. However, understanding how customers’ memberships in brand communities impact the relationship between community engagement and WOM behaviors is limited. Therefore, in this study, we also explore how the e<sup>f</sup>ects of community engagement on customer WOM behavior will be di<sup>f</sup>erent for shorter-tenured consumers and longer-tenured ones.

We conduct an empirical study addressing these questions with data collected from a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s e-commerce platform and its OBC. Our data captures customers’ community engagement behavior and transactions, along with complete information on all product reviews available on the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s business web pages. We de<sup>fi</sup>ne engagement from a behavioral manifestation perspective and conceptualize it as consumers’ prosocial contributions to the communities [48]. We also use a control function approach to overcome the self-selection issue of customer engagement in a brand community. Our empirical analysis yields three major <sup>fi</sup>ndings. First, our results provide empirical evidence on the impact of community engagement on customer WOM behaviors. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that community engagement has a positive impact on their online review ratings after purchase (the valence of WOM), which is consistent with previous arguments that engaged consumers produce positive WOM [2,48]. Our study also extends prior <sup>fi</sup>ndings by showing that customers’ engagement in brand communities has a positive impact on generating online reviews (the volume of WOM). Second, customer tenure in the community positively moderates the relationship between community engagement and WOM behaviors. In other words, the effects of community engagement on online WOM become stronger with longer customer membership. Third, for shorter-tenured consumers, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from this study indicate that brand community engagement in<sup>fl</sup>uences consumers’ behavior in generating online reviews after purchase, but does not a<sup>f</sup>ect their behavior in online review ratings. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings contribute to both online product reviews and community engagement research by explicitly introducing community engagement as a powerful social in<sup>fl</sup>uence that a<sup>f</sup>ects customer behavior in online reviews and enriching our knowledge concerning the e<sup>f</sup>ects of community engagement.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Community engagement

A brand community is a social aggregation of a brand’s users and the relationships among them. An OBC takes place in a virtual setting in which the users interaction is primarily Internet-mediated [42]. When people discuss brand communities, they concur on the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of community engagement in the context of such communities [11]. Community engagement takes a central role in the sustainability of OBCs [48] and is regarded as an e<sup>f</sup>ective strategy for <sup>fi</sup>rms to improve customer value [64]. The prevalent use of the concept of engagement in OBCs leads researchers to explore what community engagement is. Despite preliminary claims and insights into consumer engagement in social media, the de<sup>fi</sup>nition and operationalization of engagement are inconsistent and mixed [5,31]. Community engagement can be comprehended and conceptualized from various perspectives. For example, Baldus et al. [5] developed a measure of OBC engagement and de<sup>fi</sup>ne it as consumers’ motivations to interact with a brand community. In addition, much research conceptualizes consumer engagement from the perspective of the consumer’s relationship with a <sup>fi</sup>rm and de<sup>fi</sup>nes it as the intensity of consumers participation and connection with a <sup>fi</sup>rm s o<sup>f</sup>erings and activities [58]. Recently, Brodie et al. [11] developed a holistic view of the concept of community engagement. In their opinion, community engagement is “a multidimensional concept comprising cognitive, emotional, and/or behavior dimensions, and involves speci<sup>fi</sup>c interactive experiences between consumers and the brand, and/ or other members of the community.” In this study, in line with van Doorn et al. [56] and Pham et al. [46], we de<sup>fi</sup>ne engagement as a behavioral manifestation and conceptualize consumer engagement in OBCs as customer activity types and patterns, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally their contribution behaviors. In fact, despite the diverse conceptualization of community engagement, most studies agree that contributing community consumers act predominantly out of commitment to the community [59] and should be engaged members [11,48]. Consumers’ sharing of personal information, knowledge, and experiences through the process of active contributions to the online community highlights the interactive, two-way nature of community engagement [2] and re<sup>fl</sup>ects its behavioral and/or cognitive dimensions [11]. Therefore, studies suggest that community contribution, such as posting activities, re<sup>fl</sup>ects the multidimensional processes and high level of customers’ community participation [43], and it can be used to measure community engagement [33].

In addition to the theoretical development of a community engagement concept, recent studies have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed antecedent variables of community engagement and provided evidence that it o<sup>f</sup>ers substantial marketing value for <sup>fi</sup>rms. On one hand, researchers explore the drivers of community engagement. Tsai et al. [55] investigated the motivations and antecedents of community engagement and found that relationship-oriented factors played a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in inducing consumer engagement on social networking sites. Verhagen et al. [57] also showed that cognitive, social integrative, and hedonic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts appear to signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumer engagement intentions in virtual consumer environments. Moreover, previous studies indicate that both community and customer characteristics drive consumers to become engaged in online communities [25]. On the other hand, research has shown that community engagement enhances customer brand loyalty [64], improves online sales [62], increases advertising e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness [12], and motivates members to recommend communities or brands to outsiders [2,48].

However, most research studies are descriptive and qualitative analyses [11,54], rather than quantitative. And there is little research directly linking community engagement to actual consumer behavior. Furthermore, although a few studies have shown that community en gagement in<sup>fl</sup>uences customer WOM intentions, how OBC engagement in<sup>fl</sup>uences customers’ volume and valence of WOM remains an open question. Community members engage progressively [11], and Raïes et al. [47] emphasize that consumer tenure, which is an exogenous factor, plays an important role in determining the impacts of community engagement. However, knowledge concerning the impact of community engagement on customer WOM behaviors over membership time is lacking. In this study, we try to <sup>fi</sup>ll these research gaps via collecting a unique dataset and empirically testing how community engagement in<sup>fl</sup>uences generating online product reviews and ratings after purchase and how these relationships are moderated by consumer tenure.

## 2.2. Online product reviews

Online product reviews have consistently been shown to have an impact on online retailers’ sales performance, although there is some disagreement about what dimension or metric associated with online reviews is most impactful. A number of prior studies found that the valence of online reviews in<sup>fl</sup>uences sales signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly [13]. Duan et al. [17], on the other hand, found that valence does not matter for movie sales but the volume of online reviews does. Other studies examine how the metrics embedded in online product reviews in<sup>fl</sup>uence product sales. For example, Archak et al. [3] decomposed online reviews into segments describing di<sup>f</sup>erent product features and examined how the product features impact customers’ choices. Their results indicate that product features embedded in online reviews in<sup>fl</sup>uence sales. Sun [53] found evidence for the role of the variance of review ratings in models of sales. Moreover, Ludwig et al. [36] extracted changes in a<sup>f</sup>ective content and linguistic style properties of online reviews. They found positive changes in a<sup>f</sup>ective cues and increasing congruence with the product interest group’s typical linguistic style directly and conjointly increase sales. Thus, online reviews signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly a<sup>f</sup>ect online retailers’ performance and o<sup>f</sup>er an easy and cost-e<sup>f</sup>ective way to measure WOM [21].

Whereas there is a good amount of agreement regarding the impact of online product reviews, the literature is far less clear with respect to what drives consumers to generate online reviews after purchase. Hennig-Thurau et al. [29] identi<sup>fi</sup>ed <sup>fi</sup>ve primary factors motivating consumers to generate product reviews online, i.e., concern for other customers, self-enhancement, social bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, economic incentives, and advice seeking. Dellarocas et al. [16] showed that individuals with extreme product experience or opinions, both positive and negative, would be more likely to post a review online than those with more moderate experience. Of even more concern is that extant research has shown that an individual’s decision of whether or not to voice his/her opinion online can be subject to environmental factors. Moe et al. [41] studied how previously posted product reviews a<sup>f</sup>ect an individual’s posting incidence. Their results reveal that consumers are more likely to generate online product reviews after purchase when the rating environment is positive. Wu et al. [61] suggested that when there are fewer existing reviews, consumers are more likely to post their reviews and thus achieve higher impact. In this study, we focus on one special environmental factor in social media platforms, i.e., community engagement, and explore how it will in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumers’ generating online reviews after purchase.

There is a growing interest around what factors a<sup>f</sup>ect customer ratings in product reviews. Similar to the driving factors of generating reviews, factors that a<sup>f</sup>ect customer ratings in product reviews can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into two types: environmental and intrinsic. Environmental factors include product price, time, and existing reviews. For example, Li et al. [34] showed that ratings are negatively a<sup>f</sup>ected by price paid by consumers. What’s more, several researchers have shown empirically that online ratings tend to decline over time [19,35]. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, Li et al. [35] argued that because consumers who place a higher valuation on a product purchase it earlier, the ratings in online reviews tend to decrease over time. Godes et al. [22] also systematically studied the sequential and temporal dynamics of online ratings and found sig ni<sup>fi</sup>cant sequential e<sup>f</sup>ect in online ratings: the nth rating is, on average, lower than the n-1th when controlling for time, reviewer e<sup>f</sup>ects, and product e<sup>f</sup>ects. In addition to the impact of environmental factors, studies examine how intrinsic consumer characteristics may a<sup>f</sup>ect online product review ratings. For instance, Ma et al. [38] found that consumer characteristics, such as geographic mobility, number of friends, and gender, moderate the relationship between prior ratings and sequential ratings in online product reviews. Goes et al. [23] also found that when reviewers become more popular, their reviews become more negative and more varied. Despite these research e<sup>f</sup>orts in exploring factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence online review ratings, no study has empirically tested how consumer engagement in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c marketing program (i.e., OBC) will a<sup>f</sup>ect their online product review behavior. Based on the theory of community engagement, this study seeks to <sup>fi</sup>ll this gap.

## 3. Theoretical development

## 3.1. Impact of community engagement on generating online reviews

Fig. 1 shows our conceptual framework, which explores the relationship between community engagement and online review behavior. The framework draws on recent studies on brand communities, member engagement, and customer WOM behavior. The framework investigates how community engagement in<sup>fl</sup>uences customers’ online WOM behavior in terms of review generating and review ratings. It also examines the moderating role of customer tenure in the relationships. Our exploration considers many control variables, including product attribute, discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation, review context, and customer attribute. This framework contributes to the literature by proposing and testing the dynamic impact of community engagement on review generating and ratings. Our main argument is that customers who are more engaged in an OBC are more likely to give positive product reviews after their purchases. We propose community engagement will positively impact customers’ review generating after purchase for two main reasons.

![](/api/attachments/A8Z7V5GQ/fulltext/images/a4eaa43f4d0931b4e19fd881ef7b14747b7788d2647c082e5a814984938b39b9.jpg)  
Fig. 1. The conceptual framework of this study.

First, community engagement improves customers’ community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, which improves their willingness to contribute to the brand or products by generating online product reviews. Community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation is a construct that builds on the theory of social identity [4]. Analogous to identi<sup>fi</sup>cation with an organization, community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation refers to the extent to which one’s sense of self overlap with his/her sense of a brand community [8]. Studies have shown that community engagement can be driven by community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation [48], and, in contrast, community engagement can also foster community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation [28]. Therefore, customers who engage in OBCs are more likely to con<sup>fl</sup>ate communal identity of a brand community with their own self-identity, and they feel that their WOM actions not only re<sup>fl</sup>ect communal values of the brand or products but also express their personal values [2]. And engaged customers tend to generate product reviews after purchase because sharing information on a brand’s products with other customers is essentially sharing a personally meaningful experience. For instance, Muniz Jr et al. [42] found that even six years after Apple Computer Inc. discontinued the Newton product, customers who engaged in the brand community still continued to advocate the product’s use to outsiders and show a sense of kinship with it.

Second, community engagement is geared toward performing helpful activities in a voluntary environment, and engaged customers are more likely to generate online reviews to help other customers make purchase decisions. Studies on job engagement suggest that engaged members of organizations not only work on essential tasks but also pursue good citizen behaviors that are informal, helpful, and conscientious [49]. Recent research also found that members who engage in online communities are more likely to help fellow members in a voluntary environment because they seek actions that are informal and have impact [48]. Generating online reviews after purchase is voluntary for customers, and those who engaged in a brand community seem to be more likely to pursue such citizen behavior and share their purchase experience to help others’ decisions. By disseminating product in formation, customers who engage in OBCs believe they help facilitate a friendly environment [14]. Hence, we hypothesize that:

H1: Community engagement has a positive impact on generating online product reviews after purchase.

## 3.2. Impact of community engagement on online review ratings

In addition to the e<sup>f</sup>ect of community engagement on generating online reviews after purchase, we propose that community engagement will positively impact customer behavior in online review ratings, for two main reasons.

First, community engagement enhances customer satisfaction and emotional bonding, and positive WOM allows engaged customers to reciprocate for such satisfaction. Engaged customers perceive a con-<sup>fl</sup>ation of one’s self-identity with a brand community [9], and studies have found that identifying with a group implies a sense of emotional involvement with the group and members derive satisfaction from social interactions within the group [2,4]. In addition, engaged members tend to be satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the organization and its products with which they identify [8]. Recent studies on online community also found that engaged customers exhibit enhanced customer satisfaction and emotional bonding [11]. According to social exchange theory, satisfaction with social interactions is a positive assessment of prior bene<sup>fi</sup>ts that signals that one must reciprocate [7]. Providing positive WOM to outsiders allows engaged customers to reciprocate for such satisfaction. For instance, Algesheimer et al. [2] suggested that community engagement is likely to be experienced positively. Such a positive experience may also lead engaged customers to indulge in positive WOM behavior because they want to “share the $ { \mathbf { j } }  { \mathbf { o } }  { \mathbf { y } } ^ {  { \mathbf { \gamma } } }$ with others and thereby restore balance in their life [29].

Second, customer engagement in OBCs is performed from a sense of public commitment [64], and such commitment will cause customers to support and defend the brand and products through positive product evaluation [18]. Organizational studies have found that engaged employees participate in broader behaviors, which include defending their group from criticism, displaying pride when their organization is represented in public, and showing concern for its image [49]. Therefore, engaged customers are more likely to become active defenders of the brand community and its products through producing positive online reviews. Therefore, we assume:

H2: Community engagement has a positive impact on online pro duct review ratings after purchase.

## 3.3. Moderating efects of customer tenure

A few studies emphasize the dynamic characteristic of community engagement [31] and highlight that community consumers engage progressively [11]. Recently, Raïes et al.’s [47] study showed that the mechanisms of how community engagement a<sup>f</sup>ects brand loyalty are di<sup>f</sup>erent for shorter-tenured consumers and longer-tenured ones. Community engagement comprises individual cognitive, emotional, or behavioral dimensions, and customer tenure, which is an exogenous factor, can in<sup>fl</sup>uence engagement processes. Therefore, it is important to consider how consumer tenure, which is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the length of the consumer’s relationship with the brand community at the time of engagement, a<sup>f</sup>ects the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of community engagement on WOM behavior. Understanding this consumer characteristic is not only managerially signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in the sense of being observable, but also actionable in the sense that it provides speci<sup>fi</sup>c guidance to managers on actions they can take.

Engaged customers exhibit enhanced commitment [11], and com munity engagement can in<sup>fl</sup>uence customer behavior through community commitment [64]. Raïes et al. [47] suggested that the combination of community engagement and commitment is essential in explaining behavior of members over their membership length. Drawing on organizational commitment theory [40], Bateman et al. [6] proposed that consumers’ behavior in the community and towards the brand is directly linked to their commitment level. They distinguish three levels of community commitment: (1) continuance commitment, which is based on the member’s belief that his/her engagement provides net bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, (2) a<sup>f</sup>ective commitment, which is a positive emotional attachment to the brand community, and (3) normative commitment, which is based on the consumer’s feeling of social obligation. Previous studies <sup>fi</sup>nd that increasing engagement in brand communities leads to increasing commitment to the community [27], and the average level of community engagement is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly higher for longer-tenured consumers than for shorter-tenured ones [2]. Langerak et al. [32] considered that shorter-tenured consumers have fewer social links and are less engaged in the brand community compared to longer tenured members because they are not familiar with the brand and the rules of the community and are mainly motivated by information search. Therefore, shorter-tenured consumers are more likely to have lower levels of engagement and commitment to the brand community. After consumers invest e<sup>f</sup>ort in the community, learning about it as well as forming relationships within it, there seems to be a shift in the dimensions dominating consumers’ commitment to the brand and the community [1]. Over time, the interest of community members seems to change from a dominating personal interest in gathering useful in formation [50] to a feeling of obligation toward the community [39]. Moreover, Raïes et al. [47] found that longer-tenured consumers tend to be associated with the strongest form of commitment, normative commitment, which re<sup>fl</sup>ects a sense of obligation to the brand community. Thus, longer-tenured consumers exhibit higher levels of engagement and commitment to the brand and the community. Marketing scholars have noted that consumers who exhibit high levels of com mitment express strong loyalty and yield positive WOM behavior [24]. Hence, we assume:

H3a: The impact of community engagement on generating online product reviews after purchase is stronger for longer-tenured consumers than for shorter-tenured consumers.

H3b: The impact of community engagement on online product review ratings after purchase is stronger for longer-tenured consumers than for shorter-tenured consumers.

## 4. Empirical analysis

## 4.1. The firm’s consumer engagement initiatives via online community

The dataset for this study comes from an e-commerce company and its OBC. The focal <sup>fi</sup>rm X (con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality reasons preclude naming the company) designs, produces, and sells female apparel in an Asian market. Consumers buy products from its independent business website and can leave product reviews on the website’s product pages after purchase. In October 2010, the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm, for the <sup>fi</sup>rst time, created an OBC with the objective of encouraging customer engagement. This community is a <sup>fi</sup>rm-sponsored website, and consumers can use their ecommerce ID to register in the community. Fig. 2 shows the re lationship between the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s business website and its OBC.

![](/api/attachments/A8Z7V5GQ/fulltext/images/de3816bf39837ab4c2912f50201e1ffcace60ed3277909574c46c4c61b4eee91.jpg)  
Fig. 2. The relationship between the business website and the OBC

Firm X set up this community to serve as a platform to engage and interact with their consumers and also to facilitate interaction among consumers. Consumers can log into the community using their accounts on the e-commerce website. Then they will be able to post messages and reply to the posts of others. The online community includes more than two dozen general and category-speci<sup>fi</sup>c discussion boards in which customers seek and/or provide information regarding various aspects of products and the brand. There are also social bulletin boards with names like Travel Together, Movie and Television, and Food and Cooking, which are primarily used by participants for socializing and entertainment.

An examination of posted messages revealed that this online com munity possesses the three characteristics of a brand community [2,42]. First, there is clear evidence of a common identity among the members. For example, members frequently and publicly assert their allegiance to the brand and express their enthusiasm for the related products. One member <sup>fl</sup>atly stated, “from now on, my clothes will be a single brand named ‘x’.” This kind of community identity also makes members feel a connection to one another. Second, members of the brand community spend a considerable amount of time assisting fellow brand owners with purchasing the brand’s products, providing evidence of a sense of moral responsibility or obligation among members. Third, members frequently write stories about the brand and share their pictures wearing the brand’s products. Such evidence indicates that this online community develops rituals and traditions that bind the members together.

## 4.2. The data

Two sources of data (online e-commerce website and OBC) were used in this study to empirically answer our research questions. The data-gathering process is not straightforward and involves a rather onerous task of downloading, recording, and matching information. First, the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm provided us with the purchase transaction data and its related online product reviews from the e-commerce website be tween May 2011 and December 2012. There are 111,266 purchase records from 10,896 customers during this time period. Approximately 20% of customers generated online product reviews on the e-commerce website after purchase. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, 2286 customers generated 12,723 post-purchase online product reviews during the time period. Our review dataset involves rich information, such as user ID, product ID, review ratings, review context, and the date of review generating, which allows us to model online review behavior accurately.

In the second phase of data collection, we wrote custom programs that enabled us to extract and analyze all the discussion threads in the online brand community. In all, 28,394 discussion threads were extracted and recorded in a dataset. These messages spanned a 26-month period from when the community was launched in October 2010 up to December 2012. Next, we located and recorded user IDs and posted dates within each discussion thread. This data collection phase resulted in a rich and unique dataset containing detailed information on discussion threads, including when and by whom they were posted. In addition, our custom programs gathered information on when members registered in the community from their pro<sup>fi</sup>le pages, which enabled us to construct the duration variables.

To construct our <sup>fi</sup>nal panel, we integrated community engagement data with the purchase data and online product reviews. For our pur poses, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne time windows of six weeks for each of the purchase records (i.e., 3 weeks before a speci<sup>fi</sup>c purchase and 3 weeks after). We then observe each consumer purchase to determine the frequency of postings in the OBC during the purchase windows. We omitted consumers who never engaged in the brand community in our dataset. This process resulted in 635 consumers who engaged in the community and 7212 observations.

## 4.3. Empirical model

Our focus is the hypotheses that community engagement in<sup>fl</sup>uences customer online WOM behavior. However, there is good reason to believe that the causality may also go the other way: the extent to which consumer engagement in a brand community is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by online review behavior. Moreover, it is quite likely that our model of consumer WOM su<sup>f</sup>ers from omitted variable problems, and an uncontrolled confounder causing both the independent variable $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ community engagement) and the dependent variables (i.e., review generating and rating). Either of these complications would make our explanatory variable of interest, community engagement, correlated with the error term in the model, and an endogeneity problem occurs [60]. Because of the endogeneity problem and the discrete nature of our dependent variables, in this study we employ a control function approach to address these problems. Control function methods have long been used for particular nonlinear models when there are continuous endogenous explanatory variables [10,45]. Next, we specify our econometric models and show how we use the control function approach to achieve consistent estimation.

Considering consumers’ WOM behavior after purchase, the latent utility $U _ { i j t }$ that consumer i obtains from his/her WOM behavior on product j at time t is speci<sup>fi</sup>ed as follows:

$$

$$

where $C E _ { i t }$ is consumer i errorimage ’s brand community engagement at time $t ,$ which is the endogenous variable. X is a vector of observed exogenous variables that a<sup>f</sup>ect the utility derived from online WOM behavior, and it consists of <sup>fi</sup>ve sets of variables: $P _ { j t }$ indicates product j errorimage ’s attributes at time $t \ ( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , product price and shipping charge), $D _ { i j t }$ captures the discrepancy between the pre-purchase expectation and the post-purchase evaluation of a product [30], $M _ { i t }$ captures consumer brand membership, $E _ { i j t }$ indicates other customer re views $( \mathbf { e . g . }$ , the valence, volume, and variance of prior online reviews), and $C _ { i }$ captures customer characteristics (e.g., customer age, gender, location, and income). σ represents the <sup>fi</sup>xed attributes of product j ; θ represents the <sup>fi</sup>xed e<sup>f</sup>ect of time $t . ~ \varepsilon _ { i j t }$ is the unobserved utility. As noted above, both reverse causality and confounding factors would make $C E _ { i t }$ correlated with the $\varepsilon _ { i j t }$ in Eq. (1) and bias the estimated coe<sup>fi</sup>cients.

The control function approach purges the model of correlation between $C E _ { i t }$ and $\varepsilon _ { i j t }$ by using instrumental variables. It posits that community engagement can be written as a function of the η exogenous variables X in Eq. (1), a set of instruments $z ,$ and an unobserved term

$$
C E _ {i t} = W (X, Z) + \eta_ {i t}\tag{2}
$$

where $\varepsilon _ { i j t }$ and $\eta _ { i t }$ are assumed to be independent of X and $z ,$ but not of each other. The key to the control function approach is to note that under the assumptions, conditional on $\eta _ { i t } , ~ \varepsilon _ { i j t }$ is independent of $C E _ { i t } .$ Thus, by entering the control function, which is the residuals from the endogenous variable regression interacting with estimated coe<sup>fi</sup>cient $\lambda ,$ into the utility function, the remaining variation in the endogenous variable will be independent of the error, and standard estimation ap proaches will be consistent. Utility is as follows:

$$
U _ {i j t} = V (C E _ {i t}, X, \sigma_ {j}, \theta_ {t}) + \lambda \eta_ {i t} + \varepsilon_ {i j t} ^ {\prime}\tag{3}
$$

In this study, we specify $V ( \cdot )$ as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} V (C E _ {i t}, X, \delta_ {i}) = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} C E _ {i t} + \beta_ {2} P r i c e _ {i j t} + \beta_ {3} F r e i g h t _ {i j t} \\ \quad + \beta_ {4} D i s c o n f i r m a t i o n _ {i j t} + \beta_ {5} V o l u m e _ {i j t - 1} + \beta_ {6} V a l e n c e _ {i j t - 1} \\ \quad + \beta_ {7} V a r i a n c e _ {i j t - 1} + \beta_ {8} T e n u r e _ {i t} + \beta_ {9} A g e _ {i} + \beta_ {1 0} G e n d e r _ {i} \\ \quad + \beta_ {1 1} R u r a l _ {i} + \beta_ {1 2} S o u t h _ {i} + \beta_ {1 3} I n c o m e _ {i} + \sigma_ {j} + \theta_ {t} \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

In this speci<sup>fi</sup>cation, CE<sub>it</sub> indicates the extent of consumer engagement in a brand community. Because customers’ community engagement is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as users’ prosocial contributions in online communities in this study [46], we measure community engagement as the number of postings that consumer i contributed to the community during a purchase window t, following precedence [43]. The money that consumers paid for products a<sup>f</sup>ects their utility in online product reviews [34]. Thus, in Eq. (4), we include two product variables: $P r i c e _ { i j t }$ and $F r e i g h t _ { i j t } ,$ which measure the price and the shipping cost that consumer i pays for his/her product j at time period t. According to Ho et al. [30], the discrepancy between the expected and experienced assessment of the same product a<sup>f</sup>ects the behavior of customers leaving online reviews. Thus, in $\operatorname { E q . }$ (4), we also include a variable Disconfirmation to capture such discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation e<sup>f</sup>ect, and it is measured as the di<sup>f</sup>erence between the average valences of posted ratings (i.e., Valence ) and the long-term average rating for all products (R ). Since consumers’ brand memberships play an important role in impacting their WOM behavior Tenure [20], we include the variable

It indicates the duration of a consumer i errorimage ’s OBC membership, measured as the number of days that have elapsed since the consumer registered in the brand community. Finally, both Moe et al. [41] and Godes et al. [22] showed that online WOM behavior is dependent on other customers’ reviews. We include three variables, Volume<sub>ijt</sub>, $V a l e n c e _ { i j t } ,$ and $V a r i a n c e _ { i j t } ,$ to control for the potential e<sup>f</sup>ects of other customer reviews. Volume measures the number of online reviews for a product at a time point, Valence measures the average rating of online reviews for a product at a time point, and Variance measures the variance of review ratings for a product at a time point. We control for customer heterogeneity in online WOM behavior by including several customer demographic variables: Age , Gender , which indicates whether a customer is a female, $R u r a l _ { i } ,$ which indicates whether a customer lives in a rural area, South , which indicates whe ther a customer live in the country’s southern part, and Income , which is a zip code-level estimate of household incomes as provided by national statistics. βs are estimated coe<sup>fi</sup>cients.

The dependent variables in our model are consumers’ post-purchase online reviews and ratings. For each purchase, we observed each con sumer i to determine whether he/she created an online product review after purchase. The generation of an online product review (Generating ) for each purchase is a binary variable that takes the value 1 if the consumer generated online product reviews in the business website after purchase and 0 otherwise. Furthermore, for each purchase with online reviews, we observed the scores that the consumers gave the products. The online product review rating (Rating ) re<sup>fl</sup>ects the score numbers (1: lowest, 5: highest) that consumer i gave in online product reviews. Because our observed outcome variables are a binary variable and an ordinal variable, we followed the probit and ordered probit speci<sup>fi</sup>cation in parametering the generating and rating behavior in online product reviews, respectively. ## 4.4. Results

The <sup>fi</sup>rst step of our approach is to estimate the community engagement function to recover the residuals by entering the control functions in the utility model. We used a linear speci<sup>fi</sup>cation and regressed the community engagement against the exogenous independent variables listed in Table 1 plus instruments. We propose two instrumental variables: (1) the average number of postings that are unrelated to products $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { i . e . } } ,$ , those posted in the discussion boards) and contributed by other customers located in the same district as customer i at period t (Neighbor \_ nppost ), (2) the personal information disclosed that indicates whether consumer i discloses his/her personal information, such as MSN and phone number, in the brand community (Disclosed ). The intuition behind the <sup>fi</sup>rst instrument is that, while a consumer’s community engagement seems to be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by his/her neighbors’ posting behavior (peer e<sup>f</sup>ect). Posts unrelated to products will not affect their online product reviews. The intuition behind the second in strument is that customer engagement in an OBC is related to concerns over information privacy (which can be proxied by personal informa tion disclosed) in the community, and personal information disclosure in an OBC will not relate to customer decision-making in online product reviews. We also test the validity of the instrumental variables. We <sup>fi</sup>rst test whether our instrumental variables are weak instruments. The F statistics are 22.299 for review generating and 24.008 for review ratings, which indicate that the excluded instrumental variables are relevant. Next, we conduct the Hansen test to assess the overidenti<sup>fi</sup>cation issue. The Hansen J statistics are 0.552 $( p > 0 . 1 )$ and 0.487 $( p > 0 . 1 )$ for review generating and ratings, respectively, which indicate that the instrumental variables do not su<sup>f</sup>er from overidenti<sup>fi</sup>cation. The results of the <sup>fi</sup>rst-stage regression are shown in Table A1 in the appendix.

In the second stage, the residuals from the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage are used as additional regressors in the main models, $\mathbf { i . e . , E q . } ( 5 )$ ) and Eq. (6). In our model estimation, we take the natural logarithm of price and customer tenure variables to control for their right-skewed nature. The variables of community engagement and customer tenure are mean-centered by subtracting their respective grand mean to form the interaction term. Such operationalization avoids potential multicollinearity. We also calculate the variance in<sup>fl</sup>ation factor (VIF) for the covariates in our models and the result shows no evidence of multicollinearity (VIF < 3). ## 4.5.1. The specification of treatment windows

We conduct robustness checks to con<sup>fi</sup>rm the results of our analyses. For our case, in order to de<sup>fi</sup>ne our community engagement measure, we use community engagement data consisting of 3 weeks pre- and post-purchase by a consumer as a time window (i.e., 6 weeks). To make sure that the results are robust to the speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of time windows, we conduct the following analysis. We run the models (for both generating online product reviews and ratings) wherein we restrict the engagement treatment period to a time frame consisting of 2, 4, and 8 weeks, the results of which are reported in Table 4. We only report the major variables for hypotheses testing due to space. We <sup>fi</sup>nd the estimates of community engagement are positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant for cases without interaction terms, indicating that consumer engagement in a brand community positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences the volume and valence of online product reviews. Moreover, the parameter estimate of the interaction term between community engagement and customer tenure in the models of generating online reviews is consistently positive and sig ni<sup>fi</sup>cant. This implies that customer tenure moderates the relationship between community engagement and generating of online product re views after purchase, and this relationship is strengthened when con sumers have longer tenure. In addition, the parameter estimate of the interaction term in the cases of online review ratings is consistently positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, indicating the impact of OBC engagement on post-purchase rating is greater for longer-tenured consumers than for shorter-tenured consumers. Therefore, the results from the di<sup>f</sup>erent speci<sup>fi</sup>cations of time windows are consistent with the results that we presented earlier.

## 4.5.4. Sample selection bias

In this study, we examine community engagement and focus on members who engage in a brand community. Our sample includes only customers who engaged in the community, which may cause selection bias. In order to alleviate the concern about sample selection bias, we conduct a robustness analysis by using propensity score matching (PSM). PSM enables us to construct treatment and control groups of customers who resemble each other in all observed characteristics; the major di<sup>f</sup>erence between these two groups is that the customers in the treatment group engaged in the OBC whereas those in the control group did not. By constructing these two groups of customers, PSM allows us to alleviate the sample selection issue [15]. We conduct PSM analysi by <sup>fi</sup>rst modeling the customers’ community engagement decision by adopting a logit model.<sup>2</sup> With a set of exogenous variables (i.e., Age , Gender , Rural , South , Income , and $T e n u r e _ { i t } ) _ { i }$ , we calculate the probability of a customer engaging in the OBC. Based on the propensity scores of customers’ community engagement, we are able to match treatment customers to a set of control customers using the single nearest neighbor matching algorithm and then test whether their behavior in online WOM is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly di<sup>f</sup>erent. The results show that engaged customers are more likely to post online reviews after purchase (Diference = 0.124, t-test=2.63) and their review ratings are higher (Diference = 0.105, t-test=2.44) than those who did not engage in the community. After controlling for the sample selection bias by using PSM, our robustness analysis consistently shows that community en gagement improves customers’ review propensity and their average review ratings.

## 5. Discussion

## 5.1. Discussion of findings

This research studies the impact of community engagement on the behavior of customers giving online product reviews. Di<sup>f</sup>erent from Ho et al. [30]’s study that focuses on customers’ discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation e<sup>f</sup>ect on their online WOM, this research investigates how <sup>fi</sup>rms’ community strategy (i.e., encouraging customers to engage in <sup>fi</sup>rms’ OBCs) a<sup>f</sup>ects customers’ online WOM and has three notable <sup>fi</sup>ndings. First, we empirically show that engagement in an OBC enhances consumer WOM behavior in both the volume and the valence. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that community engagement leads consumers to generate more online product reviews and give higher ratings on their online reviews after purchase. Second, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that such e<sup>f</sup>ects of community engagement are in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by customer tenure in the community. OBC membership positively moderates the relationship between community engagement and online WOM. Third, community engagement does not a<sup>f</sup>ect customer behavior in online review ratings for shorter-tenured customers. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, our empirical evidence a<sup>fi</sup>rms that customers engagement in OBCs improves their generation of online reviews after purchase for both shorter-tenured and longer-tenured consumers, and this e<sup>f</sup>ect is greater for longer-tenured ones. Our results also show that community engagement exhibits an in<sup>fl</sup>uential role on positive WOM for longer-tenured consumers. However, this impact is not signi<sup>fi</sup>cant for shorter-tenured consumers.

## 5.2. Theoretical implications

In this study, we examined the e<sup>f</sup>ects of community engagement on WOM behavior in terms of online product review behavior. In doing so, this study extends <sup>fi</sup>ndings from online review and community engagement literature while also contributing in several ways. First, this is the <sup>fi</sup>rst study to link actual WOM behavior (i.e., consumer behavior in online reviews) to customer engagement in a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s OBC. Online review studies primarily considered the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of online reviews on product sales, and it has been well recognized that online customer reviews have a substantial impact on purchase decisions [17]. Recently, there is a growing body of work that studies the factors that a<sup>f</sup>ect the behavior of customers leaving online product reviews. They <sup>fi</sup>nd that product price [34], review context [41], reviewer popularity [23], and the discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation e<sup>f</sup>ect [30] in<sup>fl</sup>uence customers’ online review behavior. Our study adds to this burgeoning literature by analyzing how brand community engagement a<sup>f</sup>ects consumer decisions of whether to post online product reviews or what to post after purchase. By accentuating the role of community engagement, we underscore that consumers’ online review behavior can also be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by <sup>fi</sup>rms’ brand community programs.

Second, our study contributes to the community engagement literature by adding new insights to growing research that considers the impact of community engagement on customer behavior. Most prior research on customer engagement in a brand community focused on estimating the direct economic impact of membership. For example, Rishika et al. [51] explored the impact of community participation on customer-<sup>fi</sup>rm relationships and found that OBC participation increases consumer pro<sup>fi</sup>tability. Di<sup>f</sup>erent from these studies, we focus on community engagement and its e<sup>f</sup>ects on online WOM. The term community engagement implies a more central role in online communities than community participation [48], and it has not previously been empirically measured and analyzed as a variable in the information systems literature by using archive data. We drew on the contribution nature of engagement and measured community engagement as prosocial contribution behavior in an online community. We also contribute to the community engagement literature by providing empirical evidence that engaging in an OBC can enhance consumer online WOM. Although prior studies on community engagement have shown that community engagement can in<sup>fl</sup>uence WOM intention, slim empirical evidence supports this and little is known about how engagement in-<sup>fl</sup>uences online WOM in terms of both its volume and its valence. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that community engagement can not only lead members to create more online reviews after purchase but also improve their online review ratings.

Third, our study sheds light on how the e<sup>f</sup>ects of community engagement change depending on consumer tenure in the community. According to the theory of community commitment, when consumers engage in brand communities, their commitment to the communities or the brands changes over their membership. And such changed community commitment determines the e<sup>f</sup>ects of community engagement. Our results report that the impact of community engagement on online reviews is stronger for longer-tenured consumers than for shorter-tenured consumers. Moreover, community engagement improves customers’ generating of online reviews after purchase for both shorter-tenured consumers and longer-tenured ones. However, the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of customer engagement on online review ratings is only signi<sup>fi</sup>cant for longer-tenured consumers. Therefore, consumer tenure in the community plays a critical role in OBC studies, and this study contributes to the literature by emphasizing the dynamic nature of community engagement and shedding light on the context that manifests the e<sup>f</sup>ect of community engagement.

## 5.3. Managerial implications

The results of our study yield several important managerial implications for community managers, administrators, and moderators. Researchers and practitioners agree that both online product reviews and OBCs can be e<sup>fi</sup>cient marketing tools for <sup>fi</sup>rms to in<sup>fl</sup>uence their product sales. However, in most instances, <sup>fi</sup>rms have limited promotional budgets or resources and determining how to invest in marketing programs can be complex. In closely examining the relationship between community engagement and online product review behaviors, our study provides some practices and potential actions that could help managers.

First, consumer behavior in online product reviews can be in<sup>fl</sup>u enced not only by consumer characteristics, product characteristics, and previous reviews, but also by consumers’ engagement in <sup>fi</sup>rms’ community programs. Many studies have shown that an online product review can increase product sales via its valence, its volume, or content embedded in it. However, online reviews are likely to be out of managers’ control, and there are few strategies for <sup>fi</sup>rms to adopt to enhance their online reviews. Our results show that consumer online review behavior (i.e., generating post-purchase online reviews and ratings) can be enhanced by consumer engagement in OBCs. Encouraging consumers to engage in <sup>fi</sup>rms’ OBCs can increase both the volume and the valence of online reviews for the <sup>fi</sup>rms’ products. Thus, our study provides an ideal strategy to in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumers’ WOM buzz.

Second, this study o<sup>f</sup>ers some important insights into how marketers can tap into the potential value of OBCs and helps marketers gain a better understanding of return on investment accruing to OBC initiatives. In addition to improving consumer purchase behavior through community participation, our results reveal that consumer engagement in brand communities promotes their WOM. Therefore, managers should encourage customers to frequently participate in their brand communities through posting, replying, and other activities.

Finally, our results show that the impact of community engagement on consumer WOM behaviors is contingent on consumer tenure in the community, which provides actionable recommendations for managers in terms of targeting consumers based on their membership. On one hand, if the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s goal is to improve its online product review ratings, soliciting its existing, longer-tenured consumers into the brand com munities can be the only choice. On the other hand, if its goal is to increase the volume of online product reviews, encouraging all consumers to engage in its brand community can be e<sup>f</sup>ective strategies, and enrolling longer-tenured consumers can be more e<sup>f</sup>ective. Thus, our research allows managers to selectively promote engagement among consumers.

## 6. Conclusion

In this study, we examine the impact of consumer engagement in a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s OBC on important indicators of consumer WOM behaviors, generating of online product reviews and ratings after purchase. We further examine the moderating role of consumer tenure. We draw on community engagement and online review literature to formulate our hypotheses and test them using a unique dataset, which combines data on consumer transactions, community engagement, and online reviews. We use a control function approach to rule out self-selection concerns.

Our results show that consumer engagement in an OBC increases both their generating of online reviews and online review ratings after purchase. The results also show that the impact of community engagement on online WOM is dynamic depending on consumer tenure in the community. The results of our study provide unique theoretical and managerial insights for researchers and practitioners, respectively.

This study su<sup>f</sup>ers from some limitations. First, our results are based on a dataset from a single <sup>fi</sup>rm and its consumers and brand community members. Nevertheless, the phenomenon of consumer engagement in an online community is not unique to the community we studied [48]. Second, our research context does not entail randomized trials or <sup>fi</sup>eld experimentation on community engagement constructs of interest. As such, although we spent considerable e<sup>f</sup>ort to address concerns related to the endogeneity issue, our identi<sup>fi</sup>cation strategy centering on a control function approach only a<sup>f</sup>ords us a solution to alleviate the issue. Third, OBCs can be grouped into two major types based on the criterion of who hosts them: consumer-initiated communities and company-initiated communities. The brand community in our study is <sup>fi</sup>rm-sponsored, and previous studies show that social in<sup>fl</sup>uence derived from di<sup>f</sup>erent types of brand communities could be di<sup>f</sup>erent for their members. Therefore, caution should be employed when generalizing results across di<sup>f</sup>erent forms of OBCs. Finally, the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm in our study manufactures and sells apparel, which is an experience product. Previous studies have typically argued and demonstrated that con sumers respond di<sup>f</sup>erently to experience and search products. Thus, until the product characteristics are accounted for and examined in future research, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings should not be generalized beyond thei original intent.

We foresee many avenues for future research. First, future studies can extend this study by better measuring community engagement. Researchers can use text mining to identify the discussion topics among customers and then measure community engagement by counting meaningful postings and replies in two-way conversational relationships. Second, future studies can collect data from <sup>fi</sup>rms that produce and sell other types of products and examine whether our results are generalized. Third, future study can address the concerns related to the endogeneity issue of the community engagement variable by conducting lab experiments and measuring community engagement using developed scales. Fourth, checking for generalizability of our results across di<sup>f</sup>erent forms of OBCs would be an interesting addition to this study. Lastly, OBCs are a type of marketing tool <sup>fi</sup>rms use to in<sup>fl</sup>uence customer behavior. Future studies can investigate how <sup>fi</sup>rms’ usage of other marketing tools, such as price promotions, can a<sup>f</sup>ect customer behavior in online WOM.
