# From Detractors to Enhancers: Harnessing the Power of Ad Customization for User Engagement on Media Websites

Martin Adam,<sup>1</sup> Dominick Kosin,<sup>2</sup> Alexander Benlian<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup> University of Goettingen, Germany, martin.adam@uni-goettingen.de <sup>2</sup> McKinsey & Company, Germany, dominick.kosin@gmail.com <sup>3</sup> Technical University of Darmstadt, Germany, benlian@ise.tu-darmstadt.de

## Abstract

Media websites (e.g., news, video platforms) rely on user engagement to ensure profitability, growth, and relevance. To this end, they often offer customization options to encourage users to spend more time on pages, visit more pages, and return to the website. Previous studies have focused on web customization for desirable editorial content (e.g., topics of news articles). Our research examines ad quantity customization (AQC), or how users interact with websites that offer choice-based versus direct-exposure advertising models. We reveal a counterintuitive dynamic: While ads are typically viewed as detrimental to user engagement, they can be strategically leveraged to enhance it. In a randomized field experiment covering 17,289 visits to a European music news website, only two thirds (66.6%) of visitors used AQC to eliminate ad exposure, while the remaining one third (33.4%) opted for a reduced or regular ad quantity. The provision of AQC was associated with 20.5% more time spent on the website, 9.5% more unique pages visited, and a 37.8% higher return rate. Furthermore, the impact of AQC on visit duration was particularly strong for users who visited the website using mobile (vs. stationary) devices. Supplementary analyses further revealed that participants accessing an AQC-enabled website spent 13.7% more time on the site and visited 6.4% more unique pages compared to participants accessing an ad-free website. This study counterintuitively reveals that elements that typically detract from user engagement can be strategically utilized to enhance it and outlines specific circumstances where users may prefer the presence over the absence of ads.

Keywords: Customization, User Engagement, Advertisements, Active Control, Empowerment, Field Experiment, Media Websites

## 1 Introduction

Since news and other media websites (e.g., social media, forums, video platforms) exhibit one of the highest densities of ads found online, ads represent an integral part of users’ browsing experience on these websites (Englehardt & Narayanan, 2016). In contrast to transactional websites (e.g., e-commerce platforms), whose content serves mainly to facilitate purchase decisions, the primary offering of media websites is the content itself. If media websites wish to be successful, their editorial (e.g., news) and non-editorial content (e.g., ads) need to align with users’ interests (e.g., Benlian, 2015; Köster et al., 2020). Accordingly, how users assess ads as part of the media website’s content is critical to their likelihood of engaging with the website (Liu & Shrum, 2002).

Media websites are increasingly exploring new methods to boost user engagement by moving away from directly exposing users to prescribed quantities of advertisements (e.g., banner ads) and instead offering options for ad quantity customization (AQC)—a website feature that empowers users to choose the quantity of ads displayed on the website. With AQC, websites are cautiously trading their firm grip on ad quantity and associated ad revenues in exchange for the prospect of higher user engagement with the website (e.g., Yan et al., 2022). User engagement is considered a critical success criterion for revenuegenerating media websites because while visitors’ consumption of content on these websites is typically voluntary and intrinsically motivated, it is critical to the exposure, appeal, and influence of the website’s offerings (e.g., Burton-Jones & Straub Jr., 2006; Qahri-Saremi & Turel, 2020; Venkatesh et al., 2008). Moreover, the more visitors that engage with a website—for example, by spending time on it, visiting unique pages, and returning to the website—the more likely it is that they will click on displayed ads, links, and other revenue-generating offerings (e.g., Mallapragada et al., 2016; Thorbjørnsen & Supphellen, 2004; Yan et al., 2022). As such, online news outlets such as The Guardian (Southern, 2019) and Newsquest (Newsquest, n.d.) have appealed to their users by integrating a choice-based feature that allows them to opt for fewer or even no ads. Similarly, streaming portals such as HBO (Metz, 2021) and Hulu (Hulu, n.d.) have implemented selection options for ad-light and ad-free streaming experiences. Most prominently, YouTube empowers its users by offering a skip feature that allows users to shorten their exposure to ads or even avoid them altogether (e.g., Belanche et al., 2020; Dukes et al., 2022). In light of these practices, AQC represents a key technological trend for website providers seeking long-term user base growth over short-term ad revenue (Dewan et al., 2002).

Providing AQC can impact users profoundly, given that the average user encounters more than 10 ads on every web page they visit (Miklosik et al., 2018). Even though third-party ad blockers are becoming less and less effective, as website providers are increasingly detecting and barring their use (e.g., O’Flaherty, 2023; Gritckevich et al., 2022; Todri, 2022), nearly half of all online users employ them to reduce their exposure to ads (e.g., Aseri et al., 2020; Globalwebindex, 2018). One would therefore presume that the intuitive ideal for users would be ad-free websites rather than websites that offer ad customization. However, it is unclear whether users would be more engaged on websites that display no ads whatsoever or on websites where they could control the typically immutable quantity of ads they are exposed to. Indeed, reports in practice indicate that 77% of ad-blocker users explicitly wish they could filter ads rather than blocking them completely (An, 2016). Put differently, rather than reducing engagement, we question whether the presence of ads might even increase user engagement with a website if users were empowered by AQC to customize the quantity of ads they receive. We also wish to explore how users would employ AQC to customize ads. To fully grasp the potential impact of AQC on user engagement, it is important for website providers to understand how AQC implementation influences key engagement metrics, such as time spent on websites, extent of page exploration, and number of return visits. Perhaps more interestingly, we need to understand how the level of user engagement elicited by AQC compares to the level of user engagement invoked by the intuitive ideal of a website that, by default, does not display any ads.

While previous IS research has provided valuable insights into how web customization affects user engagement, our knowledge in this field of research remains limited in three important ways. First, previous studies have directed their focus toward the customization of websites’ editorial content pertaining to the main purpose of the website (e.g., news articles, product information) (e.g., Jiang et al., 2010; Xue et al., 2020). However, the customization of ads is fundamentally distinct from the customization of editorial content because, in contrast to sought-after editorial content, users do not typically aspire to be exposed to unsolicited content in the form of ads. In fact, users may even prefer such content to be attenuated or omitted entirely and intentionally engage in ad avoidance behaviors that severely weaken the effectiveness of website ads (Seyedghorban et al. 2016). Therefore, inviting users to customize ads they essentially never asked for may backfire in terms of user engagement, particularly if the alternative were a website that had no ads at all. Second, IS scholars have mainly investigated customization with respect to information composition (i.e., what?) and format (i.e., in which way?) (e.g., Benlian, 2015; Jiang & Benbasat, 2007b; Luo et al., 2023). Given that ads are collateral elements of many websites, the context of ads is especially well-suited to exploring customization with respect to information quantity (i.e., how much?)—a thus far disregarded customization type that is critical to broadening our understanding of customization features. Lastly, previous literature has provided insights into the overall effects of web customization (e.g., Ariely, 2000; Jiang et al., 2010). However, scholars have cautioned against treating all users uniformly; rather, it is essential to account for user attributes that might critically govern the effectiveness of web customization (e.g., Benlian, 2015; Lee, 2005; Wottrich et al., 2017). Based on ads’ characteristics as sizeable website elements occupying substantial screen space, the user’s device type (i.e., stationary device with larger screen vs. mobile device with smaller screen) is particularly decisive for AQC.

Currently, mobile device use is surpassing the use of stationary devices, with likely consequences on how websites and related ad displays are experienced (e.g., Fink & Papismedov, 2023; Wu et al., 2022). Further, users might use different device types for different contexts (e.g., Ghose et al., 2013; Todri et al., 2020), further influencing the effectiveness of AQC for different device types and leading to different degrees of user engagement. It is thus important to consider the user attribute of device type in order to reveal how web customization features like AQC should be targeted given a multifaceted user base. We seek to advance the knowledge on web customization by answering the following research questions: (1) How does AQC affect user engagement with websites? (2) How is this effect moderated by the user’s device type?

To answer our research questions, we collaborated with a European music news website and conducted a randomized field experiment (e.g., Fink, 2022). Our research offers novel insights into an underexplored approach to web customization: leveraging choicebased (vs. direct-exposure) ad delivery models to boost user engagement. Our counterintuitive findings demonstrate how website elements typically viewed as engagement detractors can, under conditions of choice and empowerment, actually serve to augment engagement. Moreover, by embracing AQC within their strategic arsenal of options, website providers could initiate a paradigm shift in value capture, moving from strict ad control via direct-exposure ad quantities to a more engaging model that leverages choice-based ad quantities in order to captivate and expand their user base. Accordingly, offering AQC and related choicebased ad quantities could potentially shift website providers’ perspectives on ads as one of the most contentious elements of today’s websites.

## 2 Related Literature and Theoretical Background

## 2.1 User Engagement and Web Customization

Given the typically voluntary and intrinsically motivated nature of content consumption on media websites, visitors’ user engagement with websites is often measured in the form of visit duration, unique pages visited, and return rates (e.g., Burton-Jones & Straub Jr, 2006; Köster et al., 2020; Venkatesh et al., 2008). These measurements correlate highly with the consumption of website content and thus the website’s exposure, appeal, and influence, as well as the website’s revenue generation via ads, links, and other offerings (e.g., Mallapragada et al., 2016; Thorbjørnsen & Supphellen, 2004; Yan et al., 2022). Visit duration is considered a particularly important metric for user engagement on content websites, as some revenue generations are strongly linked not only to whether users see content (e.g., ads) but also to how long users see it (e.g., Danaher et al., 2006; Yan et al., 2022).

To increase user engagement, media websites often use web customization to allow users to exert active control over the website’s design (e.g., color, layout) and content (e.g., editorial content and advertising content) through some modification interface (e.g., selecting the website to appear in a dark theme) (e.g., Ariely, 2000; Benlian, 2015; Bockstedt & Goh, 2014; Garett et al., 2016; Luo et al., 2023). As such, web customization allows users to actively shape the website. This active participation of users is what sets web customization apart from web personalization, which autonomously individualizes the website’s content based on behavioral tracking without including users’ explicit input in the process (Tam & Ho, 2005). Web customization is gaining attention and relevance as a means for users to decide for themselves which website content to experience, rather than settling for web personalization, which involves preselected content prescribed by a monitoring system making an informed guess (Matt et al., 2019; Sundar & Marathe, 2010; Sutanto et al., 2013).

Extant literature on web customization has investigated the effects on user engagement in diverse applications, such as customizing the content of news aggregators (Benlian, 2015), choosing the order of product information (Ariely, 2000), determining which products to suggest based on desired product features (Adomavicius et al., 2011; Xiao & Benbasat, 2007; Xu et al., 2014), and specifying interactions with virtual representations of physical products (Jiang & Benbasat, 2007a; Yi et al., 2015). Notably, these contexts center around customizing the composition and format of editorial content, which is content that pertains to the main purpose of the website—in contrast to collateral advertising content, which is the focus here.

Furthermore, the web customization literature has repeatedly emphasized taking user attributes into account when studying web customization—even though the empirical investigation of such influences is still largely pending (e.g., Benlian, 2015; Gu et al., 2013; Lee, 2005). Given the rise of mobile devices (versus stationary devices) as a medium to consume website content, we find the role of device type in the context of web customization particularly intriguing (e.g., Fink & Papismedov, 2023; Wu et al., 2022). Specifically, the related differences in website presentation that users experience on their devices, such as different display sizes that cause them to experience ads and websites differently, are likely to affect users’ preferences regarding AQC (e.g., Ghose et al., 2013; Gu et al., 2013; Müller et al., 2017). Consequently, device type may be an important consideration for websites using AQC. As such, we set out to explore an underinvestigated facet of how web customization affects user engagement by studying not only how users customize the quantity of ads—as information they never explicitly asked for—but also how the user attribute of device type may influence these effects.

## 2.2 Psychological Empowerment Theory

Enabling users to customize ad quantity is a form of empowerment, characterized as “any means strengthening a person’s perception of selfdetermination and self-efficacy and reducing conditions contributing to feelings of powerlessness” (Füller et al., 2009). To understand the nature of empowerment perceptions, we draw on psychological empowerment theory as our theoretical lens. According to this theory, the notion of psychological empowerment (henceforth referred to as perceived empowerment) designates an individual’s sense of motivation that occurs when they have the authority to fully utilize an object in line with their preferences (Spreitzer, 1995; Thomas & Velthouse, 1990). Although it is related to the general sense of agency and control, perceived empowerment focuses on the sense of obtaining the possibility to meaningfully influence activities and decisions in ways that were previously unavailable (Alt et al., 2019; Amichai-Hamburger et al., 2008; Zimmerman, 1995).

Perceived empowerment has several facets. For users to feel empowered in a task (e.g., exploring a website), psychological empowerment theory suggests that at least one of three cognitions must be present: selfdetermination, competence, or impact (Spreitzer, 1995). Self-determination describes an individual’s perceived autonomy in accomplishing a task, competence refers to an individual’s belief in having the ability to execute the task effectively, and impact reflects an individual’s belief that their efforts matter for the outcome of the task.

Applied to the context of this study, enabling users to customize the quantity of ads they are exposed to on a website directly relates to the three cognitions linked to perceived empowerment. First, users are free to choose which ad quantity they would like to set. This awareness of choice promotes users’ autonomy in shaping their browsing experience, thus enhancing their self-determination. Second, since users can regulate ad quantity according to their personal needs, they will presumably be more confident in exploring the website, thus strengthening their sense of competence. Third, adjusting the quantity of ads they receive has instant and concrete consequences for users’ browsing experience, thus giving them a sense of impact in their customization choice. Taken together, we anticipate that AQC’s ability to enhance feelings of self-determination, competence, and impact will lead users to feel empowered when browsing the website (Spreitzer, 1995; Thomas & Velthouse, 1990).

Moreover, psychological empowerment theory suggests that users who feel empowered are more active and productive than users who are not (Thomas & Tymon, 1994). This sentiment has been confirmed in various IS contexts. For example, in the context of work, research has shown that empowered users are more likely to effectively utilize customer relationship management systems (Kim & Gupta, 2014) and more willing to use knowledge management systems (Kang et al., 2017). Beyond the context of work, research has shown that users exhibit a stronger intention to reuse websites that empower them by, for example, allowing them to customize the website’s appearance (e.g., Benlian, 2015; Jiang & Benbasat, 2007b; Kamis et al., 2008; Luo et al., 2023).

Overall, psychological empowerment theory provides the theoretical lens to understand why the customization of ad content, which has traditionally been viewed as an engagement impediment, can paradoxically enhance user interaction when approached from an empowerment perspective. Thus, it is well suited for understanding how AQC on websites empowers users and how this empowerment affects user engagement.

## 3 Research Model and Hypothesis Development

Building on psychological empowerment theory and the web customization literature, we argue that the presence (vs. absence) of AQC increases user engagement (H1). User engagement in hedonic and voluntary use contexts with intrinsically motivated content consumption is most prominently measured as visit duration, but also as unique pages visited and return rates to capture different nuances of user behavior (e.g., Burton-Jones & Straub Jr, 2006; Köster et al., 2020; Venkatesh et al., 2008). The gist of our theorizing is that users who are empowered by AQC will exhibit greater user engagement—that is, they will spend more time on the website (H1a), explore more pages (H1b), and return more often (H1c). Additionally, we hypothesize how the effect of AQC on user engagement is moderated by whether users experience more empowerment via mobile (vs. stationary) devices (H2a-c).

## 3.1 The Main Effect of AQC on User Engagement

We argue that as a form of web customization, AQC elevates user engagement by intensifying users’ motivation to delve into website content (Cacioppo, 1986; Kim & Gupta, 2014). This enhanced motivation is critical, as it correlates with a more thorough exploration of the website, which could in turn manifest in prolonged engagement, including longer session durations, the exploration of a wider range of pages, and more return visits. Conversely, the lack of such motivation may result in a cursory examination of the website, thereby reducing the chance of meaningful user interaction and the discovery of useful content. The impact of AQC on user engagement operates on both cognitive and affective dimensions (Ariely, 2000; Sundar, 2012). On a cognitive level, AQC has the capacity to meet varied and fluid user needs through user-driven customization, appealing to cognitive aspects of engagement. On an affective level, AQC can evoke captivating feelings of empowerment among users by promoting active participation.

We suggest that AQC enhances user engagement on a cognitive level by providing individualized content, a direct result of the customization process. Central to web customization is the concept of active control, or the ability to align information with diverse and evolving informational needs (Ariely, 2000). This heterogeneity arises from both stable individual preferences (Seyedghorban et al., 2016) and dynamic contextual factors (e.g., accessing a website on a mobile device with a small screen). AQC’s value lies in its flexibility, enabling users to tailor ad quantities to their specific needs. Contrary to the assumption that users prefer no ads, research has shown that users are interested in ads that fulfill informational needs (e.g., Brajnik & Gabrielli, 2010; Kaiser & Song, 2009), suggesting that some ads may be preferable to no ads. Therefore, we predict that users who are able to choose their optimal ad quantity will be better able to deeply engage with the website content, both editorial and promotional, leading to an increased likelihood of discovering engaging information and thus bolstering user engagement.

Moreover, we expect AQC to increase user engagement on an affective level through perceived empowerment. As outlined above, psychological empowerment theory implies that AQC induces a sense of empowerment through feelings of selfdetermination, competence, and impact. This is in line with previous IS research demonstrating that giving users the opportunity to make technological choices increases perceptions of empowerment (e.g., Füller et al., 2009; Junglas et al., 2019; Marathe & Sundar, 2011). The extant literature has found that empowerment on websites leads to enhanced user satisfaction (Liang et al., 2006), greater user loyalty (Pierrakos et al., 2003), improved personal identification with the website brand (Johnson et al., 2006), and more positive user attitudes about the website (Kalyanaraman & Sundar, 2006). Similarly, known as the “IKEA effect,” users attribute greater value to both digital offerings (e.g., websites) and physical products (e.g., furniture) when they are involved in the creation process (Franke et al., 2010). Similarly, users express a stronger intention to revisit websites that empower them (e.g., Benlian, 2015; Jiang & Benbasat, 2007b; Kamis et al., 2008). Notably, this outcome of empowerment is independent of how users customize the website. In fact, making users merely believe they have customized a website’s content—without allowing them to actually modify anything—may be sufficient to elicit more favorable interaction intentions (Bright & Daugherty, 2012). As suggested by psychological empowerment theory, users who are empowered are more active and productive than users who are not (Thomas & Tymon, 1994). Applied to the context of empowering users to configure a website, “customization can invite deliberate and careful scrutiny of the website, rather than just casual browsing” (Kalyanaraman & Sundar, 2006). In other words, users will likely engage more with a website they are empowered to influence or shape (Kalyanaraman & Sundar, 2006; Sundar et al., 2003).

Taken together, we argue that both the ability of AQC to dynamically adjust to users’ cognitive needs and the feeling of empowerment AQC induces will enhance user engagement by enticing users to spend more time on the website, explore more pages, and return more often. We thus hypothesize:

H1: The presence (vs. absence) of AQC will increase user engagement with the website such that users will (a) spend more time on the website, (b) visit more unique pages, and (c) return more often.

## 3.2 The Moderating Effect of Device Type

Our study also seeks to transcend the basic examination of AQC’s main effects, aiming to elucidate its subtler dynamics. Responding to the scholarly call for a deeper analysis of user attributes in web customization (e.g., Benlian, 2015; Gu et al., 2013; Lee, 2005), our study posits that AQC’s impact is not uniform but varies according to individual differences in ad quantity preferences. We specifically spotlight the role of device type as a critical moderating factor. This distinction is paramount, as the cognitive load imposed by ad quantity is substantially impacted by whether users access content on mobile or stationary devices and could fundamentally alter AQC’s effectiveness. In line with psychological empowerment theory’s sensitivity to context (Spreitzer, 1995; Thomas & Velthouse, 1990), we thus acknowledge the importance of situational factors in shaping the outcomes of perceived empowerment. We propose that AQC’s impact varies across users, influenced by their differential preferences for ad quantity. To this end, we specifically investigate the moderating role of device type within this context, which has important implications for users’ cognitive load in handling varying levels of ad quantities.

Applied to AQC, we argue that users of mobile devices (e.g., smartphones, tablets), as opposed to stationary devices (e.g., desktops, laptops), may be particularly apt to increase user engagement through AQC. The small screen sizes of mobile devices increase the cognitive effort of browsing online, thereby occupying processing resources that users can no longer draw on to cognitively suppress and disregard ads (e.g., Ghose et al., 2013; Gu et al., 2013; Müller et al., 2017). At the same time, since ads are often designed to maintain a similar physical size on all devices, ads may occupy relatively more of the available space on small screens, thereby exacerbating the impression of ad clutter (Visuri et al., 2017). If unsolicited ads appear more saliently on smaller screen sizes, controlling these ads through AQC becomes particularly important. Aligned with psychological empowerment theory (Spreitzer, 1995), feeling selfdetermined, competent, and impactful in handling the website experience may thus be more important for users of mobile devices than for users of stationary devices. Finally, users typically employ mobile (vs. stationary) devices in contexts that are cognitively taxing (e.g., while traveling), making control over the appearance of content particularly important (e.g., Fink & Papismedov, 2023; Wu et al., 2022).

Our argumentation is also in line with research highlighting, for example, that perceived empowerment depends on situational factors beyond individual traits (e.g., Maruping & Magni, 2015; Spreitzer, 1995; Zimmerman, 1990) and that users prefer more personal and tailored content options when using mobile (vs. stationary) devices (e.g., Song & Sela, 2023). Thus, we hypothesize that AQC will have greater effects on mobile users:

H2: The effect of AQC on user engagement is greater on mobile devices than on stationary devices such that in the presence of AQC, users of mobile devices will (a) spend more time on the website, (b) visit more unique pages, and (c) return more often than users of stationary devices.

## 4 Research Methodology

## 4.1 Website and Context of Field Experiment

We tested our hypotheses through a randomized field experiment, chosen for its robust balance of internal and external validity (e.g., Fink, 2022). Consistent with other randomized field experiments in IS (e.g., Adam et al., 2024; Roethke et al., 2020), the experiment was conducted in a real-world setting where we maintained control over the website design and manipulations. Participants were unaware of the experiment.

To conduct our field experiment, we partnered with a European news website primarily dedicated to the music industry in German-speaking countries (i.e., Germany, Austria, Switzerland). As one of the leading websites in its country and genre, it typically attracts around 6,000 users per week. A news website is ideally suited for our investigation for two reasons: First, news websites exhibit one of the highest density of ads found online, as their business models strongly depend on the display of ads (Englehardt & Narayanan, 2016). As such, ads represent an integral part of users’ browsing experience and therefore play an important role for users on news websites. Second, contrary to transactional websites (e.g., e-commerce platforms) whose content serves only to facilitate purchase decisions, the primary offering of news websites is the content itself. To be successful, news websites must provide content that captures and sustains users’ interest (Benlian, 2015; Köster et al., 2020). Accordingly, how users assess ads as part of the website’s content is critical to their likelihood of engaging with the website (Liu & Shrum, 2002).

Like other leading news websites, the cooperating website employs a uniform structure where each article page features proprietary text and images, supplemented with 10 recommendations for further articles on the website. For the duration of our field experiment, we minimized the number of links to external content (e.g., teasers for content on other websites) so that the website would not unintentionally lead participants away from the website. Figure 1 depicts an example of the website’s article page.

We set up two workshops with the website’s development team to ensure the high quality and consistency of our experimental setup. As a result of these discussions and an additional pilot test, we decided to implement the following measures: First, we utilized banner ads (also known as display ads), which are “small, typically rectangular graphic image[s] or display[s] on a webpage that link to a target site or product” (Sun et al., 2013) and constitute one of the most widely adopted online advertising formats. The content of these ads, and in some cases even their positioning, is generally determined by a third-party ad server that draws on behavioral targeting to display ads tailored to each user (Chen & Stallaert, 2014). While the personalization of ads aims to enhance the ads’ effectiveness, this practice impedes the comparability of different participants’ ad experiences, which is a vital prerequisite for our experimental design. Thus, for the duration of our data collection, we decided not to use an ad server and instead integrated a selfprogrammed script that uniformly deployed the same ads to every participant.

Second, it was important to us that the content of our ads would be considered mostly neutral while still being relevant to as many users as possible. To that end, our experiment, which was carried out during the COVID-19 pandemic, focused on the heightened need for alternatives to traditional physical activities, which were heavily curtailed due to factors like gym closures and limited outdoor options (Yang & Koenigstorfer, 2020). Accordingly, we decided to advertise and link to the provider of a fitness training app designed to help users interested in exercising at home.

Third, our ads were implemented as static images, one of the most common ad types on news websites (e.g., Lindlahr, 2021). Implementing our ads as embedded banner images and directly linking them to the website of the advertised fitness app caused potential ad blockers not to associate the banners with ad servers and consequently not to suppress our ads. To verify the efficacy of this approach, we developed a script that counted the total number of times participants’ devices prompted our ads to load during the experiment. By comparing this count to the number of ads that were expected to load, we were able to rule out potential external influences and thus confirm that our ads were indeed displayed to all participants, as intended.

## 4.2 Manipulations

We designed three conditions that participants accessing the website were randomly assigned to in a between-subjects design: (1) Our baseline condition (default ads) included four banner ads advertising the above-mentioned fitness app on every page of the website to reflect the typical ad density on websites. The ads were evenly spread out on each page without giving users any customization options. This condition closely resembles common practice on news websites. (2) Our control condition (no ads) contained no ads at all. We designed this condition to explore whether participants would prefer not to see any ads whatsoever instead of having the option to customize the number of ads to evaluate how this condition would perform in contrast to our baseline condition default ads. (3) Lastly, our treatment condition offering AQC (hereafter referred to as the website with customizable ads) allowed participants to determine the quantity of ads on the website. Specifically, participants accessing the website were shown an AQC interface that briefly explained and emphasized the possibility of customizing ad quantity along with three options: “regular amount of ads,” “reduced amount of ads,” and “no ads.” Selecting “regular amount of ads” deployed all four of the fitness app ads on each page of the website, as in the default ads control condition. The option “reduced amount of ads” cut the number of ads in half, deploying only two of the above-mentioned ads on each page of the website. Choosing “no ads” resulted in a website layout without ads. Based on feedback from participants evaluating the interface in a pilot test, we chose to add sketches to each customization option to provide corresponding visual representations of what the website would look like. Being able to see a drafted preview of the website with each customization option reduced participants’ decision effort by helping them understand which configuration would best suit their needs. We displayed the visualizations on a symbolic smartphone, as the majority of the website’s users usually employ mobile devices. Figure 2 outlines the interface participants saw in the AQC treatment condition.

To verify the design of our experimental conditions, we set up a pretest with 197 participants recruited on the crowdsourcing platform Amazon Mechanical Turk (Behrend et al., 2011). Using a fictitious news website similar to our field experiment website, we introduced each participant to one of the three conditions designed for our main experiment: the baseline website with default ads, the control website with no ads, or the treatment website with customizable ads. We performed several manipulation checks: Perceived ad clutter, measured with three items adapted from Cho and Cheon (2004), was significantly lower for no ads compared to default ads $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , and the perceived customizability of ad quantity, measured with four items adapted from Bright and Daugherty (2012), was significantly higher for customizable ads compared to both default ads and no ads $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . In addition, we found no significant difference in the perceived customizability of ad quantity between default ads and no ads $( p > 0 . 1 )$ . Hence, the results of our pretest confirmed that our design worked as intended.

## 4.3 Experimental Procedure and Data Collection

We guided participants on the website as follows: Immediately upon entering the website, we randomly assigned participants to one of the three possible conditions. Several scripts simultaneously recorded information about participants’ devices (e.g., stationary vs. mobile) and which search engine participants used to access our experimental website (e.g., Google, DuckDuckGo). Participants in the treatment condition were presented with the AQC interface depicted in Figure 2 and asked to choose one of the three AQC options. Participants in the baseline or control conditions continued directly with the next step. Participants then explored the website and its pages at their leisure. All pages showed the same content (i.e., the fitness app) and number of ads, subject to the condition the participant was assigned to and, if applicable, the selected ad quantity option. The user’s treatment group allocation and, if applicable, their chosen ad quantity was stored and reapplied when the user accessed the website again at a later point in time to ensure that users experienced the same treatment over the course of the experiment.

The data collection lasted 4.5 weeks, from January 28 to March 1, 2021. We utilized the open source web analytics application Matomo (Matomo, n.d.) as well as self-developed scripts to capture the clickstream data of each participant’s visit. By drawing on session data instead of self-reported data, our results were not confounded by issues of recall or social desirability bias (e.g., Huang et al., 2009), thus ensuring high external validity.

## 4.4 Dependent Variables and Controls

The primary dependent variables of our experiment were visit duration (in seconds), or how long participants stayed on the website during their visit; unique pages visited, or the number of unique pages participants explored during each visit; and return rate, or how many of our participants returned and visited our website at least one more time during our experiment (i.e., returned vs. not returned). These measurements—especially visit duration—represent useful proxies for user engagement, particularly in hedonic and voluntary use contexts, which is the case in users’ engagement with a news website (e.g., Demers & Lev, 2001; Köster et al., 2020; Qahri-Saremi & Turel, 2020; Venkatesh et al., 2008). Moreover, these metrics are crucial for content-centric websites like news platforms, where sustained and regular content consumption is a primary indicator of success. Thus, the longer users linger, the more they see; likewise, the more often they return, the more revenue the website generates through subscription models, ads, and sponsored articles (e.g., Danaher et al., 2006; Luo et al., 2020).

We started measuring our dependent variables only after participants were given full access to the website. Thus, for the AQC treatment condition, the time required to read and interact with the AQC interface was excluded to avoid confounding effects. We furthermore captured device type by collecting technical properties such as operating system and screen area to identify whether participants were using a mobile (i.e., phone, tablet) or stationary device (i.e., desktop, laptop).

To increase the robustness of our analyses, we collected various controls. First, we incorporated the control condition no ads to demonstrate the robustness of the customized ads effect against a website without any ads, which is generally considered most desirable for user engagement on media websites. Next, we included return rate for the analyses on visit duration and unique pages visited, controlling for whether a participant had already visited the website during the experiment and whether this variable affected the other dependent variables. Drawing on the available log files, we also compared whether the participants reached our website through organic traffic (i.e., via a search engine or paid partnerships) and identified the country (i.e., Germany, Austria, Switzerland) and city from which participants accessed the website. Based on the identified participants countries, we used official databases to look up the number of people in the respective city and used population size to capture and control for different urbanization levels. Moreover, we measured privacy sensitivity by checking whether participants accessed our experimental website through a privacy-protective search engine, such as DuckDuckGo or MetaGer, so that we could evaluate whether privacy-sensitive users respond to AQC differently. Lastly, for our treatment condition customizable ads, we measured time on AQC interface— that is how much time participants spent on the AQC interface before selecting an option and subsequently experiencing the main part of the website—to account for potential unintended influences from users’ latencies or rushed behaviors.

## 5 Analysis and Results

## 5.1 Sample Description and Controls

During our field experiment, we recorded a total of 20,511 participants accessing our website over 21,800 visits. The customizable ads group had an intentionally larger sample size regarding visits (N = 10,463) than the default ads (N = 5,882) and no ads groups (N = 5,455) in order to ensure a sufficient sample size for potential supplementary subsample investigations and comparisons (e.g., selecting “no ads” vs. “reduced/regular amount of ads”). Of the 21,800 visits, we filtered out 3,222 (14.8%) instances of instant departure before the website completed loading, characterized by no engagement actions such as scrolling or clicking, indicating accidental website access or internet connection issues. Specifically, we removed 599 (10.2%) participants in the default ads group, 455 (8.3%) in the no ads group, and 2,168 (20.7%) in the customizable ads group. The larger percentage of participants immediately leaving the website in the customizable ads group was anticipated and likely due to the website not directly showing the content and instead requiring the users to interact with the AQC interface. Since in these incidences, participants showed no sign of interacting with the website in any way, participants could not have been aware of the website’s content or manipulations, so we removed these visits. Furthermore, we had no means of ascertaining whether participants were continuously exploring the website or whether they suspended their exposure by switching to other tabs or programs or leaving their device temporarily before returning to the website at a later point.

## 5.2 Model-Free Results

First, we looked at the descriptives in Table 2. Our analysis concentrates on the overarching effects of customizable ads versus default ads in accordance with our stated hypotheses. We discuss more detailed insights into the subgroups of customizable ads in the Supplementary Analyses Section 5.6.

Participants in the customizable ads group had, on average, a higher visit duration, more unique pages visited, and a higher return rate than participants in the default ads group. This higher user engagement of participants in the customizable ads group is interestingly also higher in all three metrics compared to participants in the no ads group. Moreover, we found that mobile device users in the customizable ads group had a longer visit duration than stationary device users but fewer unique pages visited and a marginally lower return rate than participants in the default ads group.

As Table 3 shows, our regression analyses revealed a significant two-way interaction of customizable ads and device type (i.e., mobile device) on visit duration (model $\mathrm { A } 2 \colon \beta = 0 . 1 4 1 ; S E = 0 . 0 4 6 ; t = 3 . 1 0 9 ; p = 0 . 0 0 2 )$ compared to the baseline of default ads and stationary device users. Specifically, the combination of customizable ads and device type yielded a 12.0% (= exp(0.113)-1) higher visit duration, supporting H2a. However, we did not find a significant interaction effect on unique pages visited (Model B2: β = 0.044; SE = $0 . 0 3 7 ; z = 1 . 1 7 0 ; p = 0 . 2 4 2 )$ and return rate (Model C2: $\beta = - 0 . 1 3 2 ; S E = 0 . 2 7 6 ; z = - 0 . 4 7 7 ; p = 0 . 6 3 3 )$ . Thus, we did not find significant support for H2b and H2c.

## 5.5 Robustness Checks

We conducted additional robustness checks to further validate the reliability of our findings. In line with established robustness checks in prior IS studies conducting randomized field experiments (e.g., Huang et al., 2021; Lee & Hosanagar, 2019; Sun et al., 2019), we selected multiple subsamples of our dataset to rule out potentially spurious influences.

First, we examined whether outliers profoundly influenced our observed effects by removing the top and bottom 5% of users in terms of time spent per page for each of our website conditions (Aguinis et al., 2013). As we found highly similar result patterns in our main and interaction effects compared to our full sample, we conclude that the observed effects did not arise from a few unusually long or short visits to the website.

Second, we checked whether the time of participation could have affected our findings by comparing the first 25% of visits to the last 25% of visits, based on the date participants accessed the experimental website (Fowler, 2013). Using the Wilcoxon signed rank test for our dependent variables and performing several one-way analyses of variance for the distributional properties (e.g., device type), we did not find any significant differences (all p > 0.05) for any of our conditions between the first and last quartile of our data, indicating that our data sample was not affected by the time of participation (Lambert & Harrington, 1990).

Third, we checked whether our findings would have meaningfully changed if we had considered tablets to be stationary rather than mobile devices. However, in our field experiment, less than 2% of visitors (332 out of the 17,289 ) viewed the website via tablets, making it unlikely that recoding tablets would have substantially impacted the results. Indeed, recoding resulted in no significant differences in our hypotheses testing, making our results robust to different tablet categorizations.

Fourth, we checked for potentially spurious influences resulting from variations in participants’ locations by creating subsamples of our data set based on the three primary countries where participants were located (i.e., Germany, Austria, and Switzerland). Since the result patterns in each subsample were consistent with those of our full data set, we concluded that our observed effects are robust with respect to participants’ locations.

Fifth, we verified potential influences from the larger group size of the treatment condition compared to the baseline condition, even though group sizes do not generally need to be equal to derive statistically reliable inferences (Schulz & Grimes, 2002). We evened the group sizes by taking the full set of data points from the baseline group while reducing the sample size of our treatment condition to match that of the baseline group using random selection. Analyzing our main and interaction effects, we found that the results remained substantively unchanged from those of our full data set. As such, we found no indication that the effects revealed in our full data set were affected by the larger group size of our treatment group compared to the baseline group.

Sixth, we verified whether the effect on unique pages visited could have been the result of participants’ curiosity about whether the next page would reflect the same selected ad quantity as the first page. To do so, we compared how far down participants scrolled on the last page they visited before ending their session. Among participants who explored a second page or more, we found that those in the customizable ads group scrolled down to see about as much of the page (55.0%) as those in the baseline default ads group (57.5%) and the no ads control group (56.1%). Thus, we are confident that the increase in unique pages visited is indeed a sign of genuine user engagement and not an artifact of participants’ curiosity about the AQC feature.

Lastly, regarding returning participants, we compared how long they stayed and how many pages they visited during their first visit versus their subsequent visits when presented with the same condition. We found indications that the positive effects of AQC may persist over time. Without being able to control ad quantity, default ads participants spent 34.5% less time on the website upon returning (123.9 seconds on first visit vs. 81.2 seconds on subsequent visits) and visited 12.8% fewer unique pages (1.392 on first visit vs. 1.214 on subsequent visits), whereas no ads participants spent 52.0% less time upon returning (294.1 seconds first visit vs. 141.2 seconds subsequent visits) and visited 19.3% fewer unique pages (2.560 first visit vs. 2.067 subsequent visits). In contrast, customizable ads participants stayed almost as long on the website upon returning, exhibiting only a minor downtick in visit duration of 5.7% (263.6 seconds on first visit vs. 248.7 seconds on subsequent visits), and visited 22.9% more unique pages (2.176 on first visit vs. 2.674 on subsequent visits). We speculate that returning visitors presented with default ads were not excited about seeing a common, non-empowering, and thus unexciting website, making a second visit to the website even less exciting. For no ads participants, we presume that visitors were excited about encountering a news website without ads on their first visit but that this effect wore off upon their return to the website. In contrast, we presume that customizable ads participants who experienced the website again with the ad quantity they originally chose may have remembered the empowerment they felt upon first visiting the website. We hope that these results will inspire more fruitful research on the transfer of AQC effects to future visits, raising the intriguing question of whether AQC may be capable of sustainably increasing user engagement over longer periods of time.

## 5.6 Supplementary Analyses

In addition to our main analyses designed to test our hypotheses on the effects of AQC on user engagement via comparisons of customizable ads versus default ads, we provide supplementary analyses that reveal additional insights concerning participants’ reactions to customizable ads versus no ads. We also examined participants’ specific choices regarding customizable ads vs. default ads by examining the different choice subgroups. Table 4 provides details on each condition and subgroup.

Regarding the comparison between the customizable ads and no ads websites, our insights shed light on whether websites using AQC can outperform not only websites with regular amounts of ads but also websites with no ads at all in terms of user engagement. We report the modelfree results by comparing the means of our dependent variables across our conditions based on a Wilcoxon signed-rank test. The results demonstrate that customizable ads participants had a 13.8% higher visit duration (W = 16,076,090; p < 0.001) and a 6.4% higher unique pages visited (W = 17,734,693; p = 0.048) than no ads participants; the difference in return rate was not significant (W = 17,205,437; p = 0.331). Further, a linear regression analysis revealed that when specifically examining the mobile device type in conjunction with the customizable ads website, visit duration was 12.1% (= exp(0.114)-1) higher than the No Ads baseline, suggesting that mobile users accounted for most of the increase in visit duration. These results largely corroborate our main findings that providing AQC can increase user engagement, even when compared to websites that do not display any ads by default, and that device type impacts the effectiveness of AQC.

## 6 Discussion

Our study was motivated by the intriguing question of how offering AQC features on websites affects user engagement. Against this backdrop, we conducted a large-scale randomized field experiment on a European news website and found that participants who were allowed to customize the number of ads on a website exhibited greater user engagement than participants who were not. Specifically, users given the choice to customize ad quantity spent 20.5% more time on the website, visited 9.5% more unique pages, and had a 37.8% higher return rate compared to those provided with default ad settings. Notably, the boost in visit duration was more pronounced for mobile users, whereas there was no significant difference in the number of unique pages visited and the return rate between mobile and stationary device users. Interestingly, we found similar results in the supplementary analyses when comparing a website offering AQC at the presumed intuitive ideal—a website free of ads by default. We found that participants accessing the AQC-enabled website spent 13.7% more time on the site and visited 6.4% more pages, compared to participants accessing the ad-free website. Notably, 33.4% of participants voluntarily opted to view some ads, challenging the assumption that users universally prefer ad-free experiences.

## 6.1 Contributions to Research

Our study makes three pivotal contributions to research. Our primary contribution lies in revealing a largely ignored dimension of web customization—the customization of ad quantity. While previous research has investigated website features allowing users to customize editorial content (e.g., Ariely, 2000; Benlian, 2015; Jiang et al., 2010), these studies have not examined ads, which is an important omission due to the assumption that ads reduce user engagement (e.g., Seyedghorban et al., 2016; Todri et al., 2020). Our investigation reveals that allowing users to customize ad quantity goes beyond merely enhancing the alignment between editorial content and user preferences. Rather, it represents a form of user empowerment by giving users control over a traditionally non-customizable website element, which can improve the user experience and increase engagement. This distinction places ad customization in a separate category from the wellstudied area of editorial content customization. By investigating user engagement on websites offering choice-based versus direct-exposure ad quantity delivery models, our study demonstrates a pivotal shift in web customization in that an element typically seen as an engagement deterrent is actually revealed as a catalyst for increased user engagement—above and beyond the intuitive ideal of simply omitting the element. This discovery challenges the traditional negative connotations associated with ads, prompting a reevaluation of existing knowledge of web customization and opening up new avenues for IS research in line with the growing trend toward individualized user experiences (e.g., Alt et al., 2019; Tam & Ho, 2005; Vodanovich et al., 2010).

Second, our study significantly enhances the understanding of web customization types, moving beyond the traditional focus on content composition (i.e., what?) and format customization (i.e., in which way?) (e.g., Benlian, 2015; Jiang & Benbasat, 2007b; Luo et al., 2023) and introduces a novel dimension—the customization of quantity (i.e., how much?)—which is particularly relevant in ad-rich environments where clutter impedes user engagement (e.g., Cho & Cheon,

2004; Todri et al., 2020). This focus on choice-based ad quantity customization (vs. direct-exposure ad delivery) expands the boundaries of IS research, paving the way for a more nuanced understanding of the impact of customization on user engagement.

Third, our study also highlights the influence of device type, a crucial yet often overlooked user attribute, on the efficacy of AQC in enhancing user engagement. Building upon previous research emphasizing the importance of user attributes in understanding web customization (e.g., Benlian, 2015; Gu et al., 2013; Lee, 2005), our analysis reveals a noteworthy distinction: Users on mobile devices demonstrate more engagement in terms of time spent on websites where AQC is present, compared to users on stationary devices. However, this effect is limited to visit duration and does not extend to the number of unique pages visited or return rates. Although media sites largely consider visit duration to be the primary metric for user engagement—especially in the context of mobile devices (e.g., Burton-Jones & Straub, 2006; Venkatesh et al., 2008)—it is also important to examine the role of device type for other metrics that may be relevant for websites. Our findings suggest that the influence of AQC may be contextually bound, especially in mobile use scenarios with inherent time and cognitive constraints. Taking device type into account is important for research on web customization because it surfaces a thus far neglected boundary condition that determines how web customization is received. As such, considering device type could help to enhance the predictive accuracy of existing and future models (Sun & Zhang, 2006).

In addition to our main contributions to the IS research on web customization, we contribute to psychological empowerment theory by extending the theory to a wider range of decision-making contexts. Existing studies on psychological empowerment theory have primarily focused on perceived empowerment in terms of desirable elements, such as the distribution of information and resources (Dhillon et al., 2020; Spreitzer, 1995; Thomas & Velthouse, 1990) and the collaborative creation of products (Füller et al., 2009). Our scholarly contribution lies in the investigation of perceived empowerment in terms of elements that have traditionally been considered undesirable. Specifically, our empirical findings challenge the conventional view that perceived empowerment is exclusively dependent on positively valanced elements, demonstrating that negatively valanced elements can also be leveraged in a way that empowers users. We thereby broaden the theory’s purview to encompass a more diverse range of decision-making contexts.

## 6.2 Managerial Implications

Our study provides website providers with actionable guidance on ad quantity customization, particularly for news and other media websites. As our field study findings reveal, AQC can significantly boost user engagement—resulting in longer visit duration, more unique pages visited, and a greater number of return visits—ultimately benefiting the website’s bottom line. This boost in user engagement largely translates to comparisons between AQC-enabled and ad-free websites. Moreover, we discovered that AQC has a notably stronger impact on the visit duration of mobile users. Thus, managers should consider specifically targeting mobile users for AQC because they are more likely to respond positively. This approach could allow website providers to strike a balance between retaining control over the number of ads displayed and enhancing user engagement.

In this vein, AQC may be a particularly valuable strategy for website providers prioritizing user engagement over ad revenue, especially during the growth phase of a website. To expand their reach, website providers have traditionally been advised to reduce ad quantity initially and delay unrestrained ad display until a stable user base is established (Dewan et al., 2002). We propose an alternative perspective: rather than withholding ads by default, website providers could use AQC to allow users to determine their preferred ad quantity. This approach could enhance user engagement overall, strengthening the business model during and potentially beyond the growth period.

While AQC effectively increases engagement among users, it may also impact website revenue, specifically when considering the associated reduction in potential ad impressions and clicks in the short term. Indeed, as our field experiment shows, a rather high proportion of participants immediately departed the website when encountering the AQC interface (20.7% for customizable ads vs. 10.2% for default ads and 8.3% for no ads), indicating that the employment of AQC may have an immediate negative effect for the website. However, 33.4% of participants who remained on the website voluntarily chose to view ads, indicating that websites using AQC still have the opportunity to display and leverage ads. Indeed, AQC users may show increased ad click behavior and recall rates, as users may perceive the displayed ads to be more relevant when given the opportunity to “opt in” to ads (e.g., Hartemo, 2016; Luo et al., 2023). Moreover, even though, on average, AQC leads to users experiencing a reduced quantity of ads per web page, this does not necessarily mean that users experience fewer ads across the entire website experience. Specifically, in the long run, the higher user engagement associated with AQC may indirectly result in users experiencing the same number or even more ads than websites displaying the regular amount of ads because the higher number of pages visited by AQC adopters could compensate for the smaller amount of ads shown per web page. In this vein, crucial website metrics, such as below-the-fold ad impressions, are likely to improve with AQC.

Additionally, AQC could entice users employing ad blockers to allow a level of advertising that is acceptable to them in order to support the website and its content creators. These strategies are decisive not just for website providers seeking larger audiences but also for content creators, who can use their content to influence ad acceptance (YouTube, n.d.). As such, empowering users to customize ad quantity would not necessarily lead to a decline in website revenues and other crucial metrics and may even improve these metrics through user empowerment and better-tailored ads.

## 6.3 Strengths, Limitations, and Directions for Future Research

The present study has various strengths, including (1) the incorporation of a large-scale randomized field experiment with real consequences for a news website and its users (e.g., substantial impact on actual user engagement), (2) a sophisticated experimental setup that accounts for context-specific influences (e.g., the non-detectability of our ads by ad blockers), and (3) advanced measurement techniques that enabled us to detect crucial user attributes (e.g., identifying the device type used). Therefore, our findings are not only ecologically valid but also highly reliable. Like any study, our research has limitations; however, they offer important avenues for future research.

Our field experiment allowed us to demonstrate how web customization can be applied to advertising content on media websites. Nevertheless, future research could further the understanding of ad customization by uncovering the mechanism that drives the effect of AQC on user engagement—for example, by measuring and validating the mediating mechanism of perceived empowerment. Randomized online experiments with post hoc surveys and interviews could collect additional data and consider more controls in the analyses, which could shed further light on these underlying mechanisms. In this vein, it would also be interesting to investigate why one third of our participants opted to receive some quantity of ads and how perceived empowerment may have affected this choice.

Our findings could be further corroborated by taking different cultures into account (e.g., US, Asia), investigating website contexts beyond music news websites (e.g., streaming or social media platforms), considering different user motives when accessing the website (e.g., browsing vs. searching), or factoring in influential user traits (e.g., need for control). We also call for future research exploring how AQC affects users’ long-term engagement with the website after becoming accustomed to AQC—particularly in relation to rival websites lacking ad quantity customization options. For instance, future research could explore whether AQC can sustainably increase user engagement over multiple website visits, as suggested by our supplementary analyses. It would also be interesting to explore how AQC affects the amount of ads displayed over a series of website visits and to investigate whether it can prevent users from using ad blockers—factors that could ultimately expose AQC-empowered users to more ads.

We explored device type as an inherent user characteristic, deliberately refraining from its active manipulation to avoid artificial influences and thus preserve its natural effects. Future research could delve into the nuances of how device type moderates the effect of AQC and related user-driven ad customization strategies on user engagement. Subsequent studies could enhance the depth and robustness of these findings by examining both the observable and latent mechanisms driving the moderating effects. Such research could be instrumental in understanding different responses to ad customization across various devices and clarifying why certain moderation effects are strong and observed (e.g., visit duration) while others are not (e.g., unique pages visited, return rate).

Additionally, our study employed a largely standardized AQC interface targeted at briefly explaining the AQC option and increasing user engagement via empowerment without more specific website goals in mind. For instance, our AQC interface design did not aim to convince users to select higher ad quantities, to reduce user bounces, or to experiment with providing reasons for why the website uses ads and AQC, which future research could investigate more. Similarly, as our supplementary analyses revealed that users opting to receive some quantity of ads were the most engaged with the website. Future research could investigate the reasons for this by designing interfaces that guide users to support the website while enhancing user engagement. Such research could draw on research on the viability of adbased business models (e.g., Dewan et al., 2004; Fan et al., 2007; Lin et al., 2012) to reveal the potential financial benefits of offering AQC. By influencing users in their customization decisions, websites may be able to reap the benefits of increased user engagement while simultaneously maintaining or even increasing ad revenues. If users pay more attention to the ads they choose to be exposed to, this may result in higher ad click rates. Thus, through optimizing the interface, AQC could substantially strengthen the business models of evolving websites seeking to grow their user base as well as mature websites interested in further monetizing their traffic.

Lastly, to ensure comparability, our study concentrated on the quantity of ads, specifically focusing on a uniform ad type (i.e., banner ad), ad content (i.e., fitness training), and presentation style (i.e., static and neutral). However, future research could explore the empowerment potential inherent in more diverse and customizable ad formats, including variations in placement, size, and animation, as well as content diversity, such as new product releases and personalized promotions. Moreover, it would be interesting to see whether users would want to increase ad content beyond the default in certain situations of empowerment. Additionally, it would also be interesting to explore the potential customization of desirable and undesirable elements and to examine whether the synergy of customization and third-party personalization could optimize user engagement. Such investigations could not only deepen our understanding of ad customization strategies for maximum engagement but could also illuminate the complex interactions between various forms of customization and other web elements, thereby uncovering novel pathways to enhance user engagement.

## 6.4 Conclusion

Our study investigates the transformative potential of AQC to elevate user engagement on media websites. We demonstrate how offering user features to customize the quantity of ad content, traditionally viewed as an engagement impediment, can paradoxically enhance user interaction when approached from a novel empowerment perspective. The findings lay a solid groundwork for future inquiries, both in academia and practice, reshaping and expanding the current understanding of web customization. Our paper also sets a precedent for future research by reinterpreting user interactions with web elements as a function of user agency. Thus, we hope that our work not only advances the theoretical discourse but also motivates further practical explorations into the dynamic interplay between website content and user empowerment.
