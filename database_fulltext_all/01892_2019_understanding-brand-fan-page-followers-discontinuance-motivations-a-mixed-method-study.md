# Understanding brand fan page followers’ discontinuance motivations: A mixed-method study

Zhenya Tang<sup>a,⁎</sup>, Leida Chen<sup>b</sup>, Mark L. Gillenson<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup> College of Business, Mississippi State University, Starkville, MS 39762, United States

<sup>b</sup> Orfalea College of Business, California Polytechnic State University, San Luis Obispo, CA 93407, United States

<sup>c</sup> Fogelman College of Business and Economics, University of Memphis, Memphis, TN 38152, United State

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Brand fan page Discontinuance Followers Social commerce Unfollowing intention

## A B S T R A C T

As companies recognize the e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of social media as a marketing channel, <sup>fi</sup>rms are leveraging brand fan pages (BFPs) to foster deeper relationships with their customers. While most extant studies focus on the motivations behind customers’ participation in BFPs, only little is known about the discontinuance behaviors of BFP followers. This study identi<sup>fi</sup>es a comprehensive inventory of BFP followers’ unfollowing motivations. By conducting a mixed-method study, the study develops and empirically validates a <sup>fi</sup>ve-factor scale that measures BFP followers’ discontinuance motivations. The impact of the motivations on BFP users’ unfollowing intention is also examined. Theoretical and practical implications are subsequently discussed.

## 1. Introduction

Social media is changing how businesses communicate with their customers. In the past few vears, social media services such as Facebook, LinkedIn, and Twitter have gained much popularity among both individuals and companies [1,2]. To engage younger generations of customers and convey brand information and messages, an increasing number of companies have established brand fan pages (BFPs) in the social media space [1]. According to a recent report by DMR, as of September 2016, the number of BFPs on Facebook has reached 60 million attracting over 1 billion visitors monthly [3]. Similarly, Zhang et al. [4] report that 80% of Chinese micro-blogging users are connected with BFPs and that one popular BFP, Xiaomi, alone has attracted more than 10 million followers in Sina Weibo, a dominant social media platform in China.

A brand fan page is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a social media pro<sup>fi</sup>le created and managed by a company with the intention to market its products and services and foster closer relationships with consumers [1,2,5]. It provides marketers with an “unparalleled opportunity to disseminate commercial messages to literally hundreds of thousands of potential brand buyers without the cost associated with traditional mass media” ([6], p. 205). Consumers’ BFP participation has also led to a positive word of mouth (WOM) and consumption. Almost 70% of Twitter users report that they purchase the products marketed on the BFPs they follow, and 60% of Facebook users would recommend the brand they follow to friends [7]. Therefore, consumer participation in and loyalty to BFPs are critical to the success of a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s social media strategies. Despite their increasing reliance on BFPs for fostering closer relation ships with customers, many companies <sup>fi</sup>nd it di<sup>fi</sup>cult to retain their follower bases partially because social media users can interact with multiple companies or switch to other BFPs for similar contents, products, or services with minimal e<sup>f</sup>ort [8]. Studies have shown that 30% of Facebook users between the ages of 16 and 64 have unfollowed or unliked BFPs, with that <sup>fi</sup>gure rising to 38% in the sub-group of users aged 16 to 24 [9]. Because a sound follower base is a prerequisite for the survival of a BFP [10], failure to retain existing followers would seriously jeopardize the viability of BFPs and a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s social media strategies. Therefore, understanding the reasons why social media users unfollow BFPs is critical to customer retention and, ultimately, the success of BFPs. In the current study, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne BFP discontinuance as the user’s action to unfollow a previously followed BFP to remove the brand’s social media presence or interactions from the user’s pro<sup>fi</sup>le page.

Despite this practical pertinence, the extant information systems (IS) literature is limited in o<sup>f</sup>ering a theoretically grounded under standing of the motivations that drive follower discontinuance with BFPs. Prior research on this topic has mostly examined the impact of BFPs on customer-brand relationships (e.g. [2,11],) and the user’s motivations to follow and participate in BFPs (e.g. [12,13],). Little research attention has been devoted to understand unfollowers, who are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as individuals who stop following BFPs after their initial following decisions [14], and their discontinuance motivations. We argue that the viability of BFPs depends not only on how many followers they are able to acquire but also on how many followers are retained and continue to participate. Thus, there is a clear need to extend the extant literature by developing and empirically examining a research model of BFP discontinuance motivations. Therefore, the major objectives of thi research include the following:

1 Qualitatively investigate the motivations that drive BFP followers discontinuance intention;

2 Develop and purify a scale for measuring individuals’ motivations to unfollow BFPs;

3 Validate the unfollowing motivation scale on a separate sample of BFP followers;

4 Examine the impact of the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed motivations on BFP users unfollowing intention.

To achieve the above objectives, a multi-method study was under taken. Our study begins by formulating a sensitizing theoretical fra mework for guiding our exploration of BFP users’ discontinuance motivations. In the qualitative part of our mixed-method study, a categorizing process developed by Lincoln & Guba [15] was used to guide an open-ended survey of followers who stopped following at least one BFP in the past 3 months in an e<sup>f</sup>ort to inductively identify salient reasons why followers’ discontinuance intention was formed. A scale that measures BFP followers discontinuance motivations was formulated on the basis of the insights derived from the open-ended survey and subsequently incorporated in the two quantitative studies of Chinese social media users to identify the factorial structure of the scale and examine the reliability and validity of the scale. Data obtained from the second quantitative survey were also analyzed by the structural equation modeling (SEM) techniques to examine the impact of the motivations on BFP unfollowing intention.

The present article makes several contributions to the emerging literature on social media-based BFPs. First, to the best of our knowl edge, this study is among the <sup>fi</sup>rst to provide theoretical insights into understanding BFP followers’ discontinuance motivations. The sensitizing framework of BFP discontinuance motivations will likely generalize to other social media applications to enhance our understanding of IS discontinuance in the social media context. Second, the BFP dis continuance motivation scale, which is developed and empirically validated in this study, will be useful not only to future academic research in this area but also to practitioners whose organizations want to maintain strong and stable follower bases. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of our study are expected to o<sup>f</sup>er rich insights on how to improve the viability of BFPs in emerging social media platforms.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. First, in the subsequent sections, we review the relevant empirical and theoretical work on BFPs and develop a sensitizing framework of BFP followers discontinuance motivations. The hypotheses and research design are then explained, followed by data analysis and discussion of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from our qualitative and quantitative studies. We discuss the theoretical and practical implications of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings before concluding the paper.

## 2. Related work and theoretical foundation

## 2.1. Brand fan pages

Brand fan pages are public social media pro<sup>fi</sup>les created and managed by companies to promote corporate brands and engage existing and potential customers [1,2,5]. Once a social media user clicks “follow,” “like,” or “subscribe,” depending on the functionalities o<sup>f</sup>ered by each social media platform, the user becomes a “fan” of the brand, and updates from the BFP will appear on the fans’ social media timeline automatically [16]. On the BFP, a company creates brand posts containing anecdotes, photos, videos, or other interactive contents; fans can then interact with the company and other social media users by “sharing,” “liking,” or “commenting on” these brand posts [17].

Given the fact that BPFs are increasingly recognized as an e<sup>f</sup>ective marketing channel, literature on BFPs has started to emerge in the recent years. A review of the existing BFP literature reveals three distinct categories of studies: (1) studies that provide a conceptual understanding of the BFP phenomenon, (2) studies that focus on understanding consumers’ BFP following and participation motivations, and (3) studies that focus on understanding the impact of BFPs on consumer brand relationships. The <sup>fi</sup>rst stream of literature is interested in explaining how BFPs di<sup>f</sup>er from traditional brand communities. For instance, [16], pp. 348) argue that BFPs di<sup>f</sup>er from traditional brand communities because “they are embedded in an organically grown and not brand-related network of social ties.” Thus, members of a BFP are not only linked to other fans of the brand but also connected to their own social networks, thus creating an extensive network for WOM marketing opportunities. Zhang et al. [4] compare BFPs in microblogging site, BFPs in social networking site (SNS), and traditional brand forums, and their <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that BFPs in SNS have the highest media richness, fastest communication speed, and largest network structure [4]. Shi et al. [10] point out that interactivity is one of the most distinctive characteristics of BFPs, distinguishing them from traditional online brand communities, which largely rely on unidirec tional information delivery from companies to customers.

The second stream of BFP studies has generally sought to reveal followers motivations to join and participate in BFPs. Several theoretical frameworks (e.g., uses and grati<sup>fi</sup>cation theory, self-determination theory, and consumption value theory) have been extensively used to explain users’ following behaviors in prior literature [10,12,16,18,19]. They argue that followers are more value-conscious in social media context and primarily focus on the role that individuals’ perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (e.g., usefulness and enjoyment) play in driving members’ BFP participation. For instance, Kang et al. [18] <sup>fi</sup>nd that the consumers’ use of BFPs is mainly driven by social-psychological bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and hedonic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. Adapting the reasoned action perspective, Seol et al. [12] indicate that user satisfaction has the strongest impact on predicting users’ intention to participate in BFPs, followed by perceived enjoyment and perceived usefulness. Some studies also point out that BFP following and participation decisions may be contingent on followers’ own characteristics. For example, Shi et al. [10] <sup>fi</sup>nd that female followers are more socially and emotionally oriented, while male followers tend to be more functionally oriented. A recent study by Zhang et al. [4] suggests that when followers perceive that their own characteristics to be similar to those of the BFP, they would rely more on messages generated by the BFP.

The third stream of BFP literature is mostly concerned with the positive results from utilizing BFPs to manage customer relationships. Some studies suggest that BFPs can help businesses soften the distanced corporate image, increase customer satisfaction, and cultivate loyal customer bases, thus enhancing the customer-brand relationship [2]. For example, Zhang et al. [4] indicate that consumers are more inclined to be loyal and commit to a brand after following its BFP. Kim et al. [11] also show that BFP followers are more likely to retweet to generate brand-related word of mouth (WOM) than other consumers. Some studies also suggest that companies’ BFP usage indirectly in<sup>fl</sup>uences <sup>fi</sup>rms’ equity value. By using a large sample of <sup>fi</sup>rms across industries, Hitt et al. [20] demonstrate that a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s market value increases with active social media usage. However, the study also points out that the ROI of using social media depends on having a data analytic savvy workforce. Furthermore, on the basis of the longitudinal data collected from a sample of 63 South Korean <sup>fi</sup>rms over 3 years, Chung et al. [21] <sup>fi</sup>nd that a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s BFP usage is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly associated with the increase in the <sup>fi</sup>rm s market performance.

In conclusion, although the aforementioned studies provide valuable insights on the BFP phenomenon, research e<sup>f</sup>ort in this area remains in its nascent stage. The majority of the extant literature aims to distinguish BFPs from other traditional brand communities, understand consumers’ motivations to follow and participate in BFPs, and identify the economic impact of BFPs on <sup>fi</sup>rms. Few studies have empirically examined consumers’ intention to unfollow BFPs; thus, these studies could not comprehensively explain why some BFP followers withdraw after initial adoption. In the current study, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne BFP discontinuance intention as the follower’s intention to remove a BFP from his or her following list and no longer wish to receive updates from the BFP. In the next section, we review the extant literature on IS discontinuance and develop a sensitizing framework for understanding BFP followers’ discontinuance motivations.

## 2.2. A sensitizing framework of BFP discontinuance motivations

In the past three decades, a large number of studies have looked into the conditions and consequences of IS adoption, usage, and continuance (e.g [22–28].). However, only limited attention to date has been devoted to the <sup>fi</sup>nal stage of the IS lifecycle, discontinuance [29]. Furneaux & Wade [30] was the <sup>fi</sup>rst to examine organizational-level decisions to discontinue the use of an IS. They argue that fundamental di<sup>f</sup>erences exist between continuance and discontinuance decisions: continuance represents “a status quo alternative that can occur in the absence of any conscious choice or planning. In contract, discontinuance represents a rejection of the status quo that requires con scious commitment of the necessary organizational resources and e<sup>f</sup>ort to e<sup>f</sup>ect the associated change ([30], p575). Turel [31] suggests that IS discontinuance is especially relevant to situations where system use behaviors are under the volitional control of the user, such as in the case of social media applications. He also argues that while discontinuance intention may moderately correlate with continuance in tention sometimes, it should be viewed as a di<sup>f</sup>erent behavior intention that can exist independently. Drawing upon existing discontinuance research, we have developed a sensitizing framework to guide our search for BFP discontinuance motivations. The purpose of the framework is to sensitize us to the wide range of motivations that potentially drive BFP discontinuance decisions. The sensitizing framework includes the following three categories of motivations: motivations of IS discontinuance through the lens of the technology acceptance model (TAM) and expectation con<sup>fi</sup>rmation theory (ECT), changing dynamics during technology use, and users’ <sup>fi</sup>t/un<sup>fi</sup>t perception, which are discussed in detail in this section (Fig. 1).

As most studies have treated discontinuance as the opposite of continuance where low levels of continuance intentions often lead to discontinuance, existing theoretical frameworks for IS continuance would likely o<sup>f</sup>er important insights to help us understand discontinuance motivations [24,30,31]. Two primary schools of thought on the psychological motivations leading to IS continuance intention exist in the current literature. The <sup>fi</sup>rst school views continuance as an extension of acceptance behaviors and uses the same constructs found in the technology acceptance model, namely perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use, to explain continuance behaviors [32]. Studies have also argued that the e<sup>f</sup>ect of perceived ease of use tends to diminish during postadoption as the user becomes more familiar with the technology; therefore, only perceived usefulness of the technology in the user’s future work is deemed relevant in explaining continuance intention [24,26]. The second school <sup>fi</sup>nds its theoretical support from the expectation con<sup>fi</sup>rmation theory (ECT) and considers the distinc tions between initial adoption and continuance behaviors. Bhattacher jee’s [24] model of IS continuance suggests that rational users of IS undergo a non-trivial decision process before making an informed IS continuance decision choice. It posits that IS continuance intention is primarily determined by users’ satisfaction with their prior IS use. User satisfaction is determined by users’ expectation of the IS, which is represented as ex post perceived usefulness, and con<sup>fi</sup>rmation of expectation following actual use. Consistent with the TAM, perceived usefulness is also a direct predictor of IS continuance. Finally, users’ con<sup>fi</sup>rmation of expectation tends to a<sup>f</sup>ect usefulness perception in order to keep user expectation consistent with reality. While the two schools of thought are not entirely contradictory to each other and have been integrated in numerous research projects (e.g. [26,33,34],), the ECT has found overwhelming empirical support in its ability to better explain continuance. For example, Lin & Shih’s [35] research <sup>fi</sup>nds that postadoption constructs such as con<sup>fi</sup>rmation and satisfaction have signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly greater in<sup>fl</sup>uence on continuance intention than the preadoption constructs do. Other studies such as Hong et al. [33] <sup>fi</sup>nd that a hybrid model that integrates the TAM and ECT has the highest explanatory power of user continuance intention for mobile Internet services. Furthermore, Bhattacherjee and Lin [26] uni<sup>fi</sup>ed model of IT continuance suggests that in addition to the TAM and ECT perspectives, habit in<sup>fl</sup>uences continuance behavior directly and moderates the relationship between continuance intention and behavior. We believe that the existing theoretical frameworks of IS continuance o<sup>f</sup>er an excellent starting point for our exploration of the user s discontinuance motivations in the BFP context.

Past studies have also suggested that one should consider the possibility that continuance and discontinuance may not be the exact opposite extremes on a continuum and that predictors of continuance may be di<sup>f</sup>erent from those of discontinuance [31,36]. Much of the em pirical evidence to support this notion can be found in research that focuses on organizational-level IS discontinuance (e.g. [30,37–40],). These studies suggest that both continuance and discontinuance in tentions may co-exist and are likely driven by di<sup>f</sup>erent motivations. For example, Furneaux and Wade [30] posit that IS discontinuance is a result of “disruption in an equilibrium between inertial tendencies and change forces.” In their study, they identify three forces that would positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence discontinuance intention: performance-oriented forces (e.g., declining system performance), internal forces (e.g., changing organizational initiatives), and external forces (e.g., changing environment). Furthermore, they point out that understanding the in dividual-level discontinuance of IS use would help us better understand and anticipate organization-level discontinuance. The three change forces identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by Furneaux and Wade [30] are largely applicable to individual-level IS discontinuance. For example, when a user experi ences declining system performance (performance-oriented forces), changes in personal interests and needs (internal forces), and/or emergence of more attractive alternatives (external forces), he or she is likely to contemplate the discontinuance of the current system. While the performance-oriented forces that lead to system discontinuance are consistent with the TAM and ECT perspectives outlined above, the in ternal and external forces represent factors beyond those suggested by the TAM and ECT. These two forces have received overwhelming sup port from prior literature. For example, in retailing, interactive media, and telecommunication services, changes in consumer taste, interest, and needs have been found to reduce product or service loyalty and increase switching intention [41–43]. Attractiveness of alternatives has also been widely acknowledged as a reason for users switching beha viors in online services [44–46].

Turel’s (2015) research points out that the co-existence of con tinuance and discontinuance intentions may also be the result of con <sup>fl</sup>icts between immediate needs and long-term considerations and that they are especially applicable to the hedonic IS context. For example, one may spend more time playing videogames to satisfy his or her immediate cravings but at the same time contemplate stopping playing videogames in the future when considering the negative consequences of videogame addiction. This stream of studies often turns to changing interests and problematic system use to search for motivations of dis continuance. They conclude that users would stop using a social media service when their interest changes; when they become addicted to the service and experience tiredness, stress, guilt, and boredom from using it; or when a more attractive alternative emerges [47–50]. Zhang et al. [49], for instance, indicate that perceived system feature overload, in formation overload, and social overload individuals experience from using an SNS exert positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on social network fatigue, which further increases users’ intention of discontinuance. Similarly, Maier et al. [47] demonstrate that discontinuance is used as a coping strategy to avoid the stress induced by SNS activities.

We term this category of motivations for system discontinuance changing dynamics as they represent changes that occur during the use of the technology such as changes in users’ interest or objectives, changing environment, emergence of more attractive alternatives, and newly discovered system limitations, costs, problematic use, and potential harm. As these changing dynamics intensify, they may undermine continuance inertia and lead to the discontinuance of the current system. As Furneaux and Wade [30] point out, some of the change forces that lead to discontinuance intention are highly system speci<sup>fi</sup>c; therefore, we rely on the subsequent qualitative study to help us identify the changing dynamics that are most salient to the BFP context.

Furthermore, while the TAM and ECT perspectives may be able to explain user continuance or discontinuance by focusing primarily on the role of utilitarian, hedonic, and social bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (e.g., performance improvement and enjoyment) using the technology, they do not explain why some users are able to achieve the anticipated bene<sup>fi</sup>ts while others fail to do so. These perspectives often characterize technology continuance and discontinuance as task related and rational; however, as one study points out, in this age of rapid social media technology diffusion and imitation, functionality-based loyalty motivations are no longer su<sup>fi</sup>cient to explain users’ continuance behaviors. Instead, “emotional, relational and experiential appeal” proves to be more ef fective in fostering follower loyalty [51]. We posit that this appeal can potentially come from the <sup>fi</sup>t between the follower and the BFP, which is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the follower’s perceived congruence of their own characteristics with those of the BFP and other followers of the same BFP. A BFP is a relationship platform that exhibits brand personality, the anthropomorphization of brand values and culture, and the personality of its users. Prior studies have found that consumers who perceive brand’s personality to be consistent with their self-concept are more likely to consume the brand [52,53]. Furthermore, the similarity-attraction paradigm and social identity theory support the notion that followers gain a positive social identity from their association with a group that has similar personality traits as theirs [54]. The shared identities that users derive from a social network lead to a stronger sense of belonging and motivate them to continue using the SNS [55]. In the context of social media, the personality congruity between a social network site and its followers has been found to strengthen followers’ emotional ties with and commitment to the site [51]. These rationales led us to investigate the role of <sup>fi</sup>t in explaining BFP users’ discontinuance motivations.

IS research has long touted <sup>fi</sup>t as a factor important to user adoption and performance. The dominant <sup>fi</sup>t theory used in the IS domain is the task-technology <sup>fi</sup>t (TTF) model [56,57]. TTF is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the match between the requirements of a task and the capabilities of the technology designed to support task accomplishment [58], and it has been empirically found to result in better performance in terms of both ef-<sup>fi</sup>ciency and e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness [59] and user adoption and continued use of technology [60]. The TTF model has been successfully adapted to study a wide range of IS under both voluntary and mandatory use. The interconnectedness and destruction of the communication barriers between all parties, both inside and outside the organization, make social media-based BFPs a unique type of system that o<sup>f</sup>ers a new level of collaboration and co-creation of value by the organization and its followers [61]. To fully appreciate the collaborative aspects of BFPs, the conceptualization of <sup>fi</sup>t should be expanded from focusing solely on the task-technology perspective to a broader person-environment perspective. Prior studies have posited that human behavior is a product of interactions between the person and the environment and highlighted <sup>fi</sup>t as a key condition for personal and organizational commitment [62,63]. While the e<sup>f</sup>ect of <sup>fi</sup>t perception has never been studied explicitly in the social media context, recent social commerce research has used similar concepts such as self-social network services personality match, brand relationship quality [51], self-brand image congruency [64], and self-congruency [65] to explain how <sup>fi</sup>t-related factors may a<sup>f</sup>ect BFP participation behaviors. Following this logic, we believe that exploring users’ <sup>fi</sup>t or un<sup>fi</sup>t perception in the BFP context to investigate the motivations behind individuals’ decisions to discontinue following BFPs beyond the utilitarian, social, and hedonic bene<sup>fi</sup>t perspectives that are often found in the extant literature is both appropriate and promising.

In conclusion, IS discontinuance research is still in its infancy. While the existing body of literature has signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly advanced our understanding of the <sup>fi</sup>nal stage of the IS lifecycle, much is still needed to be investigated in order to develop a comprehensive theory of IS discontinuance motivations. Furthermore, most existing studies on social media discontinuance have overlooked the extreme variability of applications that exist in the social media space and their di<sup>f</sup>erences in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing user discontinuance behaviors. Findings on SNS discontinuance may be too general and have limited usefulness in understanding the user discontinuance of various specialized applications in social media services (e.g., marketing vs. knowledge sharing) [66]. One area that has not been su<sup>fi</sup>ciently studied is the BFP follower discontinuance, i.e., the phenomenon where an individual continues to use social media service as usual but stops following certain BFPs after his or her initial following decision [14]. To best understand discontinuance intention, one should identify the motivations that drive such behavior intention. To partially <sup>fi</sup>ll this void, this study aims to extend the extant literature by developing and empirically examining a research model of BFP followers’ discontinuance motivations. A review of relevant theories has provided us with a sensitizing framework to guide our search for a wide range of motivations that potentially drive BFP followers’ discontinuance intention. The sensitizing framework suggests that potential BFP discontinuance motivations can be found in (1) motivations of IS discontinuance through the lens of the TAM and ECT (e.g., performance expectation and con<sup>fi</sup>rmation), (2) changing dynamics during technology use (e.g., changing objectives, interest, and environment; system limitations; costs ; and potential harm), and (3) users’ <sup>fi</sup>t/un<sup>fi</sup>t perception. Given the lack of strong a priori theory in this area, this study <sup>fi</sup>rst conducts a qualitative study to develop a grounded understanding of the motivations that drive BFP followers’ discontinuance decisions. Our understanding of the roles of these motivations will evolve on the basis of subsequent empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings. This grounded approach is widely used and endorsed by prior research work on emerging issues (e.g., [30,67]).

## 3. Research methodology and data analysis

To extract the underlying dimensions of an individual’s motivation to unfollow a BFP, a widely accepted scale development and validation procedure recommended by Churchill [68] was followed. A two-stage mixed-method approach was employed by the current study. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst step, an open-ended questionnaire was administered to social media users to uncover the reasons that motivated users to unfollow BFPs. Subsequently, two quantitative studies were performed to validate the proposed scale and examine the impact of the proposed discontinuance motivations on the unfollowing intention of BFP followers. Fig. 2 summarizes the instrument development procedure employed in this study, and the process is discussed in detail in the subsequent sections.

## 3.1. Study 1: qualitative study for item generation

An open-ended questionnaire was designed to uncover the reasons why users unfollowed BFPs that they initially followed [69]. The online survey was divided into two parts. The <sup>fi</sup>rst part included questions regarding user demographics (e.g., age, gender, and occupation) and usage frequency. The second part consisted of two open-ended questions: (1) Which BFP did you most recently unfollow? and (2) Why did you stop following this BFP?

The target subjects of the qualitative study were individuals who had unfollowed at least one BFP in the past 3 months on Sina Weibo, which is one of the largest social media platforms in the Chinese market. Respondents were recruited within a 5-week period through announcements from the authors’ Sina Weibo account and in a university campus discussion forum. The selection of college students and sta<sup>f</sup> as the subjects of this study is appropriate as they represent the largest group of Chinese social media users (72.3%) [70].

Out of 399 total responses, 372 valid responses were collected and varied from one-word statements to few surprisingly rich narratives. As shown in Table 1, among the 372 respondents, 57.53% were females, nearly 83% were 25 years old or younger, and 61.02% used Sina Weibo everyday. The demographic characteristics of the respondents are consistent with earlier empirical studies conducted using Sina Weibo users as research samples (e.g. [71,72],); thus, sample selection bias is likely not a concern of the current study.

Content analysis was conducted to code and categorize the textual responses to acquire an overview of the reasons that motivated users to unfollow BFPs. Two well-trained graduate students familiar with Sina Weibo were hired to carry out the content analysis independently. Two meetings were held to let the coders reach a common understanding about the coding scheme. While the sensitizing framework of BFP discontinuance motivations was explained to the coders, they were asked to keep an open mind when reviewing the responses to allow new items to emerge from the data. Moreover, the coders were asked not to spend more than an hour each day coding the data in order to minimize coding errors due to fatigue [73]. A comparison of the coding outcomes from the two coders showed a very high inter-rater reliability. Occasional discrepancies were discussed between the two coders to reach consensus.

## 3.2. Qualitative results

Table 2 provides a detailed description of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from the qualitative data. A total of 459 remarks were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed, and similar remarks were grouped in the same subcategories and categories. Five broad categories of users’ BFP unfollowing motivations emerged from the qualitative data. The <sup>fi</sup>ve categories are as follows: dissatisfaction with information quality, dissatisfaction with service quality, expectation discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation, interest shift, and person brand un<sup>fi</sup>t. Each of these motivations is brie<sup>fl</sup>y de<sup>fi</sup>ned and discussed in the following sections.

## 3.2.2. Dissatisfaction with service quality

The second category of remarks that emerged is labeled “dissatisfaction with service quality,” which refers to unfollowing because of the user’s dissatisfaction with the reliability, assurance, empathy, and responsiveness of the BFP’s services o<sup>f</sup>ered online [74]. Consistent with prior studies, the respondents’ remarks suggest that, in the BFP context, service quality relates to how promptly a BFP respond to followers’ requests, the extent to which it listens to user feedback, how it provides care and attention to users, and how it delivers reliable and trustworthy services [76]. Many respondents mentioned a lack of response from the BFP as the reason for their discontinuance decisions. As one stated, “I was so angry that this BFP didn’t reply to my comments about their products.” Other respondents cited BFPs’ failure to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll promises as reasons for unfollowing. As one noted, “The BFP sends at least 20 messages per day. I can’t read them all.” However, BFPs’ inactivity was also viewed negatively by users, as another participant indicated, “I unfollow this BFP because I barely see this BFP update any new messages.” Similar to dissatisfaction with information quality, dissatisfaction with service quality also represents users’ negative postadoption performance expectation that was found to lead to discontinuance in the qualitative data.

## 3.2.3. Expectation disconfirmation

The third category of remarks is labeled “expectation discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation,” which refers to unfollowing because of the discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation of followers ex ante expectations. In the post-acceptance model of IS continuance [24], con<sup>fi</sup>rmation refers to the user’s realization of the expected bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of IS use. In other words, this construct captures preconsumption expectations and con<sup>fi</sup>rmation of those expectations after system use. Prior studies on information-oriented IS also found that the con<sup>fi</sup>rmation or discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation of the expected bene<sup>fi</sup>ts was driven by system quality variables such as availability and system features such as personalization [75]. Therefore, expectation con-<sup>fi</sup>rmation is conceptually distinct from the aforementioned ex post performance expectation dimensions (i.e., dissatisfaction with information quality and dissatisfaction with service quality). The ex post performance expectation dimensions measure the user’s perception about how well the system will meet his or her future performance requirements after the user had the opportunity to use the system, while con<sup>fi</sup>rmation refers to the extent to which the user’s prior expectation of the system is con<sup>fi</sup>rmed following actual use. Although these two constructs are often correlated, they are conceptually distinct and have been found to in<sup>fl</sup>uence continuance intention di<sup>f</sup>erently [24,75]. Our qualitative data show that many respondents admitted that mismatch between their expectations and what the BFP o<sup>f</sup>ered motivated them to unfollow. As one respondent stated, “I expected that the interactive features of BFP would help me gain a deeper understanding of the auto industry when I followed Toyota China, but I was disappointed after following it for one year in how limited the BFP’s features were.” Another respondent stated, “I wanted to meet other people who also bought the product when I decided to follow the BFP, but it seemed that this was not what this BFP was designed for. The qualitative data show that expectation discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation was reported by users in terms of brand learning, social interactions among followers, and interactions between the follower and the BFP.

## 3.2.4. Interest shift

The fourth category is labeled “interest shift,” which refers to unfollowing due to changes in interest after the user’s initial following decision. A signi<sup>fi</sup>cant number of respondents mentioned that they stopped following the BFP as they were no longer interested in the brand. As one respondent stated, “I followed Xiaomi because I had a Xiaomi cellphone at that time. I bought a new iPhone 7 this year and don’t need the information provided by Xiaomi anymore.” Similarity, another respondent stated, “I followed Nike due to the fact that the brand was endorsed by my favorite basketball star. I stopped following Nike after it changed its spokesperson.” Some participants also indicated that they unfollowed because they switched to a more attractive alternative. As one respondent mentioned, “I found a better BFP which provided more interesting and useful messages.”

## 3.2.5. Person brand unfit

The <sup>fi</sup>fth category is labeled “person brand un<sup>fi</sup>t,” which refers to the mismatch between a follower and the brand that operates the BFP and between the followers of the same BFP in terms of values, objectives, and personality. The concept person brand <sup>fi</sup>t describes the congruence, correspondence, or match between a consumer and the brand. Previous literature has indicated that consumers are more inclined to stop using current products or services when their values, preferences, or needs are no longer aligned with those of the brand [77]. In the BFP context, followers learn about a company’s culture and values from its messages and tweets over the social media platform and other tradi tional channels $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . g . } } ,$ , news and publications). Followers are more in clined to discontinue following the BFP when they <sup>fi</sup>nd out that what they value misaligns with what the <sup>fi</sup>rm or its brand(s) values. As one stated, “What I care about is not what the company cares about.” Another participant indicated, “The BFP sends out too many aggressive messages in order to get followers’ attention, and this approach does not align with my values and personality.” Some participants also indicated that they unfollowed the BFP when they realized that their personalities are not similar to those of the other followers of the BFP. One stated, “I feel that my personality is di<sup>f</sup>erent from that of the other followers of this brand. Most of them are too aggressive.”

The <sup>fi</sup>ve categories of motivations to discontinue following a BFP identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by the qualitative study are consistent with our sensitizing framework of BFP discontinuance motivations, suggesting that they are theoretically sound. As suggested by our framework, remarks from survey respondents led to the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of motivations relevant to IS discontinuance from the lens of TAM and ECT. In particular, an overwhelming number of respondents attributed their discontinuance to dissatisfaction with information quality. dissatisfaction with service quality, and discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation of expectation. The <sup>fi</sup>rst two constructs refer to the negative ex post performance expectations of BFP followers, while the third construct represents the discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation of ex ante expectations, and all the three constructs have been deemed important in traditional continuance research through the lens of TAM and ECT. In terms of changing dynamics during system use, interest shift was identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by the respondents as a motivation for discontinuance that is most salient to the BFP context. A small percentage of users also cited the emergence of attractive alternatives as a reason for discontinuance. Nevertheless, problematic use such as technostress and addiction, which were often cited as motivations for discontinuance in the social media context did not emerge from the responses. This is likely due to the application-speci<sup>fi</sup>c nature of research <sup>fi</sup>ndings in the social media context. Therefore, attempts to generalize motivations of dis continuance across all social media applications with extreme variability in their audiences, functions, and objectives may prove to be problematic. Finally, as our sensitizing framework suggests, users’ <sup>fi</sup>t/ unfit perception was identified as an important motivation for BFF discontinuance. Findings from the qualitative study suggested that <sup>fi</sup>t/ un<sup>fi</sup>t perception in the BFP context be assessed not only between the follower and the brand but between the follower and other followers of the same BFP as well. thus expanding the notion of fit bevond the traditional scope of TTF. In conclusion, our sensitizing framework and results from the qualitative study have provided strong theoretical and empirical support for the model of BFP discontinuance motivations, which consists of dissatisfaction with information quality, dissatisfac tion with service quality, expectation discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation, interest shift, and person brand un<sup>fi</sup>t.

## 3.3. Study 2: quantitative study for scale purification

Following the qualitative study that identi<sup>fi</sup>ed an inventory of motivations for unfollowing BFPs, a quantitative study designed for scale puri<sup>fi</sup>cation was conducted. The scale puri<sup>fi</sup>cation process involved a survey of social media users who have unfollowed at least one BFP in the past 3 months, exploratory factor analysis to extract the underlying factors, and con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory factor analysis to improve the congeneric measurement properties of the scale and obtain an initial assessment of scale reliability and validity [68,78]. Based on our sensitizing framework, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the qualitative study and prior studies that have investigated similar constructs, an initial item pool that consisted of 30 items was created: 9 items for dissatisfaction with information quality, 6 items for dissatisfaction with service quality, 6 items for expectation discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation, 5 items for interest shift, and 4 items for person brand un<sup>fi</sup>t. When a construct of the model has been measured and validated by a prior study, the existing scale was adapted to <sup>fi</sup>t the context of the current study. For example, some of the items for measuring dissatisfaction with information quality were adapted from Chen et al. [75], the items for measuring expectation discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation were adapted from Bhattacherjee [24], and the items for measuring person brand un<sup>fi</sup>t were adapted from Cable and DeRue [79]. New items were generated where it was necessary on the basis of the remarks and subcategories of BFP discontinuance motivations identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by the qualitative data displayed in Table 2.

To verify content validity and readability of the survey instrument, the initial items were reviewed by three IS academicians and three business practitioners who demonstrated extensive experiences in social media applications. The reviewers were asked to evaluate the validity of the items and suggest any new items to be included. The review resulted in minor wording changes to improve readability, but no new items were suggested as the reviewers deemed the list of items to be comprehensive. To ensure the full comprehension of the questionnaire by the intended samples of this study, Chinese BFP users, the items, originally designed in English, were professionally translated into Chinese. To verify that there were no translation biases, the items were back-translated to English by a researcher who was not associated with this study. No signi<sup>fi</sup>cant distinctions were detected between the ori ginal items and the back-translated version of the items suggesting the absence of translation biases in the <sup>fi</sup>nal questionnaire. An online questionnaire was constructed that contained the 30 BFP discontinuance motivation items using a 7-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Users of Sina Weibo, a popular Chinese social media platform, were recruited to participate in the survey. A total of 197 returned questionnaires were found to be complete and usable. As shown in Table 2, among the 197 respondents, 64.47% were females, nearly 72% were 25 years old or younger, and 65.99% used Sina Weibo every day.

## 3.3.1. Exploratory factor analysis

An exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was performed to extract the underlying dimensions of the individual’s BFP discontinuance motivation scale. Before conducting the EFA, the appropriateness of the 197 responses for factor analysis was examined. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) value in this study was 0.919, which exceeded the cuto<sup>f</sup> value of 0.50 [80], indicating a desirable sampling adequacy. The Barlett’s test of Sphericity was signi<sup>fi</sup>cant $( \mathbf { p } < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , indicating su<sup>fi</sup>cient correlations among the selected variables.

The EFA was conducted using principal component analysis with varimax rotation on the collected data [81]. The number of dimensions was identi<sup>fi</sup>ed using the criterion of eigenvalue greater than 1.0 suggested by Kaiser [82]. Further reduction of items was performed to eliminate items with low factor loadings (< 0.60) and/or high cross loadings (> 0.45). Items that met these two criteria were deleted one at a time in order to ensure accuracy [83]. After each deletion, individual KMO was re-examined in case of any item failing to meet the criteria. After iterative rounds of EFA, a total of 21 items remained, thus producing a <sup>fi</sup>ve-dimensional factorial structural. The detailed results of EFA including factor loadings are presented in the second column in Table 3, Factorial Structure. The <sup>fi</sup>ve factors explain 70.179% of the total variance in BFP discontinuance motivations, which exceeds the suggested threshold value of 60% [83].

Overall, the EFA produced a 21-item, <sup>fi</sup>ve-dimensional structure for the BFP discontinuance motivation scale with <sup>fi</sup>ve items extracted for “Dissatisfaction with Information Quality”, four items identi<sup>fi</sup>ed for “Dissatisfaction with Service Quality,” <sup>fi</sup>ve items converged on “Expectation Discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation,” three items extracted for “Interest Shift,” and four items extracted by “Person Brand Un<sup>fi</sup>t.” The factorial structure derived from the EFA is consistent with the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the qualitative study described above.

To con<sup>fi</sup>rm the factor structure obtained in the EFA. A con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory factor analysis (CFA) using LISREL (version 8.70) with maximum likelihood (ML) method of estimation was performed on the 21-item scale. All <sup>fi</sup>ve dimensions were allowed to freely inter-correlate with one another [81]. The <sup>fi</sup>t indices of CFA are shown in the second column of Table 4, including $X ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { d } . \mathrm { f } . = 1 . 9 2 8$ , adjusted goodness of <sup>fi</sup>t index $( \mathrm { A G F I } ) = 0 . 8 1$ , normed <sup>fi</sup>t index (NFI) = 0.96, non-normed <sup>fi</sup>t index (NNFI) = 0.97, comparative <sup>fi</sup>t index (CFI) = 0.98, and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) = 0.069. ## 3.3.3. Reliability and convergent validity

Reliability and convergent validity were assessed in this study by computing the average variance extracted (AVE) and composite reliability (CR) [88]. A CR of 0.70 or above and an AVE of more than 0.50 are deemed acceptable [88]. The results of the reliability and convergent validity assessment are presented in Table 3. Our results indicated that all CRs were above 0.70 (ranging from 0.783 to 0.922) and that all AVEs were above 0.50 (ranging from 0.509 to 0.632), indicating satisfactory internal consistency and reliability of the measurement model [88,89].

## 4. Discussion of <sup>fi</sup>ndings and implications

## 4.1. Discussion of findings

The goal of the current study is to develop a scale to measure individuals’ BFP discontinuance motivations and understand the impact of the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed motivations on BFP users’ unfollowing intention. In doing so, a rigorous scale development process proposed by Churchill [68] was followed to explore the dimensions of BFP discontinuance motivations that exhibit a high reliability and validity. Guided by our sensitizing framework of BFP discontinuance motivations, the qualitative study identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a <sup>fi</sup>ve-factor structure scale of BFP discontinuance motivations, namely, dissatisfaction with information quality, dissatisfaction with service quality, expectation discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation, interest shift, and person brand un<sup>fi</sup>t. EFA results suggest that the <sup>fi</sup>ve motivation constructs explain a large portion (70.179%) of the variance in users’ BFP discontinuance motivations. The scale was then empirically puri<sup>fi</sup>ed and validated in two subsequent quantitative studies using Chinese social media users. In addition to high content validity, CFA results show that the current scale demonstrates good internal consistency, convergent and discriminant validity.

In addition to validating the scale using an independent sample, study 3 also examined the impact of the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed motivations on the user’s BFP unfollowing intention using the SEM techniques. Among the hypothesized relationships, dissatisfaction with information quality (H1), expectation discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation (H3), interest shift (H4), and person brand un<sup>fi</sup>t (H5) were found to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the user’s BFP unfollowing intention signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly. Expectation discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation shows the greatest impact (with a path coe<sup>fi</sup>cient of 0.28 and t-value of 3.03) on the discontinuance intention. Many respondents of the qualitative study also suggested that their initial following decisions were driven by the expectations such as product-related learning, career-related learning, entertainment, and socializing. Discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation of these expectations resulted in BFP discontinuance. This further con<sup>fi</sup>rms that ECT serves as an important theoretical foundation for understanding both continuance and discontinuance behaviors. The second most in<sup>fl</sup>uential discontinuance motivation is person brand un<sup>fi</sup>t (with a path coe<sup>fi</sup>cient of 0.28 and t-value of 2.95). This <sup>fi</sup>nding suggests that the congruence between the values, norms, and goals of the users and those of the brand and other brand followers plays a critical role in shaping the user’s discontinuance decision. This con<sup>fi</sup>rms the notion of prior studies that the user’s <sup>fi</sup>t perception should be viewed as a key theoretical construct in the BFP discontinuance context beyond the traditional perspectives of utilitarian, social, and hedonic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. The third most in<sup>fl</sup>uential motivation construct is interest shift (with a path coe<sup>fi</sup>cient of 0.27 and t-value of 3.42). The <sup>fi</sup>ndings from our qualitative study corroborate this result. A large percentage of respondents cited changes in their personal interests as the reason that led to decisive unfollowing of the BFP. This suggests that managing user attrition is a continuous process as changing dynamics during the use of the system can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly impact the discontinuance decision. Finally, dissatisfaction with information quality was also found to signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uence discontinuance intention, con<sup>fi</sup>rming the importance of the rational, taskdriven aspect of discontinuance decision making as touted by the TAM and ECT.

However, the hypothesized relationship between dissatisfaction with service quality and discontinuance intention was not statistically supported, thus leading to an opportunity for future exploration. This outcome may be attributed to the fact that most users view a BFP pri marily as a channel for acquiring information and interacting with other followers of the brand; therefore, the service aspect of the BFP is often overlooked or underutilized. ## 4.2. Theoretical contributions

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this research can contribute to the existing literature in several regards. First, although recent studies have shown the importance of maintaining social media follower bases, the majority of current literature on BFPs has focused on explaining the business implications of BFPs or individuals’ initial motivations to join BFPs. This study is one of the very few empirical studies that attempt to investigate factors that drive BFP followers’ discontinuance. Thus, we believe that this study makes a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant contribution by enriching the extant lit erature pool on social media-based BFPs. Our sensitizing theoretical framework for identifying BFP discontinuance motivations was empirically supported by the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from both our qualitative and quantitative studies. The framework supports the notion of prior studies that discontinuance and continuance should be viewed as related but distinct concepts in the social media context, and the search for dis continuance motivations should extend beyond the traditional perspectives of the TAM and ECT. The framework will likely generalize to social media applications other than BFP and o<sup>f</sup>er researchers interested in discontinuance behaviors in the social media context a solid theoretical foundation for their research.

Another contribution of this paper is the development of a comprehensive measure of individuals’ motivations to discontinue following BFPs. As we argued above, although researchers are increasingly attempting to explore individual-level discontinuance of IS use because many leading social media platforms such as Facebook and Twitter face severe user retention issues, limited attention has been paid to individuals’ unfollowing behaviors in the social media context [14]. The identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of the motivations behind discontinuance through a rigorous scale development process employed in this study o<sup>f</sup>ers the theoretical underpinnings for the future development of discontinuance frameworks in various IS domains. Thus, we believe that this study has provided a theoretical framework and measurement for studying individual-level discontinuance of IS use in the social media context. The scale is especially relevant and applicable to information-oriented social media application use where system use behaviors are under the volitional control of the user.

Finally, a sound instrument for measuring user BFP discontinuance motivations provides a solid foundation for future research that in vestigates the relationship between BFP discontinuance motivations, social media experience, user satisfaction, commitment, trust, and <sup>fi</sup>rm and brand perceptions. For example, future studies may center on relating discontinuance motives to speci<sup>fi</sup>c online behavioral indicators such as frequency of use and textual feedback of the users. Furthermore, discontinuance motivation may vary with regard to users’ socioeconomic, demographic, or technographic characteristics. The scale developed in this study will help facilitate future empirical research in this area that urgently needs attention.

## 4.3. Practical contributions

We believe that our <sup>fi</sup>ndings also have important implications for practitioners. Maintaining a stable social media follower base has long been recognized as one of the core pursuits for marketers [21]. Many companies invest considerable amount of resources in social media marketing campaigns to acquire and retain followers. The results of the present research show that dissatisfaction with information quality, dissatisfaction with service quality, expectation discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation, in terest shift, and person brand un<sup>fi</sup>t are the key factors that explain users’ motivations to stop following BFPs. Our study also provides empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the impact of the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed motivations on unfollowing intention. While one of the motivations, dissatisfaction with service quality, warrants further exploration, the other four motivations were found to drive users’ unfollowing decisions signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly. Focusing on identifying early warning signs of discontinuance and developing strategies to improve retention with regard to various discontinuance motivations will likely improve <sup>fi</sup>rms’ performance in their social media marketing and customer relationship management e<sup>f</sup>orts. The following recommendations are directed at addressing the <sup>fi</sup>ve discontinuance motivations identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in this study.

## 4.3.1. Dissatisfaction with information quality

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant number of followers left the BFP due to the consistently low-quality updates from the BFP. In other words, the lack of high quality information can inhibit the long-term sustainability of BFPs. In the social media context, while the cost of information distribution is negligible, <sup>fi</sup>rms must be mindful of the attention cost to the audiences. Low-quality information that o<sup>f</sup>ers little relevance to the user would lead to severe user defection. This suggests that BFPs’ updates must be carefully crafted to provide followers with content that displays the characteristics of high-quality information. Untargeted commercials and low-quality messages should be replaced by content with a greater usefulness, timeliness, originality, and appeal. An enterprise-wide structured content development and approval pro cess would help in this regard to provide quality assurance for communication with customers in social media platforms.

## 4.3.2. Dissatisfaction with service quality

Our qualitative study <sup>fi</sup>nds that some users stop following a BFP due to their dissatisfaction with the service provided by the BFP. In order to improve follower retention, BFP operators are encouraged to provide reliable and trustworthy services to their fans. For instance, timely feedback should be provided to followers’ comments and suggestions.

Moreover, BFPs should always follow-up on their promises for actions or economic rewards to their followers. The traditional SERVQUAL framework, which includes tangibles, reliability, responsiveness, assurance, and empathy, can o<sup>f</sup>er guidance on how to improve service quality in the BFP context [93].

## 4.3.3. Expectation disconfirmation

Our study <sup>fi</sup>nds expectation discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation to be an important motivation for discontinuance. Prior studies on IS continuance suggest that con<sup>fi</sup>rmation is achieved by meeting users’ experiential expectations of the system [75]; therefore, the evaluation of whether the BFP’s functionalities are consistent with the follower’s pre-usage expectations is critical to avoid discontinuance because of expectation discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation. This evaluation can happen at the point when the user starts following the BFP through a brief orientation that familiarizes the follower with features of the BFP that are most relevant to achieving the fol lower’s expectations. Continuous maintenance of expectation con-<sup>fi</sup>rmation should be conducted through user feedback to ensure the longevity of the follower base. In addition, analytics techniques such as text mining, topic modeling, and semantics analysis could be utilized to identify any discrepancies between followers’ expectations and the BFP’s functionalities from user comments. By treating the con<sup>fi</sup>rmation of followers’ expectations as a core component of the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s social media strategy, the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s BFP will likely enjoy continuing success in follower retention.

## 4.3.4. Interest shift

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest interest shift to be a key driver for BFP followers’ discontinuance decisions. Therefore, the content o<sup>f</sup>ered through BFPs should not be stale or generic. Customized content based on follower pro<sup>fi</sup>le or content related to trending issues tends to be more e<sup>f</sup>ective in enticing social media users. At the same time, instead of communicating with followers in a broadcasting mode, BFPs should encourage follower interaction and feedback in order to detect potential interest shift before discontinuance intention can be formed. Moreover, SNSs, such as Facebook, frequently add new features or functions to their platforms to keep users engaged. BFP operators are encouraged to incorporate these new features to develop innovative content and services to entice followers.

## 4.3.5. Person brand unfit

Our results indicate that followers are not only attracted by the utilitarian bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of using BFPs such as information acquisition and social interactions but also pursue values congruence with the brand and other followers. This suggests that BFPs should not be used simply as a promotional tool but a means of conveying corporate and brand culture and values to their social value-conscious customers. Content concerning corporate culture, values, and social responsibility issues should permeate throughout the BFP’s communication with customers. Moreover, BFPs are suggested to develop both online and o<sup>fl</sup>ine activities that allow fans to meet and share their enthusiasm for causes that are congruent with the values of the company to build a more cohesive follower base.

## 4.4. Limitations and future research direction

The current study is not without limitations, suggesting directions for future research. First, culture has been proved to exert important in<sup>fl</sup>uence on individuals’ values, way of thinking, and perception [83]. The samples of Chinese social media users used in this study may prevent the results of this study from generalizing to other country or cultural settings directly. While cross-cultural validation is beyond the scope of this study, it is recommended that the <sup>fi</sup>ndings be interpreted with caution and that future studies validate the instrument in di<sup>f</sup>erent cultural settings.

Second, we acknowledge that a wide range of other antecedents to

BFP users’ unfollowing decision such as personal traits, which are not included in the current study, may exist. Our study focuses only on understanding the psychological motivations that drive BFP discontinuance. Furthermore, it should be noted that our study does not account for the interactions among the motivations as our objective is to identify the direct e<sup>f</sup>ect of the motivations on users’ BFP unfollowing intention. Future studies are recommended to expand our research model by including other factors and accounting for the interactions among discontinuance motivations.

In addition, the cross-sectional design used in this study may limit the ability of observing the changing patterns of subjects over time, which may cause misidenti<sup>fi</sup>cation of the dimensions of BFP unfollowing motivations. Future studies should address this shortcoming by using longitudinal approaches to detect and monitor variations and trends among subjects over time.

Finally, the current study is designed to focus on the scale development process. While as part of the process, we examined the impact of motivations on unfollowing intention, future studies are recommended to examine the predictive validity of the proposed scale on a number of other outcome variables, such as user satisfaction, commitment, trust, and brand loyalty, in order to demonstrate the useful ness of the scale.

## 5. Conclusion

Despite the high attrition rate of BFP followers on social media platforms, the extant IS literature is limited in o<sup>f</sup>ering a theoretically grounded understanding of BFP follower discontinuance behaviors. To further examine this important yet underexplored issue, this study undergoes a rigorous process that includes a qualitative study, an empirical measurement development and puri<sup>fi</sup>cation process, and an empirical validation of the measurement and structural models. Our model of BFP discontinuance motivations that consists of dissatisfaction with information quality, dissatisfaction with service quality, expectation discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation, interest shift, and person brand un<sup>fi</sup>t was empirically validated to be useful in understanding the overall discontinuance motivations of BFP followers. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings have provided the much-needed theoretical foundation for an underexplored area of user behaviors in social media, and they have important implications for practice as they elaborate upon strategies for improving BFP follower retention.
