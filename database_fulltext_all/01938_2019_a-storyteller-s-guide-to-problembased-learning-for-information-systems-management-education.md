## Abstract

More than a decade ago, evidence‐based recommendations emerged regarding what students of information systems (IS) management education should learn and how should they learn it. Although these recommendations for how IS management should be taught remain valid, they need to be updated to account for recent advances in technologies that enable multimedia learning. Promoters of such technologies promise enhanced cognitive and behavioural outcomes, but this promise remains unreached, reflecting the underdeveloped multimedia‐enabled learning literature. To help attain this promise and rejuvenate the literature of multimedia learning, we offer a roadmap for new areas of research that would inform the design and use of a novel form of multimedia materials: narrative animated videos (NAVs). NAVs represent a form of self‐determined learning that features immersive, story‐based content. We argue that their use will intrinsically motivate users to process the materials to completion, thereby enhancing cognitive and behavioural outcomes, and thus catalysing the effectiveness of the team‐based learning and self‐regulated learning modes for problem‐based learning (PBL) delivery of IS management education. This compelling roadmap corresponds to meaningful IS research because it centres on a topic that the IS literature has long examined—the role of user motivation—and because its theoretical contributions invite specific paths of research for informing the design of the PBL delivery of IS management education within an information systems artefact.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

To be competitive in the workplace, what should students of information systems (IS) management education learn and how should they learn it? G. Lowry and Turner (2005) addressed these questions more than a decade ago by surveying 273 Australian IS practitioners and decision makers. They found that beyond the traditional emphasis on the transfer of domain knowledge—much of which has a progressively shrinking half‐life—IS management education must emphasise the development of soft skills; in particular, teamwork skills and problem‐solving skills. Because their sur vey respondents regarded those skills as highly important or essential to success for new IS professionals, the authors recommended that the traditional didactic model—featuring lectures, tutorials, and readings—should be abandoned i favour of the problem‐based learning (PBL) model of instruction.<sup>1</sup>

Although multimedia‐enabled instruction has become popular in practice (Lawrence & Tar, 2018), its promise to significantly enhance cognitive and behavioural outcomes remains unrealized, indicating an inadequate theoretica foundation (Ayres, 2015; Mayer & Pilegard, 2014). For this reason, and because of recent advances in technologies that enable multimedia learning, G. Lowry and Turner's (2005) recommendations need to be updated. To help bolste that effort, we offer a roadmap for research that would inform the evidence‐based use of multimedia instructiona materials within the PBL delivery of IS management education

This roadmap presents exciting opportunities for scholars to make substantial and ongoing intellectual and practical contributions. The pursuit of these opportunities is especially relevant for IS scholars for two reasons. First, the unre alized promise of multimedia learning reflects a failure to consider the role of user motivation, which the IS literature ha: long examined. Second, when multimedia-enabled, the PBL. delivery of IS management education facilitates three modes of learning that together constitute an IS artefact, as A. S. Lee, Thomas, and Baskerville (2015) define that term

These three modes represent a sequence that begins with self‐determined learning (i.e. homework); continues with team-based collaborative learning: and concludes with self-regulated learning (SRI). For each mode, we offer research guidance that is grounded in established theory regarding the role of user motivation. We focus primarily on the use of multimedia instructional materials within the initial mode of self‐determined learning because success in this mode propagates across the other two modes of team-based collaborative learning and SRL. We propose that within self‐determined learning, the instructional imperative should be to design multimedia instruction materials that, by achieving immersion, intrinsically motivate learners to process the materials to completion so as to achieve targeted cognitive and behavioural outcomes. We support this claim by leveraging recently developed native IS theories namely, the multimotive information systems continuance (MISC) model (P. B. Lowry, Gaskin, & Moody, 2014) and the hedonic‐motivation system adoption model (HMSAM) (P. B. Lowry, Gaskin, Twyman, Hammer, & Roberts, 2013).

We claim that multimedia instruction materials can achieve immersion when their content is conveyed accordin to the narrative form of sense‐making as opposed to the expository form of sense‐making. We further suggest that the effectiveness of the narrative form of sense-making can be significantly enhanced when the artistic telling of the story is supported by the skilful use of images and sounds within the novel artefact design represented by narrative animated videos (NAVs). Our claim aligns with the observation that humans are natural storytellers, consistent wit the narrative paradigm (Fisher, 1989), according to which humans communicate with each other through narratives because doing so is an effective means to absorb and understand complex information.

Hollywood has long understood the informative and persuasive power of storytelling whose artistic telling is sup ported by the skilful use of images and sounds. So powerful is this potential that the recollection of a single word (eg, “Rosebud,” “Plastics,” “the Force,” “Spidey‐sense,” “Jedi,” “Muggles,” and “Hobbit”) or a short phrase (eg, “They call me Mr. Tibbs,” “Life is like a box of chocolates,” “Frankly, my dear, I don't give a damn,” “I'll have what she's having, and “I'll be back”) can summon distant memories that retrieve abundant explicit and tacit knowledge of the movie and its enduring social and personal meaning. Hence, we claim that such storytelling techniques are suitable for the design o NAVs and that the use of immersive NAV's as a device for promoting self‐determined learning can catalyse the effectiveness of the team-based collaborative learning activities of PBI, instruction, and thereby transform IS management education and fulfil the recommendations that G. Lowry and Turner (2005) made more than a decade ago. This leads us to our research question:

RQ: How can the use of NAVs inform a research agenda for the development of problem‐solving skills in IS manage ment education?

To establish a proper foundation for our claims, we begin with a literature review in which we (1) describe the ories regarding how and why humans learn; (2) deconstruct how multimedia learning research fails to consider user motivation; and (3) identify the associated consequences. We then review the MISC model and the HMSAM, whic inform our guidance for the development of theory that predicts immersion in multimedia learning. To illustrate how such theory can be operationalised as NAVs, we develop three NAVs that provide instruction on a topic—cybersecu rity compliance training—which is central to IS management education.

## 2 | BACKGROUND ON MULTIMEDIA LEARNING

Early humans did not study in large‐scale classrooms or online, but they learned well enough to survive and thrive in hostile environments. Although the how and why of human cognition are unchanged since the dawn of behavioural modernity 50 000 years ago, our understanding of human cognition has not sufficiently informed the design of multimedia instructional materials. The how and why of human cognition are represented by the literature of cognitiv psychology and human motivation, as depicted in the bottom two rectangles (boxes 4 and 5) of Figure 1 (see more details in Table S1). The top rectangle (box 1) in Figure 1 indicates that the multimedia learning theory's main contribution is to explain how to design the display<sup>2</sup> of multimedia artefacts to comply with the cognitive psychology theories identified in the bottom two rectangles. However, because the multimedia literature largely overlooks the role of motivation theory, available studies are unable to explain how to design the content<sup>3</sup> of multimedia artefacts. This represents a wide gap in the literature

Our contributions aim to fill this gap in two ways, indicated by boxes 2 and 3 in Figure 1. First, we identify two fresh IS theories (box 3) that contextualise motivation theory for multimedia learning. We then apply that contribution to invoke other theories that explain how to design multimedia content (box 2). Central to the design of the con tent is the use of storytelling techniques to prompt cognitive evaluation and arouse affective perceptions, a concept that dates back to Aristotle and is still applied in contemporary literary and cinematic arts. The use of such techniques is central to our theorising because, by evolution, humans are natural storytellers. Second, we lay a foundation for th rest of our arguments by drawing upon established theory (represented by box 5) regarding how humans learn.

## 2.1 | How people learn: the architecture and operation of human cognition

Cognitive theory presumes a three‐part model of human cognitive architecture consisting of sensory memory, working memory, and long‐term memory, as illustrated in Figure 2. These three components operate according to three prin ciples: dual‐channel processing, limited capacity, and active processing (Sweller, van Merrienboer, & Paas, 1998). Cog nitive load theory (Cl T) (Sweller. 1994) is an instructional approach based on knowledge of (1) the architecture anc operation of human cognition; (2) the organisation of information within long‐term memory; and (3) the interactions between working memory and long‐term memory. CLT identifies conditions in which cognitive load—defined as the total amount of mental activity imposed on the limited processing capacity of working memory (Sweller, 1994)—can be successfully managed to facilitate the acquisition and automation of schemas, or cognitive devices for indexing combinations of subject‐matter knowledge and relevant procedural knowledge within long‐term memory. Schemas determine how new information is processed (Bartlett & Burt. 1933: Sweller. 1994) and their automation enhances the efficiency of cognitive processing within working memory. Table 1 summarises the types of cognitive load according to the type of processing required and their instructional design objectives

Constructivist learning theory (CoLT) comprises settled theories that explain how humans learn (Bransford, Brown, & Cocking, 1999; Lambert & McCombs, 1998; Mayer, 2001). CoLT posits that learners construct meaning by a process consisting of three steps, as illustrated in Figure 2. In step 1, learners actively select relevant incoming stimul within their sensory memory. In step 2, and within the independent dual channels of working memory, learners organise those selected stimuli into distinct and faithful representations, creating causal connections that Mayer (1999b calls internal connections. Concurrently, learners retrieve prior relevant knowledge that has been stored in long-term memory as schemas. In step 3, learners integrate those internal connections with existing schemas, creating what Mayer (1999b) describes as external connections and what Paivio (1986) describes as referential connections

When the learner's store of available schemas is not sufficient or suitable for the creation of referential connections the learner must create new knowledge. To do so, the learner must appeal to the randomness as genesis principle. According to this principle (by which all learning could be classified as “problem solving”), the learner generates problem‐solving strat egies and then subjects them to tests of effectiveness (Sweller, 2008). This mechanism is analogous to the random mutation process by which evolution by natural selection operates. It is the means by which nature has endowed humans with the cognitive capacity to adapt to the environment (Sweller, 2008) and aligns with the PBL method of instruction Step 3 results in new combinations of schemas, which are then indexed within long‐term memory.

Cognitive theory, CLT, and CoLT further the understanding of the architecture and operation of human cognition. However, the human mind does not operate within a vacuum, nor do human cognitive processes. Instead, humans are social creatures, and the social environment strongly affects cognition and learning. Social learning theory (Bandura, 1977) explains that most learning occurs through the process of socialisation and the modelling of others behaviours. Bandura (1986) later relabelled social learning theory as social cognitive theory (SCT).<sup>4</sup>

In summary, people learn from their own experiences and those shared by others, and they index those experi ences within long‐term memory as schemas. These schemas are later retrieved and expanded to solve novel problem via the sense‐making process of constructivist learning (Mayer, 1999a). The use of multimedia within instructiona materials can enhance the salience of those schemas and thereby enable more‐effective problem solving

## 2.2 | Why people learn: an overview of motivation theory

Cognitive evaluation theory (CET or self‐perception theory) explains the relationship between human motivation and subsequent behaviour (Deci & Ryan, 1985; Hirschfeld & Lawson, 2008). According to CET, individuals are motivated by either intrinsic or extrinsic motivation. Intrinsic motivation can be defined as what people will do in the absence of external inducement (eg, have fun) (Malone, 1981; Malone & Lepper, 1987). In contrast, extrinsic motivation refers t what people will do following external inducement (eg, go to work) (DeShon & Gillespie, 2005; Dweck & Leggett 1988; VandeWalle, Cron, & Slocum, 2001).

There are clear limits to relying solely upon extrinsic motivation to encourage persistence in processing multime dia instructional materials. According to CET, fulfilling extrinsic motivation is almost always insufficient to keep one satisfied and engaged over the long term because the appeal to extrinsic motivation substantially reduces one's sense of self‐efficacy and control, and it can eventually be demoralising (P. B. Lowry et al., 2013). In contrast, intrinsic moti vation has been shown to be consistently more predictive of human behaviour than extrinsic motivation (Deci, 1977; Deci & Ryan, 1985). Thus, intrinsic motivation is key to explaining and predicting why learners persist in processing multimedia materials sufficiently to achieve learning objectives

## 2.3 | The design of the display in multimedia learning

The CTML (Mayer, 2005a) builds upon cognitive theory, CLT, and CoLT to explain and predict the cognitive processes involved in learning from multimedia materials. CTML reflects the multimedia principle, which states that indi viduals learn better from words and pictures than they do from words alone (Mayer, 2005a). Supporting multimedi learning are 10 multimedia design principles for promoting constructivist learning (Mayer, 2005c), which aim to (1 reduce extraneous processing (or extraneous cognitive load), (2) manage essential processing (or intrinsic cognitiv load), and (3) foster generative processing (or germane cognitive load).

From about 1988 to 2008, the literature of multimedia learning benefited enormously from breakthrough theoretical and empirical studies conducted by numerous pioneering academics, as catalogued by a leader among those pioneers—Professor Richard Mayer, the editor of The Cambridge Handbook of Multimedia Learning (Mayer, 2005a). However, since then—and, ironically, coinciding with the widespread adoption of technologies that enable the development and systems‐based deployment of multimedia instructional materials—the multimedia literature has stagnated. That literature lacks the comprehensive theories needed to support a wide scope of research (Ayres, 2015) The existing literature focuses too much on “contrived laboratory tasks,” instead of practical tasks; that is, it exces sively relies on the use of multimedia instructional materials that are too short and narrowly scoped for testing or deployment within “ecologically valid conditions” (Mayer & Pilegard, 2014) and largely neglects the role of motivation in technology‐based learning and instruction (Leutner, 2014).

Characteristic of the gaps in the literature of multimedia learning is the following dichotomy: On the one hand scholars have contributed numerous studies that validate techniques for mitigating extraneous processing in instruc tional materials (Sweller, 2016; Van Merriënboer & Ayres, 2005). However, this represents the collection of low hanging fruit: Of the three instructional design objectives for managing cognitive load identified in Table 1, mitigatin extraneous processing is the most attainable. Conversely, as observed in Wong, Leahy, Marcus, and Sweller's (2012) review of the literature, there is a paucity of studies that tackle the challenging and vital task of validating techniques for fostering generative processing and promoting the acquisition and automation of schemas. The result is that multimedia learning literature, although sufficiently well developed to provide useful guidance for designing the dis play the multimedia message (to reduce extraneous processing), is not sufficiently developed to provide useful guid ance for designing the content or substance of that message (to foster generative processing). We next provid background for understanding this gap.

## 2.4 | The design of the content in multimedia learning and the role of motivation theory

Ever since Moreno and Mayer (2007) proposed the cognitive affective theory of multimedia learning (CATML), the literature of multimedia learning has properly assumed that motivation mediates the cognitive processing of instructiona materials, such that experimentally induced differences in motivation will produce differences in learning outcomes (Leutner, 2014). Unfortunately, this literature has also assumed that motivation is a state construct akin to emotion that may or may not occur in a given learning situation (Leutner, 2014). Thus, multimedia learning research has hypothesised that the use of emotional design features, such as decorative illustrations and appealing graphics (Magner, Schwonke, Aleven, Popescu, & Renkl, 2014; Plass, Heidig, Hayward, Homer, & Um, 2014), and the use of induced confusion (D'Mello, Lehman, Pekrun, & Graesser, 2014) would promote deeper processing of learning materials and effortfu deliberation. Unsurprisingly, these and related studies have produced inconclusive results (Mayer, 2016)

This outcome prompted a leading multimedia researcher to assert that the failure to establish “the crucial link between motivation/emotion and cognitive processing” is “presumably due to the unavailability of appropriate mea sures of cognitive processing,” meaning that “the effects of motivation/emotion on learning can, at least for the present papers, only be interpreted in terms of modified cognitive processing” (Leutner, 2014, p. 175). These assertions by Leutner are problematic in several ways. First, Leutner fails to distinguish motivation from emotion. Second, he does not recognise that motivation is not “a state construct that … may, or may not, occur in a given learning situation” (Leutner, 2014, p. 175). In fact, motivation comprises many constructs and is relevant to all learning situations given its foundational role in human cognition. Third, Leutner overlooks the crucial distinction between extrinsic and intrinsic motivation. Finally, in lamenting the absence “in the present papers” of “appropriate measures of cognitiv processing” (Leutner, 2014, p. 175), he assumes the possibility that such “appropriate measures” (ie, measures of transfer) could have been constructed. This ignores a substantial body of literature that has long contested the nature and utility of transfer as a useful measure of learning outcomes (Bransford, 1999; Bransford, Brown, & Cocking, 2000; Lobato, 2006). As Barnett and Ceci (2002, p. 612) observed, “there is little agreement in the scholarly commu nity about the nature of transfer, the extent to which it occurs, and the nature of its underlying mechanisms.”

We argue that the underdeveloped state of multimedia learning literature is a direct consequence of the failure to theoretically integrate the role of user motivation. We further claim that this failure can be remedied by invokin fresh IS theory to bridge foundational theories of human cognition to new theory that informs the design of multi media instructional materials.

## 3 | INDUCING MOTIVATION IN MULTIMEDIA LEARNING

## 3.1 | Linking motivation theory to the fostering of generative processing

To account for the role of motivation in multimedia learning, we invoke theoretical models from two recent IS empirical studies. First, the MISC model (P. B. Lowry et al., 2014) is based largely upon expectations‐disconfirmation theory (Brown, Venkatesh, Kuruzovich, & Massey, 2008), which extends the role of motivation in interactions between humans and computers. The MISC model explains the separate roles of intrinsic and extrinsic motivations and how the fulfilment of each motivation affects system‐use outcome variables differently through met expectations. Second, the HMSAM (P B. Lowry et al. 2013) is based upon cognitive absorption (CA) constructs associated with floy theory (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990). We propose combining the MISC model and the HMSAM into an integrated process/variance model to generate an improved theory of multimedia learning

The left portion of Figure 3 illustrates the nomology of the MISC portion of the model, corresponding to a user's initial encounter with the multimedia materials. The MISC model accounts for the cognitive processes that link var ious motivations to expectations and then to behaviours. Motivations operate as direct antecedents to the expecta tions that users form when predicting the performance of the materials. Users' decisions to continue versus abando will turn on whether their expectations are either positively disconfirmed (ie, exceed their expectations) or negativel disconfirmed. The HMSAM, as we contextualise it for multimedia learning, is illustrated with the middle of Figure 3. It explains and predicts our additional theorising that when multimedia instructional materials induce immersion, users will find the materials satisfving: will complete their use of the materials: and will achieve enhanced cognitive and behavioural outcomes as represented by the right potion of Figure 3.

The MISC model and the HMSAM are each originally variance models, whereas in our adaptation, the arrows pointing to and from “decision to continue or abandon” indicate a decision point and thus invoke process logic. I the MISC model, motivations are key to expectations. In the HMSAM, motivations are represented as the mediators. Invoking the integrated MISC‐HMSAM model begs the question: How should the content of the multimedia instructional materials be designed so as to foster generative processing and thereby induce the effect that motivations have on expectations and important outcomes, such as satisfaction, completion, and learning outcomes?

## 3.2 | Story‐based content as a key to fostering generative processing

For multimedia instructional materials to foster generative processing, they must convey a relevant message with a high degree of comprehensibility because such comprehensibility enables the operation of the randomness as genesi principle (Sweller, 2008). Stories are key to such conveyance. As noted, cognitive theory explains that memories are experiential and are indexed as schemas within long‐term memory. Case‐based reasoning theory (CBR) (Kolodner, 1993; Riesbeck & Schank, 1989; Schank, 1995) builds on cognitive theory to explain that those schemas are indexed as stories (or “cases”) that are constructed around experiences. Thus, if memories are experiential and are indexed as schemas within long‐term memory, it follows that multimedia instructional materials can convey a relevant message with a high degree of comprehensibility when their content is designed around a story. As Schank (1995, pp. 84–85 puts it:

The bulk of what passes for intelligence is no more than a massive indexing and retrieval scheme that allows an intelligent entity to determine what information it has in memory that is relevant to the situation at hand, to search for and find that information

The link between CBR and CET (described earlier) is especially clear: Given that people learn from what others do, say, and write (Bandura, 1986), much of the content that people index in long‐term memory is borrowed from th “cases” that others share with them.

It follows that if memories are story‐based, then multimedia instructional materials will benefit from a content design that invokes proven storytelling techniques. Ever since the cognitive revolution<sup>5</sup> of the mid‐20th century, many scholars have recognised stories as essential to a person's cognitive capacity for sense‐making, understanding remembering, explaining, and reasoning (Bruner, 1991; Jonassen & Hernandez‐Serrano, 2002; Orr, 2006; Polkinghorne, 1988; Randall, 1999; Schank, 1995; Schank, 1999). Stories are powerful for this purpose because the narrative method of framing experience is less cognitively demanding than exposition (Bruner, 1990). Thi explains why stories are often used to solve complex problems in the workplace (Henning, 1998; Orr, 1996 Polkinghorne, 1988; Schön, 1983)

Cognitive theory suggests that humans, by evolution, are natural storytellers and thus that early humans used storytelling (oral histories) to relay vital knowledge and skills from one generation to the next—well before the invention of writing systems (Harari, 2015). Recent scholarship regarding the iconic images of horses and bison from the Palaeolithic era confirms this view (Petzinger, 2016). It follows that the use of storytelling as a cognitive device can and should be updated for the age of multimedia learning. Given that our ancestors invoked an ancient version of the multimedia principle by supporting their storytelling with pictures—such as drawings on cave walls and anima hides—it follows that story‐based multimedia instructional materials should make use of relevant images.

A modern analogue of cave drawings is the concept map, such as a box‐and‐arrow diagram depicting a nomological framework. Cognitive learning assimilation theory (Ausubel, Novak, & Hanesian, 1978) proposes that the use of concept maps aids comprehension. Many studies have confirmed that applying concept maps to teaching material enhances motivation, satisfaction, and learning outcomes for learners. Consequently, we propose that story‐based multimedia instructional materials endowed with relevant concept maps operate as a cognitive device that facilitates the effects of motivation and produces the desired results (eg, satisfaction, completion, and enhanced cognitive and behavioural outcomes). We acknowledge that for this argument to be sufficient, it must consider the design of the story and the effect of that design on the salience of the message. ## 4 | THE DESIGN OF STORY‐BASED CONTENT IN MULTIMEDIA INSTRUCTION

To be effective story-based multimedia materials must attract the user's initial interest However educatior researchers have long observed that an initial interest in a topic is not sufficient to sustain a user's attention an engagement. Willingham (2009, p. 280) explained why this happens, using a humorous anecdote:

[I]t's easy to get bored even when you usually like the topic. I'll never forget my eagerness for the day my middle school teacher was to talk about sex … But when the big day came, my friends and I were absolutely disabled with boredom. It's not that the teacher talked about flowers and pollination—he really did talk about human sexuality—but somehow it was still dull. I actually wish I could remembe how he did it; boring a bunch of hormonal teenagers with sex talk is quite a feat.

Willingham's story reveals the consequence of failing to combine interest with relevance. Interest is a motivational variable that refers to the psychological state of engaging with ideas over time (Hidi & Renninger, 2006). The degree of a person's interest has been found to significantly influence attention (Ainley & Hidi. 2014: Hidi Renninger, & Krapp, 2004), goals (Harackiewicz, Barron, Tauer, Carter, & Elliot, 2000), and levels of learning (Durik & Harackiewicz, 2003). Interest includes both affective and cognitive development (Ainley & Hidi, 2014; Hidi et al. 2004). Interest begins with situational interest, which is always motivational (Hidi & Berndorff, 1998), and proceed to the point where that situational interest is perceived as relevant to individual interest (Hidi & Renninger. 2006) The progression from situational to individual interest is an outcome not only of cognitive evaluation but also of emotional (ie, affective) arousal via the release of a neurotransmitter (ie, dopamine) through neural circuits (Ainley & Hidi 2014; Renninger & Wozniak, 1985).

Affective arousal that produces individual interest can be prompted by a well‐told story. Studies have shown that such a story can be enabled by a three‐act narrative structure (Ackerman, 2003). To be effective, a story must describe people who pursue desires against challenging odds (Ackerman, 2003). This approach usually takes the form of a three‐act narrative: problematisation, struggle, and resolution. The formulation of a three‐act narrative structur has a timeless and cross‐cultural salience dating to the role of ancient myths as devices that relate the human condition (Campbell & Moyers, 2011; Jung, 2014; Parry & Parry, 1987).

## 5 | GUIDANCE FOR RESEARCH OPPORTUNITIES IN IS MANAGEMENT EDUCATION

S. Lee et al. (2015) define that term. In this section, we offer research guidance that is grounded in settled theory regarding the role of user motivation for each mode. In doing so, we focus principally on the use of immersive NAVs in the stage 1 activities. We propose that, as an alternative to online lectures, the use of immersive NAVs during stage 1 will promote cognitive evaluation and affective arousal. Consistent with CoLT and CBR, immersive NAVs can produce story‐based schemas that are indexed within long‐term memory for problem‐solving retrieval. According to CoLT, the meaning of those schemas is idiosyncratic (Hase. 2016: Sumara & Davis. 1997), It follows that the use of immersive NAVs wil produce divergent views among individual students regarding how to solve the ill‐defined problem. It further follows that when those students collaborate in a small team (during stage 2), their views will converge via the social pro cesses that, according to SCT, inform most learning. This divergence and convergence describes the double-loop learn ing cycle (Argyris & Schön, 1997). The purpose of this mechanism, advocated by Hase (2016), is to reframe a problem so as to suggest radically different potential solutions and thereby promote discontinuous improvement in problem solving (Kim, 1997). We claim that in this way, the use of immersive NAVs during stage 1 will catalyse the team‐based collaborative learning activities of PBL instruction.

Our multimedia‐enabled flipped‐classroom model of PBL concludes with SRL in stage 3. Although the capacity for SRL is a hallmark of competent disciplinary practice (Zimmerman, 2002), SRL theories have yet to be designed and contextualised for operationalization within a technology‐enabled information system (Hull, 2017). In the subse quent discussion of research opportunities regarding SRL, we provide guidance for research that would inform such operationalization.

## 6 | CONCLUSION

In updating and building upon G. Lowry and Turner's (2005) recommendations for what students of IS management education should learn and how they should learn it, we offer a roadmap for three new areas of research, Our rec ommendations are timely because they guide research that can rejuvenate the underdeveloped literature of multime dia learning and thereby provide an evidence-based foundation for the widespread and accelerating adoption of multimedia instruction. They are bold because they present the promise of developing that literature in ways that are unique to the IS field and that position IS researchers to make substantial and ongoing intellectual and practica contributions in this area of research. Moreover, they are original because they invite new types of systems‐based deployments to invigorate the use of PBL for students in IS management education. Our pivotal contributions anticipate these and many other breakthroughs in the theory‐based and empirically validated instructional design of mul timedia instructional materials. Such breakthroughs would represent significant scholarly contributions because of their potential to enable high‐utility outcomes, not only for science but also for the practice—and, indeed, the democratisation—of higher learning. Because such materials will be deployed within an increasingly influential form of IS, IS scholars are uniquely well‐positioned to lead this exciting research.
