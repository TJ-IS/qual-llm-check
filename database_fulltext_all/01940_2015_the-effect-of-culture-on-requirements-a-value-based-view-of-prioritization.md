# The effect of culture on requirements: a value-based view of prioritization

Tuure Tuunanen<sup>1</sup> and I-Te Kuo<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Department of Computer Science and Information Systems, University of Jyväskylä Jyväskylä, Finland; <sup>2</sup>Department of Information Systems and Operations Management, University of Auckland, Auckland, New Zealand

Correspondence: Tuure Tuunanen, Computer Science and Information Systems, University of Jyväskylä, PO Box 35, Jyväskylä FIN-40014, Finland. Tel: +358 400 362 601: Fax: +358 8 553 1890

## Abstract

This paper examines how culture affects requirements and their prioritization. We analyze the requirements of a mobile service, which were collected from Helsinki, Hong Kong, and Las Vegas. We argue that the current prioritization techniques for requirements do not sufficiently account for the cultural effects, nor does the literature offer information on ways of prioritizing such requirements according to users’ values. We see this as problematic because the literature suggests that culture should be viewed as a set of values. Our findings support the argument that we should use a value-based approach in prioritizing requirements. Furthermore, by focusing on the links between needs and values, we should be able to develop prioritization techniques not only for multicultural settings but also for information systems (IS) development projects that involve subcultures, such as adolescent users or members of specific organizations. Our findings show that this can be a powerful way of understanding the differences between various subcultures of IS users. In addition, we demonstrate how the laddering interviewing technique can be used to support this work. The proposed value-based requirements prioritization approach is also ready for industry applications and it is agnostic to the underlying IS development method used by developers. ## Introduction

Information technology has reshaped every aspect of our lives. It has led to the globalization of societies. Communication has never been easier. It is now dif<sup>fi</sup>cult, if not impossible, for a society to live isolated from the in<sup>fl</sup>uences of other cultures (Walsham, 2002), as each society directly or indirectly engages with foreign cultures through both social interaction and the <sup>fl</sup>ow of information. With regard to the globalization effect, early researchers like Levitt (1983) have predicted the ‘homogenization’ of multiple cultures and the emergence of a new global market in which all products are globally standardized.

This prophecy, however, was never ful<sup>fi</sup>lled. Instead, abundant empirical evidence has suggested that globalization has, in fact, enlarged the con<sup>fl</sup>icts and contradictions between different cultures (Walsham, 2002). The literature has pointed out that the in<sup>fl</sup>uence and presence of culture are becoming more prevalent as information systems (IS) researchers <sup>fi</sup>nd themselves facing increasingly contradictory social consequences that the traditional, technical-economical rationales fail to explain even in studies where culture is not the main focus (Leidner & Kayworth, 2006; Pauleen et al, 2006). There is a need to enhance our understanding of culture to inform different studies. Tuunanen et al (2006) found substantial differences in user requirements for IS across three different data gathering locations and national cultures, namely, Helsinki, Hong Kong, and Las Vegas. They concluded that there is a need for research examining how culture in<sup>fl</sup>uences the feature preferences of individuals.

Much research has posited that requirements are essentially social constructs (Luff et al, 1993), and it is critical that we gather both social and technical aspects of the requirements to better re<sup>fl</sup>ect the users’ wishes. Furthermore, Robey & Azevedo (1994) emphasized that a better understanding of culture allows us to ‘penetrate the surface of the users’ requirements’ and thus gain insight into the underlying values or assumptions of the users. In light of this, it is surprising that, to date, there has been a lack of research on how culture impacts user requirements. The only notable exception is a study by Thanasankit (2002), which found that culture plays a critical role in affecting users’ requirements as it provides a context for de<sup>fi</sup>ning the behavior of everyone involved.

In this paper, our objective is to study how culture affects users’ requirements and how this in<sup>fl</sup>uences their preferences for the features of an IS, especially when considering requirements prioritization. In our opinion, Robey & Azevedo’s (1994) suggestion to investigate the value structures of users is intriguing. Tuunanen et al (2006), and more recently Kukreja et al (2013), have similarly proposed a value-based prioritization of requirements. Thus, our research question is the following: How can we facilitate the value-based prioritization of requirements for users from different cultures or geographical locations?

In order to investigate this, we used the data collected in a previous study by Tuunanen et al (2006). We believe that this data set provides an excellent opportunity for the study of culture because it consists of laddering interviews (Reynolds & Gutman, 1988; Peffers et al, 2003) that allow us to gain insights into the values of each participant. In this study (Tuunanen et al, 2006), substantial value differences across the different locations were observed but could not be explained. By applying Rokeach’s (1973) value model, we have attempted to investigate whether these observed differences can be explained by a valuebased view of culture. The analysis was conducted at regional levels across three research locations and at individual levels within each research location.

In the next section, we will review the literature on requirement prioritization. This is followed by a review of culture and an argument that we should conceptualize culture as a set of values. Next, we describe our research methodology and present our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Finally, we discuss the implications of the study and present our conclusions.

## Requirements prioritization

Traditional IS development methods such as the waterfall or the spiral consider requirements prioritization to be a part of requirements engineering. Requirements engineering is also traditionally seen as a linear process. One of the traditional views of requirements engineering is the four-step activity model (Kotonya & Sommerville, 1998). In the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage, analysts work with users to discover their requirements (Pohl, 1994; Kotonya & Sommerville, 1998). In the next stage of requirements analysis and negotiation, different stakeholders analyze the requirements and negotiate to decide which should be chosen and prioritized. This is followed by requirements documentation that aims to create a requirements speci<sup>fi</sup>cation at an appropriate level of detail (1999). The <sup>fi</sup>nal stage is requirements validation, in which requirements are also systematically checked for consistency, completeness, and validity.

In this paper, we focus on requirements prioritization as part of the overall requirements engineering process. According to previous literature, requirements prioritization has recently become even more important to both research and practice (Tan & Pawitra, 2001; Lehtola & Kauppinen, 2006; Mathiassen et al, 2007; Racheva et al, 2010; Bakalova et al, 2011; Berntsson Svensson et al, 2011; Mathiassen & Tuunanen, 2011). One of the reasons for this change is that many of the IS today are developed for markets. Market-driven development often means that there are no easily identi<sup>fi</sup>able customers, so requirements need to be discovered (Karlsson et al, 2007). There may well be thousands of requirements remaining to be considered after the requirements discovery process has been completed (Tuunanen et al, 2006). Furthermore, agile IS development methods are quickly changing the way that many of these projects are currently being carried out. Agile development emphasizes small iterations and con stant feedback between the developers and the customers with regard to requirements. This emphasizes developers need to understand which requirements should be met <sup>fi</sup>rst and which might be stored in the requirements back log and roadmap for the IS (Lehtola et al, 2005; Racheva et al, 2010). For example, in SCRUM (Schwaber, 1995; Rising & Janoff, 2000; Salo & Abrahamsson, 2008), the requirements backlog is initially developed at the beginning of the project and then continuously updated during follow-up iterations or sprints. Another similar approach is ‘work item lists’ that have been adopted, such as Open Uni<sup>fi</sup>ed Process (OpenUP) and Agile Uni<sup>fi</sup>ed Process (AUP; Ambler & Lines, 2012). Thus, we see increasing pressure on <sup>fi</sup>rms that originates not only with the need to manage an increasingly diverse set of customers and users but also with the way IS development is carried out today. It is very dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for any <sup>fi</sup>rm, large or small, to satisfy all of the desired requirements of their customers (Karlsson et al, 2007). Moreover, it is essential to <sup>fi</sup>nd the ‘sweet spot where requirements meet the target market’s important needs, after which the system or application can be varied to prioritize the remaining requirements according to market needs.

We recognize <sup>fi</sup>ve metrics that have been applied to requirements prioritization: resources, performance, adaptation, design, and usability. These are summarized in Table 1 and further discussed below.

Mathiassen et al (2007) and Mathiassen & Tuunanen (2011) have adopted a resource-based view for de<sup>fi</sup>ning the objectives of requirements prioritization. According to them, requirements prioritization activities are resourcecentric. They have applied various types of analyses and decision-support techniques to help designers and managers decide how to use their <sup>fi</sup>rm’s limited resources: technologies, skills, and time. Mathiassen et al (2007) have approached this from a risk management point of view, which is similar to that of many linear IS development methods. Hence, requirements prioritization demands create risks for the project at hand, and these risks should be mitigated. To that end, Mathiassen et al (2007) proposed using a contingency model to select speci<sup>fi</sup>c requirements prioritization techniques for eight stereotypes of projects that were characterized according to each project’s requirements risk pro<sup>fi</sup>le. Later, Mathiassen & Tuunanen (2011) also offered ways to identify and analyze the requirement risks for IS project management purposes.

Other IS researchers, in turn, use a more technical approach and apply a quality framework for assessing different prioritization techniques on requirements (Keller et al, 1990; Karlsson et al, 1998). Keller et al (1990) have proposed a framework that is divided into three categories of prioritization metrics for requirements: performance, design, and adaptation. Performance-based metrics consider the functionality of a system in terms of ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, integrity, and reliability. Keller et al (1990) also mentioned the survivability of requirements, which is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the capability of a system to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll its mission in a timely manner in the presence of attacks, failures, or accidents (Ellison et al, 1999). Design-based metrics are more focused on technical features, such as maintainability, which is important for longer-term architectural planning and future releases of new systems or applications. Correctness and veri<sup>fi</sup>ability, in turn, are closely linked to assuring that the requirements are ones that the users will actually value and that those requirements can be validated. Adaptation-related metrics are similarly technical in nature as they can be linked to the object-oriented development and modularization of codes in application development; see, for example, Rothenberger (2003). The reusability of requirements is the backbone of these metrics, and others build on it. Portability and interoperability are tied to how code modules can be reused in different projects and also to how standardized communication between objects and classes of objects is. Flexibility and expandability, in turn, illustrate how receptive to change the requirements are.

Finally, usability has also been promoted as a prioritization metric for requirements (Jokela et al, 2003). Usability means designing IS so they are easy to learn, contain functionalities that users really want and need, and are easy to use (Gould & Lewis, 1985). Later, Lauesen & Younessi (1998) identi<sup>fi</sup>ed <sup>fi</sup>ve more speci<sup>fi</sup>c features of usability. They are: ease of learning – the system must be easy to learn for users with varying skill levels. Task ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency – the system must be ef<sup>fi</sup>cient. Ease of remembering – the system must not be cognitively stressful to use. Understandability – the user must understand what the system does. And, subjective satisfaction - the user must feel satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the system. Berander et al (2006) have proposed one additional feature: the attractiveness of the IS in terms of functionality and user interface.

The fact that researchers have recently emphasized the importance of the value-based prioritization of users’ requirements (Tuunanen et al, 2006; Kukreja et al, 2013) is particularly interesting. This is in line with Robey & Azevedo’s (1994) earlier argument in favor of a more thorough understanding of the requirements and needs of users as well as a deeper appreciation of how culture enables us to gain insight into the underlying values or assumptions of the users. To this aim, we next review the literature on the value-based view of culture and develop a typology that can potentially be applied to prioritization activities for requirements.

## Value-based view of culture

Myriad attempts by researchers have been made to de<sup>fi</sup>ne the concept of culture (Kroeber & Kluckhohn, 1952). Hofstede’s (1980) has de<sup>fi</sup>ned culture from a value perspective and subsequently stated that culture is a ‘set of shared assumptions that result in a common frame of reference by members of a society or more simply as mental software’. This de<sup>fi</sup>nition has been popular in recent years. Lytle et al (1999), in turn, de<sup>fi</sup>ned culture as ‘the inferred pattern of beliefs and values that assists us in interpreting our past and future behaviors, which would be meaningless without them’. Similarly, Schein (1985) has stated that culture is multi-faceted, and Triandis (1995) has argued for the subjective nature of culture, viewing each individual as the product of a unique combination of cultural patterns. Finally, Avison & Myers (1995) have suggested that culture is dependent on the subjective interpretations of all individuals, which creates dif<sup>fi</sup>culty in de<sup>fi</sup>ning culture because it is forever changing.

In a more detailed inspection of the culture models, Morden (1999) has argued that they can be categorized into three types. According to him, the <sup>fi</sup>rst category of culture model distinguishes cultures based upon a single dimension or variable, and he points out that there is an abundance of such culture models available in the literature. One example is the culture model by Hall (1997), which proposed that cultures can be differentiated, based on their level of ‘context’, in terms of how individuals seek information and communicate. Another was proposed by Lewis (1992), who pointed out the ‘monochronic-polychronic’ nature of individuals, which describes the ability of individuals to multitask. Triandis (1995), in turn, has stressed the importance of the ‘idiocentric-allocentric’ dimension. Finally, Hui & Triandis (1986) have suggested the bipolar opposites of ‘primary control’ vs ‘secondary control’, which, according to them, describe the perceptions of individuals about their abilities to change the environment and, especially, describe the differences between Eastern and Western cultures.

The second category of culture focuses on several dimensions at the same time. The accepted cultural model has been developed by Hofstede (1980). Hofstede proposed that national cultures could be distinguished based upon their differences in four dimensions: uncertainty avoidance, power distance, masculinity, and collectivism–individualism. A <sup>fi</sup>fth-dimension, long-term orientation, was later added (Hofstede & Hofstede, 2005).

The last set of culture models differs from the previous two in the sense that the dimensions of the models are historically or socially informed. According to this literature, the formation of culture does not occur in one day but is constructed and reconstructed through historical events (Myers & Tan, 2002). By referring to studies done by anthropologists, Avison & Myers (1995) have criticized the approach of traditional culture models, which treat cultures as if they are something that can be easily identi<sup>fi</sup>ed and differentiated according to a set of variables. Instead, they argued that we should be more interested in the process by which cultures emerge and are henceforth constructed. They argued that culture emerges gradually and that it is historically and socially informed. While culture assists human beings in constructing the world, humans are also engaged in the process of perpetually reconstructing culture. Corbitt et al (2004) have provided an interesting complementary interpretation of the socially and historically informed view of culture (Avison & Myers, 1995; Myers & Tan, 2002). Using the theory of code systems, they examined the process through which cultures are produced and interpreted. Social construction theory, which is the foundation of the code system theory, identi<sup>fi</sup>es two levels of the world: the physical world and the social world. Tangible, natural objects represent the physical world. The meanings that are attached to these objects are dependent upon the social world or the social context to which the individual belongs. In other words, diverse interpretations can be derived from an identical object depending on the social world of the interpreters. They argued, therefore, that culture can be represented as a set of codes that help culture bearers interpret the world. These codes are often taken for granted by the culture bearer.

Furthermore, Corbitt et al (2004) emphasized that being a member of a particular culture requires understanding not only of the codes but also of their social contexts. The dominance of the codes is determined by the social context. For example, a French person living in an Asian country may personally possess French traditions, but in order to <sup>fi</sup>t into the Asian culture, these traditions, in the form of codes, are not socialized and spread. In addition, they pointed out that these types of codes are also historically and geographically situated. They argued that codes are multi-layered with the result that cultures tend to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the codes that exist at the macro-level (Danesi, 1994) but not at the micro-level. This implies that the behavior of individuals is not totally determined by the culture to which they belong. A similar argument was provided in Straub et al (2002), who visualized an individual as a ‘virtual onion’ in which each layer represents a speci<sup>fi</sup>c culture that the person embraces. These layers dynamically shift and swap places depending upon the social contexts.

In this study, to analyze our data, we have conceptualized culture as a set of values. We de<sup>fi</sup>ne value as:

… an enduring belief that a speci<sup>fi</sup>c mode of conduct or end-state of existence is personally or socially preferable to an opposite or converse mode of conduct or end-state of existence. (Rokeach, 1973)

We believe that there are bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to this conceptualization. It is currently the commonly used approach in studies about building culture models in various disciplines (e.g., Hofstede, 1980). Taking advantage of the multi-disciplinary nature of the term ‘value’, by conceptualizing culture as a set of values will make it easier for us to assimilate the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from various disciplines into our discussion. According to Schein (1985), using values as lenses for cultural studies analysis is appropriate because they are more visible and thus easier to interpret than cultural artifacts.

There are, however, various perspectives to consider with regard to the nature and characteristics of values. Hofstede (1980, p. 18) stated that value, which is ‘a tendency to prefer a certain state of affairs over others’, is an attribute of the individual as well as of the collective. Values are, therefore, in<sup>fl</sup>uential in guiding our thinking, behaviors, and choices. According to the literature, values hold the following <sup>fi</sup>ve characteristics: Values are concepts of beliefs, desired end-states, and behaviors that transcend speci<sup>fi</sup>c situations and guide the selection or evaluation of behavior and events, and they are ordered by relative importance (Schwartz & Bilsky, 1987).

Owing to the differences in the nature of how value in<sup>fl</sup>uences the behavior of an individual, it has often been suggested that there is more than one component underlying the construct of values. Hofstede (1980) proposed that values could be split into value and practice. Human beings acquire values in their early childhood before the age of 10, as asserted by the literature on development psychology, whereas practice is acquired later in life through social experiences (Hofstede & Hofstede, 2005). In comparison, practice is much easier to change and more visible as it is manifested through symbols, heroes, and rituals whereas values tend to be much more enduring than practice in terms of ease of modi<sup>fi</sup>cation (Hofstede & Hofstede, 2005). As identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by Karahanna et al (2005), a similar distinction was made by Erez & Earley (1993), who argued that values can be categorized into core and peripheral values. They suggested that core values are relatively more important than peripheral values at higher culture levels (e.g., national culture), but peripheral values are relatively more important than core values at in lowerlevel culture (e.g., professional culture).

The value typology proposed by Rokeach (1973) divides values into four types. First, we can distinguish terminal and instrumental values. A terminal value represents a goal value or a so-called ‘end-of-state’ that an individual aims to achieve; an instrumental value represents a behavior value or a so-called ‘mode of conduct’ value, which is used to achieve an individual terminal value. Second, values can be further divided into ‘interpersonally focused’ or ‘intrapersonally focused’ values. Rokeach (1973) empirically developed a list of 36 terminal and instrumental values in six consequent studies. Rokeach also suggested that value constructs should be categorized according to interpersonal and intrapersonal focus and according to four types of values, namely social, personal, moral, and competency. He argued that interpersonally focused values refer to values that are concerned with other people whereas intrapersonally focused values refer to values that are personal. Finally, Rokeach stated that instrumental values can be further divided into moral and competency values and that terminal values can be divided into personal and social values.

Later, Karahanna et al (2005) promoted Rokeach’s value typology in order to assess the different levels of culture that affect organizations and IS users. They argued that there are <sup>fi</sup>ve levels of culture: supranational, national, professional, organizational, and group. Supranational covers regional, ethnic, religious, and linguistic matters. Karahanna et al (2005) also wanted to re<sup>fi</sup>ne the original typology by linking the value constructs to the four abovementioned types of values: terminal, instrumental, interpersonally focused, and intrapersonally focused. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, Karahanna et al (2005) proposed that moral values include values such as being honest and polite, and competency values include values like ambition or courage. Furthermore, Karahanna et al (2005) suggested that personal values would include, for example, self-respect and inner harmony and that social values would include security, equality, and freedom.

We have summarized this value typology in Table 2. The value typology is based on the original framework by Rokeach (1973), and it is divided into terminal and instrumental values and interpersonal and intrapersonal foci. We have extended these concepts according to the suggestions of Karahanna et al (2005) by creating a matrix of social, personal, moral, and competency values. Finally, Rokeach’s 36 original value constructs have been placed into the matrix. We see that this extended value typology provides a unique opportunity to study the impact of culture at different levels of analysis.

## Research methodology

We have conducted a quantitative content analysis to interpret the data on hand. According to Weber, content analysis is a ‘research method that uses a set of procedures to make valid inferences from text’ (Weber, 1990, p. 82). We argue that this approach offers several bene<sup>fi</sup>ts that complement our research objectives. It has been shown that content analysis is suitable for ‘re<sup>fl</sup>ecting cultural patterns of groups, institutions, or societies’ (Weber, 1990). It has been widely employed in other disciplines, where it has been found useful in providing insights (Holsti, 1969; Myers & Avison, 1997; Krippendorff, 2004). Lastly, it is well suited to our research approach as content analysis enables us to investigate the values of our study participants and thus interpret their understanding of culture.

In this study, we employed an earlier data set for conducting our analysis (Tuunanen et al, 2006). This data set was gathered for the purpose of developing requirements for a set of mobile services, or apps, supported by various IS. The requirements elicitation and analysis phase of the study included more than 20 Finnish organizations interested in digital marketing. We recruited 13 marketing professionals from <sup>fi</sup>rms that participated in the study to help us with ours, and we designed a group support system session to de<sup>fi</sup>ne the scope and the goal for the project (Tuunanen et al, 2011). Nokia, the key participant, wished to ascertain how consumers might want to use ‘presence’ status information in new mobile apps and services when users included their presence status in their mobile phone contact lists, enabling subscribers to know their availability for a voice call or other interaction. Presence information was a technology that was not available to public at that time. Consequently, the <sup>fi</sup>rms had only a limited understanding about which potential service features would be worthwhile or how they might differ in various cultures or locations around the world. The results of the group support session included recommendations for three application areas for mobile presence-based services: (1) presence-enabled mobile travel services while en route; (2) presence-enabled mobile service while out and about in the city; and (3) presence-enabled mobile information service for special interest group members or communities. We added one extra stimulus to the <sup>fi</sup>nal list: a presenceenabled mobile service ‘for you’ (Tuunanen et al, 2011). The stimuli list is presented in Appendix A.

We collected the primary data from potential service users in three locations: Finland, China, and the U.S. To develop a sample of lead users, we used the ‘snowball’ method to recruit participants starting with a small sample of known lead users (von Hippel, 1986; Rogers, 1995). We obtained referrals from all participants to others whom they thought might also be lead users, and then we used a knowledge and technology-use screening process to validate each participant’s status as a lead user (Tuunanen et al, 2006). To screen the participants as lead users, we asked each participant a set of six questions plus a second set of questions to ascertain the experience the participant had with mobile services (see Appendix B). The questionnaire responses were compared with benchmark responses to determine whether the prospective participant was a lead user. Experience scores were benchmarked separately for each location because of local differences in technology diffusion (Tuunanen et al, 2011). This recruitment process resulted in a panel of 81 participants: 28 from Helsinki, 27 from Hong Kong, and 26 from Las Vegas. The demographics of the participants are summarized in Table 3.

We conducted interviews using the laddering technique. In each location, we asked users to think about ideas for new services, using presence and location data, which would be valuable to them. The interviews began with the presentation of a list of stimuli (Appendix A) intended to suggest ideas about possible service applications. These stimuli were intended to suggest ideas to the participants about possible applications. We then asked the participants to rank the stimuli in terms of their importance. Then, one at a time, for the two highest-ranked stimuli, the interviewer asked each participant to describe the applications that would be important to him or her, including the desirable features of said applications. The interviewer proceeded to ask the subject to explain why each particular feature was important, so as to elicit the consequences that the participant expected from the feature. The interviewing process continued with a series of ‘Why would that be important?’ questions to elicit what the subject expected as an end result. To elicit more concrete system attributes, we asked the participants a series of questions, such as ‘What would there be about the system that would make you think that it would do that?’ The data were recorded as chains of feature – consequence – values, as described by Peffers et al (2003). The data were recorded in our notes as chains of feature, consequences, and values and resulted in nearly 3500 individual requirement statements.

More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the data collection was performed with a variation of the critical success chains method (Peffers et al, 2003) that employs a laddering interview technique to elicit tacit needs and requirements. Laddering interview technique is based on the personal construct theory developed by George Kelly (1955). Kelly was a practicing school psychologist in the Midwest in the 1930s, and he saw an increasing need to understand his patients better. He attempted to resolve this by modeling how his patients saw the relationships between states of the universe, the consequences of those states, and the impact of those consequences on their individual values, in order to better understand how they and their teachers understood the world differently. These personal constructs (Pervin, 1993) result from our individual observations and interpretations of events. Each of us has individual multi-dimensional models (constructs) that describe the attributes and behavior of objects and events, their resulting consequences, and their effect on our values.

Later, Gutman (1982) proposed that product attributes are relevant to consumers because of the consequences derived from consumption behavior and that these consequences are relevant for the personal values they help satisfy for the consumer. A complete description of a sequence of associations is referred to as a means-end chain. To study the means-end structures consumers have about a product, Reynolds & Gutman (1988) developed an interview approach called laddering. In laddering, interview participants are typically given a choice or decision task within a product category and then asked to describe what particular product attributes were the basis of their decision. Then, participants are questioned to uncover the relevant product consequences that they derive from the attribute. Probing questioning continues until they describe the <sup>fi</sup>nal personal values they have satis<sup>fi</sup>ed through product consumption.

## Coding scheme and classi<sup>fi</sup>cation scheme

Because there was an extremely large amount of raw data with a total of 478 laddering interview chains (Reynolds & Gutman, 1988; Peffers et al, 2003) available, we constructed a coding scheme to selectively include only the information that was relevant to our study to avoid becoming overwhelmed (Miles & Huberman, 1994). In constructing a coding scheme, we <sup>fi</sup>rst de<sup>fi</sup>ned our recording unit at the sentence level. Minimizing the size of the recording unit for coding improved the objectivity of our research by reducing the potential ambiguity and con<sup>fl</sup>icting cues that may occur in larger recording units (e.g., a whole paragraph) (Weber, 1990). In addition, only values attributes at the end of each chain were coded because values have been the main focus of our research. However, if the values attributes were not present in the chain (e.g., half-completed), the last attribute at the end of the chain was coded if it provided enough information about the value for coding. Otherwise, the chain was discarded. To simplify our study, we have adopted the value typology framework as the theoretical basis for our coding scheme (see Table 2). We constructed a dictionary of categories that de<sup>fi</sup>ned the rules for tagging words to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c category (Table 4). This strategy helped us improve coding ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and standardize our classi<sup>fi</sup>cation (Weber, 1990).

We have also addressed various issues relating to the validity of our classi<sup>fi</sup>cation scheme. Our coding categories were derived from the study performed by Rokeach (1973). We carefully constructed a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation dictionary (Table 4) based on Rokeach’s category de<sup>fi</sup>nitions to ensure coding consistency and face validity. To code words that have more than one meaning, we adopted the single classi<sup>fi</sup>cation strategy, that is, each text is assigned to one and only one category. This strategy allowed us to employ quantitative statistical analysis techniques later in the interpretation process. However, to code words that may have multiple meanings, our disambiguation rule was to examine the product attributes and performance consequences that informed the values in the same laddering interview chain. This helped the coder interpret the true meaning of the value mentioned by the participants. We have attached exemplary chains and their coding in Appendix C. ## Discussion

We set out to discuss whether a value-based view of culture facilitates the prioritization of requirements, that is, which IS features are more important to users in different cultural settings. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are promising. First of all, the laddering interview data set (Reynolds & Gutman, 1988; Peffers et al, 2003; Tuunanen et al, 2006) adapted well to our study. The laddering interview data structure enabled the analyst to apply the value typology to distinguish value differences between user groups at different levels of the analysis. In addition, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings provide a foundation for further engineering methods and the development of prioritization techniques for requirements so that practitioners will be able to more carefully develop IS like mobile services for particular user groups. Finally, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings contribute toward providing insights into the growing body of literature on IS research (Leidner & Kayworth, 2006) that recognizes the importance of understanding the cultural context of the users. In what follows, we <sup>fi</sup>rst discuss the theoretical contributions of the study and then elaborate on the practical implications of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

## Theoretical contributions

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings demonstrate that looking at the values behind IS features can create a fruitful foundation for understanding cultural in<sup>fl</sup>uences at different levels. The proposed value typology is a useful foundation for this kind of analysis, and it can be applied when using current techniques for discovering requirements like laddering interviewing or when developing new ones that focus on supporting value-based requirements prioritization (Kukreja et al, 2013) or the co-creation of value in requirements and IS development work (Tuunanen et al, 2010). We observe that more applied research is needed to develop new, comprehensive requirements for engineering methods that would package together requirements discovery and analysis techniques so that they would purposefully support the value-based prioritization of requirements for different user groups.<sup>1</sup>

The results of our research allow us to state that the presented typology <sup>fi</sup>ts well with the requirements collected via the laddering interview technique (Reynolds & Gutman, 1988; Peffers et al, 2003), which incorporates a feature–reasoning–values or goals structure into the data on requirements. However, we observe that other discovery work on requirements and analysis techniques can also bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the application of value typology. One example is the analytic hierarchy process technique (Saaty, 1990; Karlsson et al, 1998). Thus, practitioners could have the needed tools to accomplish a value-based prioritization of requirements in a timely manner. In addition, we see that the tools should facilitate ef<sup>fi</sup>cient and easy development, for example, graphical illustrations of the prioritized requirements, and support the creation of development roadmaps for IS.

By using the value typology, we also observed statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences among the research locations. This is an intriguing <sup>fi</sup>nding as recently there has been some debate over Rokeach’s original argument that values form the basis of cultural differences between regions; see, for example, Fischer & Schwartz (2011). Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are contrary to the criticism that Rokeach has received, and we argue in favor of a value-based view of culture. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, therefore, strengthen the earlier observation by Tuunanen et al (2006) that there are differences in how participants value the features of mobile service in the three given geographical locations. However, whereas Tuunanen et al (2006) studied the level of the system features and provided anecdotal evidence, we are able to both quantify the differences and show more detailed levels of a value-based analysis of the data set on requirements.

The analysis also illustrates interesting differences between the Eastern (Hong Kong) and Western (Helsinki and Las Vegas) research locations and also between the two Western locations. In Table 13, it is apparent that in terms of terminal values, the participants in the Western locations put less emphasis on these values than was observed in Hong Kong. However, we should note that we did not <sup>fi</sup>nd statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant results in social values between the locations but only in personal values such as a comfortable life, a sense of accomplishment, happiness, pleasure, and so on. This is an interesting <sup>fi</sup>nding given that our analysis also indicates that participants from Hong Kong tended to express their values in terms of goals and were thus goal-oriented. We found dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to explain this observed difference on personal values between the Western and Eastern locations. One suggestion might be to look at the level of happiness by comparing countries. The United Nations recently published a country happiness index (Helliwell et al, 2013) that places Finland 7th, the U.S. 17th, and Hong Kong 64th in the world. Therefore, one might argue that in Finland, for example, it is less important to explicitly pursue personal values such as a comfortable life, happiness, and so on, as, according to the country happiness index, they are more commonplace than, for example, in Hong Kong.

When we examine instrumental values, we can see an opposite pattern in both moral and competency values. The study participants in Western locations considered them to be more important than the participants in Hong Kong. Hofstede’s earlier <sup>fi</sup>ndings on levels of individualism argue that study participants in Finland and the U.S.

Thus, Hong Kong participants more often associated themselves with intrinsic values that are intangible and are intrinsically rewarding (see Table 4). This <sup>fi</sup>nding is in line with our <sup>fi</sup>ndings on the regional level (cf. Table 13). Furthermore, it is, therefore, not surprising that in the case of instrumental values, the distributions are the opposite and the Western locations are skewed towards higher percentile frequency categories with the difference being that 12 Hong Kong participants responded with more than 50% of instrumental values. This, however, shows that when considering a more detailed level of prioritization on requirements, it is necessary to consider as well the value distribution at the individual level. The differences are not as clear when looking at the interpersonal and intrapersonal values, but this perhaps re<sup>fl</sup>ects the nature of the IS in question – a mobile presence service – which was supposed to enable users to share their current location information such as status updates like, ‘I am in a meeting’ or ‘I am free for lunch’. Therefore, it is not surprising that intrapersonal values dominated participants’ thoughts about the IS in question.

Thus, we can observe that the devil is in the details when we look at the individual level of the distributions of values. We argue that the observed differences can be important when we consider how to support the valuebased prioritization of requirements as the individual-level distributions can be used as, for example, weights in prioritizing requirements at a more detailed level. This phenomenon can potentially provide more re<sup>fi</sup>ned solutions for the prioritization of laddering-based requirements than have been used before. Previously, Tuunanen et al (2006) and Peffers et al (2003) have either asked their study participants to give weights to the laddering chains developed during the interview or, alternatively, used different clustering techniques to develop network maps of requirements detailing the relative importance of requirements in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c project. By using weights that are derived from regional and also from individual distributions of values, analyzing laddering-based requirements data will be more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient but also more transparent. This, in turn, will impact the development of graphical network maps and their applications in development work, such as how <sup>fi</sup>rms develop roadmaps for their IS.

Finally, we posit that there is a shift in how we perceive culture. We concur with Fischer & Schwartz (2011) on their notion that individuals’ values are strongly personal; however, we have not found that there are differences between the values of groups of individuals, in our case, the three geographical study locations that seem to form subcultures of their own. These subcultures probably revolve around topics of interest such as a new mobile service or, alternatively, around a place of work like the university or a corporation. Therefore, we feel that our <sup>fi</sup>ndings can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly alter the way we develop IS and, more speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, how we prioritize requirements. Our literature review also indicates the dominance of the technical aspects in prioritization activities concerning requirements including performance, design, and the adaptation of IS. The literature also recognizes the importance of understanding project resource constraints and different usability-related issues like ease of learning, task ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, ease of remembering, ease of comprehension, subjective satisfaction, and attractiveness. However, what is lacking is a deeper understanding of users’ preferences, or, in other words, a value-based prioritization of requirements. The literature is silent on this topic with the exception of a few articles (Thanasankit, 2002; Tuunanen et al, 2006; Kukreja et al, 2013). We believe that the insights offered in this research make several contributions toward <sup>fi</sup>lling this gap in the literature.

## Practical implications

We argue that the proposed value typology can successfully aid practitioners in conducting a value-based prioritization of requirements. This understanding enables us to address the needs of speci<sup>fi</sup>c user groups more successfully when developing IS, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally when value typology is used in combination with laddering interview data sets. This supports earlier claims by Peffers et al (2003) that laddering interviews can be a powerful tool for understanding contextual information and reasoning behind users’ preferences and requirements regarding IS.

We have also demonstrated that cultural differences have an effect. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings offer valuable insights on increasing project success rates by helping analysts obtain a better understanding of what values are espoused by users. Our value typology can be applied to prioritizing requirements based on instrumental-terminal and interpersonal and intrapersonal qualities. The reported study provides a template for accomplishing this analysis task. The value typology also enables the analyst to distinguish between the competency, moral, personal, and social value sets of users. When developing IS that have, or will have, users in multiple regions or cultural settings that are different in character, this information can be used to design systems so that they will more adequately facilitate the needs of targeted user groups. In practice, this can involve reconsidering the user interface and how it is presented to different user groups, but more importantly, it can involve revealing which features are most important to different user groups.

Furthermore, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that by simply looking at a national culture, we cannot suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently understand what users actually want from their IS. Robey & Azevedo (1994) have argued that we should ‘penetrate the surface of the users’ requirements’ and understand the underlying values or assumptions of the users. We concur and further emphasize the importance of understanding the value structures and systems of the users. However, we should also study how different user groups value different system features and how to prioritize their preferences. Rivard et al (2011) have come to similar conclusions in their recent paper about organizational, culture-based theory on clinical IS implementation in hospitals. Rivard et al (2011) stated that understanding users’ values is vital in order to understand the organizational culture. Leidner & Kayworth (2006) have argued, in a similar vein, that the more users experience a difference in values between the developers and users, the greater the cultural con<sup>fl</sup>ict will be between these two groups. Our study contributes to this literature by providing value typology that can be used by both researchers and practitioners to facilitate the resolution of culturally bound con<sup>fl</sup>ict situations in IS development, and, more speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, in the prioritization of requirements.

Finally, we also see that our <sup>fi</sup>ndings contribute towards facilitating the more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient use of agile IS development methods, such as SCRUM (Schwaber, 1995; Rising & Janoff, 2000; Salo & Abrahamsson, 2008). Traditionally, the agile IS development methods have not emphasized requirements documentation and speci<sup>fi</sup>cation. This does not, however, mean that it would not be important for <sup>fi</sup>rms to understand how to <sup>fi</sup>nd the ‘sweet spot’ of requirements that meet the customers’ important needs. The presented laddering interviewing and analysis technique can be used in any IS development. Thus, we see it as agnostic to IS development methods. However, given that the analysis technique does not require substantial time resources, or in-depth knowledge of social science research or statistics, we feel that the proposed value-based requirements prioritization approach <sup>fi</sup>ts well with the agile philosophy. Furthermore, although laddering interviewing contributes signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly to the powerfulness of the requirements analysis, other interviewing techniques can also be used for requirements discovery purposes. In addition, we see no reason why the results from the proposed way of conducting value-based prioritization of requirements cannot be integrated into the current ways of managing requirements in different agile IS development methods, such as the SCRUM requirements backlog etc.

## Limitations and future research

This study has its limitations. Caution should be taken in interpreting the results of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings due to the limitations in our research design. There is a built-in cultural bias featured in our interpretation process. Our interpretation is subjective, just as in the cases of the other culture theories and models mentioned in our literature review. Thus, potential interpretation bias exists along with other errors that may potentially affect the validity of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. However, this limitation is inherent and cannot be avoided because interpretation is necessary. In this study, we have compared cultural differences using only the four value categories established by Rokeach (1973). Even though they are simple to apply, this also restricts our ability to make <sup>fi</sup>ner comparisons of the actual differences among participants regarding a speci<sup>fi</sup>c value. For example, even though all of the participants may have emphasized the importance of personal values, they may still differ in the actual values they espouse within the same value category (e.g., one may espouse the value of ‘a comfortable life’, while another may espouse the value of ‘an exciting life’). Therefore, we encourage future research to analyze values at a deeper level in order to provide insights to improve the discovery of requirements and elicitation processes.

We also encourage future researchers to focus on identifying more similarities and differences in the models of culture. It has been recognized that one’s behavior is a function of one’s personality and environment (Bandura, 1977). However, to our knowledge, there is a lack of research on speci<sup>fi</sup>cally de<sup>fi</sup>ning what constitutes context and providing a way of measuring and distinguishing differences in context. In this study, we have classi<sup>fi</sup>ed context only into consumer and organizational contexts based on differences in the ability of an individual to make voluntary decisions. We believe that establishing a classi-<sup>fi</sup>catory framework would be valuable in adding to our understanding of culture.

We have demonstrated that culture does have an in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the requirements of users. Thus, one potential research avenue would be to investigate more thoroughly the role of culture in the design and implementation of requirement engineering methods when they are employed in different cultural settings. We believe that it is important that future requirement discovery and analysis techniques incorporate these kinds of insights in order to enhance the accuracy, effectiveness, and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of the engineering process for requirements.

## Conclusions

In response to research calls for enhancing our understanding of culture’s effect on requirements, we have analyzed data collected on requirements in an effort to develop a mobile presence service. We found that there are, indeed, differences among the participants and the values they espouse with regard to the usage of the proposed IS. Based on the proposed value typology, we have provided a foundation for the value-based prioritization of requirements. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings have indicated that by applying the typology, we can differentiate user groups’ requirements based on different cultural settings or geographical locations. Our research has been a response to earlier calls for the investigation of how the value-based prioritization of requirements can be achieved (Thanasankit, 2002; Tuunanen et al, 2006; Kukreja et al, 2013), which implies that more focus should be placed on developing requirements prioritization techniques that encompass understanding of values of the potential and current users of these systems. This will enable us to better address their preferences on features in new IS. That said, we also see that value-based prioritization should be more integrated into the existing prioritization techniques that are used in the industry. Our typology provides an easily adaptable solution for this purpose, which can be used with, for example, the analytic hierarchy process technique (Saaty, 1990; Karlsson et al, 1998) or other techniques that distinguish the values of users such as the critical success chains method by Peffers et al (2003).
