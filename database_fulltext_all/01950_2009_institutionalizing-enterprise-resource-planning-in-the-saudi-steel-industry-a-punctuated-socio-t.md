## Abstract

In this article, we analyze institutionalization as a process of transferring and stabilizing material artifacts and routines in the form of enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems. Although past studies have analyzed institutionalization as structuring around scripts or discourse moves, we emphasize the material role of artifacts and routines as carriers of institutional logics. In addition, insitutionalization is not linear and incremental, but goes through sudden, nonlinear disruptions. To this end, we apply punctuated socio-technical information system change (PSIC) model that draws upon Gersick’s model of change to identify and trace moves that are critical during the institutionalization. The model accounts for ERP institutionalization by chronicling complex interactions between socio-technical elements in the implementation system, the work system, and organizational and environmental context which together account for the institutionalization outcome. We use the model to analyze a longitudinal case covering 11 years (1993–2004) of ERP implementation processes in a large Saudi steel firm. Our analysis shows that the proposed material and punctuated lens toward institutionalization offers rich insights how and why ERP systems become institutions and why their institutionalization is difficult and unfolds in unpredictable ways. We conclude that the normally held assumptions of successful linear and incremental adaptation to new institutional patterns logics out by ERP systems do not hold.

## Introduction

A major challenge in many today’s large organizations is adopting huge and standardized software packages such as customer relationship management (CRM) and enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems (Kumar and van Hillegersberg, 2000; Beard and Sumner, 2004). These systems consist of a large set of highly integrated software modules utilizing a common database and handling business functions such as finance, human resources, materials management, sales, and distribution (Pollock and Williams, 2008). To wit, multiple factors speak for adopting these systems including lower overall development cost, seamless integration of data and functions, and access to ‘best business practices’ (Davenport, 1998; Lozinsky, 1998; Markus and Tanis, 2000). Not surprisingly, during the last decade, ERP markets have grown at a compound annual rate of 13.5% hitting \$187 billion in 2006. Because of their pervasive scope and complexity, ERP systems are also claimed to have the most profound effect on modern organizations (Davenport, 1998; Rajagopal, 2002; Shang and Seddon, 2002).

ERP systems promise significant cost reductions, enhanced revenues, and improved management control (Brown and Vessey, 1999; Markus and Tanis, 2000; Fahy, 2001; Quattrone and Hopper, 2006). Also because of the presence of strong normative and mimetic pressures, a large body of organizations have joined the ERP bandwagon (DiMaggio and Powell, 1983; Taylor, 1998; Gosain, 2004). Yet, promised benefits of ERP are surprisingly rare and difficult to achieve (Davenport, 1998; Taylor, 1998; Bingi et al., 1999; Callaway, 1999; Al-Mashari, 2000; O’Leary, 2000; Berente et al., 2007). This is not surprising, as ERP systems institutionalize select managerial actions, beliefs, and rationalities in the form of institutional logics (Tolbert and Zucker, 1983; Zucker, 1991; Gosain, 2004; Berente et al., 2007, 2008; Pollock and Williams, 2008) that clash with local institutions and face major implementation and use problems (Davenport, 1998; Markus and Tanis, 2000; Nandhakumar et al., 2005; Ward et al., 2005; Berente et al., 2007). Often too late organiza tions learn that ERP introduces substantial rigidity and generates new levels of business complexity adding cost and increasing risks (Poston and Grabski, 2001). Others, more unlucky, have seen their highly reputed ERP projects to crash. ERP calamities reported by several world class companies like FoxMeyer, Mobil Europe, Dell, Dow Chemical, Siemens, Panasonic and Bruno Magli are numerous (Davenport, 1998; Robey et al., 2002; Berente et al., 2007). The failure rates have been as high as 60–70% (Griffith et al., 1999).

One fruitful way to understand ERP implementation challenges is the institutional lens (Gosain, 2004; Berente et al., 2007, 2008; Currie, 2009). While adopting this lens, we ask how ERP systems as ‘actualities come to take on a rule-like status in social thought and action’ (Meyer and Rowan, 1977: 350). According to such a lens, ERP systems need to be viewed as material carriers of ‘ossified’ institutionalized patterns of organizational action with associated meanings, norms, and rationalities. While being adopted and implemented, they become a nexus of institutionalized action and elements of ‘embedded’ institutions in the organization’s operating core. This also explains why the implementation of ERP systems is hard, as it involves overcoming inertia related to institutional change and new institution building (Zucker, 1991; Hargadon and Douglas, 2001).

This paper contributes to our understanding of ERP implementation as institutionalization. Institutionalization is defined here as the process by which institutions as organization-wide information systems are produced and reproduced. It is a ‘social process by which individuals come to accept a shared definition of social reality’ that enacts an institution (Scott, 1987: 496). Overall, institutionalization has been underexamined in the past research (Seo and Creed, 2002; Phillips et al., 2004; Currie, 2009) and as Phillips et al. (2004) note ‘as a result, institutional research has tended to focus on the effects rather than the process of institutionalization, which largely remains a ‘‘black box’’ (Zucker, 1991)’. We know of no institutionalization studies around ERP systems that have opened this black box and analyzed ERP implementation as institutionalization (for outcomes-related studies, see, e.g. Berente et al., 2007, 2008).

This paper contributes in three ways to understanding ERP institutionalization. By addressing these three issues, we also expand the theoretical richness and accuracy of accounts that explain the implementation of ERP or any similar relatively complex IT system. First, we argue that ERP institutionalization is nonlinear. Although the nonlinear nature of this phenomenon has been noted in passing in the past ERP studies (e.g. Fleck, 1994; Pozzebon and Pinsonneault, 2005), we argue that assuming a nonlinear view of change is critical in understanding how ERP systems as institutions emerge. This requires that we recognize the differences between events, which truly change the institutionalization trajectory and render the ERP system from a ‘potential’ into actuality. Second, we argue that the institutional lens leads us to analyze ERP implementation as a multilevel phenomenon. Simply, both broad organizational or environmental events and local events affect the implementation process and outcomes. Naturally, this is not news to institutional scholars, who have always emphasized the value of broad brush institutional analysis in binding together explanations around global institutional forces and local agency (Barley and Tolbert, 1997). Although some ERP studies (Nandhakumar et al., 2005) have observed the multilevel nature of the ERP implementation, most past studies have examined the process on a single level (e.g. Markus and Tanis, 2000; Ward et al., 2005).

Finally, we seek to delineate the engine behind the ERP institutionalization process. Here, we look specifically at the micro-level processes that seek to stabilize the ERP artifact. What has been published on this topic in institutional theory adopts a different route as it focuses either on dialectics between the general institutional forces and local agency (Barley and Tolbert, 1997; Seo and Creed, 2002), or on discourses as formative elements !in new institution building (Phillips et al., 2004). Although we do not underestimate the value of such approaches !in narrating institutionalization in broad institutional fields, we feel that such positions miss important characteristics of implementing ERP systems locally as institutions. For us, ERP systems act as material carriers of institutional logics (Berente et al., 2008) or in Jepperson’s terms: ‘socially constructed, routine-reproduced programs or rule systems’ (Jepperson, 1991: 149). We posit accordingly that material features and the nature of these artifacts should constitute a significant element in any account of ERP institutionalization. To this end, we turn our attention on theories of socio-technical change and punctuated change models called punctuated sociotechnical information system change (PSIC) model (Lyytinen and Newman, 2008). These theories, though not typical in institutional analysis, are valuable for our purposes, as they reveal the importance of technology artifacts in shaping local organizational action. In sociotechnical accounts, technology is seen as incomplete, unreliable and poorly configured, and thus affects how rule systems and behaviors carried in the ERP system are legitimized and internalized or ‘translated’ (Creed et al., 2002). This also overcomes limitations of some past studies on ERP implementation, which emphasize solely social influences (Nandhakumar et al., 2005; Ward et $a \dot { l } . ,$ 2005).

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. We first clarify the key concepts of our model: ERP systems as institutions, institutionalization as socio-technical change, and the nature of such change. Then we apply process analysis using the PSIC model to a longitudinal data set (1993–2004) depicting several ERP system implementations in a steel company in Saudi Arabia. The case analysis uses the theoretical vocabulary of PSIC to analyze the organization and impact of events in institutionalizing their ERP systems. The paper concludes with a discussion of limitations, a review of the contributions, and some suggestions for future work.

## A model of a punctuated socio-technical change

The need for institutional and process explanations for ERP Implementation

We define ERP institutionalization as an attempt to reconfigure existing socio-technical networks viewed as ‘routine reproduced programs or rule systems’ (Jepperson, 1991: 149). The managerial agenda underlying ERP introduction is to build an organization-wide system of legitimate behaviors as to execute, coordinate, manage, and sustain ‘effective’ organizational activities. In a sense, ERP system introduction can be viewed as an entrance of a new institutional form, which interacts with existing institutional arrangements. This generates a momentum to create structures and processes where general concepts and principles of the global ERP (Pollock and Williams, 2008) become translated as they are locally transmitted often producing divergent outcomes (Czarniawska and Sevon, 1996). Carriers at work are artifacts and routines (Scott, 2001), but their use mobilizes new symbols and relations. In the following, we are primarily interested in ERP systems as material carriers of instituitions.

The ‘transfer,’ in the case of ERP, happens through an intervention (project) by which the new socio-technical network is erected by transforming and readjusting existing behavioral scripts and beliefs. The success of the ERP implementation depends whether the final network, as ‘actuality’, can be built through a stream of events that will modify existing networks and related scripts and beliefs so that the new institution will eventually emerge. These events seek to guarantee that the final technological artifact and surrounding social behaviors are effectively connected and the related business practices and the local capabilities align (Fleck, 1994). Implementation success also requires that both the social and technical elements are institutionalized and thus effectively resist future fluctuations. Therefore, we propose a process view as the most appropriate in accounting for ERP institutionalization. In such view events, antecedent conditions, plans, actions, and outcomes are used to chronicle how a socio-technical network was established (Markus and Tanis, 2000). In a sense, the process analysis builds a story of episodes and events that refers to the past and the present and which explains why a certain ERP outcome emerged (Abbott, 1988; Kimberly and Bouchikhi, 1995; Langley, 1999; Pentland, 1999).<sup>1</sup>

In the following we build more comprehensive multilevel and contextual process model of ERP implementation as a form of institutionalization. In particular, we address mutual causality of multilevel events required in the institutional analysis (Seo and Creed, 2002). Second, we recognize the material nature of ERP artifacts and their potential for nonlinear upheavals during institutional change (Dacin et al., 2002). Finally, we cater for the path dependent and contextual nature of change as recognized by institutional theory (Barley and Tolbert, 1997).

## Multilevel nature of change

ERP introduction is inaugurated through a ‘chartering phase,’ which generates institutional mandates for a range of conceived changes (Markus and Tanis, 2000; Markus et al., 2000). This charter defines relatively strict boundaries in which the ERP implementation events are expected to unfold (Chae and Poole, 2005). Yet, the charter itself tends to change due to ambiguity, uncertainty, learning, and the dynamism of the environment. This makes it necessary to analyze ERP implementation charters as a way to legitimate and provide sense to the change and how they interact with the actual change (Markus and Tanis, 2000). The investigators need to thus probe ERP implementation as a multilevel change process that involves three parallel (hierarchically) organized event streams (Figure 1): (1) the plans, actions, and shifting charter associated with the ERP system (ERP implementation system (I)), (2) work system activities (business structure and process), and their change that is expected to reflect the plans and the charter (the work system (W)), and (3) the broader organizational and institutional context (the Environment (E)). Both the work system and ERP implementation system are characterized in the beginning by a set of antecedent institutional conditions such as their past history, norms, pertinent institutional logic, and capabilities.

Typically, the ERP implementation system (I) does not exist before the implementation charter is introduced. It is configured from the antecedent elements of the organization’s IT function and business functions by pooling new external capabilities like consultants and vendors (Fleck, 1994; Pozzebon and Pinsonneault, 2005; Pollock and Williams, 2008). These implementation systems typically demonstrate significant longevity due to the long time span required to implement and coordinate large ERP implementations (Markus and Tanis, 2000; Markus et $a l . ,$ 2000). Work system activities (W) refer to changes in the organizational routines that carry out the organization’s main task(s) and conform to the dominant institutional logic. These deal with the business functions, their activities, and related ‘best business practices’ (Davenport, 1998). Many times, these activities are taken for granted and demonstrate significant institutional inertia (Gersick, 1991; Lyytinen and Newman, 2008). These activities are expected to operate in their current form until the ERP implementation system overcomes the inertia and effects a change as defined in the implementation ‘charter’ (Markus and Tanis, 2000; Al-Mudimigh et $a l . ,$ 2001). Finally, Environment (E) refers the overall organizational environment and related events, including changes in organization’s competitive position, strategy, organizational structure, or leadership.

As noted, most process analyses in the past focused on project level, that is, the implementation system (I), but only occasionally recognized outside events. There is, however, a critical need to expand the process analysis to involve all levels as to cater for the increased complexity and dynamism of associated institutional change (Seo and Creed, 2002; Phillips et al., 2004; Berente et al., 2008). This is also in line with Pettigrew’s idea of contextualism (Pettigrew, 1987, 1990) and his three elements that shape an organization’s actual strategy: content, process, and context. In our case, the content refers to the ERP implementation charter. Process refers to the actions, reactions, and interactions between components within the ERP implementation system and the work system during the change. Finally, the context refers to the broader institutional environment that affects both the implementation content and the process. The focus on the interactions between these three levels leads to analyze ERP implementation as a continuous ‘dance’ among three streams of activities that constitute the ‘moves’ towards institutionalization or deinstitutionalization. This requires that we also detect vertical downward and upward interactions between the levels and horizontal interactions within levels.

## Socio-technical change as institutional change during the ERP implementation

If we view ERP systems as material carriers of institutional logics, a successful ERP implementation is expected to result in new socio-technical networks with new institutional characteristics. Therefore, we need to identify ERP implementation to involve socio-technical change (Markus and Tanis, 2000; Hong and Kim, 2002) as part of the institutional change (Gosain, 2004). In spite of this general observation, there are few systematic attempts to analyze ERP implementation as a sequence of socio-technical events. Most process analyses view ERP implementation solely as a social process of change and negotiation neglecting the role and influence of the material carrier. For example, the model of ERP drift by Nandhakumar et al. (2005) recognizes intentions, affordances, and social structures that shape ERP implementation. All these are fundamentally social and recognize technical changes only indirectly, for example, as a set of new ‘affordances’ that may influence both the social structure and the intentionality. Accordingly, technological failures (other than the lack of affordances altogether) cannot be accounted for with this model. In the similar vein, Ward et al. (2005)’s model of negotiations focuses only on the dynamics of negotiation, but largely ignore how actors relate to the technological artifacts, or their specific properties that influence the negotiation arena.

To overcome these limitations, we adopt Leavitt’s open socio-technical system model to describe the dimensions of a material ERP intervention (Leavitt, 1964; Lyytinen and Newman, 2008). The model conveys a relatively simple, but yet a rich vocabulary to describe ERP introduction as a stream of socio-technical events.<sup>2</sup> The model views ERP institutionalization to result in a relatively stable and persistent configuration of four interacting components: Structure, Technology, People, and Task (Figure 2). This configuration forms the institutionalized ‘composition’ of the business system with its specific logic whereby people sustain certain beliefs and values to conform to the socially constructed rules conveyed by structure and technology components to accomplish a legitimate task of the business. Institutional approach also suggests that organizations as social systems generate high barriers to change and promote stability by instituting socio-technical networks that convey specific institutional logics. These institutional logics mobilize cognitive bias (e.g. limited capability to think of alternatives), direct motivations (e.g. affecting people’s feelings of uncertainty and fear of failure), or preach false necessities (e.g. creating commitments to the status quo by making this seem natural) (Tushman and Romanelli, 1985).<sup>3</sup> This also involves structural choices such as maintaining rigid organizational forms, investing in specific technological competencies, or channeling boundary spanning. They can also include technology features such as choice of standard technologies and highly restrictive functions, and finally task-related choices like decreasing task variation.

But such stability in the end always can be threatened by unexpected changes in some of the components (e.g. radical reorganization due to new leadership) or due to external threats (e.g. disruptive technology). Changes in the network can be accordingly defined as events, which involve either one of these components and/or their connections. Thus, events affecting ERP institutionalization can be defined as changes involving Task (T), Technology (Te), Structure (S), or People (P). To account for the inherent dynamism between the different levels of ERP implementation, we will view both the implementation system (I) and the work system (W) using a symmetrical socio-technical model, and accordingly note whether the event involved the implementation system (I/S, I/P etc) or the work system (W/S, W/P etc). We also recognize their interactions as events that will affect’ upwards’ or ‘downwards’ the states across the levels. For example, the roll out of an ERP artifact by the implementation system (I/T) changes the technology and the structure of the work system configuration (W/Te, W/S). In the following, we discuss the four elements in the context of ERP implementation system – the analysis for the work system is similar and not carried out here due to space limitations.

The implementation task describes the planned composition of the work system as a socio-technical system: which artifacts and rules and in what way they will be embedded in the work systems as dictated in an ERP project’s charter. (Markus and Tanis, 2000; Markus et al., 2000; Al-Mudimigh et al., 2001). An ERP implementation task typically consists of heterogeneous activities including among others redesign of routines (W/S), managing and legitimizing change in the structures (W/S), configuring the software system (W/Te), and training and hiring people (W/P) (Larsen and Myers, 1999). It may also involve new goals for the business process (W/T) (Markus and Tanis, 2000). This task will change in its form and scope over time due to learning and dynamism involved. Thus, it neither implies a static set of implementer roles (I/S), nor assumes fixed procedures with predetermined goals or outcomes (I/S). This may also imply new types of learning at the implementation system (I/P). This is partly due to the ambiguity and complexity of the task, and partly due to unexpected changes in the environment. Thus, dynamic implementation tasks (I/T) induce a dynamic set of implementation activities (I/S), reconfiguring new technologies (I/Te) and hiring and training new people (I/P).

People in ERP implementation system represent, in general, an organisation’s key members and other stakeholders such as consultants, vendors etc (Lyytinen et al., 1998). Therefore, the people component (I/P) includes individuals in multiple roles (I/S) such as users, developers, analysts, designers, consultants, project managers, and representatives of broader groups such as module implementation teams, or project steering committees. The people behaviors in an ERP implementation have been shown to play a critical role in shaping implementation outcomes (Boudreau and Robey, 1999; Langenwalter, 2000).

Leavitt (1964) denotes technology as ‘tools – problem solving inventions like work-measurement, computers, and drill processes’. Technology in ERP implementations refers mostly to tools and resources that underpin the technical platforms including configuration tools, business process modeling tools, and the like (Markus and Tanis, 2000). We use technology (I/Te) also here in its broader meaning to include not only the application software and system software and hardware, but also other acquired equipment such as telecommunication infrastructures, documentations’ guidelines and reports, software configuration tools, and business processes analysis techniques and/or tools.

Leavitt (1964) defines structure as systems of communication, systems of authority, and systems of work flow. Structure includes an institutional dimension such as values and norms and role expectations; and behavioral dimension such as management of control and procedures for implementation (I/S) (Lyytinen et al., 1998; Lyytinen and Newman, 2008). In an ERP implementation, project structure includes its work organization, decision making and workflow, management style and implementation methodologies, communication process, and project leadership. Normally, an ERP implementation project follows a complex work flow as it interacts with multiple external organizational activities and constituencies. Therefore, multiple structures can be observed during the ERP implementation process that cater for the project’s relationships with the rest of the organization and its environment (Pettigrew, 1987, 1990; Walsham and Waema, 1994).

The nonlinear, contextual, and path-dependent nature of change The socio-technical model admits a strong mutual interdependency between the four elements (Leavitt, 1964;

Lyytinen et al., 1998; Lyytinen and Newman, 2008).This assumes in mutual causality and circularity in explaining institutional change (see e.g. Barley and Tolbert, 1997; Seo and Creed, 2002). As a result, institutionalization exhibits both positive and negative feedback loops that result in nonlinear changes in the system’s composition and behavior (Weick, 1998). This complexity and circularity forms the key for the analysis of ERP institutionalization.

Ideally, ERP implementers are organized into specific relationships and interaction patterns conditioned by technological capabilities and prevailing organizational norms. This I-system configuration will deliver a sociotechnical change by institutionalizing a chartered change in the work system. In real situations, however, the four components will not behave and interact as planned due to the inherent complexity and circularity of interactions within and between them and the environment. This affects the chartered change to the extent that the ERP implementations ‘drift’ (Markus et al., 2000; Nandhakumar et al., 2005; Berente et al., 2008). In this situation, the implementer’s role is to initiate, monitor, and balance such interactions in order to create some level of institutionalized change.

Figure 3 highlights how a socio-technical system at the start exhibits some institution like stability due to the deep engrained institutionalized logic. In socio-technical terms, this results in low component variation and strong interdependencies (tight coupling). Therefore, a significant change in any of the components (like T) will propagate through the relationships and create systemic gaps (i.e., institutional breakdowns, shifts or imbalances), changing the system composition and its behavior. We call such an event a critical event. Thus, if one component in the system becomes incompatible with others due to a critical event that results from either intended (created by the ERP implementation) or non-intended change (environmental event), we can detect a mismatch between the components creating on event that affects the level of institutionalization (Lyytinen and Newman, 2008).

Singular events are critical and realizable to the extent that they break down the equilibrium (e.g. a technology failure or a strategy reorientation). A realizable event denotes an event that must be recognized by the implementers as affecting the equilibrium, and which calls for reacting to such an event. An example of a realizable critical event would be to radically change the project organization that does not align with the new development tools. On other occasions, the system may gradually drift so that a seemingly innocent event can create a ‘tipping point’ that pushes the system into disequilibrium. For example, a small change in the system’s output volume can break down the technology, or a small addition to speed can change the nature of the task.

Should a critical event occur, the capability of the system to accomplish its task is highly reduced or threatened. When the system experiences one or more such gaps, we call it unbalanced; otherwise the system will have a balanced status. In many cases, processes sustain an unbalanced status for long periods – coined as drift by Ciborra et al. (2000). Whatever conditions the system originally entertains, the change during the ERP implementation will take place due to variations in the sociotechnical components of the work system and implementation system as triggered by the internal changes or external events. These variations can break loose in highly circular and unexpected ways that can result in the demise of the whole initiative (deinstitutionalization). As a result, ERP implementers have a tendency to organize implementation processes in ways that seek to maintain the status quo, that is, that aligns with the existing ‘logic’ unless an external change increases gaps. The proposed dynamic view also leads us identify and assess changes in socio-technical components and their interdependencies, and then to investigate whether these changes lead the work system to restore a balance, and thus institutionalize the chartered ERP change, whether they sustain an existing gap, or whether they push the system more out of balance due to a failure to actualize the system and related behaviors.

Finally, ERP changes happen in a context and have a history (Pettigrew, 1987; Walsham, 1993). Context emphasized the penetrating role of institutionalization forces in shaping local action. We will divide the context into two levels – organizational context (the inner level) and environmental context (the outer level) – where organizational context (Pettigrew (1987, 1990) is defined in terms of organizational structure, corporate culture, and political climate, whereas environmental context covers social, economic, political, and competitive environments. By making such a distinction and by considering variations in these two contexts, we can chronicle a contextual analysis of ERP implementation. The major difficulty in this analysis is its multilevel nature, which requires us to systematically investigate the interplay between process content, context, and outcomes. A contextual focus invites investigation of ERP implementation in two different modes: vertical and horizontal. The vertical analysis unpacks interdependencies between the context and implementation process. The horizontal analysis deals with interactions at one system level at a time.

Overall, the idea of a history emphasizes the role of path dependency in explaining institutionalization as an ongoing process (Zucker, 1991; Seo and Creed, 2002). Any ERP implementation outcome is thus only understandable by referencing to a string of antecedent events which impact current choices (Newman and Robey, 1992; Lyytinen and Newman, 2008). Therefore, small and innocent can have a deep impact on the process and outcomes (Arthur, 1989).Each system carries its history in multiple ways: (1) technology choices are historically constrained through legacy systems and infrastructures; (2) people’s capabilities, skills, beliefs, and values that are historically formed; (3) routines and tasks are historically shaped by interactions with the environments; and (4) socio-technical change is shaped by past learning by doing, using or trying (Fleck, 1994). These histories affect and constrain choices for each system level and shape how systems react to disequilibria.

## The PSIC

In line with the above discussion, we next outline the PSIC model as a means to articulate micro-behaviors related to institutionalization. We chose this model as it integrates four lenses needed to account ERP implementation as institutionalization: (1) socio-technical theory describing the content of the ERP implementation/institutionalization moves organized into a hierarchy multilevel changes (Leavitt, 1964; Lyytinen and Newman, 2008); (2) the punctuated equilibrium model describing the nonlinear nature of related institutional change (Tushman and Romanelli, 1985; Gersick, 1991; Newman and Robey, 1992; Robey and Newman, 1996); (3) the impact of the broader institutional context in shaping ERP implementation (Pettigrew, 1987, 1990); and (4) process theories as a general logic to account for institutionalization as a process (Mohr, 1982; Van de Ven and Huber, 1990; Langley, 1999; Pentland, 1999; Monge 1990).

The PSIC allows us to analyze ERP implementation as a series of socio-technical change episodes that in the end lead ERP systems to emerge as actualities, and it recognizes that organizations,as institutions, are occasionally punctuated by critical events (Gersick, 1991). Figure 4 shows how a PSIC model can be formulated to depict such punctuated change. Here, a socio-technical system as an institution is characterized by a period of stability (Eq) with only minor variations in socio-technical components and their structure. This can be challenged by one or several critical events that push the system out of equilibrium by producing gaps (DisEq). Such events can result (e.g. from betting on a poorly conceived direction of change) in trying to implement a system where users’ needs are not taken into consideration.

## Research method and case description

## Research design and data collection

Next, we chronicle a turbulent, decade-long journey of ERP selection, design, and implementation processes by applying the institutional lens. This process took place in a large steel manufacturer in Saudi Arabia. In Markus and Tanis (2000), the terminology we cover is the analysis of an ‘IT conversion’ process from ‘ideas to impacts’; in institutional terms, we review the translation of ‘global’ institutional logic into a local system of institutionalized rules and beliefs. The research site was the Saudi Iron and Steel Company (named HADEED), which is fully owned subsidiary of the Saudi Basic Industrial Corporation (SABIC), the largest non-oil company in the Middle East. It is the first fully integrated steel producer of rebars and wire rods in the Middle East and was established in 1983 at Al-Jubail Industrial City, the eastern province of Saudi Arabia’s Arabian Gulf coast. Today, the company produces 2.7 million tons of steel a year.

The company’s ERP implementation was selected for the case study for several reasons (Yin, 2003). First, it was a highly complex initiative, which involved nearly all organizational units and used an untried ERP technology. This made them an ideal subject for a longitudinal case study and a place to study institutionalization. Second, HADEED was one of the first companies in Saudi Arabia and in the whole Middle East to acquire an ERP solution – in this sense, the ERP represented a truly challenging ‘material’ carrier of institutional change. Third, the system was complex and large and involved several separate implementations denoted ERP-1 and ERP-2 of the system in different factories and with different modules. Therefore, its development lasted over a long period. This enabled us to trace patterns of change over extended periods. The company completed its first ERP project in 2000 after struggling over 7 years with its implementation, but our data covers period from 1992 to 2004. During this period, the company suffered initially huge drawbacks and failed to implement the system. But, later it turned the initial failure into a success by learning from the experience and changing the ways in which the ERP systems were implemented and thereby institutionalizing the system.

The data were collected as part of a longitudinal case study (Pettigrew, 1992). The connections of one of the authors had with the company’s personnel gave us an easy access to case data. One of the authors visited the study site four times in 2002, twice in 2003, and twice in 2004. Data were collected mainly in face-to-face semistructured interviews with key members of the ERP implementation team. Eleven out of twenty employees involved in the project and who were still working at the company in 2004 were interviewed more than once (see Table A1).

Data collection followed a realist ‘ethnographic’ approach, that is, to get accounts of people involved in the design and implementation of the system concerning what had happened and why. To this end, we obtained 63 interviews, which focused on the system’s selection, design, and implementation. The interviews were formulated as a series of narratives about project events and its outcomes using mirroring and other interview techniques (Myers and Newman, 2007). We asked similar questions to the same employees who were interviewed more than once to increase reliability. Likewise, similar questions were asked of several interviewees as to enable data triangulation and reveal contradictions and agreements in the reporting of events. During interviews, additional notes were made that were added to data corpus. Also on-site observations and informal talks supplemented data collection as one of the authors joined the interviewees during their lunchtimes and engaged in informal discussions about the project. Data were collected in the early stages through open and general questions concerning the project history. As the study progressed, the questions became more specific in relation to ERP implementation events, their causes and outcomes, and they focused on explaining those outcomes. Most of the interviewees expressed informally their comfort with such a method, which allowed them to speak openly and honestly.

The data collection approach has some limitations. First, the interviews were primarily conducted using Arabic, and because of the high cost of translating the interviews, we had to rely on one of the author to do this task. Secondly, the collected data covered both historical and current events, but focused mainly on historical events (Kimberly and Bouchikhi, 1995). One disadvantage of asking past topics, though, is that the informants may have problems in recalling some actions that might be of importance to the story, or they can selective in their responses (Glick et al., 1990). In order to militate against such limitations, the third author translated some interviews from Arabic to English. The author then analyzed the Arabic transcript, and after a month, the second author analyzed the English one. There were no significant differences between the two analyses. Moreover, and to minimize the impact of the present on past data, we collected historical reports and compared them with the interview data. Additionally, we tried to meet some employees who participated in the implementation but no longer worked at the company and as such were not exposed to the internal company pressure.

## Data analysis

All these interviews were transcribed and integrated into a research database. Data analysis with such large longitudinal qualitative data sets is arduous (Sabherwal and Newman, 2003; Yin, 2003). There is no single set of steps to conduct analysis rigorously (Robson, 2002). In our case, we used then the PSIC model as a sensitizing device to identify critical events in the environment and assessed their impact – especially their nonlinear effects – and how they influenced the final outcome. We followed both bottom-up and top-down analyses. The initial analysis was descriptive (Klein and Myers, 1999); in that no rigorous a priori constructs were used. We outlined the main narrative of the implementation process, and the analysis was informed by the evolving conceptual framework outlined above. The main goal was to develop plausible accounts why the observed outcomes were what they were, by using the vocabulary of events, gaps, and system trajectories.

During the early steps, we followed Strauss and Corbin’s (1998) ‘microscopic’ technique. The approach follows a detailed paragraph-by-paragraph analysis. Our focus was to identify and construct a sequence of events during the project. We scanned each paragraph of the interview transcripts looking for potentially significant problems and issues that related to implementation events. When a paragraph or section was singled out, the line-by-line analysis was used for more detailed investigation. We abstracted and classified each identified issue into one of the model categories. For example, during an interview with the company’s IT manager, one paragraph of the transcript was as follows:

The start or the planning of the implementation y we had to start by selecting team members to work full time in the project. They have to document all their existing process from A to Z in a process called by the PwH consultant ‘Current business process or ‘‘ASIS’’ ’y (IT Manager, 17th June 2002, p.2)

In this paragraph, we observe an event that relates to ‘I’ (implementation) level of people activities, and how they relate to a task of the project. In analyzing the sociotechnical content generally, we thereby categorized project events to involve one or several of the four socio-technical components. After that, we investigated interrelationships between such four components and judged them as either balanced (no gap) or punctuated change activities that rendered the project in an unbalanced state (Klein and Myers, 1999; Lyytinen and Newman, 2008). The sociotechnical change was detected by investigating variations in data and which could be assigned to any one of the four components. The interactions between the components would be identified when such an event was deemed critical. We applied Keil and Flatto’s (1999) principles to justify our interpretation of such events – whether they were planned or not – and then traced the cause of such events and analyzed their consequences. Events were sorted into sequential patterns based on their chronological order for three levels. By doing so we constructed a narrative consisting of chains of events that connected each event to its antecedents and consequences in a process trajectory at each system level (Newman and Robey, 1992; Newman and Sabherwal, 1996; Pentland, 1999). As a result, we could decide whether such an event was the result of either a change in the organizational context (e.g. top management pressure) and/or the environmental contexts (e.g. outside competition), or whether such contexts did not have any effect and the change was generated from within the implementation system or the work system.

The final step in the analysis was to construct the full narrative of events and their interactions in a visual form (Langley, 1999; Pentland, 1999). This form would chronicle the project from its birth to its end as a sequence of events that affected four socio-technical components at the implementation system level or the work system level, and thus created variations in them and their interactions. The chronicled process was then interpreted using the punctuated equilibrium model and ideas of institutional change as to understand how the project outcomes resulted from moves at different levels and resulted in the institutional outcomes.

## Interactions between the context and process

Overall, The ERP implementation followed a punctuated pattern. The project was initiated because the company’s current system (PRIME) lacked technical support within the IS department. Other contextual factors also pushed for the replacement: the Y2K problem (mimetic pressure), increased competition, and the normative and coercive pressures in Saudi industries to ‘modernize’. This decision was made by the IT-manager (DisEq1). He submitted a memo to the company’s top management and requested for mandates to build an alternative system. After management approval, the project was initiated (Eq 2):

The PRIME was so critical for HADEED. Basically if the system [went] down the company would not be able to sell any of its product and there would not be any income because all sales must go through the system. At the same time, and despite the high cost of technical support contract, we did not get any valuable technical support for the system from the Saudi or the international providers of the system. In fact, many of the providers either have gone out or business of did not provide such services any more. We just were not able to tolerate such environments so I personally report all that to the top management who agreed to change the system as soon as possible’ (IT manager, 2003, p. 250)

The ERP implementation project began by appointing a consultant from a system integrator as a project manager and selecting other project members. As presented in Figure 6, the project crafted its own charter (row 5) as it also sought to legitimize the change in the company’s activities (row 6). To guarantee continuity, the company signed a contract with team members to not to leave the company unless the project was completed (Eq14 – Eq15). The company decided also to pay a bonus to the team members (30% of their basic salary) when the project was completed. The project then proceeded and as long as the project performed its activities within its expected path (row 5), the company’s organizational context (row 2) acted as a shelter. Thus, the project was not affected by all potential events emerging in the environment (row 1). This is exemplified in Figure 6 by empty slots on row 2.

As presented in Figure 6, the implementation project interacted with the environmental context when the project started to digress from the expected project path (row 5) as shown in the company’s activity path (row 6) (events Eq 8, 12–13, 15, and 17). These situations emerged mostly during the project testing and when the system went ‘live’. In Eq 8, for example, the system was regarded as a failure after having poor service levels that resulted in poor financial results. One manager expressed this as follows

SD [Sales module] was a nightmare. HADEED started dispatching wrong products to wrong customers with wrong volumes. Our money was transferred to wrong banks. Our customers started complaining and we got in conflict with them and with the banks’ (Production Planning & Quality Assurance Section Manager, 2003, p. 51)

This event affected the environmental context and, forced the company’s top management to get involved and put the whole project on hold. There was one exception to such pattern: the implementation of the General Ledger module that was implemented during Eq 8. This module had no significant interactions with the environmental context for two reasons: (1) it had a high similarity (and institutional ‘congruence’) with the General Ledger module in the earlier system; (2) it served only as an internal accounting system and its scope was limited thus demonstrating institutional isomorphism with the earlier situation. As one interviewee noted,

General Ledger implementation was not a big deal y . The General Ledger in any system that use the same basic accounting principles will show debit and credit account balances whether that system was ERP or PRIME (the legacy system). Therefore, the implementation of General Ledger was really a real test taking in consideration also the General Ledger was an internal financial document’ (Accounting Manager, 2002, p. 20)

## Overall structure of the process

The ERP project in HADEED enjoyed some stable periods. For example, the project had a smooth launch (Eq 2). It involved a relatively well-defined task to select the best technology Te with the help of a consultant. This resulted in choosing SAP R/3 as a replacement for the current system. Similar patterns can be identified in stages Eq 9–10 Eq 13–16. This analysis also shows that a project can operate also well with some gaps (i.e. without being totally balanced). For example, in Eq 5, the project manager did not define a well-defined task for the implementation team. This created a gap between the project structure, people, and task, but the project continued to operate. In Eq11, the task of recustomizing the modules to integrate the whole ERP system was not welcomed by the team. Likewise, although the company’s showed a growing number of gaps in its work system before the project in Eq1, during the implementation it remained operational. The same can be found in Eq 2–8, 11, and 12. Thus, ‘disequilibrium’ should not be equated with a non-operational, or totally failing system. Some variations in the components, in fact as a process of institutionalization, are needed to push the process forward, and thus, gaps are necessary in a large project:

Since the start of HADEED until the early 1990s, we used to have different environments in different departments. Everybody used to work alone with a lot of repetitions and a lot of paper work wasting employees’ time, cost, and effort. In the IT department, for example, we were bombarded almost on a daily basis with requests to build in-house software that could have been built before for some department but because there was no work coordination, every request was dealt with as a separate work-order in ongoing workshops (IT Manager, 2002, p. 2)

Sometimes these stable periods lasted for relatively long periods, which demonstrates what institutional theory calls loose coupling (Berente et al., 2008). A plethora of persistent gaps, however, can be seen as a strong indicator of major problems thus demonstrating a growing deinstitutionalization calling for interventions (as occurred in late 1995–1996).

Not surprisingly, the implementation did not always proceed according to the charter. In DisEq 7, for example, the implementation system failed, which caused the implementation trajectory to shift significantly. In DisEq 11, we note another example when hired expatriate ERP specialists left the company without serving notice:

During the 1990s ERP skills were in demand in the local and international market. We lost a lot of expert employees in the system unexpectedly. They posted their resumes as ERP experts after the amount of free of charge training they obtained from HADEED during the implementation project. The problems were serious and unfortunately the company’s top management did not do anything to stop such turnover (IT manager, 2002, pp. 12).

## Vertical interactions

As predicted by institutional analysis, we could observe both horizontal and vertical interactions. For analyzing outcomes of institutionalization moves, we distinguish between three outcomes related to interventions: increasingly stable configuration, a failure, and a deepening crisis. As illustrated in Figure 7, the intervention can be interpreted as a success when it eliminates some or all of the gaps. A deepening crisis is indicated by an increasing number of project gaps. Failure indicates that there is no change in the system state. These three outcomes were all identified in both in the work and the implementation system at specific points in time.

As shown in Figure 6, the process could be interpreted as moving towards an implementation ‘success’ during stages Eq 2, 6, 9–10, and 12–17. In these moments, the project entertained fewer gaps in comparison to the previous condition. The introduction of ERP was interpreted as a failure in Eq 8 when the project failed in its planned interventions. Finally, the process was interpreted as deepening in crisis when the implementation system deteriorated during events Eq 3–5, 7, and 11. In brief, the project had a successful start that ended with selecting the ERP system. The implementation team then adapted the ERP project consultant’s plan, which had to be modified later multiple times because of either top management pressure (Eq 5) or the team’s inability to follow the plan (Eq 8). Several external and ‘vertical’ issues intervened showing institutional or environmental pressures: appointing a new manager (DisEq 8), or the passive and negative influence of top management, or poor financial results (Eq 12). Also unexpected issues affected the process like people leaving the project (Eq 11). Some issues were also of internal making like selecting unqualified team representatives (Eq 3). Our analysis shows the importance of investigating a complex ERP implementation through consecutive episodes not artificially separating the process from its contextual and historical settings.

## Path dependency

The idea of path dependency is illustrated in Figure 6 by an arrow in the opposite direction to the flow of time. The case shows multiple examples of such path dependency. In ERP-2 (events Eq14–15), project management did not want to repeat their earlier mistakes of customizing the ERP package, or failing to manage the turnover of project members. To accomplish this goal, the project adapted practices from the earlier project. For example, the company promised all project team members a bonus for completing the project, which helped keep the implementation system in balance and militate against risks of not accomplishing the interventions:

HADEED-2 is a factory that produces to sell right away because the factory produces what HADEED customers asked for in specific and with their own and different requirements. We had to build a system that is capable of accommodating any possible orders in that matter. ERP project was not an easy project to do at all. However, we knew before the start of ERP-HADEED-2 of all the problems and obstacles that HADEED’s long product [had] ERP had gone through. We did not assume the project would not have any problems which [it] almost did not but we tried to avoid any potential problems based on what happened before (Senior Application Analyst (SD), 2003, pp. 130)

Another example of the importance of historical sequences was the amount of knowledge that the company’s teams were able to garner during the first implementation (Eq 1–12). Such skills had a significant impact in implementing ERP-2 in less time and with less cost, and without many of the earlier problems:

We know before the start of ERP-HADEED 2 of all the problems and obstacles that HADEED’s first implementation y had gone through. We did not assume the project would not have any problems, which [it] almost did not, but we tried to avoid any potential problems based on what happened before (Senior Application Specialist in ERP, 2003, p. 125).

## Escalation of commitment

Many gaps were truly serious and perturbed the trajectory questioning the likelihood of institutionalization and local translation. Surprisingly often, they were not heeded by project managers or they were too big or hard to address in the planned time. For example, in Eq 4, users were not trained to use the system, which resulted later on in massive changes and customization of software. But, this was not recognized by the project managers. In Eq 8 (the sales data conversion), a large number of users as well as external contractors converted the sales data without supervision and without any coordination within the conversion team. This resulted in a failure in later stages. As a result, the project was many times delayed and demanded significantly more resources than expected, creating what many scholars (Keil, 1995) call escalating commitment. (see Eq 2–8). Due to incentives and reporting structures, the escalation was hidden from top management. During these gap-creating events, the project management did not foresee these threats, which led the project to eventually fail during its introduction. We see escalating commitment as a common ‘evil’ of ERP implementations, because of their scale and complexity, and the resistance of the prevailing institutions to accommodate changes carried by these artifacts (Berente et al., 2007). This commitment is strengthened by the visibility and significant institutional mandates associated with the implementation of these systems.

## Multiple institutionalization cycles

For a complex and lengthy project, as the one studied here, it is necessary to garner new project management skills and perform tasks that influence institutionalization (row 3). From this viewpoint, managing a large ERP implementation is not a calculative monitoring, but is a dynamic adaptation and legitimation process. Yet, the traditional view of ERP implementation is to portray it as a sequence of stages in a teleological problem solving stages organized around notions of rational choice (Van de Ven and Poole, 1995; Markus and Tanis, 2000). The actualization of the ERP system that we observed did not follow, however, such process pattern, it never does. Rather, we need to interpret the process through the lens of trial and error learning, trying to generate new forms of institutions and institutional adaptation (Fleck, 1994). In light of this interpretation, managers have to learn from new forms of instability observed during ERP implementation, for example, why the ERP system continued to fail and on several occasions had to be reconfigured. Such persistent efforts of ‘trying’ signaled and fostered the legitimacy that was needed to execute the change. On the basis of this, we can distinguish three major learning institutionalization cycles that institutionalized different forms of how to adapt, implement, and justify information systems as institutions covering a period of 22 years (1982–2004).

The learning cycle originated during the company’s inauguration and ended at the start of the first ERP project when the first ERP systems was tested and subsequently failed (i.e. Eq 1 through Eq 8).

After what happened in the SD module, HADEED’s top management got involved directly and said enough is enough. The project had another direction since (IT Manager, 2001, p. 3).

The second cycle emerged after the top management restarted the project with a new plan (Eq 9–12). This lifecycle was more successful and resulted in a successful implementation of some modules:

We use to be clerks in the department of accounting, now we are accounting. We, instead of doing a lot of paper work that does not add any value, have more time to spend in work and business analysis. We are able to issue our financial statement more accurately by the fifth day of the next month compared to the day 15 or 20 using the old system (Senior Cost Analyst, 2002, p. 18).

By the end of 1999, the company completed its third cycle (Eq 14 and 15) by building ERP-2. A number of experienced specialists were now deployed to implement the second ERP system where no major obstacles in the process could be detected. The project followed a big-bang approach and implemented the whole suite of SAP/R3 modules. This was eased by the fact that the implementation took place in a new factory without institutional ‘legacy’. After using the system for some time, the company started train its employees extensively in the use of the system to overcome the shortage of expertise. After that we note the effort to migrate the system to operate on a NT platform and SQL server (Eq 16 and 17), but this does not change the institutional alignment anymore. Overall, these learning cycles are not separate processes, but illustrate the continuous process of adjustment and institutionalization of how to configure information processing functions within the company.

## Conclusion

The primary contribution of this paper is to build a process account at the micro-level how ERP systems as material carriers of institutional logics are locally adopted and institutionalized. To do so, we apply the PSIC model to narrate ERP institutionalization as multilevel, punctuated and path-dependent change that involves change in behaviors and their legitimation. We apply the model as a sense-making device to ‘read’ sets of change activities that influence the fate of institutionalizing a complex ERP system. The model is distinct from other institutional process models (Barley and Tolbert, 1997; Phillips et al., 2004), as it focuses on material artifacts as carriers of institutional logics and recognized nonlinear nature of institutional change. It thereby draws upon four streams of research: socio-technical theory (Leavitt, 1964; Lyytinen and Newman, 2008), a punctuated equilibrium model (Gersick, 1991), process theory (Mohr, 1982; Van de Ven and Huber, 1990), and institutional contextualism (Pettigrew, 1987, 1990). The model investigates ERP implementation in terms of interactions between four socio-technical components and their variations, and how these act both as deterrents to and drivers for ERP implementation (Lyytinen and Newman, 2008). We note that these interactions can result in nonlinear changes in the implementation and they carry a history. These interactions analyzed at two levels: the implementation system and the work system. Contextualism and institutional context are integrated in the form of the vertical process analysis by recognizing external events that shape the process and its institutional context. Separating the horizontal from vertical change dimensions in this way offers necessary theoretical richness in explaining implementation as an institutionalization.

We demonstrate the value of this approach by analyzing an extensive and long series of ERP implementations within a steel company in Saudi Arabia. We show the strength of the proposed approach through its ability to analyze multi-level activities that are integral in institutionalizing complex ERP systems within their organizational contexts. This view augments generic process models of ERP implementation (e.g. Markus and Tanis, 2000) and other proposed process approaches to analyze the social dynamics of ERP implementation (Nandhakumar et al., 2005; Ward et al., 2005). Overall, the studied implementation trajectory followed the predicted pattern of complex set of consecutive equilibria and disequilibria. We also note that misalignments, that is, gaps form a necessary and positive factor in institutionalizing ERP. Such mismatches trigger adaptive behaviors, such as changing the implementation approach, or configuring a new type of ERP necessary to carry out the translation. Our analysis also shows that gap creation and elimination was not only the outcome of planned project activities or conscious local adaptation to the institutional forces. In fact, most gaps emerged through unanticipated events, but which yet significantly affected institutionalization. Such changes in the institutionalization status happened frequently and often with unknown consequences. Specific sequences of events also created path dependencies and their impact was significant in redirecting implementation processes. Because of this, the project experienced escalating commitment as unexpected gaps constantly emerged. Many of these were also hidden from managers, who thus failed to observe and respond to the actual problematic status of the process.

This study adds to the growing body of literature on institutionalization (Barley and Tolbert, 1997; Dacin et al., 2002; Phillips et al., 2004), and institutionalization of (information) technologies, in particular (Hargadon and Douglas, 2001; Gosain, 2004). Our study examines institutionalization in the context of specific technological artifacts (ERP systems), which we view as material carriers of institutional logics (Gosain, 2004). Our main interest is to narrate how does the institutional embedding of such artifact into the social system take place. This question calls for narrating micro-level processes, which translate the global logic of the ERP system (Berente et al., 2008) into a local context and augments earlier studies of the institutionalization of technologies, which have looked at the content of the institutional logics embedded in technologies (Gosain, 2004), differences in the impacts of colliding institutional logics (Berente et al., 2007), the specific nature of this embedding though loose couplings (Berente et al., 2008), or how beliefs and norms related to technology change at the macro level (Hargadon and Douglas, 2001). In short, our use of the PSIC model helps reveal the process narrative and illustrate how some adaptive processes generate stable, institutionalized sociotechnical networks conceived as’ socially constructed, routine-reproduced programs or rule systems’ (Jepperson, 1991: 149). Such processes operate, as argued in the paper, through socio-technical interventions. In line with institutional theory, the model observes the multilevel and path-dependent nature of these interventions and their outcomes. But, unlike several analyses of institutional change, it also observes the nonlinear, transformative, or ‘revolutionary’ nature of some of them (Greenwood and Hinings, 1996; Seo and Creed, 2002). This suggests that ERP systems need to penetrate through tightly coupled institutional fields, and will follow Greenwood and Hinings’ prediction that ‘radical change in tightly coupled institutional fields will be unusual, but when it occurs, it will be revolutionary’ (1996: 1030). We feel that such an analysis can be extended to many forms of institutionalization that involve or depend on new and radical technologies as carriers of institutional logics. The downside of our approach is high complexity both in data analysis and in theoretical triangulation. The benefit is deeper understanding of the micro-level processes that constitute institutions in modern organizations. There is also some ‘relativity’ and ambiguity in determining the nonlinear changes of some processes and attributing causality into specific events. But, our experience tells that such challenges are no different than any interpretation challenge associated with complex, longitudinal data sets.

In practical terms, this research provides valuable insights to project manager’s and executives’ understanding of what challenges they will face with large ERP implementations. To understand how ‘actualization’ will happen, the implementation system and the work system need to be first distinguished and contextualized. Project management must further focus on how their interventions interact with the context and process over time. Rather than asking whether we are finishing the project within budget and/or within time the key question to ask is ‘did the implementation actualize the planned socio-technical change and eliminate gaps?’ This view also suggests that it is unconceivable for any large and complex IT project, such as implementing an ERP to proceed without experiencing major gaps. Hence, one needs to understand the political and institutional arena in which the change will proceed. Indeed, the managers need to occasionally fashion gaps as to motivate the change and mobilize bias, should the project become mired in conflict or delays. Because of deep institutional change associated with ERP implementations, such projects are also highly risky. Moreover, technological change is but one small part of the effort and will cause other parts of the business system to change radically through mutual adaptation. Also, because of unanticipated events and environmental changes, ERP implementation outcomes cannot be predicted using linear models. Simply, a two-dimensional map is not a good depiction of a rugged three-dimensional terrain. In this regard, the suggested model offers a new mindset to monitor change activities through reviewing socio-technical components, revealing gaps, and asking how to deal with them. The analysis of this case and others shows the near impossibility of predicting success or failure of large projects as institution building. In fact, the evidence at hand suggests that the best way to success is to treat such projects as sociotechnical experiments to change institutional orders.

For the future, we are conducting research in Finland, Norway, China, and in the United Kingdom where we are using and refining the model to analyze other ERP change cases as to establish a better understanding of ERP implementation trajectories. These analyses should help formulate ‘middle range’ process explanations as to why specific sequences lead to specific institutional outcomes, or why specific interventions lead either to improved adaptation or further crises. We should also like to investigate in more detail the role of path dependency and especially the balance between institutionally defined radical change and stability and between institutionalized (permanent) and intermittent changes (temporary). In particular, we would like to explore the role of negative project signals and how they are attended to by project managers and other stakeholders.

## Appendix B

A sample of detailed analysis for the events in the process diagram

The ultimate goal for HADEED employees was to accomplish their daily activities and get the work done using a variety of disjointed technologies, countless paper, and manual work processes. Every employee’s outcome had to be printed out in hard copies and sent to another employee who uses such output as input and feeds the system again. Therefore, there were growing gaps in between the four socio-technical components that leave HADEED in an unbalanced state.

HADEED was in a stable (Eq1) state of performing normal activities until the IT manager proposed the change (DisEq1). The need was generated by external forces, such as the Y2K problem, and outside competition that HADEED was facing in both the Saudi and international markets. However, the need for change was mainly encouraged by a general lack of support for the PRIME system. The system representative in Saudi Arabia provided poor services and some of the system hardware providers have gone out of business. In addition, internally, HADEED was planning to build another new factory called HADDED-2 Flat Product, which needed an appropriate technology. All previous external and internal (hybrid) forces were interpreted as positive because they were forcing HADEED from a crisis situation toward a better one (Figure B1).

The IT manager submitted by the end of 1989 a proposal to HADEED top management level describing HADEED’s current and future situation. HADEED top management realized the problem and supported the process further by establishing a project team to look for a new system.
