Abstract. Disciplines that progress faster have a discipline-wide culture of strong inference. Notwithstanding our strong tradition of empirical cumulation, we argue how practicing strong inference can also cultivate greater theoretical cumulation in management information systems studies. We develop and illustrate two philosophy-of-science ideas—nomological rubberbanding and retroduction—to foster it.

History: Ritu Agarwal, Senior Editor; Hock-Hai Teo, Associate Editor. Supplemental Material: Supplemental material is available at https://doi.org/10.1287/isre.2019.0848.

Keywords: theoretical cumulation • models • retroduction • nomological rubberbanding • strong inference • unobserved heterogeneity nomological string • theory construction

Half a century ago, physicist John Platt (1964) noticed that some fields, such as molecular biology and nuclear physics, progressed much faster than others. He attributed this to their discipline-wide research culture of strong inference, defined as a Bacon-inspired (Bacon 1620) continual iteration between a working theory and observation. In contrast, disciplines pursuing weak inference disconfirm ideas one study at a time and privilege empirical over theoretical cumulation (see Table 1).

Empirical rather than theoretical cumulation characterizes the management information systems (MIS) discipline. Individual studies with substantive standalone merit do not readily assemble into a larger theoretical edifice unique to MIS phenomena. In Forscher’s (1963) analogy, they’re loose bricks that don’t form an edifice.<sup>1</sup> Just 4%–18% of the 697 papers published over a decade (2005–2015) in three representative MIS journals (Information Systems Research, MIS Quarterly, and Journal of Management Information Systems) attempt to be theoretically cumulative, even though most are empirically cumulative (details are in the supplemental material). As a discipline, we rely on a division of labor between empiricist “brick producers,” leaving theoretical integration to occasional review papers. This is analogous to neatly restacking littered bricks, which is not the same as constructing an edifice. This impedes systematic theoretical knowledge cumulation within MIS.

Theoretical cumulation matters immensely because “it is theory, not practice, that gives a discipline an identity” (Belardi 2014, p. 96). Platt (1964) ominously cautioned that, although “a mathematical box” (a model) is a beautiful way to grasp a problem, it will not hold the phenomena unless it has been caught in “a logical box” (theory) to begin with. We contend how MIS—catalyzed by its strong culture of empirical cumulativeness—is veritably situated for cultivating stronger inference.

We develop two philosophy-of-science ideas— nomological rubberbanding and retroduction—to foster strong inference through greater iteration between a working theoretical idea and observation. We highlight untapped opportunities right under our nose—not any failings—in current MIS research. Nomological rubberbanding cultivates theoretical cumulation across nomologically adjacent MIS research streams. Retroduction inductively uses observations on hand to iteratively evolve a working theory. Such iteration—foundational to the scientific method— otherwise happens over a series of individual studies of a phenomenon, not within a single study, as we advocate.

The approach advocated here can systematically foster strong inference in MIS research within two broad boundary conditions: (a) research on choices about information technology (IT) made by individuals, managers, or firms; or (b) oft-confronted situations of “unaffordable experiments.” First, many MIS models attempt to explain consequences of IT-related choices, which are laden with observed and unobserved het erogeneity.<sup>2</sup> Second, many MIS research streams often confront the problem of what Sorenson (1992)

## Cycles of Iteration Between a Working Theory and Observation

A theory is the overarching story that conceptually consolidates a research model (Merton 1949). This conception encompasses all MIS research traditions, including behavioral, economics of information systems (IS), and design science. The demarcation between theory development and theory testing is largely moot for MIS research because empirical studies in our major journals routinely have a strong element of theory development. Although Bacon’s (1620) theorizethen-test approach to disconfirming ideas undergirds positivist MIS research, iterative cycles within a study where observation evolves an initial “working” theory are rarer.

We eschew the widely indoctrinated, one-shot approach in favor of an iterative approach, where a working theoretical idea evolves through dialogic cycles of deduction and induction within a study (Alvesson and Karreman¨ 2007, Swedberg 2015). Becker (2017) recently encouraged this in arguing that researchers work back and forth between ideas and observations, which become evidence for further ideas. That is more faithful to Bacon’s intention. Repeated deduction– induction cycles within the same study generate new hypotheses that feed into the theory-construction process in Platt’s (1964) spirit of strong inference. We believe that his perspective—despite its hard-sciences origins—is germane for MIS research.<sup>3</sup> Figure 1 illustrates how the two ideas—nomological rubberbanding and retroduction—that we develop next facilitate such iteration.

The forward loop represents a Kuhnian, ideas-driven approach, and the backward loop represents a Galisonian (Galison 1997) data-/tools-driven approach. Although a typical MIS study focuses on the forward loop (e.g., in the positivist tradition) or the backward loop (e.g., qualitative studies, analytical modeling, and some economics of IS studies), the two can go much more hand in hand (Dyson 2012). We show how iteration keeps our theorizing process open to the fortuitous contribution of serendipity. A personal preference for a data-driven or theory-driven starting point should not stall it.

## Forward Loop: Nomological Rubberbanding

Nomological rubberbanding—the working theory→ observations forward loop in Figure 1—refers to the process of theoretically instrumenting predictors in a hypothesized model with nomologically upstream constructs. In a generic hypothesized model in Figure 2, the dependent variable (y) is a function of a predictor (x) plus an error term (ε). If a manager chooses x influenced by some overlooked considerations (denoted collectively as i), x’s correlation with ε violates a critical assumption of their independence, leading to erroneous conclusions.<sup>4</sup> This requires expanding our thinking of IS phenomena from nomological networks to a nomological “strings.”

A nomological string refers to a set of causally ordered nomological networks—each typically a research stream—where one stream’s dependent variable is the next stream’s predictor. They can then be arranged in a theoretically sequenced set, as Figure 3 illustrates. A nomological string is the focal phenomenon’s evolving theoretical edifice. Appendix A illustrates the construction of a nomological string for IT outsourcing.

An archetypal study in Figure 2 hypothesizing x→y, rubberbanded using i constructs would theoretically integrate existing, nomologically upstream explanations of x in explaining x→y. x itself theoretically (and empirically) builds atop nomologically upstream blocks. It represents theory cumulation via conceptually synthesizing a set of causally ordered research streams. Nomological rubberbanding therefore allows integrating into a hypothesized model ideas transcending MIS research traditions and streams.

MIS studies often control for constructs from upstream blocks of a nomological string. This might accomplish empirical—but not theoretical—cumulation because we have not conceptually integrated nomologically upstream blocks into explanations of downstream phenomenon (e.g., x in Figure 2).<sup>5</sup> They become rival empirical explanations of y rather than cumulative theoretical explanations of x.

Leaving theoretical synthesis to occasional metaanalyses or review papers fritters away opportunities to foster strong inference. They are big-bang attempts at gathering of dispersed findings rather than systematic cumulation of theoretical ideas, gradually stitched together study by study. The big-bang approach implicitly condones a discipline-wide research culture that does not treat theoretical cumulation as a responsibility that every empirical study should bear.

Such discipline-wide culture of theoretical cumulation is pivotal to Platt’s characterization of strong inference. Otherwise, we end up with individually sound, yet collectively noncumulative, studies across blocks of the nomological string; a litter of loose bricks rather than an edifice in Forscher’s (1963) parlance.

## Rubberbanding’s Utility: A “Kabuki” Analogy

The rubberband demarcates the hypothesized model from its nomologically adjacent “stagehands.” The stagehands notion comes from Kabuki (traditional Japanese theater <sup>歌舞伎</sup>), which offers an analogy of what rubberbanding does. A peculiarity of Kabuki is that the stagehands handle props in full view of the audience. The audience is undistracted by stagehands because the Kabuki patron is supposed to know what to pay attention to and what to ignore to follow the storyline.

Analogously, nomological rubberbanding demarcates the new idea that a study intends to add (the hypothesized model; what we don’t know) from its stagehands (the rubberbanded part; what we already know about the phenomenon). This separation puts in the spotlight a model’s unique contribution within the cumulative theoretical context of nomologically upstream MIS work. Such cumulation elevates a collection of empirical statements into an ongoing theoretical conversation about the focal phenomenon. Each new brick coalesces into an evolving edifice, which provides a context to judge a study’s incremental contribution. A new brick that stabilizes a wobbly edifice is dearer than a perfect standalone brick. Rubberbanding’s reductionist approach also increases the hypothesized model’s idea density by packing more ideas into a littler model.

## Backward Loop: Retroduction

Retroduction—the observations→theory backward loop in Figure 1—refers to inductively using observations from the forward loop to iteratively refine a working theory. Building on Peirce (1903), we define retroduction as a form of logical inference that uses observations on hand to seek the most plausible explanation. Synonyms for retroduction in the philosophy-of-science literature include abduction, abductive inference, and abductive reasoning. Both counterfactuals and retroduction bricolage on-hand observations to explore yet-untestable ideas. Unlike counterfactual analysis that represents a contrast to an observed outcome (e.g., what if the same firm made a different choice), retroduction inductively mobilizes observations on hand to develop alternative plausible explanations and feed them back to refine the working theory. It potentially facilitates—in a single study—multiple cycles of inference-strengthening iteration between theory and observation, which ordinarily requires multiple studies.

To study how action x (e.g., outsourcing IT) leads to outcome y (e.g., agility), MIS researchers observe firms that took action x. The underlying philosophy of science hiccup with this approach is that we cannot derive inferences about firms that did not take action x by observing firms that did; as Hume (1739) would say, one cannot derive an ought from an is. The ideal solution of conducting field experiments quickly runs into Sorenson’s (1992) problem of unaffordable experiments. Even quasiexperimental designs can be implausible in the field. Imagine the implausibility of a researcher randomly outsourcing some corporate IT projects or decentralizing IT in some firms. Retroduction circumvents this challenge.

The key to retroduction is using the rubberbanded model in Figure 2 to estimate: (i) observed heterogeneity and (ii) unobserved heterogeneity.

(i) Observed heterogeneity is the theoretically predicted x in the rubberbanded model grounded in the nomologically upstream.

(ii) Unobserved heterogeneity is a residual of x $( x _ { r e s i d u a l } )$ from the rubberbanded model included in a test of the hypothesized model. (Control function approaches can generate such residuals.) It reveals previously obscured consequences of actions diverging from what the working theory predicted. For example, how a firm that outsourced IT would have fared if it had instead insourced it. Feeding back such insight to refine the working theory differentiates retroduction from counterfactual analyses.

The researcher’s plausible argument for the existence of a “counterfactual” can become an integral part of the working theory, then testable iteratively by using on-hand observations. Such modeling-as-aninput-to-theorizing combines the advantages of thin descriptions that positivist studies typify with thick explanations that qualitative studies typify (Folger and Turillo 1999). Such grounded-theory-like inference to additional conjectures from conventional empiricism is underappreciated as a means to stronger inference.

Finding an impact of $x _ { r e s i d u a l }$ further signals that mechanisms absent in block I might underlie the x→y relationship. Offering new plausible explanations for the impact of $x _ { r e s i d u a l }$ lays theoretically cumulative breadcrumbs for future research.

## Retroduction’s Utility

The unique value of retroduction is leveraging—in the theory-construction process—an affordance for strong inference. Just as new tools do for the hard sciences (Dyson 1998), preliminary results from an initial test of a working theory $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ the forward loop in Figure 1) precipitate plausible new ideas. For example, telegraphy tools preceded Shannon-Weaver’s information theory; and Rosalind Franklin’s X-ray diffraction images led Crick and Watson to $^ { \prime \prime } \mathrm { s e e } ^ { \prime \prime }$ DNA’s double helix. Similarly, using a then-frivolous tool (a crude particle accelerator) led Ernst Rutherford to see the unseen—an atom’s nucleus that occupied a trillionth of its volume—and spawned theoretical nuclear physics (Dyson 2006).

Similarly, first tests of a working theory offer us the affordance to generate further hypotheses that can intentionally be used retroductively to refine the working theory through further cycles of iteration. Precisely such capacity to theorize distinguishes a scholar from a tool-wielding technician (Belardi 2014). Retroduction thus bootstraps conceptual progress by making the researcher a vicarious participant in the theory↔observation iteration instead of being an objective observer passively reporting tests. Allowing our tools to augment our theorizing breaks the unfruitful segregation between “theory-driven” and “data-driven” MIS research.

## Recommendations for Cultivating Strong Inference in MIS Research

a. Forward loop (preobservation): Delineate what we don’t know while recognizing what we know by systematically sketching out a phenomenon-specific nomological string.

1. Rubberband what we know by identifying studies from upstream MIS research streams that explain x or its conceptual proximates. To keep the study satisficingly tractable, sample representatively from the buckets in the nomological string.

2. Increase the hypothesized model’s idea density by rubberbanding nomologically upstream MIS constructs and non-MIS constructs from adjacent disciplines.

b. Backward loop (postobservation): Retroduce theoretical insight from rubberbanded constructs by asking:

1. What relationships from an initial test of the working theory warrant revising it?

2. What additional plausible hypotheses can be drawn from an inconsistency between x as observed and x as predicted by the rubberbanded model?

In conclusion, the value of the two approaches to strong inference articulated here is not in the novelty of the ideas, but in sensitizing MIS scholars to the nuanced, untapped opportunities for stronger inference. They are an allegory for a more aggressive interplay of conceptual ideas and observations, as Bacon and Platt envisaged. Only a discipline-wide use of such practices can foster strong inference in MIS, where each study contributes to an evolving theoretical edifice that is useful, durable, and truly our discipline’s.

## Appendix A. An Illustration of Nomological String Construction for the IT-Outsourcing Phenomenon

Figure A.1 illustrates the idea of a nomological string constructed for IT outsourcing. The IT-outsourcing litera ture spans three streams explaining:

(i) How three broad buckets of constructs influence an outsourced IT project’s contract choices.<sup>8</sup> Envision them as three conceptual containers:

(a) Project attributes (e.g., uncertainty, technical in terdependence, customization, and size);

(b) Client–vendor relational attributes (e.g., their knowl edge, power asymmetry, and history); and

(c) Environmental pressures

(ii) Explanatory mechanisms (e.g., agility, penalties, coordination, process performance, and service quality); and

(iii) How they, in turn, drive downstream outcomes

We can thematically cluster these streams by what they explain: IT-sourcing choices, explanatory mechanisms, and consequences. Arranging them in a theoretically sequenced set gives us the IT-sourcing nomological string in Figure A.1. The string’s logic is that a firm’s context drives its IT-sourcing choices, which have performance consequences. Such a string is not a quest for a grand theory of IT outsourcing but, rather, greater theoretical—rather than just empirical—cumulation.

## Appendix B. An Illustration of Nomological Rubberbanding in IT-Outsourcing Research

Mani et al. (2012) illustrate how a seasoned research team applied nomological rubberbanding in an IT-outsourcing study. They hypothesized an association between outsourcing contract choice (fixed price versus time and materials; block II in Figure A.1) and information structure (joint action, relational emphasis on coordination, and technological ca pabilities) and their joint effect on outsourcing performance (service satisfaction and process ownership cost). Their rubberbanding efforts involved two steps.

First, recognizing that firms choose the type of out sourcing contract intentionally, they identified a set of nomologically upstream constructs for contract choice tha can be conceptually mapped to block I in Figure A.1. Sampling from all three buckets in block I, they included as instruments project attributes (e.g., process analyzability), relational attributes (e.g., prior history), and environmental pressures (e.g., competitive intensity), as well as instruments from block II (e.g., use of performance guarantees) and firm size. (Our arguments would include only instruments from block I.) This illustrates how sampling from the three buckets in block I in Figure A.1 (rather than being exhaustive) per mits sufficient theoretical cumulation without making the study intractable. Therefore, sampling within buckets from a nomological string allows satisficing rubberbanding. De marcating the hypothesized model of outsourcing performance from its nomologically upstream rubberbanded model on contract choice made their contribution distinct while also being theoretically cumulative.

Second, they included the residual obtained from these instruments’ influence on contract choice in their test of the hypothesized model, allowing their focal predictor (contract choice) to theoretically integrate upstream explana tions in examining its effect on outsourcing performance. This simultaneously integrates from economics the idea of minimizing the trade-off between ex ante costs of contrac design and ex post costs due to contractual inefficiencies. This deconstruction illustrates how the process of nomological rubberbanding puts within reach of all MIS researchers the theoretical cumulation practiced tacitly by seasoned ones.

## Appendix C. An Illustration of Retroduction in IT-Outsourcing Research

Chang and Gurbaxani’s (2012) exemplary study illustrates how systematically applying the ideas developed in this commentary can elevate rubberbanding-driven counterfactuals to retroduction. Their working theory was that firms choose to outsource IT based on expected results and therefore should be heterogeneous in their gains from IT outsourcing. Both observed and unobserved heterogeneity are used in this working theory. They rubberbanded a firm’s choice to outsource IT using content from the environmental pressures bucket in Figure A.1, block I (sales and cost efficiency, financial leverage, sales fluctuation, and IT intensity). They showed that firms that should not have outsourced but did benefited less than firms that should have outsourced and did. Because what firms “should have” done is defined by the rubberbanded content, an opportunity for expanded theoretical cumulation would surface by pur posely sketching out the nomological string in Figure A.1, block I. Although their unit of analysis was a firm rather than a project, Figure A.1 is still a useful conceptual device for cultivating strong inference in single-level models after excising constructs clearly mismatching their unit of analysis.

Annotated Figure A.2 shows where their study fits within the nomological string in Figure A.1 and reveals additional possibilities. This would have prompted them to also sample instruments representatively from the other two buckets in Figure A.1, block I, to enhance theoretical cumulation by accounting for other known buckets of drivers of IT-outsourcing choice (e.g., aggregate project attributes and interfirm relational attributes). It would also reveal alternative, plausible explanatory mechanisms from block III. The rubberbanded part could also selectively encom pass theories in adjacent disciplines (e.g., transaction-cost economics or agency theory) that also inform interfirm boundary choice that IT outsourcing represents.

The first retroductive step would be to feed back to the working theory their counterfactual insight into the performance of firms that erroneously outsource IT work that should have been done in-house, relative to firms that do not make such misjudgments.

Second, retroducing their observed positive interaction between a client firm’s IT intensity and a vendor firm’s IT knowledge could lead to grafting the idea of interfirm ca pability complementarity into their working theory.

Third, retroducing the observed positive interaction of vendor IT knowledge with software but not hardware outsourcing could lead to further differentiating between these two classes of IT assets in the working theory.

The working theory thus evolves in one retroductive cycle into a more nuanced story of performance penalties for a firm’s misjudgments in IT-outsourcing decisions for IT software and hardware. This is more nuanced and distinctive in its unique MIS contribution, yet simultaneously more theoretically cumulative vis-a-vis their initial work-\` ing theory.
