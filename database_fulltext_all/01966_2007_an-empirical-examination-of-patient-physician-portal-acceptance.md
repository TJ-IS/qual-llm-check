# An empirical examination of patient-physician portal acceptance

Richard Klein<sup>1</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Department of Management, College of Business and Behavioral Sciences, Clemson University, Clemson, South Carolina, U.S.A.

Correspondence: Richard Klein, Department of Management, College of Business and Behavioral Sciences, Clemson University, 101 Sirrine Hall, Box 141305, Clemson, South Carolina 29634-1305, U.S.A. E-mail: rklein@CLEMSON.EDU

## Abstract

Healthcare providers have recently begun deploying Internet-based patient– physician portals. These applications allow patients to both communicate with their providers and access personal medical information, such as laboratory results. Research within medical informatics explores, and debates, the utility of such functions from the patient and physician perspectives. However, work does not examine factors influencing patient use, assuming that once built and deployed patients will come. Hence, the current research empirically investigates patient behavioral intentions (BI) with respect to (a) use of electronic communications functions and (b) accessing of individual medical information through an Internet-based patient–physician portal. Surveying 294 patients, this work incorporates individual factors, namely computer selfefficacy and personal innovativeness in the domain of information technology, with technology-based factors identified in the Technology Acceptance Model. The analysis finds that usefulness and innovativeness have a positive direct effect on BI with respect to both functions, namely communications and information access. Additionally, patients with greater healthcare needs foresee increased use of portals to access their personal medical information. Finally, patients in primary care, as opposed to specialist, provider settings intend to engage in electronic communications. 75|-760

Keywords: computer self-efficacy; healthcare; patient–physician portals; persona innovativeness with information technology; TAM

## Introduction

Recent efforts within the healthcare industry see providers engaging consumers through technology innovations in an effort to increase the overall quality of service (Wareham & Klein, 2003). The market further finds healthcare consumers demanding ever-increasing convenience and access to providers (Ball & Lillis, 2001). Deployment of information technology (IT) represents a tremendous opportunity for healthcare professionals to improve both the quality of service provided and efficiency of the system (Schoen et al., 2006). Academics within the medical informatics community acknowledge the need for healthcare to exploit emerging IT (Tuttle, 1999). Internet-based patient–physician portals include both electronic communications capabilities and online patient access to personal medical information. These portals have emerged as a key technology innovation within the patient–physician context aimed at improving the quality of service and efficiency of interactions.

Work within medical informatics examining Internet-based access to electronic medical records finds patient opinions highly favorable and providers positive only after a period of extended system use (Earnest et al.,

2004). In general, patients find online medical records easy to use (Hassol et al., 2004), with frequent checking of laboratory and radiology results prevalent (Weingart et al., 2006). Additionally, research finds these applications more beneficial to patients with chronic conditions (Kim & Johnson, 2002), although efforts note that such patients desire greater medical records integration (Winkelman et al., 2005).

Similarly, patients favor electronic communications as an efficient mechanism for scheduling appointments and communicating questions (Kleiner et al., 2002), alleviating current frustrations with limited access to providers (Kassirer, 2000). Patients even express a willingness to pay a nominal fee (Adler, 2006), while also acknowledging privacy and confidentiality concerns (Bernhardt et al., 2002). Providers are more opposed to the medium in clinical settings (Kleiner et al., 2002), seldom interacting with more than a small, select number of patients (Patt et al., 2003). Studies focusing specifically on electronic communications find providers concerned with large message volumes (Kassirer, 2000; Kleiner et al., 2002; Hobbs et al., 2003), a lack of reimbursement for electronic interactions (Kassirer, 2000; Patt et al., 2003), an absence of context resulting in limitations in diagnosing without physical examination (Mandl et al., 1998), and questionable confirmation of receipt of communications and content understanding (Kassirer, 2000).

Existing medical informatics works examining Internet-based patient–physician portals employ inductive (e.g., Kleiner et al., 2002; Hassol et al., 2004) and casebased (e.g., Bernhardt et al., 2002; Kim & Johnson, 2002) approaches to explore barriers to adoption among patients and providers. By employing theoretically grounded models and constructs, the information systems (IS) community is uniquely positioned to add substantial value. Such research initiatives can complement existing efforts, advance our collective understanding of the phenomenon, and inform future application development and deployment. While initial adoption of patient portals occurs at the medical practice or healthcare provider level (Mantzana et al., 2007), the ultimate success of such systems depends on patient use (DeLone & McLean, 1992). Hence, research needs to specifically enhance our understanding of patient acceptance. Additionally, as noted in medical informatics studies, patient–physician portals encompass two distinct functional activities at the patient level, specifically communications and access to personal health records. Accordingly, research should model efforts in the context of these activities consistent with recent calls for more innovative approaches in gaining a deeper understanding of the use construct (Burton-Jones & Straub, 2006). Recent health information systems (HIS) work similarly proposes a more in-depth examination of use, focusing on telemedicine use quality from both clinician and patient perspectives (LeRouge et al., 2007).

One of the few theoretically driven empirical studies examines an e-health systems that offers patients the ability to access general, encyclopedic health information and e-mail their respective provider’s office (Wilson & Lankton, 2004). This work draws upon the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) and finds patient intentions to use the system influenced by beliefs about usefulness. The current research adopts a more innovative approach to examining multiple patient use intentions, building upon the existing study. The work reported here focuses on (a) communicating with providers and (b) accessing personal information. Both functions contribute toward enhancing the ability of providers to better serve patients and caregivers. Prior application of TAM to patient-based portal adoption explores communications and accessing, non-patient specific, encyclopedic medical content. Additionally, unlike the current effort, the existing research makes no distinct between patient intentions with respect to the two primary portal activities.

A review of HIS research within the IS community categorizes work as IS only, IS-healthcare, healthcare-IS, or healthcare only (Chiasson & Davidson, 2004). While IShealthcare efforts ‘focus on developing or testing IS theories, with some consideration of general or specific institutional issues in healthcare settings that influence study findings,’ healthcare-IS works employ ‘theories or concepts, drawn from or applied in IS research, to inform the analysis of the empirical findings, but also to explore contextual influences on IS/IT within the healthcare setting’ (pp. 159–160). Chiasson & Davidson (2004, p. 175) note that the healthcare-IS strategy bears the greatest potential for ‘pushing the contextual envelope of IS research with respect to a specific industry setting.

Accordingly, in examining the patient–physician portal phenomenon from a theoretical perspective, the current work adopts the healthcare-IS perspective and draws upon established IS constructs in evaluating individual patient and technology innovation constructs. Individual factors include computer self-efficacy (CSE) (Compeau & Higgins, 1995b) from Social Cognitive Theory (SCT) (Bandura, 1986) and personal innovativeness in the domain of IT (PIIT) (Agarwal & Prasad, 1998) drawn from Diffusion of Innovation Theory (DOI) (Rogers, 1995) and related marketing work (Midgley & Dowling, 1978). Constructs developed within the TAM (Davis, 1989) grounded in the Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA) (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975) serve to inform technology-based factors.

Additionally, consistent with the pursuit of a healthcare-IS strategy, the current research further incorporates constructs specific to the industry context. Namely, individual patient healthcare needs and primary care, vs specialized medical, provider settings are considered. Furthermore, in examining behavioral intentions (BI), this effort aims to adopt the healthcare-IS orientation and move beyond a pure focus on use of a given system or technology as has been the IS tradition with TAM. This study looks to gain a deeper understanding of patient intentions with respect to independent patient-centered functions at the heart of the patient–physician context, specifically communications and access.

## Research model and hypotheses

The noted theoretical models serve as the basis for development of the research model depicted in Figure 1. The current study captures BI with respect to using the portal to (a) communicate with healthcare providers and (b) access personal medical information. Research examining individual IT acceptance focuses on measures of intentions and/or use. Theory posits that intention to carry out a behavior is a requisite condition to behavior (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975). Prior IS efforts establish the predictive role of intentions on use (Taylor & Todd, 1995; Venkatesh et al., 2003). Moreover, IT adoption research often captures BI, absent behaviors (Agarwal & Prasad, 1998; Karahanna et al., 1999), as studies have validated the relationship between intentions and behaviors (Sheppard et al., 1988; Venkatesh & Davis, 2000).

Although initially operationalized in organizational contexts, TAM serves as the basis for a growing body of research examining IT innovation adoption outside organizational settings (Lucas Jr. et al., 2007). Similarly, patient acceptance of Internet-based portal applications falls outside the direct control of the practice setting. TAM posits that perceived ease of use (PEOU) and perceived usefulness (PU) shape use intentions (Davis, 1989). Work defines PEOU as a user’s expectations of the extent to which a given innovation is free of effort, while PU is an individual’s assessment that use of a target technology will increase their performance (Davis et al., 1989). With this model, usage is a direct function of use behavior that is a weighted function of attitude toward usage through PEOU and PU.

Prior works summarize the extensive research efforts employing TAM (Lee et al., 2003; Jasperson et al., 2005). Consistent with this established body of research including work integrating TAM with self-efficacy (Taylor & Todd, 1995; Agarwal & Karahanna, 2000; Venkatesh et al., 2003) and PIIT (Yi et al., 2006), both PEOU and PU are hypothesized to influence BI. H1(a) and H2(a) reflect direct effects for the two with respect to (a) patient– physician communication, while H1(b) and H2(b) consider (b) online access to patient medical information. Moreover, PEOU is posited to predict PU, H3, as originally hypothesized and demonstrated (Davis, 1989).

H1(a/b): PEOU will have a positive effect on BI with respect to (a) patient–physician communication and (b) access to patient medical information.

H2(a/b): PU will have a positive effect on BI with respect to (a) patient–physician communication and (b) access to patient medical information.

## H3: PEOU will have a positive effect on PU.

SCT recognizes the existence of a continuous reciprocal interaction between ones’ environment and their cognitive perception through self-efficacy and behaviors (Bandura, 1986; Wood & Bandura, 1989). CSE is defined as an individual’s ability to use technology in accomplishing a given task (Compeau & Higgins, 1995b). Early work examines self-efficacy in the context of performance (Compeau & Higgins, 1995a), while subsequent efforts focus on use (Compeau & Higgins, 1995b; Compeau et al., 1999) providing for extensions to acceptance and use of IT (Venkatesh et al., 2003). This work posits that CSE is an antecedent to use, while at the same time successful interactions with technology can influence development of CSE (Compeau & Higgins, 1995b; Compeau et al., 1999).

Prior empirical research finds CSE has positive effects on outcome expectations, affect, anxiety, and usage (Compeau & Higgins, 1995b; Compeau et al., 1999). Subsequent work employing TAM finds for a relationship between self-efficacy and PEOU (Lewis et al., 2003). The current research posits a like hypothesis, H4. Additionally, given the demonstrated relationship between selfefficacy and outcome expectations (Compeau & Higgins, 1995b; Compeau et al., 1999) coupled with noted similarities between expectations and PU (Venkatesh et al., 2003), H5 is stated. This research also explores potential relationships between efficacy and both BI constructs, H6(a) and H6(b), given findings with respect to use in initial studies (Compeau & Higgins, 1995b; Compeau et al., 1999).

DOI posits that individuals possess predisposed tendencies with respect to innovation adoption (Rogers, 1995). Specifically, everyone reacts differently toward new ideas, practices, or objects as a result of variations in innovativeness. DOI operationalizes innovative individuals as early adopters of innovations as opposed to majority and late adopters (Rogers, 1995). In contrast to such propositions, marketing efforts note potential issues in using time of adoption to define individual innovativeness (Midgley & Dowling, 1978; Flynn & Goldsmith, 1993). Accordingly, work advances the notion that personal innovativeness in IT (PIIT) constitutes an important construct in understanding individual acceptance of technology innovations (Agarwal & Prasad, 1998). Researchers define PIIT as ’the willingness of an individual to try out any new information technology’ (p. 206).

In considering differences in individual-based factors influencing adoption behaviors among patients, neither CSE nor PIIT can be assumed to operate independently. Hence, the current research recognizes potential connections between CSE and PIIT, both noted individual-level factors (Lewis et al., 2003). Accordingly, individuals’ positive perceptions of their own ability with respect to computer technology, CSE, are posited to positively influence willingness to try out a new IT, PIIT, as articulated in H7.

In developing the PIIT construct, it was further hypothesized to moderate individual perceptions of IT. Subsequent empirical efforts found little to no support for such a relationship, while seeing weak direct effects on BI and strong on PEOU and PU (Yi et al., 2006). Research similarly demonstrates a relationship between PIIT and both PEOU and PU (Lewis et al., 2003), with this work stopping short of consideration of BI or use. The current study posits direct effects on PEOU and PU, H8 and H9 respectively. Additionally, given prior evidence of an influence on BI (Yi et al., 2006), H10(a) and H10(b) are hypothesized.

H7: CSE will have a positive effect on PIIT.

H8: PIIT will have a positive effect on PEOU.

H9: PIIT will have a positive effect on PU.

H10(a/b): PIIT will have a positive effect on BI with respect to (a) patient–physician communication and (b) access to patient medical information.

Individual patients face dramatic differences with respect to their specific healthcare needs. In turn, patients suffering from chronic conditions, as an example, might in all likelihood engage in more frequent interactions with their doctor. Therefore, an additional construct, healthcare need, is included in the model as a potential influence on BI, H11(a) and H11(b) respectively. Healthcare need captures the frequency of past patient interactions with providers, numbers of different healthcare providers engaged, and presence of chronic conditions. Prior work within the medical informatics field examining electronic health initiatives similarly employs this construct (Wilson & Lankton, 2004).

H11(a/b): Healthcare need will have a positive effect on BI with respect to (a) patient–physician communication and (b) access to patient medical information.

Additionally, different practice settings likely impact patient utilization of portal applications. Primary care providers include practitioners seeing patients for common medical problems as well as serving as the initial point of contact for continuing health management and undiagnosed problems. By contrast, specialists focus on the treatment of specific organ systems. In developing a model predicting utilization of Internetbased patient–physician portals, primary care settings, focused on management of continuing care and initial diagnosis, afford greater opportunities for interacting with providers. With respect to personal medical information, patients likely record medical history with primary care providers. Moreover, as the front line for diagnosis, primary care environments should promote more frequent accessing of laboratory and radiology results, prescription and vital statistics history, in addition to chart notes. Accordingly, H12(a) and H12(b) are posited.

H12(a/b): Primary care practice setting will have a positive effect on BI with respect to (a) patient–physician communication and (b) access to patient medical information.

## Research methodology

In testing the proposed model and hypotheses, this research employs a field study design (Cook & Campbell, 1979). The study was conducted in collaboration with an IS vendor providing an Internet-based portal solution to healthcare providers across a broad spectrum of medical specialties and practices both large and small. Prior Internet-based patient–physician portal research studies narrow clinical settings, namely academic providers (Liederman & Morefield, 2003; White et al., 2004) and large healthcare networks (Hassol et al., 2004; Winkelman et al., 2005). Hence, the current study examines respondents across different clinical settings and providers.

The portal offers patients secure access through advanced authentication routines and secure socket layering (SSL) technology commonly employed in online banking and brokerage systems. SSL conforms to noted security recommendations (Prady et al., 2001). Moreover, the system offers integration with numerous commercially available practice management and electronic medical records applications in addition to other internal systems. Specifically, communications functions afford patients the ability to direct messages to individual providers, request prescription refills, as well as direct inquiries to scheduling and billing personnel. Personal medical information functions permit user updating of medical history in addition to accessing laboratory and radiology results, chart notes, scheduled appointments, as well as prescription and vital statistics history. The Internet-based portal was consistent with stated research objectives, and the potential sample population offered diversity in both provider specialty and practice size.

The sample included patients accessing the portal for the first-time to communicate with their provider and/or access their medical information over a 3-month period. As this research examines an IT innovation within a nonwork-related setting, the study explores perceptions and intentions of first-time users. Previous experimental research efforts examining TAM with respect to nonwork-related Internet-based applications apply a similar approach (Szajna, 1996; Gefen et al., 2003). The current effort adopts a field study, over an experimental, research design. All participants were informed that their responses were confidential and would not be reported to either their healthcare provider or the vendor. Additionally, patient and provider identifying information were not provided to the research team, ensuring anonymity of the target sample population.

A total of 294 users responded out of a potential 1473, yielding a response rate of 20%. Some 53.2% of the respondents were female, with all ranging in age from 18 to 74, averaging 39.7 years. In total, 54.4% were employed full-time, 25.9% homemakers, 12.2% retired, and 2.4% students. Of respondents, 83.7% were white, with 48.1% holding higher education degrees. Patients sourced from 231 individual providers within 151 different practices across 17 medical specialties with the majority in primary care functions. This distribution is reflective of the greater population of healthcare providers. Organizations range in size from single providers to a group of 23, overall averaging 2.9 providers per practice.

Direct assessment of potential non-response bias was not possible. The vendor had, however, subsequently emailed non-respondents 72 h following initial use soliciting participation for a second time. Comparing construct means between the initial respondents and the second wave, namely those responding to the second solicitation 72 h after initial use, provided a means for assessing potential bias. Using an accepted ’wave’ technique (Armstrong & Overton, 1977) latter respondents served as a proxy for non-respondents. Some 52 of the 294 respondents, or 17.7%, completed the survey based upon the subsequent solicitation. Differences across the two groups were assessed with respect to study variables, age, education level, employment status, gender, race, and provider’s medical specialty. Analyses of variance (ANOVA) tests find no significant differences (Po0.01) indicating that non-response bias did not impact the data collection effort.

This research also employs different and some reverse scale types across measures in an effort to safeguard against common method bias. Further, the Harmon onefactor test (Harman, 1967), prescribed within methodological literature (Podsakoff & Organ, 1986), results in eight extracted factors. No single factor accounts for the bulk of the covariance (Po0.01). Hence, common method bias does not appear to impact the data. Moreover, the analysis results see different levels of significance for path coefficients, providing further evidence of an absence of such bias with the data and results.

Within PLS, a series of ordinary least squares regressions estimate the dependent paths among the hypothesized theoretical relationships. The sum of the direct and indirect effects constitutes the total effect of the predictor on the endogenous variable. Meaningful path coefficients should exceed 0.3, with the minimum value above 0.2 (Chin, 1998a). ## Discussion of implications for HIS

This research explores patient acceptance of providers’ Internet-based portals, applying theoretically grounded constructs developed within existing IS literature. Results provide valuable insights into emerging patient-focused technology innovations proliferating within the healthcare field (Wareham & Klein, 2003). This research also makes contributions toward increasing our understanding of antecedent relationships within TAM and advancing the applicability of TAM to specific use intentions and diverse research contexts.

Medical informatics research has often pursued inductive and case-based approaches over theoretically driven empirical research efforts. The current study provides results based on more theoretically grounded constructs and measures and serves to validate some of the earlier findings. Additionally, the current study builds upon Wilson & Lankton (2004), a noted exception of medical informatics research grounded in theory. The current research extends Wilson and Lankton by acknowledging individual patient-based factors, namely CSE and personal innovativeness. In applying TAM to the patient portal phenomenon, results reported here support past efforts finding that patients must perceive some level of usefulness for expectations of future use to exist. This relationship further mediates the role of ease of use. Additionally, CSE constitutes an antecedent to both PEUO and personal innovativeness, with more innovative patients anticipating greater future use.

Clarifying the role of these individual patient and technology-based factors serves to inform and guide HIS application development. As patient self-efficacy and innovativeness shape perceptions of technology ease of use and usefulness, vendors must ensure that solution complexity aligns with patients’ technological aptitude. One approach might target deployment to younger, educated users with requisite skills, while an alternate course of action focuses on advancing user interfaces geared toward patients with limited skill sets. Additionally, healthcare provider requirements, such as triaging of electronic communications, potentially results in development of more complex applications, which may demand more time and input from patients. Here, the objective is to accommodate functionality requirements of providers yet limit such features’ impact on patient users. As an example, one vendor’s portal solution requires patients complete multiple screens of qualifiers in order to compose a message, while a different application facilitates triaging through a single dropdown menu. In sum, it may be important for developers to understand and balance both patient and healthcare provider user requirements.

Results particular to the HIS context highlight the importance of the setting in which patient portals are deployed, namely primary care or specialist. Findings indicate that patients in primary care settings show a propensity for future use of communications functions, while patients seeking specialized care tend to see future use directed toward accessing their personal medical information, perhaps due to greater healthcare needs. Researchers exploring other HIS should similarly consider the specific setting in an effort to gain a greater understanding of the phenomenon. By not taking into account the nuances of various healthcare settings in data collection and ultimately analysis, results related to use, care-based impacts, and user satisfaction may be incomplete and/or misleading. From a practical perspective, these insights aid both HIS solution vendors and healthcare providers in effectively targeting design and deployment for realizing the greatest number of adopters and, ultimately, the greatest impact on the level of service provided. For example, primary care providers might invest more in integrating portal capabilities with internal email, scheduling, and accounting applications, similarly aligning internal workflows in anticipation of greater patient use of electronic communications.

Additionally, this work finds healthcare need to be another relevant construct to technology use in the healthcare context. Wilson & Lankton’s (2004) original ehealth study employing TAM detected no significant effects with respect to healthcare need. The current study yields different findings. The conflicting results may be attributed to the fact that the current study explores intentions across multiple functions, while Wilson and Lankton did not. Breaking BI across both communications and personal information access functions, as done in the current study, allows for a deeper understanding of potential use. By studying BI within each function, the current effort finds for a significant relationship between healthcare need and BI with respect to accessing personal health information. From a practical perspective, clinical settings with larger numbers of patients with chronic conditions, and potentially recurring and more frequent healthcare needs, will likely encounter greater demand for access to personal healthcare information. Here, medical practices should pursue integration of laboratory facilities with electronic medical records. This integration could help address the needs of patients with chronic conditions by allowing them to review laboratory results as well as access prescription, vital statistics, and chart note history.

This study also contributes to existing portal research in terms of demographics. Prior works examining the patient–physician portal phenomena generally sample single large provider networks or medical school facilities (e.g. Hassol et al., 2004) that might not be reflective of the larger population, which also consists of small independent medical practices. In contrast, the current study explores a broad cross section of provider organizations of varying sizes and specialties. Hence, findings can be generalizes to a broader set of healthcare settings than possible with some existing works.

## Contributions to technology acceptance research

In considering the existing body of work devoted to TAM, the current research establishes CSE as an antecedent to personal innovativeness, consistent with contentions that neither can be assumed to operate independently (Lewis et al., 2003). Additionally, prior work incorporating both constructs does not consider (Lewis et al., 2003) or finds mixed results (Yi et al., 2006) for a direct relationship with intentions. Within the current research, CSE, personal innovativeness, and BI are considered, finding personal innovativeness an antecedent to intentions.

Second, prior TAM efforts focus on use of a focal IT, treating BI as specific to the innovation as a whole, while the current effort recognizes greater depth in innovation use. Specifically, within the healthcare context, given that portals encompass two distinct primary functions at the patient user level, the current work focuses on electronic communications and online access to personal medical records. This approach acknowledges that independent activities potentially exhibit different use patterns, informing our understanding of each. Clearly, patient intentions with respect to different functionality vary. The current effort finds that differences in individual healthcare needs and primary care provider settings have a major bearing on intentions to employ specific functionality.

Finally, while TAM serves as the basis for a significant body of research examining IT innovation adoption within organizational environments, works examining the phenomenon outside such settings focus on online purchasing activities. The current research in examining an external, voluntary environment, not motivated by commercial transactions, advances our understanding of the utility of TAM in diverse IT contexts. Future work may introduce other TAM constructs from prior research involving commercial transaction portals to determine how they hold in the healthcare setting. For example, research employing TAM in the study of consumer acceptance of online portals within the banking industry (Cheng et al., 2006) suggests perceived security an antecedent to intentions and might merit future research on patient–physician portals.

## Limitations and conclusions

The current study is not without limitations. First, data were collected in conjunction with initial system exposure and use. Research should also explore constructs longitudinally assessing changes in variables over time. Moreover, the current data set only reflects a single patient–physician portal application potentially limiting generalizability. Research could consider different vendors’ applications capturing a broad spectrum of solutions to gain an understanding of differences in constructs within the context of varied platform and functionality offerings. Findings in the current study would indicate that potential differences might exist with respect to divergent vendor functionality. Similarly, practices employing the innovation may not be representative of the greater population of healthcare providers.

The current data may also suffer from self-selection on the part of first-time users, many of whom may be highly computer literate and/or heavy Internet users. Such issues could be explored through experimental research designs. Future work should also explore acceptance on the part of healthcare providers. This might allow for a multilevel examination of matched pairs of patients and providers in an effort to gain a greater understanding of the interplay between constructs across the relational dyad. Finally, future efforts might explore alternate models and theories of technology acceptance, antecedents, and constructs shaping intentions.

This research conducted an empirical examination of patient acceptance of an Internet-based patient–physician portal, surveying 294 first-time users. The current study incorporates TAM constructs with CSE and PIIT in examining BI with respect to both (a) electronic communications and (b) patient access to personal healthcare information. Use intentions are further examined with respect to differences in individual patient healthcare needs as well as across primary care, vs specialist, clinical settings.
