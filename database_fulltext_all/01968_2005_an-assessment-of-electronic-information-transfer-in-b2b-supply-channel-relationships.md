# An Assessment of Electronic Information Transfer in B2B Supply-Channel Relationships

KYUNG KYU KIM, NARAYAN S. UMANATH, AND BUM HUN KIM

KYUNG KYU KIM is a Professor of Information Systems at Yonsei University, Korea. He has been a faculty member at the University of Cincinnati, Pennsylvania State University, and Inha University in Korea. His research interests are in the areas of B2B e-commerce, supply-chain management, and knowledge management. He has published his research works in Accounting Review, Journal of Management Information Systems, MIS Quarterly, Decision Sciences, Information and Management, Database, and Journal of Information Systems.

NARAYAN S. UMANATH is a Professor of Information Systems at the University of Cincinnati, Ohio. Entering academia after 15 years of technical and managerial experience in software development, he received his Ph.D. in Business Administration from the University of Houston in 1987. His undergraduate and graduate educations are in mechanical engineering and industrial engineering, respectively. Dr. Umanath’s current research interests include electronic integration in supply-chain relationships, organizational issues pertaining to information systems, and data modeling and data warehousing. His research publications have appeared in Communications of the ACM, Decision Sciences, Information and Management, Information Resources Management Journal, Journal of Management Information Systems, Journal of Managerial Issues, and Management Science.

BUM HUN KIM is a Ph.D. candidate at Inha University, Korea. He is the CEO of Winlogis, a consulting firm in Korea specializing in supply-chain planning software. His research interests are in the areas of supply-chain management and ubiquitous computing.

ABSTRACT: The basic premise of the extant literature related to electronic integration has been that the higher the integration, the higher will be the organizational performance. However, excessive electronic integration can be dysfunctional too. We make a conceptual argument that more is not always better and that the fit between contextual factors and electronic information sharing should be achieved to seek optimal channel performance. We empirically examine the fit between electronic information transfer (EIT) and contextual factors of a supply channel, our specific contribution being the assessment of fit in terms of multivariate congruence. The data required for this field study was collected from 124 managers/buyers responsible for supplier relationships in six multinational enterprises in two different industries (automobile and heavy shipbuilding) headquartered in Korea. The results ratify our hypothesis that multivariate congruence between EIT components and supply-channel contextual factors indeed exists. Follow-up drill-down analysis indicates that the monitoring component of EIT has a significant influence on demand uncertainty, and complexityin-use is influenced by the coordination aspect of EIT. However, both the coordination and monitoring aspects of EIT are significantly relevant to interdependence of partners in a supply channel. A post hoc exploratory analysis suggests that the supplychannel performance is influenced by the fit between the contextual factors and the channel design factors. An inference of practical value that emerges from our findings is that more or less electronic integration is not the real issue. What is critical is the fit between supply-channel context and the level of electronic integration.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: B2B procurement, electronic information transfer, empirical research, organizational information processing theory, supply-chain management, survey methods.

## Motivation

THE USE OF THE INTERNET TO FACILITATE business-to-business (B2B) commerce has attracted much attention from both academics and practitioners because of its potential to impact industry structure and the way business is conducted in the digital economy (e.g., [5, 20, 32]). B2B markets can take different forms (e.g., spot markets, electronic hierarchies, cooperative arrangements) depending on the characteristics of products being exchanged (e.g., manufacturing inputs versus office supplies) [20], market variability resulting from market fragmentation and market volatility [6], and the continuity of business relationships between the channel partners [34]. One of the key differences between various forms of B2B markets is the extent of information transferred between the trading partners. Information transfer in a supply channel is defined as the regulated flow of information from one unit (e.g., firm, work group, or individual) to the other unit in the supply channel. In spot market transactions, a minimum amount of information transfer between the channel partners may be sufficient since the buyer’s goal is to fulfill an immediate need at the lowest possible cost. Thus, information sharing may not be a critical issue in spot market exchanges. On the other hand, in a systematic sourcing arrangement where regular transactions are expected to occur on a long-term basis via negotiated contracts with qualified suppliers, organized information transfer between the trading partners may be crucial to achieve efficiency in recurring transactions (e.g., [2]). Also, the level of optimal information transfer between the channel partners will vary depending on the characteristics of B2B transactions.

How does electronic information transfer (EIT) fit here? The economic argument in favor of information technology (IT) in general is that IT can help curtail costs of coordination in the supply channel. Clemons and Row [7], however, articulate how information transfer using IT has the unique capability of simultaneously trimming both the firm’s costs of decision and operation, and the transaction costs of its channel partner. Does this mean arbitrary increase of EIT capability is the solution for enhancing channel performance in a B2B engagement? Information systems (IS) researchers (e.g., [4, 21]) make a conceptual argument that the fit between contextual factors and electronic information sharing should be achieved to seek optimal channel performance. Specifically, when a supply channel faces high environmental uncertainty, it is necessary for the supply-channel partners to have tight electronic integration in order to better cope with environmental requirements [21]. Based on organizational information processing theory [11, 38], it is possible to assert that tight or loose electronic integration does not mean more or less EIT, respectively, but just the “right” fit—no more, no less.

This research provides an empirical examination of the congruence between EIT and a set of contextual factors in a B2B supply channel. We also explore if the congruence between EIT and the supply-channel context influences supply-channel performance. The specific B2B supply-channel context chosen for the study is a components procurement process in the automobile and heavy shipbuilding industries. Our selection of the automobile and heavy shipbuilding industries reflects the following considerations: both industries have been known to use electronic linkages with their trading partners to different degrees. Specifically, automakers have a high degree of vertical integration with their vendors, coordinating all the production process from raw material production to final assembly through the use of electronic data interchange (EDI). The trend, however, is toward an automobile company being the coordinator of an increasingly intricate production network [4]. Automobile manufacturers purchase a significant proportion of core components from outside, thus increasing the number and relative importance of relations with suppliers [3]. Meanwhile, heavy shipbuilding can be characterized as a long, drawn out process, in some cases, stretching a few years. Since it often takes a significant time for suppliers to produce certain parts that fit the overall design, close coordination among the supplychain participants is crucial. Otherwise, the production sequencing and scheduling bottlenecks can cripple the entire project. Thus, close relationships between the shipbuilder and its suppliers are required to produce a ship that meets the customer’s design specifications and delivery schedule. For the reasons described above, these two industries provide appropriate contexts for this study.

## Background

THE ECONOMIC RATIONALE FOR EIT is that, by decreasing coordination costs associated with doing business within and outside the firm, EIT will provide opportunities for cost reduction and revenue expansion. In order to reduce transaction costs in supply channels, most retail chains have traditionally used captive distributors, vertically integrating to combine retail stores and distribution, whereas some manufacturers have vertically integrated into distribution by providing direct store delivery for their products. However, information transfer using electronic linkages between firms has been transforming the nature of the supply-chain relationships of firms with their external business partners [15, 30, 47]. EIT has the unusual quality of providing significant benefits to members on both sides of a supply channel [31]. The firm can deliver cost savings to its channel partners and also enhance the service it provides while simultaneously reducing its own costs of operation. When the firm can reduce another channel member’s transaction costs, the entire channel can compete more effectively with the alternative mechanisms [7]. The linchpin in this complex supplychannel relationship is the extent of EIT between the participating members.

Although EDI (electronic data interchange) has been studied extensively, theoretical (e.g., [4, 49]) and empirical (e.g., [6, 17, 26, 48]) studies of electronic integration and its principal component, EIT, have been few. As EDI emphasizes technological interconnectivity between the trading partners, existing assessments of electronic integration have mainly been technological, such as volume, diversity, breadth, and depth of EDI usage [26]. Zaheer and Venkatraman [48] examine the antecedents of EIT in the insurance industry. The same two authors [44] also investigate the performance effects of EIT by comparing a group of insurance agents that are electronically interfaced with a group that is not. The groups are formed based on electronic integration adoption behavior. Suggesting that a simple EDI link that automates the transmission of orders from a buyer to a seller does not create any electronic integration at all, Choudhury [6] proposes a scheme to classify the degree of electronic integration between the two firms. For instance, a basic level of electronic integration may occur when the linked firms develop product code translation tables so that employees at the participating firms can place and receive orders using their internal product codes. A higher level of electronic integration may be possible when the buyer’s computer determines a need for a product, based on preset reorder levels, and automatically transmits an order to the supplier’s order entry system with no human intervention. At the highest level of electronic integration, the firms can create close electronic coupling among the processes that create and use the information being exchanged. Bensaou and Venkatraman [4] conduct an exploratory study to uncover dominant configurations of interorganizational relationships across the United States and Japan in the automotive industry. They use the presence or absence of EDI adoption as a surrogate measure for IT capabilities.

However, the prior research shares three common limitations. First, prior research on EIT has relied mainly on a rational economic perspective [22]. Certainly, other theories ought to add value in capturing the wholeness of interorganizational relationships. For instance, organizational information processing theory [11, 38] suggests that simply increasing the amount of information transferred is not always better in supply-chain management [21]. Second, a holistic assessment of the theorized relationship between sets of variables representing the supply-channel context and EIT is still missing in this area of research. The only holistic assessment of fit reported in this area of research is the work by Bensaou and Venkatraman [4], who, using organizational information processing theory, conduct a set of cluster analyses to uncover the dominant interorganizational configurations on the basis of natural selection perspectives of fit—that is, fit is the result of an evolutionary process of adaptation [9]. Procedures such as cluster analysis and q-factor analysis are essentially inductive approaches, exploratory in nature, and only provide an implicit notion of fit [43, p. 5]. This is probably why Bensaou and Venktaraman [4] clearly label their study as exploratory. Drazin and Van de Ven [9] advocate the systems approach to fit as a means for testing theorized phenomena, where one can examine fit as a gestalt, simultaneously addressing the many contingencies and structural alternatives in a holistic fashion.

The third limitation of prior research is their use of EDI usage as a surrogate measurement for EIT (e.g., [30]). A simple EDI link that automates the transmission of orders from the buyer to the seller is not an adequate measure of EIT [6]. In the IS literature, the term electronic integration and EDI have often been loosely used as synonyms. Venkatraman and Zaheer [44] assert that there is a mistaken tendency to equate electronic integration with EDI in existing electronic integration research. Electronic integration is a broader construct that essentially subsumes EDI, which emphasizes only the technological interconnectivity between the channel partners. Electronic integration, on the other hand, encompasses the integration of business processes of the participating firms via electronic means as well as the associated technological interconnectivity [48]. EIT is a crucial aspect of electronic integration and refers to the regulated flow of information from one unit to another in a supply channel. Thus, in an electronically integrated environment, EIT represents more than just the technological means for efficient information flow in the supply channel. Prior research calls for an assessment of the degree of electronic integration beyond the mere presence/absence of EDI (e.g., [24]). Few empirical studies have adopted such a measure.

Our research attempts to extend the work reported in the literature by remedying the three limitations stated above. First, we measure EIT as a multidimensional construct. Second, we apply organizational information processing model [11, 38] as the principal reference theory in an interorganizational context; transaction cost economics is used to reinforce the conceptual development. Finally, we employ the systems approach to fit [27] for the assessment of the phenomenon.

## Theory

FIGURE 1 PORTRAYS A CONCEPTUAL MODEL of the supply-channel relationship for an interorganizational context developed by us as an extension of the organizational information processing theory [4, 11, 38]. The model postulates that various forms of uncertainty in the supply channel are the contextual factors that give rise to information processing needs that a channel faces; seeking optimal channel performance, the firms participating in the channel relationship generate appropriate information processing capabilities. Some structural designs of the supply channel are more efficient and effective than others for a given level of transaction-related uncertainty [11]. That is, alternative structural designs of the supply channel vary in their capabilities for effective information processing such as collection of appropriate information, its timely movement, and transmission without distortion. For example, when the demand pattern is highly uncertain, the supply-channel partners can generate strong information processing capabilities by forming a close collaborative relationship such as vendormanaged inventory (VMI)—as opposed to the traditional order-based relationship. Or, depending upon the partner’s absorptive capacity—namely, the ability to recognize and assimilate valuable knowledge from a particular supply-chain partner [1, 25], they can choose from alternative forms of supplier–buyer relationships such as quick response, continuous replenishment, or profile replenishment [5]. The supply-channel partners can design the way in which they can exchange business-related information as a dimension of the structural design of the supply channel.

As transaction-related uncertainty in the supply channel increases, trading partners tend to intensify collaboration to leverage inventory, transportation, planning, and so on. As a consequence, the need for information transfer and sharing between the participants in a channel increases. Information transfer through electronic linkages represents a major mechanism used by the channel participants to augment interorganizational information processing capabilities. For instance, simple EDI links support the exchange of explicit and well-structured operational information (e.g., quotes) and provide participants fast, accurate, and efficient exchange of data. Exchange of documents and multimedia data between the channel participants over highspeed communication lines provides another example of rich information processing capabilities. Electronic hub–based B2B marketplaces [20] are capable of supporting fast acquisition, storage, and aggregation of large volume data as well as qualitatively rich information with multiple trading partners, otherwise impossible to share in a timely fashion.

## Supply-Channel Design: Electronic Information Transfer

A firm may seek a competitive advantage by creating assets that are specialized in conjunction with the assets of a supply-channel partner. This partner-specific asset would entail implementing interorganizational information systems (IOS) that allow collaborating firms to systematically identify valuable information and then transfer it across organizational boundaries. An IOS constitutes a valuable resource as a channel or a conduit for information transfer. Having the necessary IT infrastructure in place may promote information exchange between trading partners in a supply channel. From the organizational information processing theory perspective, our research interest focuses on the EIT component of the supply-channel design generating the information processing capabilities.

Using transaction cost economics (TCE) as the theoretical foundation, Kim and Umanath [22] conceptualize EIT based on the two dimensions: coordination of business operations and monitoring of operations. The rationale is that channel partners transfer information through electronic linkages to expedite the coordination of business operations and to monitor the other party’s operations.

Breaking down the transaction costs of cooperation into costs of coordination and costs of transaction risk, Clemons and Row [7] argue that IT is capable of reducing both of these costs. Coordination costs are costs of coordinating decisions and operations among economic activities that occur between channel members [7]. The decision and operational activities in the procurement cycle include ordering, receiving and storing products, quality assurance, vendor invoices, and payments [45].

Transaction risk is the possibility of opportunistic behavior by the supplier, leading to uncertainty surrounding the level and division of the benefits from the increased integration of decisions and operations [7]. The focus of TCE has historically been on risks generated by transaction-specific capital (e.g., [46]). However, efforts to build a greater degree of integration for decisions and operational activities with or without the use of IT, while reducing costs of coordination, exacerbate transaction risk resulting from additional sources. Clemons and Row [7] identify information asymmetries and loss of resource control as possible sources of transaction risk that results from greater integration of decisions and operations. Information asymmetry can be reduced when partners in the interorganizational relationship freely share information relevant to the supply channel, such as electronic exchange of production and sales data, promotion plans, VMI, and so on. Further, the difficulty in accurately measuring the specific contribution of inputs in generating outputs creates the opportunity for performance shirking by the supplier. If the buyer lacks the ability to monitor the status of the supplier’s production process, such as the production capacities, inventory levels, shipping and delivery schedules, quality of the products being produced, and so on, the supplier has the opportunity to shirk its obligations. The consequent difficulties in performance monitoring entail transaction risk. To manage transaction risk, trading partners allow electronic access to internal information allowing an organization to become more transparent to external parties.

## Supply-Channel Context

The need for information processing in an interorganizational relationship will be determined by the contextual variables: demand uncertainty, channel interdependence, and product complexity (see Figure 1). Framing the gestalt argument of Drazin and Van de Ven [9] in a supply-channel context, the anatomy of an interorganizational relationship is complex and its richness can be better captured as a gestalt by simultaneously addressing the many (possibly conflicting) contingencies and structural alternatives in a holistic fashion. Other scholars [11, 27] also argue that dynamic interaction among contextual variables generates organizational configurations or adaptive patterns that are holistic in nature. This leads to the general congruence hypothesis relating the supply-channel context generating the information processing need in the channel to the supply-channel design providing the channel capabilities for EIT.

H1 (General Fit Hypothesis): EIT in a supply channel will have a significant association with the supply-channel context.

While properly conveying the multivariate congruence relationship as a gestalt, H1 is necessary, but insufficient, to explicate the finer aspects of the causal connections. For instance, it is quite conceivable that different contextual characteristics are related to different aspects of supply-channel design and to different degrees. As we embark on this detailed level of investigation, we also develop the necessary deeper understanding of the constituents of the supply-channel context and the supply-channel design.

## Demand Uncertainty

One of Galbraith’s basic premises [11] is that the higher the environmental uncertainty, the greater the information that must be processed to achieve a given level of performance [35]. Williamson [46] also notes that one of the underlying determinants of high transaction costs is uncertainty. Walker and Weber [45] identify two forms of uncertainty of demand: technological uncertainty and volume uncertainty. A product is subject to high technological uncertainty if its specifications change frequently or the probability of future improvements is high. A firm with low technological uncertainty procures a stable set of items on a frequent, ongoing basis. Volume uncertainty of demand is defined as an assessment of fluctuations in the demand for a product and the confidence placed in estimates of the demand [45]. In the supply-chain management literature, volume uncertainty of demand is also known to be a major contributor to the bullwhip effect (e.g., [5, 23]). The bullwhip effect occurs when the variance of orders may be larger than that of sales and the distortion tends to increase as one moves upstream [23]. The impact of this uncertainty on inventory and transaction costs is greater for products with higher absolute volume levels. The information processing capabilities to meet the information processing needs generated by high or low uncertainty of demand can be provided by high or low EIT between the participating firms. Hence, we propose the following hypotheses:

H2 (Demand Uncertainty Hypothesis): EIT between the supply-channel partners is positively associated with the uncertainty of demand concerning the products exchanged between the buyer and the supplier.

H2a: The higher the technological uncertainty of the channel transaction, the higher will be the EIT between the participants.

H2b: The higher the uncertainty of transaction volume through the supply channel, the higher will be the EIT between the participants.

## Channel Interdependence

In the continuum of task interdependence, Thompson [37] identifies three anchor points—pooled interdependence, sequential interdependence, and reciprocal interdependence. We apply this continuum to the interorganizational context. In pooled dependency, a mutual dependence in the relationship occurs because the supplychannel participants pool their resources. For example, the channel partners may share resources such as transportation vehicles and call center operations. In this case, the exchange of information between the channel partners would be limited to what is operationally necessary. With sequential dependency, units work in series where the output from one unit becomes input to another unit. For instance, automobile parts produced by partners in the alliance will be used in the assembly lines of an automobile manufacturer. In order to achieve smooth operations, the channel partners need to exchange information on order entry, inventory control, delivery schedules, and payments more frequently. In reciprocal dependency, units feed their work back and forth among themselves; in effect, each receives input from and provides output to the other, often interactively. For example, highly customized components or integrated subsystems require high levels of coordination between the channel participants [4]. They need to exchange information across multiple stages, from the concept design through the development of tooling and manufacturing processes at the assembler and the supplier [3, 5].

All the three forms of interdependence are likely to occur to varying degrees in a supply channel, thus contributing to information processing need. The information processing capabilities to meet the information processing need generated by high channel interdependence can be provided by a high level of EIT between the participating firms. Thus, we postulate that channel interdependence and EIT will covary.

H3 (Channel Interdependence Hypothesis): EIT between the supply-channel partners is positively associated with the channel interdependence between the buyer and the supplier.

## Product Complexity

Characteristics of a product may be viewed in terms of complexity. Complexity stems from the amount of information that must be exchanged between the buyer and the seller of a good or service in order to describe its attributes in sufficient detail so that the buyer can make an informed choice between competing suppliers [24]. Upah [40] identifies two types of product complexity: complexity-in-use and complexity-inevaluation. Complexity-in-use refers to the amount of effort required by the buyer to understand the proper usage procedure and variety of uses of the product. Complexity-in-evaluation refers to the amount of effort required by the buyer to evaluate the various features and options of the various brands or models of the product. When the products have simple, standardized descriptions such as light bulbs, there is less need for information transfer between the trading partners [3, 4]. However, highly complex product descriptions such as complicated manufacturing systems require more information transfer through the supply channel [24]. When there is a high information processing need due to complex product descriptions, higher than normal EIT between the participating firms is expected to prevail in order to generate the necessary information processing capabilities in the supply channel.

H4 (Product Complexity Hypothesis): EIT between the supply-channel partners is positively associated with the complexity of the products exchanged between the buyer and the supplier.

H4a (Complexity-in-Use): EIT between the supply-channel partners is positively associated with the complexity-in-use of the products exchanged between the buyer and the supplier.

H4b (Complexity-in-Evaluation): EIT between the supply-channel partners is positively associated with the complexity-in-evaluation of the products exchanged between the buyer and the supplier.

## Method

## Sample

WHILE THE SUPPLY CHANNEL ENTAILS a dyadic environment, this research examines the phenomenon from the buyer’s perspective in the dyad. The data required for this study was collected from managers and buyers responsible for supplier relationships in six multinational enterprises headquartered in Korea. Three companies were in the automobile industry and three in the heavy shipbuilding industry. At the time the data were collected, the annual sales of the three automobile companies for the year 2000 were \$3.3, \$9.1, and \$15.1 billion, respectively, whereas those of the shipbuilding companies were \$1.6, \$5.1, and \$5.5 billion, respectively. These figures were converted into U.S. currency based on the exchange rate of US\$1 = Korean won 1,264 as of December 29, 2000. Altogether, 124 buyers from six organizations participated in this study. Sixty-three respondents were from the automobile industry, and the others were from the shipbuilding industry.

Our fieldwork proceeded as follows. First, some field interviews were conducted with senior managers responsible for purchasing. The purpose of the interviews was to clarify the following issues: (1) to substantiate the appropriateness of the constructs and hypotheses and (2) to ensure that we have an adequate sample across the six firms. These interviews also enabled the researchers to gain access to appropriate buyers for data collection. Second, some briefing sessions with buyers were held to explain the nature of the research project and how to answer the questionnaire. Completed questionnaires were collected by the senior managers and mailed back to the researchers.

The unit of analysis in this study pertains to the interorganizational relationship— more specifically, pairs of product category and supplier. A product category (e.g., brake systems, converters, and gears and shafts) refers to a group of products with similar characteristics that already existed in the organizations sampled. Most buyers were responsible for a specific product category due to the complexity of a product and the transaction volume. Buyers often used the same supplier for a specific product category while maintaining alternative sources of supply open just to combat unexpected events. Each buyer was asked to answer a questionnaire that measured the characteristics of a product category and a questionnaire pertaining to the business relationship between the buying firm and the supplier through electronic interconnection. A data point in this study consists of a dyad made up of a product category and a supplier. The final sample had 154 pairs of a product category and a supplier. In order to verify the adequacy of sample size, statistical power analysis was performed. The power of a test is the probability of detecting the phenomenon under test [8]. Other things being equal, the larger the sample size, the higher the statistical power. The commonly used a priori value of the significance criterion, α, the risk of mistakenly rejecting the null hypothesis (i.e., the probability of Type I error) is 0.05, while that value of mistakenly accepting the null hypothesis (i.e., the probability of Type II error), β, is 0.20 implying a power (1 – β) of –0.80. For our sample size, the statistical power at α = 0.05 is 0.95. In fact, even at a more stringent $\alpha = 0 . 0 1$ , our sample size yields a power of 0.86, effectively ratifying our sample size as more than adequate [8].

## Measures

The first part of a two-part questionnaire contained 12 items representing the three supply-channel context variables—namely, demand uncertainty (technological uncertainty and volume uncertainty), channel interdependence, and product complexity. The second part of the questionnaire contained 12 items representing EIT in a supply channel.

An initial English version was first translated into a Korean version by one of the authors, which was then independently translated back into English by a professional translator to check for and eliminate inconsistencies. Preliminary testing involved structured interviews. The multiple structured interviews conducted with managers of purchasing divisions were intended to test the face validity of the instrument. Based on the feedback from these interviews, a few questions were rephrased to better reflect industry-specific situations. Pretests of the instrument were performed in two companies, and three focus group interviews were conducted with respondents to ensure that the target informants understood the wording consistent with the researchers and that the Korean version was a valid translation of the English version. The buyers were asked to indicate their responses to each of the 24 questions that had a sevenpoint scale anchored with “not at all” and “very much so.” Based on these pretests, some questions were modified to enhance clarity. The two companies that participated in the pretests of the research instruments were not a part of the final sample.

## Supply-Channel Context

All measures for the context domains were constructed by appropriately modifying instruments already developed and used by other researchers. (1) Demand uncertainty followed the conceptualization of volume uncertainty and technological uncertainty by Walker and Weber [45]. (2) Channel interdependence was based on the three forms of task interdependence originally defined by Thompson [37]. (3) The measurement for product characteristics was based on the two product complexity dimensions, complexity-in-use and complexity-in-evaluation, proposed by Upah [40]. All scales were multi-item measures using a seven-point Likert scale.

## Supply-Channel Design (Electronic Information Transfer)

The reader is reminded that the aspect of supply-channel design considered here pertains exclusively to EIT. Kim and Umanath [22] developed an instrument to measure EIT as a multidimensional construct. Twelve items—seven for the monitoring dimension and five for the coordination dimension of information transfer—were included in the research instrument.

## Instrument Validation

Since minor wording changes were made to a few of the adopted questions in order to customize the instrument to a supply-channel setting, principal components analysis was performed on the data to ascertain the integrity of the dimensionality sought [14, 19]. Principal components analyses were performed on the context variables and on the information transfer variables. Principal components analysis provided preliminary ratification of the conceptualized constructs. Table 1 provides the standardized parameter estimates (factor loadings) of the items over the underlying dimensions. We used the often-cited rule of thumb of 0.50 [14] as the cutoff value for factor loadings.

For the context variables, four factors were extracted, which accounted for 77 percent of the variance in the data set. All items were loaded together on the intended factors, except for one item in channel interdependence (item 11). Item 11 exhibited a significant loading on the volume uncertainty dimension. We discarded this item as spurious because we could not find a rationale to explain this behavior. For the information transfer variables, two factors were extracted, which accounted for 52 percent of the variance in the data set. All but two items (items 2 and 8) were loaded together on the intended factors. Item 2 in the monitoring dimension, and item 8, in the coordination dimension, did not load on any factor and were discarded from further consideration.

Thus, the final instrument consisted of 21 scale items. A copy of the instrument appears in the Appendix. ## Test of fit

Fit as a construct has occupied the attention of researchers for over two decades. Early researchers approached fit as congruence in terms of simple correlation between dimensions of interest. This was followed by a criterion-specific view of fit where the existence of an intervening effect was considered (fit as contingency) in the association between an antecedent (predictor) and criterion (response) variable [9]. The criterion-specific view of fit encompasses two distinct approaches: the mediation approach and the moderation approach. The mediation approach models a transitive effect of the form $y = f ( z )$ and $z = f ( x )$ , indicating the need for the presence of z for transmitting the effect of x on y. Mediation models are usually tested using path analysis procedures (e.g., [29]), which measures fit as an indirect effect and therefore lacks precision even when sophisticated path-analytic tools such as structural equation modeling, capable of analyzing a set of regression equations simultaneously [42], are employed. Fit as moderation seeks to predict variations in the criterion variable in terms of covariation between predictor and moderator variables using the popular multiplicative model. All the methods presented above examine pairs of variables in isolation from the rest of the domain variables.<sup>1</sup>

Pattern analysis procedure [9] and canonical correlation analysis [33] are deductive techniques for formulating fit as multivariate congruence and provide for an explicit specification and testing of the conceptualization of fit as a gestalt—that is, a holistic phenomenon.

## Test of Hypotheses

In order to check any systematic differences among the six different companies in the sample, analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed on all the independent variables. No statistically significant differences were found among the companies at the 0.05 level of significance. Univariate tests were performed on all the research variables to observe the descriptive statistics and to check if the data in each case came from a normal population. In all cases, the Shapiro–Wilk statistic ratified normality. The data analysis is performed as a two-step procedure. Canonical correlation analysis [14, 36] is first performed to test the multivariate congruence between the domain variables representing the supply-channel context and the EIT (H1).

Described as the most general case of the generalized linear model, canonical correlation analysis is a general procedure for investigating the multivariate relationship between two sets of variables [14, 19]. The procedure generates linear functions of predictor (supply-channel context) and criterion (supply-channel design) sets of variables such that the covariance across the two sets is maximized. Thus, the linear composites of each of the two sets in a canonical function represent canonical variates that are maximally correlated [36]. The procedure successively enumerates the number of statistically significant canonical functions under the null hypothesis that there is no relationship between the predictor and criterion variables. The resulting canonical correlations measure the strength of the overall relationships between the linear composites of the two sets of variables [19]. The significant canonical functions can be interpreted using one of three measures: canonical coefficients (weights), canonical loadings (structure correlations), and, sometimes, canonical cross-loadings. Canonical coefficients are susceptible to sample-specific multicollinearity and cross-sample instability problems and are often used only as a supplement to inferences made from the loadings [14]. Although cross-loadings are interesting measures, their use is controversial [14, 36].

Canonical loadings represent the correlation between each observed variable in either the predictor or the criterion variable set and the overall set’s canonical variate.

Since tests of significance for the loadings are not generally available, a range of 0.30 to 0.50 to infer covariation is generally recommended [10, 36]. In our analysis, we have chosen what Thompson [36] suggests as a conservative threshold value—that is, 0.50. Although canonical loadings are the most preferred method of assessment [14], we have provided all three measures for the reader’s perusal.

The results of canonical correlation analysis indicated a significant multivariate relationship across the supply-channel context and EIT (supply-channel design) variable sets, thus lending support to the general fit hypothesis (H1). Two canonical functions of statistical significance (canonical function 1 [CF1], $\chi ^ { 2 } = 3 4 . 6 1 3 , p < 0 . 0 0 1$ ; canonical function 2 [CF2], $\chi ^ { 2 } = 8 . 6 1 8 , p = 0 . 0 7 1 )$ ) emerged. Table 4 shows the standardized canonical coefficients, loadings, and cross-loadings of the participant variables. The loading pattern in canonical function 1 indicates a dominant relationship between technological uncertainty, volume uncertainty, and channel interdependence (i.e., supply-channel context) and both dimensions of EIT. On the other hand, product complexity exhibits a dominant relationship with only the coordination dimension of EIT and, as a consequence, emerged as a second canonical function. In essence, in order to capture the multivariate congruence, two latent constructs emerged.

The canonical correlations (0.40 and 0.24) provide a measure of the strength of the relationships between the linear composites (i.e., canonical variates) of each significant canonical function. In other words, the bivariate correlation between the linear composites of the supply-chain context variables and the supply-chain design variables is 0.40, as extracted in the first canonical function. The residual variance shared between the variables of the two domains has sufficient strength to yield a second significant canonical function. As can be expected, the correlation between the two variates constituting the second canonical function, though significant, is lower (0.24).

Because the results of the multivariate assessment—namely, the canonical correlation analysis—were significant, follow-up bivariate analyses over domain variables representing the supply-channel context and the supply-channel design (i.e., EIT) enable additional drill-down analysis. Since the theoretical development (see the section on Supply-Channel Design: Electronic Information Transfer) followed by empirical ratification (see factor condensation in Table 1) revealed that the two EIT dimensions—namely, monitoring and coordination—are orthogonal factors, independent regression procedures were run for each of these two information transfer dimensions against the context variables—that is, demand uncertainty, channel interdependence and product complexity (H2–H4). ## Discussion and Research Implications

## Discussion of Findings

THIS RESEARCH EXAMINES EIT in a supply channel as a function of specific contextual factors such as demand uncertainty, product complexity, and channel interdependence. We set out with three aims. The first is to understand electronic integration beyond EDI, which was charted out by our predecessors in this line of research (e.g., [3, 16, 26, 30, 44, 48]). IT has opened up avenues of interorganizational cooperative efforts hitherto inconceivable (e.g., [1, 12]). As IS researchers, our interest is on information flow aspects of electronic integration. EIT, the component of electronic integration that is captured in our research, subsumes EDI.

The next obvious question pertains to how much EIT. Recent assertions by scholars in this line of research [2, 21] indicate that “more” is not always “better.” So, our second aim is to establish a theoretical basis to assess the degree of EIT required for coping with the environmental conditions in which the supply channel operates. This led us to the idea of fit in an interorganizational context. Bensaou and Venkatraman [4] precede us in evaluating the application of organizational information processing theory to an interorganizational context. However, they limit their study to EDI adoption. We have articulated an application of the organizational information processing theory that can serve as a springboard for the study of electronic integration in supply chains.

## Performance Consequences: A Post Hoc Analysis

Congruence as a criterion-free perspective of fit operates under an implicit premise that fit affects some criterion of interest (e.g., performance); it does not test it. Although the assumption that superior fit leads to enhanced performance and inferior fit entails diminished performance is appealing to conventional wisdom (common sense), the implication is still a premise. Organizational information processing theory offers a rationale for this premise [11, 38]. That is, in the supply-channel context, lack of fit due to insufficient EIT entails operational inefficiency and other opportunity costs such as delayed or inappropriate decisions. At the same time, lack of fit due to excessive EIT entails similar consequences due to factors such as counterproductive information overload, and so on.

Thus, optimal supply-channel performance will result when the information processing capabilities produced by EIT precisely meet the information processing needs generated by the interorganizational context—no more, no less. Alternatively, the better the match between the information processing needs and capabilities, the better will be the channel performance. EIT adds value in a channel relationship through an increase in the efficiency of transaction processing and improvements in the coordination and communication systems [3, 4, 22]. Therefore, EIT, among other things, can be leveraged to supply the necessary information processing capabilities to cater to the information processing needs created by the contextual factors. Operationally, this amounts to the congruence between supply-channel context and supply-channel design (i.e., EIT) influencing channel performance.

In this research, we explored the criterion-specific aspect of fit by testing the impact of fit between supply-channel context and EIT on performance. We measured performance using a scale adopted from Iacovou et al. [16]. The four canonical variates from the two canonical functions connoting the latent constructs of fit between supply-channel context and EIT were regressed against performance. The statistical significance of the result confirmed our expectation that supply-channel performance is significantly influenced by the congruence between supply-channel context (factors that generate information processing needs) and supply-channel design (resources that provide information processing capabilities).

## Limitations

Our study has a few limitations that must be mentioned before concluding. One concerns the low reliability of the instrument used to measure the two independent variables, “channel interdependence” and “information transfer for coordination.” However, these measures were adopted from the sources where the dimensions were theoretically developed and empirically validated. Thus, we assume that the relatively low Cronbach’s alpha for the two variables is an artifact of our data set—not the reliability of the instrument. Also, the model R<sup>2</sup> values of the two regression tests are low. However, the hypotheses tested pertain to the presence of relationship between variable pairs that is indicated by the significance of the corresponding coefficients, which are quite strong.

Also the data for this study have been collected from six multinational enterprises, headquartered in Korea. The interpretation of this study’s results is subject to the constraints of organizational and cultural characteristics of those six organizations. In order to increase the external validity of the findings of this study, future research incorporating a large-scale field study is called for.

Another limitation of this research is in the selection of supply-channel design variables. We have confined the supply-channel design variables to EIT because we focus on the procurement process in B2B context. However, other supply-channel design variables are capable of enhancing information processing capabilities (e.g., collaborative planning and forecasting [popularly referred in the literature as CPFR]). We, however, contend that these omitted variables are irrelevant to the procurement process in a B2B engagement, even though they may contribute to information processing capabilities of a supply channel at a broader level. To that extent, caution must be exercised before generalizing the findings of this study to contexts beyond B2B procurement activities.

## Concluding Remarks

The results of this study shed new light on the prior literature on electronic integration including EDI. EDI research has followed the general proposition that EDI can make an important contribution to improving coordination between firms [44]. Researchers have thus been interested in the consequences of EDI on competitive advantages [16] and the effect of interorganizational computer networks on transaction costs [7]. The basic premise of this stream of research is that the more EDI is used, the better the organizational performance. For example, some researchers proposed that the integration level of EDI is positively related to the benefits of EDI [16].

However, our findings imply that more or less electronic integration is not the real issue. Instead, the benefits of electronic integration are determined by the congruence between the supply-channel context and the level of electronic integration. As an example of a dysfunctional effect of too much electronic integration, supply-channel partners may suffer from information overload if they receive too much information from the trading partner when the demand patterns are stable and they deal with relatively simple products. In this case, the receiving unit may have to waste its time sorting out the relevant information from the received data. Thus, the level of electronic integration should be appropriate for the contextual factors surrounding the supply-channel relationships—no more, no less.

## Future Directions

Although, in this research, we focus our investigation on a dyadic relationship in a supply channel, the findings of this study can be used to review related research issues in supply-chain management because a supply channel operates within the constraints of its supply chain. Existing literature on information sharing between trading partners in a supply chain reveals that there are two underlying counterforces at work: (1) firms are willing to share information with supply-channel partners in order to improve operating efficiencies, and, at the same time (2) firms may want to maintain some degree of information asymmetry to manage channel partners’ behaviors. Further discussion of the two counterforces follows.

First, for the argument in favor of information transfer, literature suggests that transferring ultimate sales information from a downstream site to the upstream site is a possible way to counteract the bullwhip effect (e.g., [23]). The bullwhip effect is said to be a core problem in supply-chain management because the demand information may get distorted while being transmitted up the supply chain. This supposedly happens when the supplier forecasts demand patterns based on the order history from its immediate downstream partner (buyer), instead of the sales data from the ultimate customers. EIT can be an effective mechanism to deal with the bullwhip effect and to enhance coordination across the entire supply chain (up/downstream).

On the contrary, forwarding raw downstream data upstream may result in distorted signals, data misinterpretation, and inaccurate assimilation. The consequences can be dire cumulatively across the whole supply chain. This effect may be further pronounced when the downstream sites belong to a diverse spectrum of consumers.

Second, in a supply-chain context, information asymmetry resulting from a supplier having privy to information specific to the relationship that the buyer does not have access to can also undermine channel performance [22]. Supplier-to-buyer information asymmetry increases transaction risk, leading to uncertainty surrounding the level and division of the benefits from the increased integration of decisions and operations [7]. In addition, it may be necessary for the buyer to maintain a certain level of information asymmetry with the supplier, especially regarding the downstream supply-chain data lest the supplier may use the downstream data opportunistically at the expense of the supply-chain performance. On the other hand, the buyer may also misuse information asymmetry with the supplier to control/limit agents opportunities and actions to the current supply channel.

In summary, the forces underlying the information transfer phenomenon are invariably in resonance and in conflict to different degrees simultaneously. Since two different theories underlie these behavior patterns, concurrent assessment of the opposing theories and the contingencies under which each is relevant for the supply-chain efficiency and effectiveness is a worthwhile research project for the future.

A natural extension of this research is to study factors that facilitate EIT between the participating firms beyond a single supply channel—that is, across the whole supply chain [13]. Among others, organizational trust may be of special interest in the digital economy because trading parties will naturally experience uncertainty due to the novelty of the electronic channel [18]. Several researchers (e.g., [28, 34]) argue that continuing cooperative relationships with other firms is based more on trust and equity than on monitoring and control capabilities. Their argument employs a behavioral assumption of trustworthiness rather than opportunism, which enables one to investigate how firms build interorganizational trust through recurrent transactions facilitating high level of EIT.

## Appendix. Research Instrument

## Supply-Channel Context

## 1. Demand uncertainty

1.1. Technological uncertainty

a. We expect frequent changes in specifications for this product category.

b. The probability of future technological improvements of this product category is high.

1.2. Volume uncertainty

a. We expect significant fluctuations in the daily (or monthly) volume requirements for the product category.

b. We expect volume estimates for the product category to be uncertain.

2. Channel interdependence

a. We and our suppliers pool our resources.

b. We work in series; that is, we purchase the outputs from the supplier, but we do not send them back to the supplier for further work.

c. The work goes (e.g., product designs, raw materials, work-in-processes) back and forth between us and the suppliers.

3. Product complexity

Complexity-in-use

a. When we place an order to a supplier for the first time, it requires very little amount of effort for us to explain the attributes of the product to the supplier.

b. When we place an order to a supplier for the first time, it requires very little amount of effort for the supplier to explain the proper usage procedure for this product.

c. When we place an order to a supplier for the first time, it requires very little amount of effort for the supplier to explain the variety of uses of the product.

## Complexity-in-evaluation

d. We have to exchange a number of data items with the supplier in order to place an order.

e. It takes a lot of time/effort for us to explain what we want from the supplier for this product.

## Supply Channel Design

## 4. Information transfer for coordination

a. We share common product codes with the supplier either through the same product code tables or computerized code translation tables.

b. We transmit purchase orders to the supplier electronically.

c. We make payments for the supplier’s invoices electronically.

d. We provide performance feedback such as the quality of products delivered electronically.

e. We receive suppliers invoices electronically.

## 5. Information transfer for monitoring

a. We access the supplier’s production schedule electronically.

b. W e access the supplier’s shipping/delivery schedule electronically.

c. We access the supplier’s inventory levels of finished products electronically.

d. We access the supplier’s inventory levels of raw materials electronically.

e. We use the data, electronically transferred from the supplier, in our business decisions.

f. We can monitor the supplier’s production capacities electronically.

g. We can monitor the quality of products produced by the supplier electronically.

Note: Italicized questions are not part of the final instrument due to inadequate factor loading.
