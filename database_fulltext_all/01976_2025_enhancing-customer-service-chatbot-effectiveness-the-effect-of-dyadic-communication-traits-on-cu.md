# Enhancing Customer Service Chatbot Effectiveness: The Effect of Dyadic Communication Traits on Customer Purchase Intention

Ran Tan,<sup>1</sup> Yang Li,<sup>2</sup> Qian Huang,<sup>3</sup> Hefu Liu<sup>4</sup>

<sup>1</sup>School of Management, University of Science and Technology of China, China, tanran@mail.ustc.edu.cn <sup>2</sup>School of Management, Hefei University of Technology, China, yangli@hfut.edu.cn <sup>3</sup>School of Management, University of Science and Technology of China, China, huangq@ustc.edu.cn <sup>4</sup>School of Management, University of Science and Technology of China, China, liuhf@ustc.edu.cn

## Abstract

Chatbots have been pervasively applied in online retailing to communicate with customers. However, attracting and converting customers with chatbots remains a challenge, and little is known about whether and how chatbots’ communication traits influence customer purchase intention. In this study, we draw on the stereotype content model to identify warmth and competence as two distinct traits of chatbot communication. Then, we employ interpersonal attraction theory to pinpoint perceived attraction as the underlying mechanism, where customer-chatbot similarity of communication trait plays a contingent role in shaping perceived attraction. Through a mixed methods design, we found that customers perceive higher attraction toward chatbots with the communication trait of competence, facilitating customer purchase intention. When the similarity of dyadic communication traits exists, we also found that customers perceive higher attraction toward chatbots with warmth. Our findings contribute to the literature on chatbot anthropomorphism and customer behavior. The study also provides practical insights into chatbot design for online retailing.

Keywords: Human-Chatbot Communication, Stereotype Content Model, Similarity-Attraction Rule, Customer Behavior, Online Retailing

## 1 Introduction

Artificial intelligence-based chatbots are computer programs that can interact with human users through natural language algorithms (Diederich et al., 2022). Since Facebook opened up its Messenger app for customized chatbots in 2016, over 300,000 online retailers have seized the opportunity to interact with their customers using chatbots (Pickell, 2018). Globally, 80% of online businesses have adopted chatbots (Business Insider, 2020) with the expectation of improving service efficiency (Gauri et al., 2021), accelerating sales processes (Johnston, 2020), promoting sales outcomes (Sands et al., 2021), and gaining an edge in the fierce digital economy competition. Unexpectedly, the sales conversion rate of chatbots for handling products is reported to be barely 10% of that of a human agent (Hwang & Kim, 2021; Schanke et al., 2021), and only 9.7% of online sales are made through chatbots (Hwang & Kim, 2021). Customers often feel less affiliated (Hohenstein et al., 2023) and even dislike communicating with chatbots (Rese et al., 2020), which serve as the initial contact point. Meanwhile, reports indicate that attracting customers during the initial stage of the shopping decision journey is crucial for driving customer purchases (McKinsey, 2017). Thus, to fully unlock the potential of chatbots, online retailers are in urgent need of understanding how to improve chatbot attraction and the sales conversion rate of chatbots.

Prior studies have mostly reported sufficient performance of chatbots such as usefulness, ease of use, and response success rate (Diederich et al., 2022; Seitz et al., 2022), which are contrary to the industry observations. A plausible explanation is that prior literature generally focuses on the efficiency of chatbots, and assumes that efficient chatbots will definitely have better service results (e.g., Chandra et al., 2022; Fotheringham & Wiles, 2022). However, chatbots’ technical efficiency is not equivalent to service effectiveness (Keh et al., 2006; Wilkinson et al., 2021). In an uncertain environment such as online shopping (Hong & Pavlou, 2014), an initial positive attitude towards chatbots is indispensable for positive behavioral outcomes of customers (McKinsey, 2017); thus, chatbot service effectiveness (i.e., sales conversion) can be achieved only when customers feel the chatbot is attractive. To date, the role of chatbot attraction in promoting chatbot service effectiveness has not been explored (Diederich et al., 2022). Therefore, it is necessary to explore how to increase the attraction of chatbots for customers and how this attraction can improve the effectiveness of chatbot services (i.e., customer purchase intention).

To address this issue, we build on interpersonal attraction theory (IAT) (Byrne, 1971), which explains the development of attraction. According to IAT, perceived attraction reflects the initial positive attitude toward the interaction partner (Montoya & Horton, 2014; Huston & Levinger, 1978). In the context of online service, anthropomorphic communication is one of the most recognized ways of enhancing customers’ positive attitudes towards chatbots (Blut et al., 2021), which refers to endowing the chatbot with humanlike qualities in customer-chatbot communication (Crolic et al., 2022). The process of anthropomorphism and social cognition are highly similar (Epley et al., 2007). Therefore, chatbot anthropomorphic communication traits will be evaluated by customers from the two fundamental dimensions of social cognition—namely, warmth and competence—which respectively refer to the chatbot’s positive intentions and capability to enact that intention expressed in the communication (Fiske et al., 2007; Cuddy et al., 2008; Li et al., 2019). Although IAT implies that socially desirable characteristics such as warmth and competence can promote perceived attraction (Carlos, 2004) and lead to positive behavioral intention (Byrne, 1971), the role of perceived attraction between the chatbot communication traits (i.e., warmth and competence) on customer purchase has not been studied. Hence, we seek an answer to:

Research Question 1: How does the warmth and competence of chatbot communication influence customer purchase via perceived attraction?

The literature on human-machine communication posits that the effectiveness of customer-chatbot communication is inevitably subjected to customers’ traits (Crolic et al., 2022; Rahwan et al., 2019; Roy & Naidoo, 2021). IAT also proposes that the consistency between communication parties in a particular trait is essential to the formation of attraction (Byrne, 1971). Thus, dyadic communication traits matter for the effectiveness of customer-chatbot communication. In line with the tenets of IAT, when a customer’s communication trait matches (vs. mismatch) with the chatbot’s communication trait, the customer will perceive greater attraction to the chatbot (Campbell et al., 2013). Given the potential contingent role of customer communication traits, it nevertheless remains unclear what role the customer communication trait plays in the effects of chatbot communication traits on perceived attraction. Hence, we ask:

Research Question 2: How do customer communication traits align with the warmth and competence of chatbot communication to influence perceived attraction?

To comprehensively understand how chatbot communication traits affect customer purchase intention, we propose a research model that considers perceived attraction as the underlying explanatory mechanism and customer communication traits as the boundary conditions. To examine the research model, we used a mixed methods design, including a tworound card sorting of 16 participants, a scenario-based survey of 112 respondents, and an online lab-in-thefield experiment of 162 validated responses. Our results show that perceived attraction mediates the positive effect of warmth and the competence of chatbot communication on purchase intention. Moreover, we found that the customer communication trait constrains the impact of chatbot warmth on perceived attraction, whereas the positive effect of competence remains unaffected.

The study makes three principal research contributions. First, we contribute to the research pipeline on chatbot performance (Diederich et al., 2022; Seitz et al., 2022) by shifting the research attention from technical efficiency to service effectiveness and focusing on the key threshold of customer’s initial positive attitude towards chatbots (i.e., chatbot attraction). Second, we reveal the mediating role of perceived attraction, which deepens the understanding of how to improve chatbot sales conversion and responds to the call for research exploring the influence mechanism of chatbot warmth and competence (Jin & Youn, 2021; Mozafari et al., 2022; Schanke et al., 2021). Lastly, we contribute to the research on customer-chatbot interaction (Jiang et al., 2022a; Yang & Shi, 2022; Youn & Jin, 2021) by investigating the contingent role of customers’ communication traits.

## 2 Theoretical Background

## 2.1 The Stereotype Content Model

The stereotype content model (SCM) posits that warmth and competence are two universal dimensions that people use to evaluate individuals or groups in social interaction (Fiske et al., 2007). Originally, perceived warmth and competence were developed to explain individuals’ evaluations of out-groups, which are general perceptions of a certain social group (Fiske et al., 2002). With the expansion of the SCM, scholars have also employed warmth and competence to portray the initial impression in interpersonal interactions (e.g., Cuddy et al., 2007; Halkias & Diamantopoulos, 2020). Literature on customer behavior has also applied warmth and competence as two dimensions of customer evaluation. For example, Aaker et al. (2012) proposed that customers rate brands on two dimensions—warmth and competence. Christoforakos et al. (2021) argued that assessments of robot warmth and competence can predict the affective and behavioral responses of customers. Studies such as Ahn et al. (2022) have claimed that customers evaluate AI algorithms (e.g., AI recommendation agents) based on the dimensions of warmth and competence. These studies have extended the application of the SCM which indicates that customers tend to judge chatbots in terms of warmth and competence when they communicate with chatbots.

In human-chatbot communication, chatbot warmth refers to the customer’s perceived intent of the chatbot manifested through “friendliness, helpfulness, sincerity, trustworthiness, and morality” (Fiske et al., 2007, p. 77). For instance, a chatbot could use smile emoticons to express its friendliness so that customers who communicate with it would infer its positive intention and perceive higher warmth (Li et al., 2019). The competence of chatbot communication is defined as the traits that reflect the customer’s perception of ability, such as “intelligence, skill, creativity, and efficacy” (Fiske et al., 2007, p. 77). For example, a chatbot providing external links demonstrates its ability to solve customers’ requests and thus could be perceived as more competent (Roy & Naidoo, 2021).

According to the SCM, individual perceptions of warmth and competence impact their affective and behavioral responses (Fiske et al., 2007). The prior literature on chatbot communication has shed light on the influence of warmth and competence on customers’ post-communication purchase intention. For instance, Ahn et al. (2022) found that the warmth or competence of chatbot communication does not significantly influence customer purchase intention, but the interactions of chatbot communication traits and product type affect customer purchase intent. In contrast, van Doorn et al. (2017) and Roy and Naidoo (2021) argued that both the warmth and competence of chatbots promote customer purchase intention. Moreover, Chen and Park (2021) and Kull et al. (2021) contended that chatbot warmth has a more substantial effect on customer purchase intention than competence because warmth perception tends to be more accessible, predictive, and heavily weighted in evaluation, giving it more potential to influence human responses. However, other scholars, such as Cheng et al. (2022) and Huang et al. (2021), have claimed a more salient influence of chatbot competence on customer purchase intention because customers who have transactional relationships with chatbots focus more on the professional performance of chatbots. The inconsistent findings of the above studies suggest that controversial arguments remain in the literature regarding whether chatbot warmth or competence significantly influences customer purchasing and, if so, the relative strengths of their influences.

Moreover, prior studies have mainly focused on customers’ psychological mechanisms to explain the impacts of communication traits in the form of warmth and competence on customers’ post-communication purchase intentions. For example, scholars have proposed trust (Kolbl et al., 2019; Xue et al., 2020), relational concern (Crisafulli et al., 2022), and customer interest (Singh et al., 2018) as the essential influencing mechanism of communication traits. However, since customer-chatbot interaction is occasional and temporary, long-term relationship indicators like trust and relational concern may not explain the effect of a chatbot’s communication traits. The human-computer interaction (HCI) literature highlights that the communication traits of both chatbots and customers affect their communication outcomes (Diederich et al., 2022). For instance, Li and Mao (2015) highlighted the influence of system-user communication trait similarity on the user’s perception of the virtual advisory service in the healthcare domain. Shen and Wang (2021) proposed that lexical alignment significantly improves participants’ evaluation of the interaction with computers. Also, Ruijten (2021) claimed that the interaction styles of both interactive systems and users affect the evaluation and persuasion outcomes. However, few studies have considered the dyadic communication traits of customer-chatbot interaction. Therefore, it is necessary to explore the influencing mechanism of chatbot communication traits on customer purchasing from the perspective of customer-chatbot interaction.

Taken together, as summarized in Appendix A, the previous studies on customer-chatbot communication have primarily revealed two research gaps. First, it is widely acknowledged that warmth and competence are two essential dimensions of chatbot communication traits, and it has been suggested that they have differential influences on customer response in the context of online shopping. However, the influencing mechanisms of chatbot warmth and chatbot competence on customer purchase intention remain underinvestigated. Second, social and task orientations have been highlighted as another two important chatbot communication traits, but few studies have probed into their match with warmth and competence from the perspective of customer-chatbot communication.

## 2.2 Interpersonal Attraction Theory (IAT)

IAT explains why and how people are attracted to one another (Byrne, 1971). Perceived attraction has been defined as an individual’s tendency to positively evaluate another person or a symbol of the person (Berscheid & Hatfield, 1969). According to IAT, perceived attraction explains why individuals initiate, develop, and maintain social relationships with other entities; further, it predicts individual attitudes and behaviors (Montoya & Horton, 2014). For instance, greater perceived attraction often signals more reliable and compelling sources (Zheng et al., 2020), Benbasat et al. (2020) verified that perceived attraction towards a recommender agent increases customers’ intention to accept the agent’s advice, and Sundar et al. (2017) showed that perceived attraction positively relates to senior citizens’ intention to use a robot.

Extending to the customer-chatbot interaction, Glikson and Woolley (2020) and Kull et al. (2021) proposed warmth and competence as key to customers’ initial evaluation of a chatbot, and perceived attraction can be understood as a product of these initial evaluations (Montoya & Horton, 2014). Moreover, IAT posits that perceived attraction affects relationship outcomes such as engagement (Montoya et al., 2018), retention (Zhou et al., 2012), and adoption of offerings (Park et al., 2013). Thus, IAT can be employed to understand how chatbot communication traits impact customer purchase intention through perceived attraction.

Furthermore, IAT highlights the rule of similarityattraction, which enriches our understanding of attraction formation. The similarity-attraction rule proposes that people are attracted to others who are similar (Dryer & Horowitz, 1997). Prior literature has shed light on the customer-chatbot similarity of identities (e.g., gender and ethnicity) (e.g., Benbasat et al., 2020; Go & Sundar, 2019) and personalities (e.g., extroverts and introverts) (e.g., Shumanov & Johnson, 2021; Salam et al., 2017). Although their communication traits synthesize the identities and personalities of chatbots (Ahn et al., 2022; Jin & Youn, 2021; Roy & Naidoo, 2021), there are inconsistent findings regarding the effect of customer-chatbot similarity on perceived attraction. Some scholars have found that similarity is more effective than dissimilarity in generating perceived attraction (Al-Natour et al., 2006, 2011; Go & Sundar, 2019). Others have reported that dissimilarity, rather than similarity, facilitates perceived attraction (Joosse et al., 2013; Lee et al., 2006). Moreover, instead of measuring perceived similarity, scholars have called for dyadic similarity measures (Al-Natour et al., 2011), which involve objective comparisons between the characteristics of two interaction partners. Dyadic similarity serves as an accurate reflection of the similarity that exists in reality because people may not be fully conscious of their similarity to others or the influences this similarity has on their behaviors (Al-Natour et al., 2006, 2011; Shumanov & Johnson, 2021).

The integration of the SCM and IAT provides a reasonable theoretical basis for the research model (see Figure 1). According to the SCM, we propose warmth and competence as chatbot communication traits that nurture perceived attraction to explain customer purchase intention in online services. Based on the similarity-attraction rule in IAT, we further propose that customer-chatbot similarity in communication traits plays a contingent role.

## 3 Hypothesis Development

## 3.1 Effect of Chatbot Communication Traits on Perceived Attraction

According to the SCM, individuals react psychologically and behaviorally to another entity depending on the perceived warmth and competence of this entity (Fiske et al., 2002). Two initial evaluations of the other entity—namely, perceived warmth and competence—help individuals form initial attitudes about the entity (Fiske et al., 2007). Perceived attraction is a positive reaction that reflects an interest in interacting more with another entity (Rodrigues et al., 2017). Thus, perceived attraction depicts customers’ initial attitudes towards the chatbot (Montoya & Horton, 2014), which is possibly affected by the chatbot’s warmth and competence. Prior literature has indicated that the warmth and competence of chatbot communication make customers more attracted to the communication. The warmth of chatbot communication demonstrates intimacy and provides a sense of proximity, which helps to initiate and maintain customer-chatbot interaction (Huston & Levinger, 1978). For instance, at the beginning of the conversation, a chatbot with the warmth communication trait will greet the customer cordially to show its passion for helping customers, arousing their interest in further communicating with the chatbot. The competence of chatbot communication indicates how effectively and responsibly the chatbot is able to assist customers with their shopping tasks, making it more likely that customers will ask the chatbot for help (Ahn et al., 2022). For example, a chatbot with competence may provide quick menus in conversations so that customers can place their requests quickly and accurately, which is valued and appreciated by customers, leading them to deem the chatbot an attractive shopping assistant. Therefore, this study proposes:

H1: The warmth (a) and competence (b) of chatbot communication are positively related to the perceived attraction between customers and the chatbot.

## 3.2 Effects of Perceived Attraction on Customer Purchase Intention

As pointed out by IAT, the subjective experience of attraction leads to a behavioral response (Montoya & Horton, 2020). Perceived attraction is a social lubricant that facilitates interpersonal communication (Romero & Cruthirds, 2006) and facilitates better communication quality (Fan & Ilk, 2020; Chen et al., 2008). Communication quality is a critical indicator for subsequent individual behavioral responses (Qahri-Saremi & Turel, 2020). Specifically, in customerchatbot communication, perceived attraction can increase communication success (Isbister & Nass, 2000), which means that customers’ requests will be effectively addressed, making them more likely to purchase the corresponding product. For instance, when customers are attracted to interact more with a chatbot, they will spend more time and effort in evaluating the product and retailer, increasing the probability of product purchase (Philipp-Muller et al., 2020; Gilbride et al., 2015). Moreover, customers who feel that the chatbot is attractive will be more likely to be persuaded by the chatbot (Liu et al., 2016) to make a purchase. Thus, we propose the following:

H2: Perceived attraction positively influences customer purchase intention.

## 3.3 Matching Effects of Communication Traits

IAT further implies that people are more attracted to one another when they match certain traits (Byrne, 1971). This implies that, in mutual interactions, when one party’s trait matches this of the other party to a greater extent, a stronger perception of attraction is more likely to be generated, and the positive effect of this trait on attraction will also be enhanced. In this regard, the communication traits of customers can amplify the influence of chatbot communication traits on perceived attraction by matching with the chatbot communication traits.

In customer-chatbot communication, the communication traits of chatbots are perceived by customers as warmth or competence. However, warmth and competence are dimensions that individuals use to evaluate others and typically do not apply to self-assessment (Fiske et al., 2002). The communication traits presented by customers themselves reflect how they approach communication and interact with others (Bales, 1958). In the context of online shopping, customers pay attention not only to the evaluation of chatbots but also to the way they communicate with the chatbot (Xu et al., 2022). Customers may have different degrees of goal orientation in their online purchase process, so their communication traits presented are heterogeneous in terms of task orientation (Kao et al., 2020). At the same time, customers may feel that a certain level of social interaction helps them get better help in their purchasing decisions, and thus exhibit different levels of socialoriented communication (Kao et al., 2020). Therefore, there are two dimensions of customer communication traits in chatbot communication: task-oriented and socialoriented. The social orientation of customer communication refers to a customer’s tendency to socialize in service conversations, while the task orientation of customer communication indicates the tendency to focus on the purchase task and to be highly goal oriented (Homburg et al., 2011).

Customers with high social orientation tend to socialize in their communication with chatbots (Kao et al., 2020). For instance, they often allow time for social talk and chitchat, not just for the primary objectives of the conversation (Nißen et al., 2022). Moreover, they pay close attention to relationships in service conversations, where emotional linkage plays a vital role. Therefore, they will perceive that they match with warm chatbots who provide more emotional support, and the positive influence of chatbot warmth on customers’ perceived attraction will be strengthened. Conversely, for customers with low social orientation, the social nature of the interaction is not important. Therefore, they will tend to feel that the warm chatbot is not a match with them, and the positive effect of chatbot warmth on perceived attraction will be weakened.

H3a: Compared to mismatch, the match between customer social orientation and chatbot warmth leads to stronger perceived attraction.

In contrast, the task orientation of customers’ communication reflects their aspiration for efficiency and the minimization of the “cost, effort, and time allocated to the interaction” (Verhagen et al., 2014, p. 534). This explicit goal orientation matches the competence of chatbot communication. For instance, customers with high task orientation prefer a service conversation that addresses their requests quickly and accurately, which can be accomplished by chatbots with communication traits of competence. Hence, customers with high task orientation will likely perceive that they match with a competent chatbot, and the positive influence of chatbot competence on perceived attraction will thus be amplified. In contrast, customers with low task orientation in communication will tend to feel that the competent chatbot does not match them; thus, the positive effect of chatbot competence on perceived attraction will be weakened.

H3b: Compared to mismatch, the match between customer task orientation and chatbot competence leads to stronger perceived attraction.

## 4 A Mixed Methods Approach

We employed a mixed methods technique, including a qualitative study and a quantitative study. Because previous research on chatbots has employed fragmented cues for chatbot trait design (Cheng et al., 2022; Li et al., 2019; Seiler & Schär, 2021), it is necessary preliminary work to systematically identify and summarize design cues of chatbot communication traits (Ahmad et al., 2022; Kuhail et al., 2022). Moreover, instead of merely browsing chatbot dialogue screenshots (e.g., Chung et al., 2020; Roy & Naidoo, 2021; Seeger et al., 2021), research involving communication with a real chatbot can help improve the validity of empirical findings (Tan et al., 2019).

Therefore, we conducted a qualitative study to develop instruments for chatbot communication traits, followed by the development of real chatbots. The research model was then evaluated in a quantitative study. Two pilot studies were conducted to ensure the manipulation effectiveness, followed by a lab-in-thefield experiment. The collected data was analyzed by analysis of variance (ANOVA) and response surface analysis.

Our research design strategy can be characterized as sequential and mixed methods; we conducted a lessdominated qualitative strategy followed by a dominant quantitative investigation. We summarize our studies in Table 1.

## 5 Qualitative Phase: Develop Experimental Chatbots

The first step in our work was to develop chatbots with different communication traits (i.e., warmth, competence, and none) so that in subsequent work, participants in our experiments could communicate with real chatbots and the feedback we collect from them would be more realistic.

The starting point was developing a systematic instrument of paralanguage cues in chatbot communication that can manifest warmth and competence. Prior research has employed different paralanguage cues to indicate the warmth or competence of chatbot communication. For instance, warmth can be indicated by emoticons (Li et al., 2019), intimacy (Cheng et al., 2022), or avatars (Seiler & Schär, 2021), while competence can be presented via language formality (Xu et al., 2022), message tones (Kull et al., 2021), or information richness (Roy & Naidoo, 2021). The heterogeneity in the manifestation of communication traits makes it difficult to compare the results of one study with those of another. Moreover, in real-world applications, multiple paralanguage cues often coexist. In this case, due to the lack of systematic research on paralanguage cues that can reflect warmth or competence, we are unclear about the validity of each cue. In addition, recent research has called for summarizing and categorizing these paralanguage cues from the perspective of design science (Ahmad et al., 2022; Kuhail et al., 2022).

Therefore, we conducted a semi-structured interview to collect existing paralanguage cues presenting chatbot warmth and competence traits, followed by a two-round card sorting to categorize these paralanguage cues. Chatbots with various communication traits were developed based on the systematic instrument. A manipulation test verified that our developed chatbots manifested significantly different communication traits in terms of warmth and competence.

## 5.1 Semi-Structured Interview

To identify paralanguage cues presenting chatbot communication warmth or competence in existing chatbot applications, we conducted semi-structured interviews with 23 chatbot users.

The interviewers verified that different levels of warmth and competence can be perceived in previous communication experiences with chatbots. Paralanguage cues such as intimate tones, emoticons, and greetings were mentioned, which can reflect the warmth of chatbot communication. The interviewers also mentioned that paralanguage cues such as the integrity of punctuation and length of messages were related to the competence of the chatbot communication.

## 5.2 Two Rounds of Card Sorting

Interviewees proposed various paralanguage cues that reflect chatbot communication traits; card sorting then verified that they could serve as stimuli to make people perceive the communication traits of chatbots. Card sorting is frequently used in the design of humancomputer interaction testing to understand the clustering of information from the users’ perspective (Zhang et al., 2022). It is a bottom-up, user-centered research method (Perdomo Delgado et al., 2022), revealing customers’ intuitive perceptions of paralanguage cues (Albert & Tullis, 2013) and thereby providing insights into how customers organize paralanguage cues when they communicate with chatbots (Petrie et al., 2011). Therefore, we employed the card-sorting technique to identify paralanguage cues that manifest warmth and competence.

Based on chatbot literature, industrial applications, and interview results, we initially generated 20 items for warmth and competence, which are listed in Appendix C. Eight graduate students from the information systems discipline who were unfamiliar with the purpose of this study were asked to sort those 20 items into three categories: chatbot warmth, chatbot competence, or neither. At the beginning of card sorting, they were given standardized instructions on the card-sorting procedure and definitions of chatbot warmth and chatbot competence. They were allowed to ask any questions until they fully understood the sorting procedure and categories. During the sorting procedure, the definitions and examples of each item were provided in detail, and the judges were asked to read them carefully and then sort the cards into corresponding dimensions. After the card sorting procedure, the judges were encouraged to provide additional feedback to improve the instrument items. We dropped four items after this round of card sorting because of their inadequate reliability. Then we conducted the second round of card sorting with the remaining 16 items. Eight graduate students, who were neither familiar with the study nor participants in the first round of card sorting, were invited to sort the 16 items, with the procedure being otherwise the same as the first round.

Two measurements, Cohen’s kappa and item placement ratio, were computed to assess the reliability of the sorting procedures and the content validity of the developed instrument. Cohen’s Kappa assesses the level of agreement between a pair of judges and is used to measure interrater reliability (Cohen, 1960), with an acceptable level being greater than 0.65 (Todd & Benbasat, 1991). We also calculated the item placement ratio (IPR), which indicates the overall frequency with which all judges placed the items into the intended dimensions (Moore & Benbasat, 1991), to assess the reliability of the classification scheme and the validity of the items. An instrument with a higher IPR indicates a higher degree of construct validity and reliability;

specifically, an IPR (and overall hit ratio) of 70% or greater is considered acceptable, and 80% (or greater) is deemed highly reliable (Moore & Benbasat, 1991). The results of the two rounds of card sorting are summarized in Tables 2 and 3.

Regarding the first round, the IPR value ranged from 13% to 100%, indicating a low degree of construct validity. To improve the reliability and validity of the developed instrument, we deleted four items with IPRs lower than 70%. In the second round of sorting, the overall hit ratio was 95%, and each item placement ratio was between 75% and 100%. After the deletion, the remaining items were sorted into the intended dimensions. The Table 3 results also show a decent degree of agreement between the judges in the secondround sorting, with Cohen’s kappas ranging from 0.69 to 1.00. These results indicate that the developed paralanguage instrument for warmth and competence of chatbot communication is valid and reliable.

## 6 Quantitative Phase: Test the Research Model

In the quantitative phase, we conducted an online labin-the-field experiment (Karahanna et al., 2018) to verify our theoretical grounds and test our hypotheses. As prior literature has called for experiments using live chatbots instead of static screenshots, we embedded the developed chatbots in Taobao.com, one of the biggest ecommerce platforms in China so that participants could communicate with those chatbots in a real shopping environment. Thus, in a field experiment, we leveraged the subjects’ real-world settings for the context to increase external validity and realism. The experiment was conducted via the Wenjuan.com platform since it provides the ability to deliver treatments remotely. Thus, we facilitated an online experiment, which helped expand the pool of subjects beyond local convenience samples to more representative samples so that external validity could be increased. In a laboratory experiment, we employed a between-subject design, where each participant was randomly assigned to the treatment group (chatbots with warm or competent communication traits) or the control group (chatbots without warm or competent communication traits).

## 6.1 Experimental Design

## 6.1.1 Manipulation of Chatbot Warmth and Chatbot Competence

Based on the developed instrument for warmth and competence of chatbot communication, we developed three customer service chatbots with different communication traits (none vs. warmth vs. competence). A chatbot with neither warmth nor competence was designed as the benchmark. It provided basic information to customers without any of the identified paralanguage cues. The chatbot with the warmth communication trait used paralanguage cues of warmth in its responses. The chatbot with the competence communication trait used paralanguage cues of competence in its conversation. In other words, in response to the same set of customer queries, the three chatbots with different communication traits responded to the same content using different sets of paralanguage cues.

## 6.1.2 Procedure

In the experiment, participants were presented with a Tshirt and instructed to ask the chatbot at least five questions about the T-shirt before making a purchase decision. Clothing was chosen as the test product category because fashion retailers actively apply chatbots to offer their customers a personal touch (Morozova, 2017).

## 6.1.3 Measures

The measurement scales of perceived warmth, perceived competence, and perceived informativeness were developed from previous literature (Fiske et al., 2002; Zhang et al., 2014), using a 7-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree. Demographic information was also collected at the end of the online survey.

## 6.1.4 Pilot Tests

We conducted two pilot studies to assess our experimental manipulation. The first pilot study (N = 112) aimed to confirm that our experimental manipulation effectively led to perceptual differences in chatbot warmth and competence. The second pilot study (N = 51) focused on fine-tuning our experimental task and validating the lay belief assumption implicit in the research model, i.e., individual perceptions of chatbot warmth and competence are important influencing factors in purchase decisions. Via two pilot studies, we ensured that the experimental chatbots could (1) respond consistently to participants’ specific questions in a set way, (2) be perceived in terms of warmth and competence, and (3) demonstrate distinct communication traits in dimensions of warmth and competence. We provide more details regarding the pilot studies in Appendix E.

## 6.2 Data Collection and Samples

Participants were recruited from universities in China. Before the experiment, participants completed a questionnaire revealing their demographic and other personal information. Then they were asked to purchase matching T-shirts for their classes, and the product page of a T-shirt was presented. They were asked to indicate their initial purchase intention and then guided to communicate with one of the three chatbots (either no trait, warm trait, or competent trait). At the beginning of each conversation, the chatbots disclosed their identity when greeting customers. To ensure their engagement with the experiment, participants were required to ask the chatbot at least five questions. A list of relevant questions was provided to the participant for their reference. Appendix F presents this question bank. After the conversation, all respondents completed a postexperiment questionnaire, where their purchase intention, perceptions towards the chatbot, and other variables were evaluated. All constructs were measured by reflective items adapted from prior literature, using a 7-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree. Appendix D presents the constructs and scales.

In total, 162 subjects participated in the experiment. We applied two criteria to exclude invalid samples that might not have received an adequate experimental stimulus. First, we excluded three participants who did not complete the experimental task, i.e., those who did not ask the chatbot the specified number of questions and those who failed the attention check, indicating they did not notice the paralanguage cues that reflected the chatbot’s communication trait. Second, we scrutinized video recordings of the experimental process and excluded two samples where participants encountered network issues and experienced significant delays in chatbot responses. Thus, 157 responses were valid. According to power analysis for between-subjects design via G-power (Faul et al., 2007, 2009), the sample size was sufficient to identify a medium effect (F = 0.25), achieving a desired power of 0.80 at an alpha level of 0.05 (Adam et al., 2024; Cummings & Dennis, 2018; Ye & Kankanhalli, 2018). Of the student subjects, 114 (72.6%) were female, and 43 (27.4%) were male. Most of them (78.3%) were between 18 and 25 years old, with the others being over 25 years old. Undergraduate students comprised 52.9% of the sample, and the rest were graduate students. Most participants (94.9%) reported shopping online for at least two years, and the majority (71.3%) had more than two years of experience using chatbots. There was no significant difference in gender, age, education, and prior experience distribution across the three conditions. In general, the subjects felt the experimental scenario was quite realistic (mean: 5.54/7), and no significant differences were found across the three conditions. ## 6.3.1 Manipulation Check

Manipulation checks ensured that the treatment conditions were successfully processed and interpreted by participants. We conducted an ANOVA in IBM SPSS Statistics 26 (George & Mallery, 2019) for the manipulation check. Specifically, the participants’ perceived warmth of the chatbot was significantly different $( F = 3 0 . 9 1 ; p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ between the control group $( M _ { \mathrm { { c o n t r o l } } } = 3 . 6 6 ; S D = 1 . 1 9 )$ and the treatment group $( M _ { \mathrm { w a r m t h } } ~ = ~ 5 . 1 3 ; ~ S D ~ = ~ 1 . 2 9 )$ . Likewise, participants perceived different levels of competence (F $= 5 . 7 8 4 ; p < 0 . 0 5 )$ between the control group $( M _ { \mathrm { c o n t r o l } } =$ $3 . 9 0 ; S D = 1 . 4 2 )$ and the treatment group $( M _ { \mathrm { c o m p e t e n c e } } =$ $4 . 6 7 ; \ S D \ = \ 1 . 4 5 )$ . We controlled for informativeness among groups, and the participants reported no difference in perceived informativeness among the three groups.

## 6.3.2 Reliability and Validity

We ran an exploratory factor analysis on our measurement variables—namely, perceived warmth, perceived competence, perceived attraction, purchase intention, social orientation, and task orientation. The high average variance extracted (AVE) and composite reliability (CR) scores of latent variables in Table 5 lend support to satisfactory internal consistency. The diagonal elements represent the AVEs of latent variables, while the off-diagonal elements are the correlations between latent variables. For adequate discriminant validity, the square root of the AVE of any latent variable should be greater than the correlation between a particular latent variable and other latent variables (Barclay et al., 1995). The data shown in Table 5 satisfy this requirement, supporting the discriminant validity.

Due to the correlation coefficient greater than 0.6, we examined the variance inflation factor (VIF) and the tolerance value of each construct to see if there is any evidence of multicollinearity in this study. The results show that the highest VIF value of all constructs is 4.37, which is below the recommended threshold value of 5 $( \mathrm { i . e . , \ V I F } < 5 ; $ Hair et al., 2011). We conclude that multicollinearity is not a major concern for this study. Together, these findings reveal that the validity and reliability of the measurement model are good.

## 6.3.3 The Effect of Chatbot Warmth and Chatbot Competence on Perceived Attraction

A one-way ANOVA was conducted to test the main effect. The results indicate that participants perceived higher attraction when the chatbot presented warmth in its communication $( M _ { \mathrm { w a r m t h } } = 4 . 1 1 , \ : S D = 0 . 8 9 ;$ $M _ { \mathrm { c o n t r o l } } = 3 . 5 5 , S D = 0 . 9 1 ; F = 1 0 . 2 1 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ Similarly, when the chatbot displayed competence in its communication, the participants perceived higher attraction as well $( M _ { \mathrm { { c o m p e t e n c e } } } = 4 . 1 9 , S D = 0 . 9 0 ; M _ { \mathrm { { c o n t r o l } } }$ $= 3 . 5 5 , S D = 0 . 9 1 ; F = 1 2 . 9 8 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . Hence, H1a and H1b are supported.

## 6.3.4 The Mediating Effect of Perceived Attraction

We followed the three steps outlined in Baron and Kenny (1986) for mediation testing (Campbell & Kirmani, 2000). In the first step, the results of ANOVA reveal that chatbot competence had a significant direct effect on purchase intention $( M _ { \mathrm { c o m p e t e n c e } } = 0 . 1 2 , S D =$ $0 . 8 8 ; M _ { \mathrm { c o n t r o l } } = - 0 . 3 5 , S D = 0 . 9 1 ; F = 4 . 6 0 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , but the direct effect of chatbot warmth on purchase intention was insignificant $( M _ { \mathrm { w a r m t h } } = - 0 . 1 6 , S D = 0 . 9 3 ; M _ { \mathrm { c o n t r o l } } = -$ $0 . 3 5 , \ S D \ = \ 0 . 9 1 ; \ F = 0 . 3 1 , \ p \ > \ 0 . 0 5 )$ . We already demonstrated that chatbot competence also had a significant positive effect on perceived attraction. In terms of the third step, with perceived attraction as a covariate, the direct effect of chatbot competence on purchase intention became insignificant $( F = 0 . 6 3 , p >$ 0.05), while perceived attraction was significant as a covariate $( F = 2 6 . 8 5 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ). Thus, H2 is supported, and perceived attraction mediated the effect of chatbot competence on purchase intention.

## 7 Discussion and Implications

## 7.1 Key Findings

This study examines the effect of chatbot communication traits (i.e., warmth and competence) on customer purchase intention in the context of online shopping. Our findings indicate that perceived attraction is the underlying mechanism, and customer-chatbot similarity of communication traits plays a contingent role in shaping perceived attraction. We discuss several key findings below.

First, our research reveals that the chatbot’s warmth and competence positively relate to perceived attraction (H1a and H1b are supported). According to the ANOVA analysis, a chatbot with warmth or competence communication traits is more attractive to customers than a chatbot without these communication traits. This finding confirms the existence of stereotype perceptions of chatbots, wherein customers are inclined to evaluate chatbots through the warmth and competence dimensions (Roy & Naidoo, 2021). This finding also aligns with recent studies (Glikson & Woolley, 2020; Chen & Park, 2021; Cheng et al., 2022) indicating that chatbot warmth and competence are fundamental communication traits that can shape customers’ initial attitudes (e.g., attraction) towards chatbots.

Second, consistent with H2, perceived attraction is positively related to customer purchase intention. These findings verify the view of Montoya and Horton (2020) that the experience of attraction facilitates individual behavioral responses to the attractive object. Our further analysis shows that perceived attraction mediates the influence of chatbot competence on customer purchase intention. These results are consistent with the view of IAT, where perceived attraction is an essential factor during the initial relationship development between customers and chatbots (Montoya et al., 2018; Montoya & Horton, 2014).

Third, the similarity between chatbot communication traits (i.e., warmth) and customer communication traits (i.e., social orientation) is positively related to perceived attraction (H3a is supported). Moreover, this study’s results show that chatbot warmth does not significantly affect customer purchase intention. This finding echoes the viewpoint that warmth is fundamentally lacking in chatbots (Gray et al., 2007), so customers do not have high expectations for chatbot warmth and pay little attention to the evaluation of chatbot warmth (Han et al., 2021). This finding also suggests that the chatbot’s warmth needs to match the social orientation of customer communication to heighten customers’ perceived attraction and purchase intention. Under the condition of customer-chatbot similarity, high levels of chatbot warmth and customer social orientation collectively improve perceived attraction.

Contrary to H3b, our findings show that the similarity between chatbot competence and customer task orientation does not significantly influence perceived attraction. One plausible explanation is that the participants’ conversations with chatbots were goaloriented, regardless of the extent of customer task orientation (Becker et al., 2020). This finding also echoes the findings of prior literature (Xue et al., 2020) showing that chatbot competence is essential to attract customers and the impact of chatbot competence on customers psychological responses is not bound to customers’ communication traits. We found that chatbot competence significantly impacts customer purchase intention. This indicates that, for customer-chatbot communication in online shopping, competence is a more relevant dimension when judging a chatbot and facilitating customer purchase intention. These findings are consistent with prior research showing that customers prioritize the assessment of competence when chatbots take on transactional tasks (Chen & Park, 2021; Khadpe et al., 2020) such as online shopping guidance. In this vein, our findings suggest that chatbot competence is necessary for all customers to be attracted to purchase.

## 7.2 Theoretical Implications

Our study makes three key theoretical contributions. First, this study contributes to the research pipeline on chatbot performance (Diederich et al., 2022; Seitz et al., 2022) by shifting the research attention from technical efficiency to service effectiveness. Specifically, we challenge the assumption underlying existing literature on chatbot performance that chatbot efficiency is equivalent to service outcome, which ignores the role of customers’ positive attitude towards chatbots in producing desired service outcomes. Therefore, we focused on customers’ initial positive attitude towards chatbots (i.e., chatbot attraction) and investigated the role of perceived attraction in evaluating a chatbot’s service effectiveness. Based on this problematization (Alvesson & Sandberg, 2011), the attention to the key threshold of perceived attraction allows for a more complete and in-depth understanding of chatbot performance.

Second, this study contributes to the chatbot literature applying the SCM (Fiske et al., 2002, 2007). Previous research has produced mixed results on how customers respond to chatbot warmth and competence (Ahn et al., 2022; Cheng et al., 2022; Huang et al., 2021; Kull et al., 2021; Roy & Naidoo, 2021), with many focusing on relational perspectives (De et al., 2020; Kull et al., 2021), which may be limited in the context of facilitating shopping goals (Kahn, 2005). Our study, however, aligns with the view that attraction is crucial for chatbots to boost sales conversion (McKinsey, 2017) and shows how human-machine attraction explains the impact of chatbot warmth and competence on customers’ purchase intention. In line with the SCM and prior literature on interpersonal interaction (e.g., Cuddy et al., 2007; Fiske et al., 2007; Strinić et al., 2020), our research indicates that chatbot warmth and competence can elicit social responses and that individuals have varying sensitivities to perceptions of warmth and competence. However, our findings suggest that individual judgments in chatbot communication tend to prioritize competence over warmth, which is the opposite in task-oriented interpersonal interactions (e.g., Lee & Huang, 2018; Strinić et al., 2020). A plausible reason is that warmth cues are more easily processed in human interactions (Fiske et al., 2007), whereas competence cues are more accessible in users’ evaluation of AI systems (McKee et al., 2023). Another potential reason is the widespread bias that chatbots are inherently lacking in warmth (Gray et al., 2007). This bias overshadows the assessment of warmth in humanchatbot interactions, highlighting the critical role of competence in such encounters. Our study provides a unique perspective by highlighting that the perception of warmth is influenced by the evaluator’s own characteristics, specifically the communication traits of customers. This distinguishes our research from much of the existing interpersonal research, which typically focuses on the attributes of the person being evaluated (Cuddy et al., 2007), such as the facial attractiveness of doctors (Zhang et al., 2024) and the gender of entrepreneurs (Davis & Kim, 2024; Lee & Huang, 2018). Thus, our findings emphasize that both the evaluator and the subject play a role in shaping these perceptions. In terms of the manipulation of warmth and competence, we advance the extant research by synthesizing multiple paralanguage cues instead of a single anthropomorphic cue (e.g., Jin & Youn, 2021; Li et al., 2019). This helps amplify the effect of chatbot anthropomorphic design compared to studies using a single cue. Overall, by examining warmth and competence through paralanguage in customer-chatbot interactions, this study provides a theoretical foundation for future SCM research in task-oriented HCI contexts.

Lastly, by exploring the role of perceived attraction, we enrich the research on customer-chatbot interaction (Jiang et al., 2022a; Yang & Shi, 2022; Youn & Jin, 2021) and contribute to IAT (Huston & Levinger, 1978; Montoya & Horton, 2020). Prior research has primarily focused on chatbot traits and customer traits separately (Hwang & Won, 2021; Roy & Naidoo, 2021; Xu et al., 2022), ignoring the match between the two types. Our study builds on and enriches customer-chatbot interaction research by investigating the match of communication traits between customers and chatbots with customer purchase intention as the outcome. Our work contextualizes customers’ attraction towards chatbots and validates the similarity-attraction principle of IAT within the social dimensions (i.e., warmth and social orientation) of customer-chatbot interaction. The mediation and moderation mechanisms we identified both validate the role of attraction in linking communication style to purchase intention, marking an early exploration of attraction in chatbot-human interactions and contributing to the understanding of customers’ psychological mechanism of willingness to transact with chatbots (Diederich et al., 2022). While IAT highlights the essential role of nonverbal cues for attraction (Montoya et al., 2018), AI interactions lack these, altering perception dynamics. Our study emphasizes the impacts of verbal cues on attraction within human-computer interaction, broadening our grasp of attraction’s antecedents, contextualizing attraction in customer-chatbot interaction, and extending IAT. We also examine how chatbot communication styles differently affect attraction depending on their alignment with customer communication traits, finding that similarity in warmth, not competence, influences perceived attraction. This challenges the basic assumption of IAT (Byrne, 1971) and suggests a more nuanced role of similarity in HCI.

## 7.3 Practical Implications

This work also provides guidance for practitioners in several regards. First, to improve the sales conversion rate of chatbot services, online retailers are advised to attach communication traits to their chatbots. Specifically, competence is an effective communication trait of chatbots in facilitating customer purchase intention, whereas chatbot warmth can promote customer purchase intention by attracting customer attention. Our effective manipulation in experiments also provides clues for online retailers on how to embody chatbots with communication traits. Specifically, to imbue chatbot conversations with warmth, retailers can employ intimate tones (e.g., “darling”, “sweetheart”), or emoticons (e.g., “ ”) in chatbot conversations. To endow chatbots with the competence communication trait, online retailers can design chatbots to use professional tones (e.g., being thorough and direct, formal wording, and grammatical correctness), utilize dialogue navigation (e.g., structured menus and shortcuts), and provide extra information to customers (e.g., laundry instruments for clothing products).

Second, we recommend that online retailers seek to increase their customers’ perceived attraction to chatbot communication. Conversations between customers and chatbots are usually short and tentative. Customers make further behavioral decisions only when attraction is perceived. Our research shows that one of the most effective ways to increase the attraction of chatbots in communication is to endow chatbots with competence communication traits because chatbot competence is deemed attractive to the vast majority of customers. Thus, we suggest that online retailers present chatbot competence as much as possible and also encourage them to explore other ways to enhance chatbot attraction.

We also recommend that chatbot developers design adaptive chatbots. The results of our research indicate the importance of matching the warmth of chatbot communication with the customer’s social orientation. Therefore, rather than focusing on a one-size-fits-all approach, designers should instead pursue a dynamic style. When customers show a strong social orientation in communication, the chatbot should correspondingly show strong warmth in communication traits. On the contrary, when customers show that they do not want to interact socially in communication with the chatbot, the chatbot’s warmth communication trait should be weakened as much as possible. With the development of generative AI, it has become more feasible to give chatbots dynamic communication traits. Through prompts, chatbots can be customized to exhibit different communication traits and to match the communication characteristics of customers in real time based on the customer’s social orientation.

## 7.4 Limitations and Future Research

This study has several limitations that deserve further investigation. First, to extend the understanding of the impact of chatbot communication traits, other scholars could consider factors from other parties. For example, future studies could explore whether the traits of customer purchase goals (e.g., product type, ex ante uncertainty, and product involvement) moderate the influence of chatbot communication traits on customer purchase decisions. The characteristics of online shopping platforms (e.g., platform ease-of-use, interactivity, and privacy concern) may also have an impact, and future research should consider them. While our study suggests that perceptions of chatbots may differ from those of humans, these differences have not been empirically confirmed. Future studies could explore and compare the distinct effects of human and machine interactions.

Second, further research could explore the hybrid communication trait design where warmth and competence coexist in chatbot communication. In other contexts (such as brand personality), an entity with both warmth and competence achieves the “golden quadrant” (Aaker et al., 2012). However, prior literature on chatbots indicates that the warmth of chatbots may dampen perceived competence (Li et al., 2019). Hence, it remains unknown whether hybrid communication traits can surpass warmth-only or competence-only communication trains in terms of chatbot effectiveness. Moreover, investigating hybrid designs would help deepen scholarly understanding of the SCM in humanmachine communication.

Third, although our research focuses on the e-commerce context, service chatbots are also widely used in other industries, such as education, medical care, and tourism services. Exploring the influence of chatbot communication traits on user decision-making in other contexts will also help deepen the understanding of chatbot communication traits and guide more general audiences on how to design effective chatbots.

Finally, this study relies on the existing technical level of chatbots in e-commerce services, i.e., rule-based AI. With the development and popularization of generative AI, its application in e-commerce services is just around the corner. Considering the convenience of using prompts to customize chatbots, scholars, and practitioners could explore how to use prompts to give chatbots a communication trait. In addition, future research could compare the differences in the impact of chatbot communication traits with different intelligence levels, which would also help expand our understanding of the impact of algorithmic behaviors.

## 7.5 Conclusion

The application of chatbots in online services will likely grow unabated in the years ahead. While there are advantages to using chatbots, such usage also centers attention on the issue of poor conversion rates. In evaluating how to improve customer purchase intention after communication with chatbots, this study investigates the role of two chatbot communication traits in facilitating customer purchase intention: chatbot warmth and chatbot competence. By applying the SCM and IAT to customer-chatbot communication, we argue that perceived attraction, which increases customer purchase intention, is activated by the communication traits of the chatbot and by the customer-chatbot similarity of communication traits. Specifically, chatbot competence can effectively promote customer purchase intention, and this effect is mediated by perceived attraction. Our findings show that chatbot warmth needs to match the social orientation of customers so that perceived attraction can be promoted, which, in turn, will enhance the customers’ willingness to buy. The deep investigation of chatbot communication traits facilitates the understanding of the behavior of AI chatbot systems, which is essential for practitioners to harness the advantages of and mitigate the harms of AI chatbot systems. We hope this paper will motivate further research to explore various types of AI chatbot behavior and uncover more critical nuances of the formation and influences of AI chatbot behavior.

## Appendix B: Interview Details

Interviewer recruitment: We interviewed chatbot users, each of whom had at least two years of experience using chatbots. Specifically, when recruiting, we asked applicants to detail the platform on which they have used chatbots, what types of questions they have asked, and in which year they started using them. We approached informants using a snowballing technique a week in advance before interviews. We asked our recruited interviewees to invite anyone familiar with chatbot recommendations (the invitation needed to be extended before the interview in order to avoid private discussion and possible interactions among informants). The interviews were conducted until theoretical saturation was reached, continuing until the interviews no longer generated additional information (Lincoln and Guba 1985). The 20th interviewee did not provide any new information, indicating that theoretical saturation had been reached. However, following the suggestion of Riedel et al (2018), we conducted three additional interviews to make sure we achieved theoretical saturation.

Data collection: In order to ensure consistency between interview questions and research questions, we carefully prepared a list of interview questions before conducting interviews. First, we ensured that the questions did not reveal the research intent. Second, we discussed with relevant scholars to ensure that the interview questions matched the research questions. Finally, we conducted a pre-test to check the interview questions and encouraged the respondents to make necessary modifications to the questioning method and content in order to ensure that the questions were easy to understand. Despite the guidelines providing some structure, we conducted the interviews in a semi-structured manner, allowing the informants to freely express their opinions on certain predetermined themes, while also allowing them to talk about other subjects not covered by the guidelines. The informants were first invited to describe their recent experience of chatbot recommendations and then followed the guidance of the interviewer to share their opinions regarding this experience. Indepth interviews were conducted via online meeting software so that we could avoid the space-time restriction and reach out to more representative samples. Interviews were recorded with the interviewee’s permission and transcribed. All interviews were conducted by the same interviewers to ensure consistency, and each lasted approximately 30 minutes.

The paralanguage cues from the interviews were sorted by two rounds of cards, eliminating controversial options; we retained 16 cues that reflected the chatbot’s communication trait and used them to manipulate the chatbot's communication trait. Chatbots with different communication traits developed based on this instrument were used in the pilot studies and formal experiments as experimental stimuli.

## Appendix E: Pilot Studies

Online surveys using a vignette technique (Atzmüller & Steiner, 2010) were utilized for pilot tests, which is a widely applied method in previous chatbot research (Mozafari et al., 2022; Roy & Naidoo, 2021; Seeger et al., 2021). Respondents were recruited through Wenjuan.com, an online participant recruitment platform in China that has functions similar to Amazon.com’s Mechanical Turk. The platform has been widely employed by scholars (Wang & Sun, 2016; Xu et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2016) since it has a broad user base of more than 20 million users and demographically diverse samples. In addition, the responses from Wenjuan.com are anonymous, which helps to prevent social desirability bias (Kwak et al., 2019).

In the first pilot test, the responses of the three chatbots to the same set of questions were screenshotted as three vignettes and embedded in the online survey. Respondents of the survey saw one of the three screenshots and were asked to read the presented dialogue carefully. Subsequently, respondents were asked to rate the chatbot concerning perceived warmth and perceived competence. Moreover, respondents were asked to rate perceived informativeness to test the homogeneity of conversation content. A total of 112 respondents participated in the survey and they were evenly assigned to groups regarding their demographic characteristics. Our one-way ANOVA test results show that a warm chatbot was perceived to be warmer than the chatbot with no communication traits (Means of 5.49 vs. 4.08, p < 0.001). Similarly, the competent chatbot was perceived as more competent (Means of 5.47 vs. 4.92, $p < 0 . 0 1 \AA ,$ ). The stimuli did not differ significantly on perceptions of informativeness (warmth group vs. control group: Means of 5.48 vs. 5.54, $p = 0 . 9 9 7 ;$ competence group vs. control group means of 5.62 vs. 5.54, $p = 0 . 9 9 0 )$ . Thus, we confirmed that our manipulative design of chatbot warmth and competence was effective.

In the second pilot test, respondents were first given the experimental task of imagining that they needed to buy a Tshirt, going through the product page of a T-shirt, and indicating their initial purchase intention. Then they read a textbased chat which displayed a conversation between a customer and a chatbot with paralinguistic cues of both warmth and competence. Then, the respondents were asked to rate their perceived warmth and competence and indicate their purchase intention again. They also evaluated the experimental task regarding its realism and were allowed to provide feedback, which helped us finetune the experimental task. A total of 51 participants completed the online survey. The results of the descriptive statistics showed that respondents felt the task was very real (mean of perceived realism = $5 . 5 1 > 4 . 0 0 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ; therefore, we used it in the following formal experiment. Moreover, respondents perceived a strong warmth (mean $= 5 . 2 2 > 4 . 0 0 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ) and competence in chatbot communication $( \mathrm { m e a n } = 4 . 8 0 > 4 . 0 0 , p <$ 0.001). The regression results indicate that perceived competence significantly affected purchase intention $( \beta = 0 . 5 5$ $p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , while perceived warmth had no significant impact $( \beta = - 0 . 1 1 , p > 0 . 0 5 )$ . We conclude that customers perceived chatbot communication in terms of warmth and competence, shaping their purchase intention. This also confirms the necessity of exploring whether chatbots, respectively with warmth or competence, have different effects on customer purchase intention, influence mechanisms, and boundary conditions.

## Appendix G: Design of the Experimental Chatbots

## The Development Environment for Experimental Chatbots

The customer service chatbots used in the experiment are based on Alibaba's Alimebot.<sup>1</sup> Alibaba is the largest ecommerce company in China, and it provides Alimebot, an open API platform that allows for the online customization of chatbots, for its retailers. Alimebot has consistently held the top position in China's conversational AI cloud servic market share since 2019 (International Data Corporation, 2024), establishing itself as the most prevalent and representative chatbot in the Chinese e-commerce market (iResearch Inc., 2022). To provide participants with the most authentic and natural experimental environment, we designed experimental chatbots using Alimebot as well.

One of the core features of Alimebot is “keyword-response” mapping, allowing online retailers as developers to configure the chatbot's response strategy on their own. As shown in Figure G1, a developer can set multiple sets of “keyword-response”, and when a customer's question contains the keywords, the chatbot will send the preset response. Alimebot can work independently once the “keyword-response” mappings are settled. Alimebot provides a testing tool, allowing chatbot developers to test the “keyword-response” mapping, which ensures that the chatbot can accurately respond to specific user inquiries. Before the experiment, we tested all sets of “keyword-response” for all experimental chatbots and made sure that each chatbot could execute the “keyword-response” as expected.

## Manipulation of Experimental Chatbots.

Table G1 summarizes the paralanguage cues that we used for manipulation. We selected those paralanguage cues from the pool outlined in Appendix C for the following reasons: (1) It is ensured that the linguistic elements manipulated for warmth/competence are symmetrical, such as the manipulation of tone and the incorporation of nonverbal symbolic elements (i.e., emoticons and cue symbols). (2) The interaction messages are the key part thatreflects communication traits. Hence, there was no differentiation manipulation in chatbot appearance (e.g., avatars, names), but rather uniformity was maintained within each group. 3) Only linguistic cues with a consensus rate of 100% were selected to minimize possible ambiguity in experimental manipulations.

Table G2 shows the manipulation of chatbot greeting messages. Based on the greeting message of chatbot of the control group, chatbot of the warmth group uses emoticons (e.g., a rose), intimate title (e.g., “sweetheart”, “darling”), and tone (e.g., “\~”) in its greeting message. The chatbot of competence group utilizes a professional tone (e.g., polite, professional, and well-structured) in its greeting.

An example of manipulation of chatbot responses is provided in Table G2. When customers inquire about product texture, the benchmark chatbot in the control group directly provides basic information; the chatbot with warmth responds in an intimate tone using emoticons (e.g., smiling), tone symbols (e.g., “\~”), and affectionate titles (e.g., “Dear”), whereas the response of the chatbot with competence adopts a professional tone (e.g., Formal terminologies, and structured sentence) and uses more salient cues to acquire details (e.g., an icon of a magnifying glass that, when clicked, reveals intricate details).

## The Operating Environment of the Experimental Chatbots

We launched three Taobao stores (all using the default store logo, no significant difference in store names, and presenting identical products), each store enabled Alimebot service with customized “keyword-response” mapping. The questions that customers are likely to ask the chatbots were defined by related keywords, which were consistent across the three chatbots. The customized responses reflected different communication traits of chatbots using paralinguistic cues.

We required all participants to carry out the experimental tasks on Taobao mobile app for several reasons: (1) Taobao mobile app represents the most common channel for customer-chatbot interactions, (2) the design and testing phases of the experimental chatbots were tailored specifically for the Taobao mobile app, and (3) within the e-commerce platforms serviced by Alimebot (e.g., Taobao, Tmall, Xianyu and 1688), Taobao app boasts the largest user base (Alibaba Inc., 2024).

At the initiation of the experiment, participants received a simulated task and a product link, which directed them to the product page on the Taobao mobile app, as illustrated in Figure G2a. Within the product page, participants could access the chatbot communication interface by clicking on the chatbot symbol located in the bottom left corner. The initial interface when participants entered the chatbot service was as depicted in Figure G2b. The interface featured a welcoming message from the chatbot, buttons for returning to the product page or viewing the store page, and an empty chat window. Participants could initiate dialogue by clicking to send a product link or by directly inputting queries they wished to inquire about. The interactions between participants and the chatbot were recorded throughout the communication process. Partial screenshots of experimental chatbots are demonstrated in Figures G3a, G3b, and G3c.
