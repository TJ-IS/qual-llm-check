# The cinematic mode of organizing: Media and the problem of attention in organization theory

Alexander Styhre

Department of Technology Management and Economics, Division of Project Management, Chalmers University of Technology. Vera Sandbergs Allé 8. SE-412 96 Gothenburg. Sweder

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 30 October 2007 Received in revised form 18 January 2008 Accepted 1 June 2008

Keywords: Media Perception The cinematic mode of production

## a b s t r a c t

In the first half of the nineteenth century, the concept of perception was reformulated and was gradually conceived of as a contingent and subjective capacity rather than what is impersonal and therefore essentially unproblematic. In this new perspective, the concept of attention also became a source of interest for both researchers and management writers. Theories of perception and attention are closely associated with the use of media. While media have always been used in organizing, more recent digital media are producing new forms of communication and control in organization. Following Beller’s (2006) idea of an emerging ‘‘cinematic mode of production” characterized by the reliance on digital media and the visual labour of spectators as a value-adding process, the implication for organization are examined in such a perceptual regime of accumulation. The paper concludes by calling for a more detailed analysis of how media are structuring organizations in the present regime.

## 1. Introduction

Organization is heavily determined by the use of various forms of media, that is, techniques and practices storing and circulating data and information. Beginning in the nineteenth century, the growing American railway companies established procedures for distributing knowledge and information, and media technologies such as typewriters and new filing systems produced new forms of communication and control in organizations (Yates, 1989). Today, such analog media<sup>1</sup> is gradually re placed by digital media (Manovich, 2001) operating on basis of what Flusser (2002) calls ‘‘surface codes” and hypertexts rather than the ‘‘linear codes” of writing predominating in analog media. This shift from analog media embedded in writing to digital media-based on the integration of various perceptual resources such as sounds and images is a major event pertaining to theories of organization. Rather than being a smooth and silent transformation from one medium to another, the shift to digital media has significant implications for how organizations are structured and managed. What is underlying to the new media is a new emphasis on sense-impressions embedded in a new regime of per ception. While perception has always been central to organizing (Belova, 2006, p. 94), in the new media-based forms of organizing new forms of perception are emerging and thus opening up for new managerial practices. For instance, while organization routines and standard operating procedures have traditionally been circulated through written documents or verbal instructions, today dig ital media can provide new training opportunities though integrating texts, sound, images, or even tactile impressions—so-called ‘‘haptic sense-impression” in the case of virtual reality settings (Prentice, 2005; Steuer, 1992)—in media. This paper examines these recent changes in the use of media and discusses what Beller (2006) calls the ‘‘cinematic mode of production”, the an emerging regime of accumulation in the contemporary society, based on ‘‘attention work”. The concept of ‘‘cinematic” (rather than, say, ‘‘digital”) is chosen by Beller (2006) because he regards cinema as the underlying and unifying medium to a variety of contemporary analog and digital media. Cinema’s ability to combine, sight, sound, and movement represent one of the most important changes in media since the birth of print in the fifteenth century (that is, in Europe; the Chinese master book printing five hundred years before Gutenberg. Febvre & Martin, 1997, p. 71) and the invention of the photograph in the first half of the nineteenth century (Manovich (2001, p. 132).

At the bottom of these organizational changes lie the new theories of perception and attention developed in the first half of the 19th century, in the very beginning of the modern time period. Before what Beller (2006) calls ‘‘the attention theory of value” and what other writers have referred to as the ‘‘attention economy” (Davenport & Beck, 2001; Lanham, 2006) could be formulated, a significant reformulation and rearticulation of the concepts of perception and attention were conducted.

This paper is structured accordingly: first, the concepts of perception and visuality are addressed as a historically contingent concept underlying to a series of social and organizational changes. Second, the idea of an ‘‘attention economy” and the concept of media are examined. Third, Beller’s (2006) the ory of the cinematic mode of production is discussed and thereafter, some implications for organization theory are discussed.

## 2. Visuality and attention in organizations in late modernity

In the period 1820–1840, Crary (1990) argues in his seminal work on the history of modern theories of vision, ocular perception was reconceptualized from being modeled on the camera obscura, the predominant metaphor for vision from the middle ages and onwards, to being a contingent, subjective, and contextual capacity of the individual. These changes derived from both new philosophical think ing and in new scientific procedures, theoretical frameworks and technological development. For in stance, in his The world as will and representation, Arthur Schopenhauer was one of the first to conceive of perception as an individual and essentially trained capacity, suggesting that ‘‘perception is not merely of the senses, but is intellectual” (Schopenhauer, 1995, p. 9). Perception is then not only what occurs naturally but what is actively produced through bridging individual perceptual and cognitive capacities. The major change of perspective in the theories of perception was however brought by the clinical sciences and more specifically by the scientific work of von Helmholtz whose magnum opus, the three-volume Physiological Optics (first published in 1859–1866), contributed to a new scientific view of perception. Here, von Helmholtz (2004, p. 43) is rendering perception a cognitive phenomenon and explicitly states that ‘‘[t]he theory of perception belongs properly to the domain of psychology”. Human perception is then no longer what is unproblematic but become what is first contingent and individual, and therefore, as a corollary, capable of being trained and actively influenced. Also technological development contributed to new forms of visuality. Schivelbusch (1986, p. 62) argues that the railroad experience brought a new outlook of the world which he names ‘‘panoramic vision”; ‘‘[T]he railroad first and foremost, is the main cause for [the] panoramization of the world”, Schivelbusch (1986, p. 62) says. This growing interest for ‘‘panoramic visuality” was satisfied by the building of a number of dioramas and panoramas in Paris in the 1840s. Such new theories ad practices of perception were gradually influencing common sense thinking and, more interestingly, the emerging management literature formulated from the end of the first half of the nineteenth century. The nineteenth century was also a period of quick and significant urbanization (Gay, 1984, pp. 49–50), and eventually German theorists of the Weimer epoch in the 1920s such as Simmel and Kracauer theorized on how individuals could cope with the abundance of sense-impressions in such new, densely populated environments. The most widely known theory on these coping mechanisms is Simme (1971) idea of the blasé attitude characterizing the metropolitan dweller. Rather than being able to attend to < and accommodate the manifold of sense-impression, the urban individual is highly selective in terms of what sense-impression to respond to. Being blasé is therefore to remain uninterested and emotionally detached from all the turmoil around the individual. What Simmel suggests is that the individual is capable of regulating what he or she is attending to; it is a trained capacity that is potentially subject to external influences and manipulations. In a similar manner, James explored the concept of attention in his widely acclaimed Principles of psychology (first published in 1890). Here, attention is again what is subjective and what is of necessity highly selective: ‘‘My experience is what I agree to attend to. Only those items which I notice shape my mind—without selective interest, experience is an utter chaos. Interest alone gives accent and emphasis, light and shade, background and foreground, in a word”, James (1890/1950, p. 402. Emphasis in the original) writes. For James, the value of attention derives from five sources, the ability to perceive, conceive, distinguish, remember, and ‘‘shorten ‘reaction-time’” (James, 1890/1950, p. 424). Using the expression ‘‘stream of thought” rather than the more conventional concept of thinking, James also underlined that attention is most of the time ‘‘effortless”; making individual pay attention to events or entities that they are not naturally attending to demands a certain price, an investment in effort on part of the perceiving subject. ‘‘The stream of thought is like a river. On the whole easy simple flowing predominates in it, the drift o things is with the pull of gravity, and effortless attention is the rule”, James (1890/1950, p. 451) concludes.

If there is such a ‘‘political economy of attention” as suggested by James (1890/1950), then individual attention may have a certain value to be exploited. In the merging management literature, such a view of attention was highly appealing. In 1913, the pioneering industry psychologist Hugo Münsterberg published his work Psychology and industrial efficiency making the ‘‘problem of attention”—previously discussed in detail by Frederick W. Taylor as a major challenge for industrial effectiveness—a central concern for the managerial sciences and day-to-day practices in organizations and companies. Concerned not to patronize workers or to draw on predefined morals regarding work ethics, Münsterberg claims that it is not a matter of identifying ‘‘good” or ‘‘bad” attention but ‘‘different” forms of attention that needs to be carefully evaluated and managed: ‘‘Two workingmen, not only equally industrious and capable, but also equally attentative, may yet occupy two positions in which they are both complete failures because their attention does not fit the places, and both may become highly efficient as soon as they exchange positions. Their particular types of attention have now found the right places”, Münsterberg (1913, p. 136) exemplifies. It is also the role of the industry psychologist to provide methods and measures for evaluating what capacities of attention which are suitable for different work assignments. Münsterberg (1913) is also emphasizing that ‘‘avoiding distractions” is of central importance when improving effectiveness. For instance, a woman working in a printing shop with a task demanding her ‘‘fullest attention” increased her productivity by 25% when being moved to a ‘‘quiet corner” in the facilities (Münsterberg, 1913, p. 210).

In summary, the new theories of perception and vision formulated in the first half of the nineteenth century located the concept of attention at the centre of interest for both scientific research and managerial practice. The wave of management literature published in the last decades of the nineteenth century and in the first decades of the new century (for an overview, see Guillén, 1994; Wren, 1972) addressed the concept of attention and its influence on organizational effectiveness. The aggre gate perception/attention constituted one of the major concerns for equally researchers and practicing managers.

## 3. The attention economy and the concept of media

The human capacity of attention has been of central interest for a long range of organization the ories. For instance, the behavioural theory of the firm and theories of decision-making formulated by March and a number of colleagues (Cohen, March, & Olsen, 1972; Cyert & March, 1963; March & Olsen, 1976) strongly emphasize the contingent and selective nature of human attention. More recently, organization researcher such as Ocasio (1997), Weick and Sutcliffe (2006) and Cho and Hambrick (2006) have examined attention as a mechanism of central importance for organizational perfor mance. In addition. a number of writers have leveraged the concept of attention into being the simple most important resource in the contemporary economy. For instance, Lanham (2006) argues that the so-called knowledge economy (Powell & Snellman, 2004) is characterized by an abundance of infor mation but a ‘‘short supply” of attention. In the ‘‘attention economy”, it is, Lanham (2006) argues, more ‘‘fluff than stuff” that matters, that is, material commodities are increasingly complemented by intangible services residing in digital media. As a consequence, the principal ‘‘device” regulating attention is ‘‘style”: ‘‘Attracting attention is what style is about. If attention is now at the centre of the economy rather than stuff, then so is style. It moves from the periphery to the center. Style and substance trade places”, Lanham (2006, pp. xi–xii) suggests. Other scholars such as the management researchers Davenport and Beck (2001) argue, consistently with Lanham (2006), that the contemporary economy is in essence drawing on information as its principal ‘‘production factor”. For instance, Davenport and Beck (2001) argue, one single edition of Sunday New York Times contains more factual information than all written material available to a reader in the fifteenth century; in 1472, ‘‘the best university library in the world. at Oueen's College in Cambridge, hosted 199 books". Davenport and Beck (2001, p. 4) note.

While both Lanham (2006) and Davenport and Beck (2001) are enthusiastic about the opportuni ties of the emerging attention economy and are not very concerned about the more cumbersome consequences from the media-based, ‘‘fluff-generating” regime of production, there are other authors developing a more complex view of the recent developments. Beller’s intriguing book The cinematic mode of production (2006) is a critical account of the contemporary economy emphasizing ‘‘cinematic media" as the predominant new media in society. Prior to a more detailed account of Beller's argument, the concept of media deserves some examination. In the social science literature, the concept of media is discussed under the label ‘‘media theory” by a range of scholars including seminal texts by McLuhan (1962) and McLuhan (1964) and more recently work by for instance Bolter, Hayles, and Kittler. Media are broadly defined by Krämer (2006, p. 93) as ‘‘cultural techniques that allow one to select, store, and produce data and signals”. A more detailed but complex definition is provided by Gitelman (2006, p. 7), saving that media “are socially realized structures of communication, where structures include both technological forms and their associated protocols, and where communication is a cultural practice, a ritualized collocation of different people on the same mental map, sharing or engaging with popular ontologies of representation”. Both Krämer’s (2006) and Gitelman’s (2006) definitions include all kind of media, both analog (e.g., pen and paper) and digital (e.g., computers). While analog media continue to play a very central role in society and in organizing, media theorists are often engaging in analyses of how digital media are shaping social life in various ways. Manovich (2001) is offering a comprehensive overview of the field and is articulating a formal theory of digital media. Perhaps the single most important characteristic of digital media is its ability to operate on basis of what Flusser (2002) calls ‘‘surface codes”, that is, images, sounds and other mediated sense-impres sions that are not, unlike written texts, based on linearity. When reading a text, Flusser (2002) argues the meaning of the sentence or passage unfolds en route, when each syllabus is attaining its meaning in a system of representation embedded in what de Saussure (1959) called syntagmatic and associativ relations. When looking at for instance an image (e.g., a photograph, an Internet home page) based on surface codes the whole ‘‘message” is already there, at the first glance. However, this message has t be decoded or decomposed to be properly understood: ‘‘We must follow the written text if we want to get at its message, but in picture we may get at the message first, and then try to decompose it. And this points to the difference between the one-dimensional line and the two-dimensional surface: th one aims at getting somewhere; the other is there already, but may reveal how it got there. This dif ference is one of temporality, and involves the present, the past, and the future”, Flusser (2002, p. 22) writes. The surface code is therefore deceiving because it is always immediately present, yet it is not of necessity easily decoded. For instance. both Flusser (1993/2000) and Sontag (1973)—and much earlier in the 1920s, Kracauer (1995)—are conceiving of the photography as a medium that is unable to ex plain itself, and thus, in their understanding, expressions like ‘‘a photography is saying more than a thousand words” are obscuring the nature of the photography as being in essence mute and never self-explanatory. ‘‘Photographs”, Sontag (1973, p. 23) claims, ‘‘which themselves cannot explain any thing, are inexhaustible invitations to deduction, speculation, and fantasy”. Many media theorists are emphasizing the difference between linear and surface code. For instance, Rajagopal (2006, p. 285) suggests that print emphasizes the visual to the exclusion of other senses but that digital media (o ‘‘electrical media” as he is speaking of) make use of sound and image anew: ‘‘If print redefined the boundaries of a community of experience in abstract linguistic terms, divorced from the sensor immediacy of a given context, electronic media redefine the boundaries again. They can transcend a given linguistic field with sounds and images that recreate the sense of presence with oral communi cation”, Rajagopal (2006, pp. 284–285) writes. While print is based on a linear code—‘‘one damn thing after another”, in Bowker (2005, p. 102) formulation—digital media is based on a surface code inviting all human senses—proponents of virtual reality media even speculate about the ability to engage the sense of taste in the future—and especially the auditory and visual senses. As a consequence, a numbe of media theorists suggest (e.g., Beller, 2006; Manovich, 2001) that in the regime of the surface code, it is cinema that is the predominant medium. Cinema, bringing together the impression of movement, sound and sight into one inclusive perceptual experience represents a culmination of a long series of inventions in visual media. Early media such as Zograscope, a medium capable of providing 3-D images and one of the first gadget crazes in the period between the mid-1740s and the mid-1750s (Blake, 2003), the phenakistiscope, invented in the early 1830s by the Belgian scientist Joseph Plateau (Crary, 1990, pp. 107–109), the zootrope, invented by William G. Horner in 1834, and the stereoscope, invented by the German mathematician Simon Stampfer in 1838, quickly became mass market prod ucts and attracted significant attention from audiences increasingly interested in satisfying what psychologists would later call scopophilia, ‘‘the pleasure of looking”. The pioneering cinematic work of the Lumière brothers in Lyon, France, was recognized almost instantly and in a few decades an entire film industry emerged. For Manovich (2001) and Beller (2006) cinema is not just medium among other but is in fact the medium characterizing our age. The emergence of cinema is what once and for all restruc tures equally social relations and human perception. On basis of these scientific, cultural, technolog ical and social changes beginning in the first half of the 19th century, Beller (2006) speaks of the cinematic mode of production as a new capitalist regime of accumulation.

## 4. The cinematic mode of production

Beller (2006), a film theorist, is outlining a broad critique of the role and function of perception and visuality in present economic regime. His argument is a complex one and includes a range of social economic, and technological changes in society and its institutions and organization. Similar to Hardt and Negri’s (2000) much debated Empire, Beller seeks to formulate a social critique fashioned in an updated Marxist vocabulary. In this section, some of the dominant ideas in Beller's rich text are examined in some detail. For Beller (2006), cinema is the medium that precedes a series of media innovation including television, computers, and the Internet. Such a view is shared with Manovich (2001, p. 86) saying that ‘‘rather than being merely one cultural interface among others, cinema is now becoming the cultural interface, a toolbox for all cultural communication, overtaking the printed word”. Therefore, the ‘‘visual culture” of ‘‘the computer age” dominated by the computer, the medium per excellence in the contemporary society. is “[clinematographic in its appearance, digital on the leve of its material, and computational (i.e., software driven) in its logic”, Manovich (2001, p. 132. Emphasis omitted) suggests. Cinematographic media constitute the life worlds of humans in contemporary soci ety in terms of shaping perception. Beller (2006, p. 2) here speaks of the ‘‘cinematization of the visual”, denoting the ‘‘fusion of the visual and with a set of socio-technical institutions and apparatuses” char acteristic of the present time period—the ‘‘capitalized machine interfaces prey on visuality”, Beller (2006, p. 2) claims. Today, after the emergence of Internet as a principal site of value-generation, vi sion has been ‘‘industrialized”, Beller (2006) says. That is, perception becomes bound up with produc tion and therefore becomes ‘‘sensual labour”; capitalism ‘‘expands” into the domain of perception and visuality. Beller (2006, p. 79) here speaks of the ‘‘attention theory of value” and the ‘‘production value of attention”. By these terms he means that vision is in today’s society by no means ‘‘immaculate” or ‘‘innocent” but it is always already appropriated by capitalist interests. Similar to Lacan (1998), who speaks of ‘‘the Gaze” (commonly capitalized) as the vision always already informed by the Other (Bry son, 1988), Beller suggests that capitalist production have turned vision into a productive force: ‘‘To look is to labor. . .Like capital, the gaze is a structure of organization. . .that demands a certain kind of work from the body, from some bodies. Increasingly, part of the value of the commodity, be it a painting or a Hollywood star, comes from the amount of (unpaid) visual attention it has absorbed” (Beller, 2006, p. 181. Emphasis in the original). In the new economy characterized by a cinematic mode of production, products and services are increasingly dematerialized and based on visual resources (images, pictures, computer interfaces, etc.). Beller here uses the term cinema as a synecdoche, as what is representing the entire system of visual products and services. ‘‘[A]s cinema is a dematerial ized form of circulation”, Beller (2006, p. 76) says, ‘‘the image is a dematerialized form of the commod ity, the commodity without its material content”. Speaking with Lanham (2006), it is ‘‘fluff” more than ‘‘stuff” that determines the contemporary economic regime. The cinematic mode of production is at the bottom line, Beller (2006) claims, based on the ‘‘sensual labour” (i.e., attention) that is extracted in the very moment of the apprehension of the image. In today’s economy, the largely unpaid work of the mass of spectators is what is constitutive economic value (Beller, 2006, p. 78). Beller does not hesitate to outline some of the implications from this new regime of economic accumulation: ‘‘Cinema is the development of a new medium for the production and circulation of value, as impor tant in the reorganization of production and consciousness as the railroad track and the highway” Bel ler (2006, p. 207) argues. The ‘‘cinematic organization of society” has led to a restructuring of perception and consciousness and therefore also to a change in production. This production is increas ingly dematerialized, sensual and abstract. In addition, the cinematization of the visual, society and production represent, Beller (2006, p. 295) claims, a ‘‘historico-material shattering of the linguistic alongside the rise the visual, the displacement of ontological categories, and the reconfiguration of the imaginary and the subject in the logic of the spectacle”.

What Beller proposes is that cinema must be regarded not as one medium among others but as being some kind of ‘‘supermedium” or ‘‘meta-medium” structuring and organizing a series of other media such as computer interfaces, the Internet, and television. In addition, Beller argues, very much in line with Lanham (2006) and Davenport and Beck (2001), that the key characteristic of the contem porary society is the production and consumption of information, or more specifically, information embodied in cinematic images. This argument is however complemented with the radical suggestion that not only the production of such cinematic images should be conceived of as being a value-adding activity, but so is also the consumption of such images, i.e., the ‘‘sensual-labour” of the perceiving sub ject, the spectator. Value is thus appropriated by the very act of apprehending the image, and in Bel ler’s (2006) view, there is no escape from this continuous and ongoing ‘‘extraction” of sensual labour. Beller (2006), unsurprisingly referencing Debord (1977) La societé du spectacle, is here turning individuals into perception-machines under the seamless and continuous influence of media and images. In Karl Marx analysis of capital-labour relations, the worker is never compensated for the surplus value generated in the capitalist production of commodities; in Beller’s account, the sensual labour of the perceiving subject is similarly denied such compensation. While the material mode of production generated large income differences and a most uneven distribution of wealth, thereby calling attention to lack of compensation for work among labour movement organizations and representatives, in the cinematic mode of production much of the work conducted are never conceived of as being work, Beller (2006) implies. Looking at a billboard, seeing a commercial on TV, or closing down a pop-up image on the computer screen may not even be considered labour but may, on the contrary, be even regarded as being entertaining and rewarding. For Beller (2006), this is the very trick of the cinematic mode of pro duction, the ability to render what is an act of value-creation insignificant and thereby undermining any claims of co-production and ultimately the wealth generated.

While Beller’s (2006) thought-provoking text seeks to sketch a broad critique of the contemporary regime of production, it is also, like much Marxists and critical analyses, overemphasizing the ability to manipulate and control the audiences. In Beller’s (2006) view, individuals are largely devoid of agency and an ability to refuse to contribute the sensual labour inherent to the cinematic mode of production. Yet, the idea that perception per se is productive and creates value is a point worth taking into more detailed account. As opposed to other media theorists, examining a medium primarily in relation to other medium (e.g., written texts versus hypertexts, Aaseth, 2001; Bolter, 1991), Beller wants to lo cate media in the very infrastructure of the social fabric. Cinematic media such as computers and tele vision, integrating images, movement, texts, sounds, and so forth, are not detached form the broader social context but is instead constitutive of it, an integral component of the social fabric. Since produc tion is becoming more and more abstract and the market value of Silicon Valley companies like Facebook or Google offering media-based services are skyrocketing, Beller contributes with an analysis of how and why economic value and user-value are generated in the contemporary economy. Beller’s (2006) book shows some of the complexities and intricacies involved when perception, images, media, and economic production constitute one seamless web of activities. If nothing else, Beller (2006) effec tively breaks down the conventional boundary between consumer and producer: the cinematic mode of production relies on the ‘‘prosumer”, a new hybridity transgressing the boundary between production and consumption.

## 5. From the cinematic mode of production to the cinematic mode of organizing

In an historical perspective, new media brought new means of communication and control in organizations (Yates, 1989). Yates (1989) shows that when quill pens and bound volumes gave way to typewriters, stencil duplicators and vertical files in organizations, new forms of more abstract and impersonal communication including orders, reports, and memoranda were developed and used, thereby enhancing the efficiency of companies. By 1920, the major elements of modern communication systems had been established in American corporations. In addition, the new media established a new class of worker, the clerical office worker. For instance, in 1890, the US census showed 33,000 people employed in the occupational category including stenographers, typists, and secretaries; by 1910 that occupational category had increased tenfold to 387,000, and by 1920 it had doubled again to 786,000 workers (Yates, 1989, p. 43). Today, when analog technologies are complemented or even displaced by digital and electronic media, we are again seeing a change in the occupational structure. Flusser (2002, p. 22) points out that linear code (e.g., the Western form of writing embedded in the Phoenician alphabet appropriated by the Greeks) are today becoming ‘‘less important” than surface codes to ‘‘the mass of people”. Manovich (2006), discussing the appropriation of media in the new generation, for instance his students at University of California at San Diego, says that this generation is no longer interested in ‘‘media critique”. Instead, they engage in ‘‘software critique” (Manovich, 2006, p 209) and make use of the media in a manner differing from earlier generations. For them, digital media are not external to their daily life but become an integral part of their life world. In addition, the his torical changes brought by the new analog media in the nineteenth century are having their corresponding contemporary changes. For instance, in the Silicon Valley in the San Francisco Bay Area, a new group of what Barley and Kunda (2006) calls ‘‘itinerant professionals”, enterprising selves serving the Californian computer and software cluster on basis of short-time contracts, have been established. However, while such phenomenon are arguably a idiosyncratic feature of the Silicon Valley region (Saxenian, 1994), it is still an indication of how new media and new communication, two aspects of what may be called the cinematic mode of organizing, are developed and gradually gaining ground in society. Not only does the cinematic mode of organizing lead to new organizational features and principles, it also radically changes the nature of services and products offered. For instance, a variety of widely used software and web-based applications are provided for free. For instance, personal Face book pages and a range of Google services (including GMail and free WiFi internet connections in parts of the US) are given away for free. The economic value of Facebook (speculated on Internet blogs to have a market value corresponding to 100 Billion US dollars) and Google (representing a market value of 210 Billion US dollars in October 2007, second in the IT industry only to Microsoft) thus derive not primarily from the price paid by customers but from the actual and potential value of knowing what individuals pay attention to. A Google representative is putting this quite straightforward:

For Google, being in the attention business means it’s of utmost importance for Google to find out what people are paying attention to. To do that, Google has made a history of giving away services that other companies charge an arm and a leg for. Even more amazingly, the services that Google gives away is usually better than the services that other people are charging for ... In exchange for that free services and software, Google wants to look over your shoulder and take note on what you are paying attention to and what you are ignoring. (Mitch Wagner, Google, cited in Beller 2006, p. 303).

In this economy, dominated by the cinematic mode of production, giving away things for free is a rational act because eventually you may know how influential groups are using the technology or the application. Just like Bataille (1988) discussed the dynamics of the relationship between the ‘‘restricted economy”, characterized by scarcity, and the ‘‘general economy”, characterized by abundance and the squandering of excess resources, the cinematic mode of production is what resides not in a scarcity of applications, media, tools, and web-based application but in the scarcity of attention. To give away web-based applications for free is then no longer an act of ‘‘irrationality” violating the axioms of the rational choice theory underpinning economic theory and its enacted idealtype of the homo oeconomicus, but is in fact a straightforward recognition of the mechanisms of value-creation in a re gime of cinematic mode of production. Value is not generated by the media per se, but, as Beller (2006) would say, in the looking, in the very act of paying attention. To give away and to circulate mediated services and products are therefore no longer marginal and extraordinary events in the capitalist accu mulation but rather becomes a central mechanism for generating value.

## 5.1. The cinematic mode of organizing

The new changes brought by the regime of cinematic mode of production must be addressed by organization theory. Rather than assuming that new media are added to preexisting organizatior forms, media and technology in general and organization are what Orlikowski (2007, p. 1437) calls constitutively entangled with one another; media and organizing are co-evolutionary and co-depen dent processes, forming a Moebius strip-like structure where it is virtually impossible to separate media from organization. That is, when new media (forms of materiality) are developed, new orga nizations form emerge. In the literature on virtual teams and virtual organizations (e.g., Hughes, O<sup>´</sup> Brien, Randall, Rouncefield, & Tolmie, 2001; Markus, Manviolle, & Agres, 2000) or virtual teams (e.g., Krikman, Rosen, Tesluk, & Gibson, 2004; Maznevsky & Chudoba, 2000) the use of information technology is regarded a sine qua non for the new organization forms. In addition, new modes of control will be used in the emerging organization forms. For instance, while organizations have tra ditionally relied on bureaucratic modes of control and more recently employed external control in the form of audits (Power, 1994) wherein control are essentially what Foucault would ‘‘panoptic” in its outline, in the cinematic mode of organizing control is more likely to be continuous and ongo ing (see Deleuze, 1992). The continuous control of Internet traffic and e-mail accounts then represents a new regime of control. Moreover, control is wielded through more subtle and ideological means. Ideologies of enterprising selves (Gay, 1996; Rose, 1990) and beliefs in belonging to a creative segment of the workforce impose new forms of self-regulating control, a form of ‘‘auto-surveillance” on part of the employees (Florida, 2002; Maravelias, 2003). The sources of competitive advantage and long terms competitiveness are also changing in the regime of cinematic mode of production and organizing. Consumers and clients are no longer passive recipients of goods and services but are instead actively involved in the production process; they become, with a concept coined in the marketing discourse, ‘‘prosumers”, a hybrid category transgressing the boundary between production and consumption. For instance, news companies regularly post ‘‘net-polls” on their home pages to involve readers in discussing day-to-day issues. Moreover, researchers such as von Hippel have ded icated decades of research to the understanding of how consumers could be involved in innovation work (e.g., von Hippel, 1998). The ability to exploit the attention of this new group of consumers relies on, as Lanham (2006) suggests albeit proposing the concept of style, on aesthetic knowledge (Ewenstein & Whyte, 2007; Taylor & Hansen, 2005), knowledge that is not, in Taylor and Hansen’s (2005, p. 1213) formulation, ‘‘driven by a desire for clarity, objective truth and usually instrumental goals” but by the desire for ‘‘the subjective, personal truth usually for its own sake”. Aesthetic knowledge is thus represent modes of thinking and acting that extends beyond mere utility and performativity. In addition, aesthetic knowledge is actively incorporating visuality as a central resource for organizations.

Taken together, the cinematic mode of production and its corresponding cinematic mode of organizing are tendencies in the contemporary capitalism. Other comparative tendencies is capitalism have been sketched by for instance Rajan (2006), speaking about an emerging ‘‘biocapitalism” wherein biotechnology, pharmaceutical industry and capital is producing new regimes of economic accumulation and new forms of subjectivities; in Rajan’s (2006) insightful account, ‘‘corporate biotech”, embedded in innovation, ‘‘fact production”, and information, is the contemporary form of high-tech capitalism par excellence. ## 6. Concluding remarks

The present period is dominated by the ‘‘scoptic regime” (Jay, 1996) enabled by digital media and their reliance on hypertext architecture bringing together linear and surface codes into what Bolter and Grusin (1999) calls ‘‘hypermediated” environments. Internet sites like Facebook and YouTube are embedded in a regime of visuality that integrates text and image into effective communication tools and systems of distribution. The 1.65 billion dollar acquisition of YouTube by Google in 2007 helped bringing into discussion how economic value is generated in the cinematic mode of production and the attention economy. However, the emerging economic regime of accumulation examined by Beller (2006) is by no means solely a matter of the relationship between supply and demand, but is equally an organizational matter. Therefore, one may also sketch a ‘‘cinematic mode of organization” where the new digital media and the predominant scoptic regime are producing new forms of control, communication, and organization forms. Similar to the growth of the divisional organization (Chandler, 1962), originally championed by corporations such as General Motors (Sloan, 1964), and enabled by the use of new media carefully examined by Yates (1989), the cinematic mode of organizing are likely to produce new forms of flexible, virtual, or post-bureaucratic organizations. Some examples of new organization forms include virtual organization and virtual teams, and network organization. In addition, in a regime of accumulation embedded in the active involvement, organization form is of necessity more open to external influences and develops mechanisms for actively responding to the environment. In addition, the ability to manage visuality and attention inside as well as outside of the firm may be regarded as key success factors in future. Traditionally, this has been the domain of public relations organizations and marketing firms and departments but in an emerging cinematic mode of organizing, such processes are not peripheral to the operations but are given a much more central function.

It is also important to emphasize that the emerging regime of accumulation is by no means representing a comprehensive and all-encompassing change in the economy but is instead a gradual shift in focus from the production of material resources to increasingly tacit and immaterial services and offerings. However, the ability to detect and examine such ‘‘weak signals” in the economy is of great value to understand a range of social phenomenon and tendencies in the economy. The capitalist eco nomic system is too multifaceted and complex to evolve in one single direction. In addition to the cin ematic mode of production, writers such as Rajan (2006) have outlined complementary images of the future capitalist system emphasizing the importance of the biopharmaceutical industry. Beller’s (2006) work is therefore not a work that can make claims to capture the totality of the future of the capitalist regime of accumulation but is nevertheless a contribution to the understanding o how the capitalism may evolve in the future. The implications for organization theory and management studies may be a field for fruitful future research.
