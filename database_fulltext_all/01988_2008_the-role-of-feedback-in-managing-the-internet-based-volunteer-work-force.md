# The Role of Feedback in Managing the Internet-Based Volunteer Work Force

Jae Yun Moon

Business School, Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, Clear Water Bay, Kowloon, Hong Kong, jmoon@ust.hk

Lee S. Sproull

Stern School of Business, New York University, New York, New York 10012, lsproull@stern.nyu.edu

his paper explores a new phenomenon at the intersection of digital networks and organizations—the Internet-based volunteer work force—people who use Internet applications to pursue a personal interest through volunteering contributions of time and talent that may create value for organizations and their cus tomers or members. This work force is not centrally organized, managed, or measured. It is an emergent phe nomenon resulting from discretionary small actions taken by large numbers of people, enabled by technology and human initiative. This paper proposes a general framework for understanding the phenomenon and offers an empirical investigation of one component of it—the role of feedback in producing and sustaining high-quality contributions from this work force. In a comparative study of Internet-based voluntary technical support groups for software problems, we found that in groups who implement systematic quality feedback systems (compared to those that do not), question askers return over a longer duration, answer providers contribute more often, and technical problem resolution is more effective. We also found that with systematic feedback, volunteers who produce higher quality contributions have longer participation duration, and participation duration is positively associated with community maintenance contributions.

Key words: systematic quality feedback system; feedback; volunteer work force; voluntary technical support group; volunteer turnover

## Introduction

Volunteers use the Internet to submit book reviews that help Amazon sell books. They use the Internet to answer questions for stymied customers of hardware and software companies. They use the Internet to mark craters on images of Mars for NASA. They use the Internet to proofread electronic book pages for Internet publishers. Hundreds of thousands of people contribute a modest amount of their time and talent over the Internet every day to efforts such as these in the absence of formal requirements and obligations. They are an Internet-based volunteer work force, that is, people who use Internet applications to pursue a personal interest through volunteering contributions of time and talent that help others. This work force is not centrally organized, managed, or measured. It is an emergent phenomenon resulting from discretionary small actions taken by large numbers of people, enabled by technology and human initiative. An Internet-based volunteer work force is a relatively new phenomenon that has the potential to yield benefits for online and offline organizations and opportunities for researchers.

Formal organizations might find the possibility of virtually expanding their boundaries to incorporate an Internet-based volunteer work force intriguing for several reasons. If some of an organization’s information work can be performed over the Internet by volunteers,<sup>1</sup> the organization can increase its scope of work without incurring commensurate increases in labor costs. An expanded work force draws upon an expanded talent pool and may lead to the development of new or improved work products (e.g., Jeppesen and Frederiksen 2006). Offering volunteers meaningful work opportunities may increase their loyalty and commitment to the organization (e.g., Butler et al. 2007, Callero et al. 1987, Hertel et al. 2003, Mohl 2006, Piliavin and Callero 1991, Simon et al. 1998, Wilson 2000).

Online and offline organizations and managers wishing to virtually expand their boundaries by drawing upon an Internet-based volunteer work force face at least four challenges: finding volunteers, managing the work process, retaining volunteers, and ensuring that the work is of high quality. Technology and human ingenuity contribute to meeting all of these challenges, although the component that dominates varies across challenges. (See Figure 1 for an overview of this process.)

With search engines and website links, meeting the first challenge of finding volunteers can become a trivial task—organizations do not need to find volunteers because potential volunteers will find organizations. For example, a person may be interested in astronomy but unaware of the NASA imaging project for classifying Mars craters. He searches on “astronomy” or “planetary imaging,” and might be directed to the NASA project site. He then accepts an invitation to mark some craters. A hobbyist reading a message from an astronomy email list might notice a description of the NASA project, click on the embedded link to the project site, and accept the same invitation to mark some craters. Although technology plays a dominant role in meeting the challenge of finding Internet-based volunteers, managerial ingenuity may also play a role. A position near the top of a search result increases the click-through probability (e.g., Granka et al. 2004, iProspect 2006); managers may use a variety of techniques to attempt to affect where their site falls in a search result or where links to their site are posted in order to increase the likelihood of attracting potential volunteers (Drèze and Zufryden 2004).

The second challenge—managing the work done by Internet-based volunteers—entails work design, work capture, work aggregation, and work display. Work must be designed (or a work design must be chosen) so that the basic unit of work contribution is both small and meaningful. It should be small enough that the volunteer can contribute without substantial time or effort and it should be substantively meaningful to motivate the volunteer. In the NASA project, the unit of work is marking craters in one image of a small area of Mars; typically, the size of the area covered by the image ranges from 60 km to 237 km and contains 2 to 10 identifiable images (Mars Clickworkers FAQ, available at http:// clickworkers.arc.nasa.gov/faq.html). Volunteers mark craters within small regions of an image by simply clicking on four points of an identified crater on the image (Kanefsky et al. 2001). In the Distributed Proofreaders project, which was founded to digitize public domain books, the unit of work is proofing one page of the scanned book (Distributed Proofreaders 2004. Beginning proofreaders frequently asked questions (FAQ). Available at http://www.pgdp.net/ c/FAQ/ProoferFAQ.php). The primary unit of work in technical support sites involves posting one question, one answer, or one feedback message regarding a question or answer. Work can also consist of contributing to frequently-asked-questions files or moderating forums. Some volunteers may devote substantial time and effort to making contributions, but they can do so at their convenience in small chunks of time and effort. Work design is primarily determined by people and supported by technology, but work capture, aggregation, and display are primarily governed by software applications that manage these processes relatively automatically. For example, in technical support groups, certain types of software organize and display all answers to a question in a single thread. In the proofreading project, software organizes all proofed pages (which may have been submitted in random order by many different volunteers) by consecutive pagination into a completed book. In e-commerce sites, software organizes and displays customer reviews by product.

Once volunteers have found a site and work management processes are in place, the managerial challenge is to motivate participation and ensure that work contributions are of high quality. Managers should not be tempted to overmanage their volunteers in the name of increasing quality. In the late 1990s, for example, the tight controls that AOL imposed on the behavior of its volunteer forum hosts led to unfair labor practice charges against the firm. In a different instance, at one point Microsoft tried to “fire” its technical support volunteers by announcing that it would shut down its sanctioned volunteer technical support program and, henceforth, to ensure quality would provide sanctioned support only by Microsoft employees. After a firestorm of protest from the volunteers, Microsoft reversed its decision and embraced its Internet-based volunteers once again (Nguyen and Thurrot 1999). Formal organizations manage quality and employee commitment through economic incentives, formal training and contracts, established routines for performance ratings, and formal supervision procedures; voluntary groups must rely on noneconomic incentives, discretionary feedback and peer review from other participants, and community norms to sustain volunteer commitment and manage the quality of volunteer contributions.

What are considered appropriate and high-quality contributions are group specific and collectively defined within a particular group through ongoing interactions among group members (Lampe and Johnston 2005, Lampe and Resnick 2004, Postmes et al. 2000). Social interactions have been shown to have an impact on increasing the likelihood that a participant will continue to participate in online communities (Arguello et al. 2006, Kraut et al. 2006). Recent studies have also shown that feedback from established members of an online community in the form of both explicit ratings as well as editorial reviews and comments may also influence subsequent participant behaviors (Cosley et al. 2005, Lampe and Johnston 2005). The value and importance of feedback for directing, reinforcing, and shaping people’s subsequent behaviors and performance within formal organizations has been studied extensively (e.g., Ilgen et al. 1979). Feedback within formal organizations is managerially controlled but feedback within voluntary groups is itself a voluntary activity. A unit of work in voluntary groups can also consist of moderating and commenting on others’ contributions. Feedback can include elements of both task feedback (i.e., feedback relating specifically to the usefulness and helpfulness of the answer offered or otherwise providing some response to answerers regarding their contribution content) and social feedback (i.e., feedback directed at the behaviors and attributes of the person providing the help). Through feedback provided in the context of a particular discussion thread, participants affirm or approve a participant’s actions or indicate disagreement with another volunteer’s actions. In other words, participants collectively define an admired member and a high-quality contribution through feedback provided to members who participate. The nature of these feedback processes within voluntary groups can be influenced in part through deliberate design decisions for the electronic community application used to support member interactions (Nambisan 2002, Preece 2000, Ren et al. 2007).<sup>2</sup> Among the myriad design decisions for electronic community applications, in this paper we focus in particular on feedback mechanisms because they play a prominent role in both quality control and motivating and retaining volunteers. By emphasizing one type of feedback over another, designers of virtual technical support organizations can influence a group’s interactions that define valued contributions, and thus affect the overall quality of the technical support provided. Well-designed feedback systems may foster trust in the quality of the group as a whole by providing askers with information about the attributes of well-reputed answerers within the group. We consider the role of both ad hoc and explicit feedback mechanisms, which we then investigate empirically in a comparative study of volunteer technical support groups who rely on different types of feedback. The goal of this paper is to highlight a new phenomenon at the intersection of digital networks and organizations—the Internet-based volunteer work force. We will also explore how feedback mechanisms are central to supporting and leveraging the Internet-based volunteer work force, and suggest future lines of inquiry for both managers and researchers.

## Effects of Feedback on Volunteer Participation

The unit of contribution for volunteers in technical support groups can be a technical question, an answer to others’ questions, or a comment providing feedback on a question or answer. There is other work that volunteers might perform in maintaining the community, but in this paper, we focus on the activities related to the main purpose of technical support groups—that of exchanging technical advice and tips with one another on technologies that they share an interest in questions, answers, and feedback. In this section, we discuss how feedback can influence each of these volunteer activities.

## Feedback for Quality Control

Quality control for contributions is an important managerial challenge for several reasons. High-quality contributions contribute directly to high-value products, whether they be accurately marked craters, proofed pages, informative product reviews, or helpful technical support, etc. High-quality contributions encourage repeat business, that is, they encourage people to continue to return to the site (Butler 2001). For example, people seeking to solve technical problems are unlikely to return to a group if the interactions with participants in these groups yield unsatisfactory solutions or negative emotions from hostile exchanges (Carr 1999). Without a critical mass of question askers to contribute challenging technical problems, groups cannot attract answerers who may be motivated by the personal returns of learning and enjoyment (e.g., Lakhani and von Hippel 2003). Finally, high-quality contributions contribute to attracting new people who are more likely to be motivated to contribute to the group if its perceived value is high (e.g., Connolly and Thorn 1990, Fulk et al. 2004, Markus 1987).

A simple model of reinforcement learning suggests that work quality improves both through rejecting or correcting low-quality contributions and through accepting or reinforcing high-quality ones— whether the work is performed by paid or volunteer labor, offline or online. Some Internet-based volunteer projects use software to do automatic quality control. For example, both the crater marking and proofreading projects use software to compare results submitted by more than one person to the same task. Agreement levels can be set to indicate acceptable quality. In the crater marking project, only when compared submissions from the same maps agree across multiple volunteers are the results uploaded for aggregation into the larger project. Frequently, however, quality assessment and quality control are not amenable to software solutions. In these cases, Internet sites rely upon volunteers to do quality assessment as well as to do the original work.<sup>3</sup>

When volunteers judge the quality of other volunteers’ primary contributions, their feedback may be provided systematically through explicit rating systems or in an ad hoc manner through discretionary messages sent privately or posted publicly for all to see. Feedback thus constitutes a valuable secondary contribution to online groups. Internet sites today offer a range of ways for eliciting and displaying feedback from volunteers who assess the quality of other volunteers’ contributions. Feedback may be relatively structured (e.g., using a rating or grading system) or unstructured (e.g., free text). Amazon.com, for example, constructs and displays quality ratings for book reviews submitted by volunteers by aggregating reader responses to the question, How helpful was this review? The results send a quality signal both to the person who wrote the review and to others who may read it. Some sites that feature technical news and views construct and display quality ratings for posts by aggregating readers’ ratings of those posts (Lampe and Resnick 2004). Feedback may be public (e.g., visible to all) or private (e.g., visible only to the person whose work is reviewed). It may be the specific responsibility of certain groups of volunteers (e.g., module maintainers in open source systems or question askers within technical support groups) or open to everyone (i.e., everyone reading answers in technical support groups). We define systematic quality feedback systems as systems that provide participants in online groups with a means to rate the quality of contributions by its members in a structured form. Systematic quality feedback systems can vary in who is allowed to rate contributions (participants directly involved in the exchange versus any member of the community). They may also vary in how quality feedback is aggregated (aggregate quality feedback by participant versus aggregate quality feedback only for specific units of contribution). They also vary in the format in which the quality feedback is provided—some are based on points systems while others are based on conferring various tokens of appreciation that represent valued participants within the group. All other things being equal, systems that increase the frequency, clarity, and visibility of quality feedback are likely to increase the quality of the work produced by volunteers by making the reinforcement learning process more efficient. In other words, potential volunteers will be able to easily discern the nature of high-quality contributions in a group by examining examples of contributions that received good as opposed to bad feedback. In the context of question and answer sites, and consistent with the literature on technical support services quality, if answer quality is high, question askers will experience greater satisfaction with the technical support incident (Carr

2006). Increased satisfaction with a service encounter is generally associated with repeat transactions with the service provider (Bolton 1998). In short, because of the higher quality of answers from groups with systematic quality feedback, question askers will experience higher satisfaction. Thus, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 1. Question askers return more often and over a longer duration of time to sites with systematic quality feedback.

Whether or not sites provide ways for volunteers to provide systematic structured feedback through, for example, rating systems, volunteers almost always can provide ad hoc quality feedback in the form of comments posted by readers about contributions made by other volunteers. Recipients of high-quality advice sometimes post messages of praise or gratitude with a pointer to, or repeat of, the high-quality message. Recipients or readers of questionable or erroneous advice sometimes post messages of caution, scorn, or correction. Ad hoc quality feedback messages may use vivid, exclamatory language and can be quite impactful when provided. However, their production is neither systematically cued nor cognitively structured by an external rating system. Their production relies entirely upon volunteers being vigilant for the need for feedback and being willing to expend the cognitive effort necessary to produce and submit it. In addition to the direct value from providing feedback, systematic rating systems may act as a cueing device for ad hoc feedback by explicitly reminding people of the need for feedback. They may also suggest assessment categories that volunteers can use in framing their ad hoc feedback. If systematic rating systems act as a cueing and structuring device for ad hoc quality feedback, we can hypothesize:

Hypothesis 2. Ad hoc quality feedback will be more prevalent in groups with systematic quality feedback than in groups without systematic quality feedback.

When feedback is displayed publicly, people can learn how to be high-quality contributors through vicarious learning, that is, learning how to produce rewarded behavior by watching how admired people are rewarded for their behavior (e.g., Compeau and Higgins 1995, Lim et al. 1997). The rate of learning, whether direct or vicarious, should be positively correlated with the clarity and strength of the feedback signal. All other things being equal, groups who employ systematic quality feedback systems are likely to produce feedback signals that are stronger and clearer than groups who do not. Systematic quality feedback can increase the visibility of positively assessed behavior through simple, easy-to-interpret tags, thus increasing the probability that everyone who does read the group will see examples of helpful messages. Ad hoc quality feedback may be impactful for the individual receiving and reading that feedback but it will have limited impact on other group participants. Systematic quality feedback, on the other hand, by virtue of being easily recognizable, will have a more widespread effect on the broader community of users. In short, systematic quality feedback increases the visibility of public acknowledgement of valued participants in a group and, hence, provides clearer cues as to the appropriate helpful behaviors in a group. In the context of question and answer sites, systematic quality feedback increases the visibility of both the helpful contributions of answers to technical problems as well as the positive feedback conferred on those who provide these contributions. The public visibility of the recognition gained through helpful contributions will motivate other group participants to answer other members’ questions. In short, systematic quality feedback both facilitates learning of helpful contributions and motivates such behaviors. Thus:

Hypothesis 3. Technical problem resolution will be more effective (questions are more likely to receive an answer) in groups with systematic quality feedback than in groups without systematic quality feedback.

## Retaining Volunteers

Successful groups and organizations retain highperforming volunteers and employees for longer than less successful ones do (Rothenberg 1988). Volunteer retention is important for several reasons.<sup>4</sup> When the work entails answering questions, volunteers’ prior experiences with common questions are likely to make them more efficient and effective in providing appropriate answers than newer members would be—even holding constant innate skills and abilities. Although new visitors to a group might know the answers to some questions, repeat contributors can efficiently recognize common problems expressed in different terms. Repeat contributors also have repertoires of answers they can draw upon rapidly. They can exhibit answering behaviors that reinforce norms and effectively communicate the importance of these norms to new participants in the group. Moreover, long-term participants are more familiar with group norms and, hence, are more likely to be able to provide effective peer review of others’ answers.

Feedback for quality control also contributes to developing and retaining high-quality volunteers. To this point, our explanation of how feedback operates in a volunteer setting assumes a simple model of reinforcement learning in an individual task-learning context. Feedback can have effects beyond task learning, however. When other members acknowledge a participant’s contributions, it makes the group membership psychologically real (Bartel and Dutton 2001, McKenna and Bargh 1998). Positive feedback reinforces the participant’s sense of being an important part of the group, which can increase the probability of future answering due to people’s motivation for self-esteem enhancement (Tajfel and Turner 1986, Tesser 1988). Thus, positive feedback is likely to increase the probability of engaging in similar behaviors in the future and increase the frequency of interactions with the group. Organizational researchers have suggested that increased contact with an organization is sufficient to increase identification with it (Dutton et al. 1994). Studies of telecommuters who have limited interactions with other organizational members have shown that increased communication through electronic mail can increase organizational identification (Huff et al. 1989, Wiesenfeld et al. 1989). McKenna and Bargh (1998) also report that active participation in an online newsgroup has a positive effect on the importance of the group identity. Thus, repeated interactions with an Internet-based volunteer group should result in increased identification with the group. Moreover, public acknowledgment can lead to greater identification with the group as the group becomes a more potent source of self-concept (Dutton et al. 1994). Through accumulation of quality feedback, group identity thus becomes more salient for these members (Kramer 1993, Wiesenfeld et al. 2001). Group identification is an important antecedent to commitment and loyalty to the group (Ashforth and Mael 1989, Kramer 1993, Mael and Ashforth 1995, Tyler 1999) and is manifest through behaviors that benefit the group. In the context of question and answer sites, answer contributions are critical to sustaining the group. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 4. Volunteers contribute more often and for longer duration to sites with systematic quality feedback.

Volunteer retention would be self-defeating if those who contributed more often and for longer durations are not high-quality volunteers. When contributions are identified by who produced them, any system that produces quality feedback for these contributions will necessarily also produce quality feedback for contributors—a quality signal about a substantive contribution is also a quality signal about a contributor. Direct reinforcement learning suggests that over time, people who receive positive feedback for their contributions will produce more, higher quality contributions and people who receive negative feedback will either modify their behavior or drop out (Bandura 1977, Bandura and McDonald 1963, Batson 1998). Positive reinforcement enhances selfesteem and creates a virtuous learning cycle (Pierce et al., cited in Wiesenfeld et al. 1999, p. 36). Thus, groups with systematic quality feedback systems will be able to retain high-quality contributors related to the primary group purpose.

Hypothesis 5. Within groups with systematic quality feedback systems, volunteers who receive higher quality evaluations will have longer participation durations.

If longer duration participant contributions were no different from those of shorter duration participants, then the effect of systematic quality feedback in increasing work quality of the group overall would be questionable. As people experience increased group identification through longer participation duration, they also experience increased desire for group well-being (Tyler 1999). Volunteers who report being motivated by community or group interests are more likely to engage in group maintenance behaviors than those motivated only by self-interest (e.g., Butler et al. 2007, Constant et al. 1996, Wasko and Faraj 2000). In addition to making (high-quality) substantive contributions related to the primary purpose of the group, these behaviors include, among others, providing feedback on the quality of others’ technical contributions, providing social feedback on others’ behavior, sanctioning behaviors that are inappropriate, and promoting the group to outsiders. Thus, we hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 6. Participation duration is positively associated with community maintenance contributions and contributions that enhance the sustainability of the group.

We investigated the hypotheses offered above in a comparative study of Internet-based voluntary technical support groups for software problems. We define a voluntary technical support group as an online discussion group using one or more many-to-many networked communication technologies in which users of a common technology voluntarily exchange technical advice and tips. Analysts estimate that the cost of software support can be as much as two or three times the purchase cost (Chou 2003). In a recent study of software-related costs, support costs accounted for on average over 60% of total expenditures (British Educational Communications and Technology Agency 2005). Vendors and customer firms, under pressure to reduce support costs, are increasingly encouraging voluntary technical support groups on the Internet to take on some of this work. In these groups, volunteers offer free but valuable solutions to people’s technical problems. Indicative of the value of these groups is the fact that they have won a variety of industry awards. The InfoWorld Best Technical Support Award was awarded to the volunteer Linux user community in 1997 and to the Sybase Internet volunteer newsgroups in 1999 (Foster 1998, 1999, 2000). Hewlett-Packard’s (HP) IT Resource Center, an online community of HP’s customers, won the award for best Web-based support from the Association of Support Professionals in 2004 and 2005 (CRM Today 2006). In short, technical support groups offer a fruitful practical domain in which to investigate the impact of explicit feedback system design on Internet-based volunteer behavior and retention.

## Method

## Study Groups

We selected four types of groups to investigate the effect of systematic quality feedback systems—two relying entirely on ad hoc feedback, one with questioner-based systematic quality feedback (i.e., feedback can only be provided by the question asker), and one with community-based systematic quality feedback (i.e., feedback can be provided by any member of the group). Specifically, we investigated Web-based technical support forums (WWW) and technical support mailing lists (LSOFT), which use only ad hoc feedback, and groups from TECH and COMP—Webbased communities of IT professionals—which implement systematic quality feedback.<sup>5</sup> At the outset, we acknowledge that type of feedback is not the only dimension on which these groups differ. (See Table 1 for a summary of the study groups). Although anyone can join the TECH and COMP groups, they are targeted to “IT professionals.” Members on the WWW and LSOFT groups may have more diverse occupational backgrounds. Nonetheless, unlike the general

Internet population, technical listserv and Web forum participants are more likely to have professional or academic interest in the technical subject matter itself and are more likely to reveal their offline identities and affiliation with IT-related professions (e.g., Caldas 2003, Donath 1999, Lakhani and von Hippel 2003). In particular, the subject matter for both the WWW and LSOFT groups makes it likely that members are related to the IT profession. All the groups have the same manifest purpose—providing an open Internet forum where people can voluntarily ask and answer questions about operating systems and databases. All the groups are indexed by search engines under the key words of “technical help” and “technical support” for the respective system. All of the groups use the same unit of contributing—posting one question or posting one answer or feedback. And all of the groups face the same issues of encouraging volunteer contributions and ensuring their quality.

The most notable difference across groups is in their quality feedback mechanisms. The WWW and LSOFT groups rely entirely upon members to provide ad hoc quality feedback in the form of praise or criticism messages. In TECH, members provide ad hoc feedback but they also make extensive use of a systematic quality feedback system based on points. Members are given 75 question points when they register and an additional 5 points for each day they remain a registered member. When a member asks a question, he posts the number of points that will be awarded to the accepted answer, with point value varying depending upon the asker’s perception of question difficulty. Question askers also grade the quality of an answer on a four-point grade scale from A (four points) to D (one point). Expert points awarded to the answerer whose answer is accepted are the product of the points posted by the question asker and the grade received. Thus, for example, if a question asker posts a question with 150 points and gives the accepted answer a grade of B, the answerer earns 450 expert points and the asker has 150 question points deducted.<sup>6</sup> Expert points are tallied and communicated for each person who provides answers; the names and expert point totals of members who have earned large numbers of expert points are prominently displayed on the website.<sup>7</sup> Expert points are tabulated and maintained separately from question points for each member; participants cannot use expert points for asking questions.

In COMP, members can provide explicit quality feedback by “thanking” valuable posts through awarding stars that are tallied for each participant. In COMP forums, the front page of each forum does not prominently display top contributors of highly rated answerers, but users may view the list of highly rated participants by clicking on the relevant list of MVPs. Unlike TECH in which explicit quality feedback can only be provided by the question asker, in COMP any registered member of the group reading the thread may rate answers to indicate good quality answers.

## Data Collection and Management

Message data were collected from the archive websites of the four groups from their respective founding dates to the censoring date of October 7, 2002 in order to create a time-balanced sample for hypothesis testing.<sup>8</sup> The final data set consisted of 61,458 messages in 26,281 threads for the ad hoc recognition groups (WWW and LSOFT) and 181,492 text message files from 32,638 threads for the systematic quality feedback system groups (TECH and COMP combined).

For each message, we extracted summary structural information from its header: a message “ID” to uniquely identify the message in the data set, date (transformed to a common time zone), author, subject, and message category (question, answer, feedback on answer by asker). Question, answer, and feedback were operationally defined based on the thread structure (each thread-initiating post was defined as a question), each subsequent post by participants other than the asker in the question thread became an answer, and, finally, posts within the thread by the original question asker were defined as feedback messages.<sup>9</sup> Feedback messages may be related to the content of the technical advice or the manner in which the technical advice was offered. It may take the form of questions and comments that provide information to the answer contributor regarding how to provide the technical advice in a clear and helpful manner. Summary records were constructed for each author, including the count of questions, replies, feedback messages and, for TECH participants, the number of expert points. Summary records were constructed for each thread, including the total number of messages, duration in days, total number of messages posted by the question asker, and the total number of unique participants in the thread.

## Measures

Density of Contribution. For each participant, we calculate the number of answers contributed by that participant and the ratio of answers contributed to questions asked per discrete monthly time interval during which the participant was a member of a given technical support group. For each technical support group, we calculate the ratio of resolved questions to total questions and the ratio of answers to questions per monthly discrete time interval. The two measures together provide indicators of the effectiveness of technical problem resolution within the groups, measuring how likely question askers are to receive answers to their questions.

Density of Feedback Messages. If a question asker replied to another answer within a thread, we define that reply as an explicit ad hoc feedback message to an answerer. Feedback density is calculated as the ratio of feedback messages to answers. This measure provides a conservative estimate of ad hoc feedback because it includes only thread-embedded feedback provided by question asker.

Participation Duration. We define participation duration for each participant as the amount of time from the first message posted to the group to withdrawal. Withdrawal is defined as when the participant has been inactive, i.e., has not posted a message for a period of time longer than her average intercontribution duration in the past. For each participant, the difference between the last date of a contributed answer or question and the censoring point is compared to the average interresponse or interquestion duration to determine whether it is an unusual intercontribution time.<sup>10</sup> The standard rule of thumb, which is to treat values that lie beyond three standard deviations of the mean of the interparticipation times as unusual, is based on the definition of statistical outliers (Osborne and Overbay 2004, Wainer 1976) but can be misleading because the standard deviation itself may be skewed by the inclusion of the outliers (Tukey 1977). Tukey proposed an alternative criterion for deciding whether a value is an outlier based on quartiles—a value that is greater than the sum of the 75th percentile and 1.5 times the interquartile distance is an outlier (Tukey 1977, p. 44). Therefore, for participants for whom the difference between the censoring point and the last participation point is greater than this value, the duration will be defined as the difference between the last logged answer (or question) date extended by the estimated Tukey hinge for the participant and the first logged answer (or question) date.<sup>11</sup> For all other participants, the event of interest, i.e., withdrawal, has not occurred and the record is censored.<sup>12</sup>

## Discussion

In this paper, we examined how managers interested in cultivating their online communities as a virtual extension of their organizational work force might more effectively retain volunteers and ensure highquality contributions. Because of the critical role that feedback plays in reinforcing behaviors, we focused on the role that systematic quality feedback systems can play in ensuring high-quality work and retention of volunteers. We selected a context of study where issues with respect to volunteer work design— the basic unit of work that an individual may perform or the mechanism by which individual work is aggregated—are well-defined: that of online voluntary technical support groups. The basic unit of contribution that individuals make in such contexts is a question, answer, or feedback message. We examined feedback effects in voluntary technical support groups who implemented systematic quality feedback systems and compared them to groups who relied only on ad hoc feedback. We found that question askers were more likely to sustain membership for longer in groups who implemented systematic feedback systems (Hypothesis 1).

Longer membership duration by askers is an indicator of greater satisfaction with the quality of the answers that they received and, hence, provides some evidence of the quality impact of systematic quality feedback systems. Our second measure of asker satisfaction with the groups was based on the number of questions asked in the groups. We expected that askers satisfied with the quality of answers received from the group would be more likely to ask repeat questions. We found that contrary to our expectations, askers do not ask more questions in groups with systematic feedback mechanisms. However, this needs to be interpreted cautiously because ad hoc groups had a significantly greater number of questions that were unanswered when compared to the systematic quality feedback system groups. In fact, we argue that the longer participation duration of people asking questions in systematic feedback groups is a stronger indicator that askers are satisfied and are more likely to return even after some time has elapsed. Moreover, the need for technical support is not continuous and, hence, it is conceivable that the askers were satisfied with the systematic quality feedback system groups as evident in the significantly greater duration of membership but simply had no questions to ask. Finally, because the systematic feedback systems groups provided repositories of past solutions to technical problems that had been rated by other members, askers may have been leveraging this community resource rather than directly asking questions. Hence, overall we argue that our findings indicate that overall quality of technical support is greater in groups with systematic quality feedback systems.

How might systematic quality feedback systems help meet the challenge of ensuring high-quality work from volunteers? Our results suggest some possibilities. First, systematic quality feedback systems may enable quality control in voluntary online communities by not only its main function of providing a mechanism for participants to rate quality contributions, but also by cueing and increasing the provision of ad hoc feedback for quality control (Hypothesis 2). The presence of the systematic feedback system, in essence, may make prominent the need for providing feedback regarding the quality of volunteer contributions to all members of the community. Hence, volunteer answerers will receive more ad hoc feedback in addition to systematic quality feedback, enabling them to better understand user questions and needs. Here, any message provided by the asker may be valuable, regardless of whether it is providing actual task or social feedback. Asking follow-up questions in response to a contributed answer provides valuable information to the answerer on how to improve his next answer in order to ensure that the question asker will be able to fully understand and follow his advice.

Second, systematic feedback may reinforce quality contributors’ behaviors, i.e., answering questions in a way that addresses asker needs both technically and in terms of the social dimensions of the problem solution process (Carr 1999), and thus results in these high-quality answer contributors staying longer in the group (Hypothesis 5). We did not find that answerers sustained contributions for longer in systematic quality feedback system groups (Hypothesis 4). However, this finding may be a result of the extent to which the systematic quality feedback systems were actually used by participants in our study groups. Relative to questioner-based systematic quality feedback, the responsibility for providing quality feedback is diffuse in community-based systematic feedback groups. We had hypothesized that systematic quality feedback systems would result in longer participation duration, on average, due to ongoing interaction and positive affirmation through the public acknowledgement of contributions and accumulation of quality feedback. In short, answerers who did not directly receive systematic quality feedback may not develop a sense of group identification for ongoing contribution of answers to the group. We found some support for this when we retested the hypothesis for the questioner-based systematic quality feedback groups—on average, repeat answerers (who had contributed at least four answers) in these groups had longer duration of participation when compared to volunteers in groups who had no systematic feedback systems and groups who had limited use of systematic quality feedback systems. We found, however, that there was no significant difference in average participation duration of answerers when we used the full sample of all answerers who contributed at least one answer. In short, groups implementing questioner-based systematic quality feedback systems were more likely to sustain participation by repeat volunteers but not by all new volunteers who first choose to participate in the group. Social psychological research on natural group formation lends some insight into this finding. The group socialization model includes five phases of group membership— investigation, socialization, maintenance, resocialization, and remembrance (Moreland et al. 1993). In the initial phase, individuals are in the process of investigating groups and evaluating whether they fit their personal needs and goals. In this phase, they have not yet committed to a group. The presence of systematic quality feedback systems may enable potential volunteers to quickly investigate a group; receiving systematic quality feedback from question askers may lead to quick decisions to drop out. We posit that repeat answerers who have contributed at least four answers have passed this initial evaluation phase and entered the socialization phase that comes with group membership. Our findings may indicate that it is at this later stage of group socialization, once self-selection into the groups has occurred, that systematic quality feedback systems that are utilized extensively may have an impact—by prolonging the duration of the highly committed phase of group socialization and thus contributing to group maintenance.

Third, the visibility of group members who have been rated as providing the most valuable contributions is also an efficient way of providing other members with opportunities for learning how to make quality contributions—they can observe members whom the group feels have made the highest quality contributions (Hypothesis 3). Finally, by inducing longer term participation by high-quality contributors (Hypothesis 5) as discussed above, groups with systematic feedback systems are more likely to have members who have been participating in the group for a long period and, hence, have developed a sense of group identification. These long-term participants are more likely to provide ad hoc feedback in messages to other answerers and engage in community maintenance activities (Hypothesis 6).

Our results show how systematic quality feedback may either directly or indirectly influence the quality of volunteer contributions and consequently of the group. Our data enabled us to explore the interactive impact of systematic and ad hoc feedback. Systematic feedback systems provide prominent information regarding the group members providing high-quality contributions—those whom our study found were likely to be long-term participants. Longterm participants were also likely to provide more ad hoc feedback and contribute to group maintenance activities. Further research is needed to understand how systematic feedback systems might favor one type of feedback over others as well as the long-term consequences of doing so. Systematic feedback systems emphasize certain attributes and behaviors of volunteers through a group’s publication of statistics such as “Top 10” members with highest quality feedback scores. In short, the system influences a group’s definition of what it means to be an exemplary member (Hogg 2001). Moreover, quality feedback could be simultaneously negative and positive—that is, it could provide negative task feedback regarding the usefulness of a proposed answer but positive social or interpersonal feedback showing appreciation for the attempted assistance. People are more likely to exhibit behaviors and attitudes aligned with the group interest when they feel that group members respect them (Simon and Stürmer 2003, Tyler and Blader 2000). In the context of feedback on Internet-based volunteer work, negative task feedback that is respectful in affective tone could still be construed as “positive” feedback leading to sustained involvement in the group. Without understanding the underlying processes, there is the danger that systematic quality feedback systems based on quality points could inadvertently encourage only “task feedback” at the expense of social feedback. Indeed, systems that tally and publicize the number of contributions with no effort to provide quality feedback may lead to lower quality as participants compete on quantity rather than on quality (see Sutherland 2006 for some evidence on this point). In general, future research should compare how different types of systematic quality feedback systems (e.g., expertise points versus token recognition or community based versus question asker based) may affect volunteer work quality and participant duration.

There are several limitations to this research. First, although we derived and tested hypotheses regarding the impact of feedback on volunteer behavior based on mechanisms of reinforcement learning and identification processes, it is possible that feedback may

influence behavior through other mechanisms. An alternative mechanism may be one where feedback results in selection rather than behavior modification, i.e., participants that do not fit a particular group will drop out. The current study does not enable us to determine through which mechanism feedback has an impact. Future research might adopt a developmental perspective in order to investigate at a microprocess level what mechanisms may be critical at different phases of group socialization. Second, although our results indicated that there is a statistically significant difference in participation duration between groups who implement systematic quality feedback systems and those who do not, this test alone is not sufficient to conclude that the implementation of a systematic feedback system was in fact the cause, or the only cause, of this difference. There may be multiple possible explanations for sustaining quality participation over time in an Internetbased volunteer context. Although we have sampled groups so as to control for as many possible confounding factors as possible but we cannot rule out all. The difference in participation duration may be attributable to differences in feedback content (e.g., the ratio of positive to-negative feedback) that may or may not be related to the specific feedback system implemented. Alternatively, the difference may be attributable to differences in the founding culture across the two types of groups, having nothing to do with feedback. Studies of volunteers suggest that different types of motivations (altruistic and community concerns versus egotistic and reputation concerns) may lead to different types of behaviors (e.g., Butler et al. 2007, Clary et al. 1998, Roberts et al. 2006) and may account for differences in participation duration. However, it is not likely that people motivated by either altruistic or egotistic concerns would favor one type of group over another in the initial decision to participate in either ad hoc or systematic feedback groups. It is possible, however, that within the systematic feedback groups in our study, the visibility of accrued expertise points may result in a higher probability that participants motivated by reputation and career benefits contribute for longer compared to participants in these groups with altruistic motivations. Our study demonstrates that once participants have sustained involvement within the group for a long time, they exhibit more altruistic behaviors— answering more questions—and contribute more ad hoc feedback and group norm maintenance messages. In addition, motivation for career concerns is not unique to the systematic feedback groups. Numerous studies have documented the role of career motivations in sustained involvement in technical online groups who do not implement any systematic quality feedback systems (Lakhani and von Hippel 2003, Roberts et al. 2006, Turner and Fisher 2006). Members who make high-quality contributions can gain career-enhancing reputation and visibility both within and beyond the boundaries of the group (Turner and Fisher 2006). In fact, prominent long-term volunteers in Microsoft newsgroups gain reputation and are conferred Microsoft MVP status (R. Kaplan and T. Richards, letter from MVP program available at http://mvp.support.microsoft.com/gp/mvpintro). We did demonstrate, however, the hypothesized effects of feedback within the systematic quality feedback groups. For these results, holding constant founding culture, we still see the hypothesized feedback effects. Another alternative is that the difference between groups is related to differences in the target users of the respective groups, namely that the systematic quality feedback groups are specifically geared toward IT professionals. However, the ad hoc groups are also likely to have a high proportion of IT professionals as participants due to their focus on relatively sophisticated technologies. Moreover, our findings indicate that there are also differences between the systematic quality feedback groups, more specifically in terms of duration of answerer participation that indicate that our results are not likely due to population differences. Future research might employ questionnaires and other data collection instruments to further investigate the role that differences in population attributes (e.g., job status) may play in volunteer behavior and the impact of feedback mechanisms. Finally, infrastructure technology differences between the groups might be an additional factor that may at least partially account for observed group differences. Though it was not possible to tease out this effect, the significant difference in answerer participation duration between the questioner-based systematic quality feedback and ad hoc Web forum group indicate that this is not a main driver of the results. In spite of these limitations stemming from the choice of method—namely archival field study—our study provides valuable insights into the complex feedback processes that may be at play as a result of the implementation of systematic feedback systems that would not have been possible through other more controlled methods. In addition, by framing the issue of sustaining online community participation as one of volunteer work force management, we were able to bring in discussions regarding the quality of the work done by volunteers.

Practically, the voluntary technical support groups we have investigated in this paper are instances of a more general type of Internet-based voluntary group such as health support groups and open source software development groups where members interact to produce some socially desirable output. The output may be tangible (software programs) or intangible (social capital and social support). Our findings suggest that organizations hoping to marshal a volunteer work force of sustained high-quality contributions may need to consider the design of systematic feedback mechanisms.
