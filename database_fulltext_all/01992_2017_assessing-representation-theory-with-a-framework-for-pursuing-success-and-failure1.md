# ASSESSING REPRESENTATION THEORY WITH A FRAMEWORK FOR PURSUING SUCCESS AND FAILURE<sup>1</sup>

Andrew Burton-Jones The University of Queensland Business School and Centre for Business and Economics of Health, St. Lucia, QLD 4072 AUSTRALIA {abj@business.uq.edu.au}

Jan Recker School of Management, Queensland University of Technology, Brisbane, AUSTRALIA {j.recker@qut.edu.au}

Marta Indulska UQ Business School, The University of Queensland, St. Lucia, QLD 4072 AUSTRALIA {m.indulska@business.uq.edu.au}

Peter Green School of Accountancy, Queensland University of Technology, Brisbane, AUSTRALIA {p.green@qut.edu.au}

Ron Weber Faculty of Information Technology, Monash University, and UQ Business School, The University of Queensland, St. Lucia, QLD 4072 AUSTRALIA {r.weber1@uq.edu.au}

Representation theory (RT) is one of few long-standing, native theories in the Information Systems discipline. Over the past 30 years, RT has spawned a wide program of research, primarily on modeling of information systems but also on other phenomena such as data quality, system alignment, security, and effective system use. Nonetheless, descriptions of RT are splintered across many papers over many years. RT has also attracted repeated criticisms about assumptions, tests, and results. As a result, the nature of RT, its merits (or lack thereof), and how best to progress it, are unclear. Motivated by these issues, this paper provides a much needed overview of RT. It further offers an evaluation of RT and explains how research on RT can improve, using a novel framework for evaluating theoretical programs. Our analysis shows that RT’s merits (or lack thereof) remain inconclusive because prior research has not proceeded systematically enough. In this light, we explain and illustrate how research can proceed more systematically.

Keywords: Representation theory, research program, literature review, theory evaluation, theory development

## Introduction

A central, sometimes controversial, idea in the history of the Information Systems discipline is that information systems provide representations of real-world phenomena (e.g., Biller and Neuhold 1978; Kent 1978; Langefors 1973; Rogers 1986; Stamper 1971; Suchman 1995). Along this line, in the late 1980s and early 1990s, Wand and Weber (1988, 1990, 1993, 1995) built a theory of information systems as representational artifacts. They argued the essential purpose of information systems is to provide representations of individuals’ or groups’ perceptions of real-world phenomena. They also argued users will deem information systems more useful if they provide faithful (i.e., accurate and complete) representations (Weber 1997, p. 83). Although the research they motivated does not appear to have an accepted name, we refer to it as representation theory (RT), as have others (see Burton-Jones and Grange 2013; Gregor 2006).

Most early presentations of RT appeared in our sister disciplines (Wand and Weber 1989, 1990), emerging journals (Wand and Weber 1993, 1995), and hard-to-obtain monographs (Weber 1997). Furthermore, the initial work was used mainly by a small community of conceptual modeling researchers. Nonetheless, RT has received growing attention in major IS journals (e.g., Bera et al. 2011; Bowen et al. 2009; Burton-Jones and Meso 2006; Recker et al. 2011; Shanks et al. 2008). Researchers have also applied RT to a growing number of topics, such as IT alignment (Sia and Soh 2007), enterprise system fit (Strong and Volkoff 2010), and effective use of IS (Burton-Jones and Grange 2013). Various critiques of RT also have arisen (e.g., Allen and March 2012; Wyssusek 2006).

With growing interest in RT, the time is right for a review of RT and the research program it continues to sustain. Such a review is important because descriptions of RT are splintered across the literature; indeed, we are unaware of any complete description of RT since Weber’s (1997) now out-of-print monograph. In this paper, therefore, we provide a focused, thematic, and cumulative review of the RT research program (Rowe 2014; Templier and Paré 2015). By “program,” we mean the application, over time, of RT across contexts, methods, and subjects (Berthon et al. 2002).

By undertaking such a review, we sought to answer two major questions. First, as more researchers use RT, it is increasingly important to ask: How good is the theory? Second, if research on RT is to continue, it then becomes important to ask: How should the research continue? The answers we provide to these two questions are the paper’s major contributions.

To answer these questions, we needed a rigorous framework that we could use to assess the RT research program. After an extensive search, we could find no suitable framework. Accordingly, we developed one that can be used to evaluate and guide theoretical programs of research. This framework constitutes a third, supplementary contribution of our work.

Our paper has the following structure. First, we provide an overview of RT. Second, we describe the novel framework we developed to assess RT. Third, we discuss the procedures we used to identify and code relevant literature. Fourth, we assess the outcomes of the RT research program to date. Fifth, we suggest some ways in which the RT research program might continue. Sixth, we discuss some broader implications of our review for the RT research program. Finally, we present some limitations of our review and some conclusions.

## Review of Representation Theory

Because we hope this review will be useful to readers who are familiar with RT as well as those who are not familiar with it, we begin with an overview of RT. The overview we present is the outcome of an interpretive act (Boell and Cecez-Kecmanovic 2015; Tate et al. 2015). In this light, other scholars might perceive and understand RT differently from us. Our views are inevitably colored by our involvement with RT over many years (Wall et al. 2015). For these reasons, we describe our understanding of RT in the hope that our approach to engaging with it and evaluating it is transparent. We provide definitions of key terms in Appendix A.

## Motivation and Fundamental Assumptions of RT

Wand and Weber (1990) explain how they were motivated to build RT because they saw a need to open the “black box” of the information systems artifact. They sought to build “a foundation for a theory of information systems structure and design” (Wand and Weber 1988, p. 213).

The starting point of RT was the view that information systems artifacts are developed because humans, and human organizations, have an inherent desire for information (e.g., to learn and survive) and this desire in turn fuels continual developments in technology to satisfy it (Weber 1997, p. 59). RT was designed to apply to any and all information systems, irrespective of their use, users, or technologies (Wand and Weber 1990, p. 1282). Accordingly, RT focuses on a defining, general characteristic of all information systems: how they convey information.

There are many conceptions of information (e.g., Boell 2017; McKinney and Yoos 2010; Stamper 1973; Weber 1997). RT makes four assumptions related to conveying information that all relate to the notion of meaning. First, for an information system to convey information, RT assumes it must be able to convey meaning. Drawing on Newell and Simon (1976), RT assumes information systems can do so through the capacity of computers for symbol processing and the ability of humans to extract meaning from symbols (Wand and Weber 1995, p. 207).

The second assumption is that the purpose of an information system is to provide a representation of “someone’s or some group’s perception of the real-world system that the information system has been built to model” (Wand and Weber 1995, p. 206). This assumption refers to representations because RT assumes it is through representations (symbols that stand for, or model, other phenomena) that meaning, and hence information, is conveyed (Weber 1997, p. 59). The assumption refers to perceptions because RT assumes that humans’ views and models of the world are always mediated by perceptions (Weber 1997, p. 77); no one has a God’s eye view. Finally, the assumption refers to the provision of representations as being the purpose of the IS. The rationale is that information systems are built and used because it can be more efficient to learn about the world from computerized representations than by direct observation (Wand and Weber 1993, p. 218; also Burton-Jones and Grange 2013, p. 636). The extent to which an advantage accrues depends on pragmatic factors (such as tasks, contextual conditions, and fit). RT is not a pragmatic theory, so it does not address these factors directly. Nor is it a psychological theory that explains how humans perceive or learn. Nonetheless, as our review shows later, RT can be combined with other theories to do so.

Together, the first and second assumptions imply an information system conveys meaning about a real-world domain to users through symbols that represent the domain according to someone’s or some group’s perception of it. The symbols convey meaning, and humans extract meaning from them. The meaning, in both cases, reflects the way someone or some group interprets phenomena in that domain.

The third assumption of RT is that stakeholders can articulate their perception of the meaning of phenomena that interest them. Notably, RT does not use any particular theory of meaning (e.g., Speaks 2016) to explain how users develop or articulate their perception of meaning. How they do so, and how they agree upon perceptions of meaning in the case of a group, is outside the scope of RT (Wand and Weber 1995, p. 206). Instead, RT takes a pragmatic stance: it assumes that users can articulate meaning and takes their perceived meaning as given. This pragmatic stance does not mean, however, that researchers cannot use theories of meaning to study, question, and clarify the assumptions and boundaries of RT, as we discuss later.

The fourth assumption of RT is that users will deem an information system to be more useful to the extent it provides a faithful (i.e., accurate and complete) representation of their perceptions of the meaning of the focal real-world phenomena (Weber 1997, p. 83). If the representation is not faithful, users may need to improve the representation, or alternatively seek other ways to find out the status of the real-world phenomena.

## Nature of Theory and Deep Structure Focus

Gregor (2006, p. 628) argues RT is a Type-IV theory: it explains and predicts. For instance, it explains why the presence or absence of certain properties in information system grammars or scripts enhances or undermines the ability of a script to provide and remain a faithful representation. It also predicts whether the presence or absence of certain properties in grammars or scripts will enhance or undermine the faithfulness of the representation. We also see RT as a Type-V theory: a theory to guide design and action (Gregor 2006, pp. 628-629). It tells designers what properties they must give a script if an information system is to provide a useful representation.

Like many theories, RT was developed by making inferences from its assumptions (Kaplan 1998/1964, pp. 298-299). In particular, Wand and Weber (1995, pp. 205-207) began by seeking to understand how user perceptions of meaning could be embodied in an information system (for earlier, related ideas, see Bubenko 1979; Vermeir and Nijssen 1982). To do so, they characterized an information system as comprising three structures:

Deep structure: Those characteristics of the information system that represent stakeholder perceptions of the meaning of the focal real-world phenomena (e.g., data objects and business rules embedded in program code).

Surface structure: Those characteristics of the information system that allow users to engage with the deep structure (e.g., query interface and report generator).

Physical structure: Those characteristics of the information system that implement the other two structures (e.g., servers, personal computers, and networks).

The primary focus of RT is the deep structure of an information system. The other two structures are seen as means through which users engage with the deep structure. The primary concern of RT, therefore, is the extent to which the deep structure of an information system provides and remains a faithful representation of the focal real-world phenomena (Wand and Weber 1995, p. 207).<sup>2</sup> As the phenomena change (i.e., events occur), the information system must maintain an up-to-date, faithful representation if it is to remain useful.

RT conceives of the deep structure of an information system as being embodied in the scripts (ordered strings of symbols) prepared to describe the real-world phenomena the information system seeks to represent. At different stages in the systems development process, scripts are generated and then transformed until one is created that can be executed by a machine. For instance, initially users’ perceptions of the focal real-world phenomena might be modeled via a script generated via UML. This script might undergo several transformations until a script generated via a programming language (e.g., C++) can be compiled and executed by a machine. Information system scripts are generated using information system grammars (and the entire set of all possible scripts that can be generated in this way reflects the language; see Burton-Jones et al. 2009, p. 497). RT pinpoints certain properties of grammars and scripts that are predicted to affect a script’s ability to provide and remain a faithful representation of the focal real-world phenomena and to convey meaning about them.

## The Three Models of Representation Theory

In seeking to understand how the deep structure of an information system might provide and remain a faithful representation of the focal real-world phenomena and convey meaning about them, Wand and Weber developed three models (Figure 1).

Each focuses on a somewhat different but related aspect of an information system’s deep structure. The representation model (RM) seeks to account for the ability of information system grammars to generate scripts that are faithful representations of the focal real-world phenomena. The state-tracking model (STM) imposes a set of conditions on scripts that have been used to enact (make operational) an information system if the system is to remain a faithful representation of the focal real-world phenomena as they undergo change. The gooddecomposition model (GDM) imposes a set of conditions on the scripts used to develop and implement an information system if the scripts are to communicate more meaning about the focal real world phenomena (and, by extension, facilitate stakeholders assessing the faithfulness of the representation).

## The Representation Model

The representation model (RM), also sometimes referred to as the “theory of ontological clarity,” “theory of ontological expressiveness,” or perhaps misleadingly, the “Bunge-Wand-Weber model,” is derived from RT’s assumption that information systems are embodied in the form of scripts generated via grammars. Whatever the form of a script, the RM requires that the script maintain a faithful representation of the focal real-world phenomena (Wand and Weber 1993). The RM predicts that any loss of faithfulness will mean users deem the implemented information system to be less useful.

To evaluate whether scripts can provide a faithful representation of the focal real-world phenomena, Wand and Weber (1993) focused on the grammars used to generate scripts. They argued these grammars must be able to generate instances of constructs to represent specific types of phenomena that exist in the world (as perceived by humans) in a clear, complete way. To identify the types of phenomena that exist in the real world, they adopted and modified Bunge’s (1977, 1979) ontological theory. By mapping constructs in the grammar to constructs in a benchmark ontology (such as, but not limited to, Bunge’s), Wand and Weber (1993, pp. 226-233) argued four types of defects in a grammar could be identified: construct deficit, construct excess, construct redundancy, and construct overload (Table 1). If instances of these defects existed in a grammar, they argued the grammar’s ability to generate clear, complete scripts would be undermined (Figure 1).

## The State-Tracking Model

The state-tracking model (STM) articulates four necessary and sufficient conditions that an information system (manifested in the final script that enacts it) must satisfy if it is to maintain a faithful representation of its focal real-world phenomena as these phenomena undergo change (i.e., as events occur to them) (Wand and Weber 1995, pp. 211-213):

1. Mapping Condition: A state of the information system must map to only one state of its focal real-world phenomena.

If an information system complies with the mapping condition, users of the system can tell the state of the focal real-world phenomena based on the state of the information system. They interpret and assign a meaning to the information system state. This meaning should correspond unambiguously to the state of the focal real-world phenomena if they were to observe the real-world phenomena directly. Insofar as the information system’s states may reflect implementation issues (e.g., the length of transaction queues at a point in time), multiple information system states may map to the same real-world state while still being in compliance with the mapping condition.

2. Tracking Condition: An information system must faithfully track state changes that occur to its focal real-world phenomena as a result of interactions between things within the boundary of the focal phenomena (internal events).

When a state of the focal real-world phenomena is disrupted (i.e., changed) because of some action of a thing outside the boundary of the phenomena (i.e., an external event occurs), transformation laws that are internal to the focal real-world phenomena act to restore the phenomena to a stable state. The phenomena may go through a sequence of state changes until an equilibrium (a stable state) is once again attained. An information system must faithfully represent the sequence of internal state changes that occurs to its focal real-world phenomena as a result of the actions of internal transformation laws.

3. External-Event Condition: An information system must provide a faithful representation of any change in the state of its focal real-world phenomena that arises due to the action of a thing in the environment of the focal realworld phenomena (an external event).

External events trigger state changes internally to the focal real-world phenomena (internal events). An information system must have a faithful representation of all external events that occur in its focal real-world phenomena. Otherwise, it will not be able to initiate sequences of internal events that faithfully represent the sequences of internal events in its focal real-world phenomena that are triggered by the external events.

4. Sequencing Condition: An information system must faithfully record the sequence in which external events occur in its focal real-world phenomena.

External events trigger sequences of internal events in an information system’s focal real-world phenomena. If an information system is to maintain a faithful representation of these sequences of internal events, it must update its representation according to the same sequence of external events experienced by the focal real-world phenomena.

In Figure 1, the STM and RM are shown as independent models. The two models are related, however, in that a tacit assumption of the STM is that the machine-readable script that implements the information system can provide a faithful representation of the focal real-world phenomena (the focus of the RM). For instance, if the script cannot clearly distinguish things and events, the STM conditions cannot be met.

## The Good-Decomposition Model

Wand and Weber (1995, p. 213) state:

Our decomposition model is motivated by the belief that representations which manifest “good decompositions” of the real-world system convey more meaning about the real-world system they model than representations that are not well decomposed.

In the good-decomposition model (GDM), they propose a set of conditions that a decomposition (a level structure of systems and subsystems) should satisfy if a script is to better convey to stakeholders the meaning of the focal real-world phenomena. As with the STM, the GDM and the RM are related in that the GDM assumes that the script can provide a faithful representation of the focal real-world phenomena (the focus of the RM). For example, if the script cannot distinguish between things and events, the GDM conditions cannot be met.

Initially, Wand and Weber (1995, pp. 213-215) proposed three necessary conditions:

1. Determinism Condition: Given a set of external (input) events that occur at the system level in some focal realworld phenomenon, a decomposition that represents the phenomenon is good only if for every subsystem in the decomposition the events induced in each subsystem are either external events or well-defined internal events.

Events are either well-defined (the subsequent state can always be predicted based on the prior state) or illdefined (the subsequent state cannot be predicted based on the prior state). If a script that represents a real-world phenomenon is based upon a good decomposition of it, the only events that can be ill-defined in subsystems of the decomposition are external events (those that arise in subsystems as a result of the actions of things in the environments of the subsystems). All internal events must be well-defined.

2. Minimality Condition: A decomposition is good only if all subsystems and systems manifested in a script that represents some focal real-world phenomena have no state variables, which are representations of real-world properties of things, that are redundant (i.e., state variables never needed during the life of an information system which, based on the script, is eventually implemented to represent the focal real-world phenomena).

The minimality condition seeks to ensure that the existence of redundant state variables in the system and subsystems of the decomposition does not confuse users of the script as to their meaning. In other words, the state variables that describe the decomposition should be only the necessary and sufficient set needed to represent the focal real-world phenomena.

3. Losslessness Condition: A decomposition is good only if it preserves all hereditary and emergent properties in its focal real-world phenomena.

Real-world things have two types of properties: hereditary properties that the thing and the thing’s components possess; and emergent properties that only the thing possesses (they are properties of the “whole”). Emergent properties bear some type of relationship, sometimes unknown and/or undefined, to hereditary properties. Both types of properties are needed to represent the focal realworld phenomenon faithfully. They must be preserved in any decomposition of it.

In addition to the above three conditions, Weber (1997, pp. 158-163) subsequently drew on prior work (e.g., Simon 1996; Yourdon and Constantine 1979) to propose that a good decomposition needed to satisfy two other necessary conditions:

4. Maximum-Cohesion Condition: A subsystem of a decomposition is maximally cohesive if its output state variables (those whose values are changed by internal events) cannot be partitioned based on a partition of its input state variables (those whose values are changed by external events) (Dromey 1996).

In other words, a subsystem is maximally cohesive when it cannot be broken up into two independent subsystems—its output and input state variables are inextricably connected via the transformation to their values that occur through events in the subsystem (Dromey 1996).

5. Minimum-Coupling Condition: A decomposition has minimum coupling if and only if, for all subsystems of the decomposition, the number of external events occurring in each subsystem of the decomposition is less than or equal to the number of external events occurring in any other decomposition that has the same and equally cohesive subsystems.

The minimum-coupling condition posits that as many events as possible occurring in the subsystems of the decomposition should be internal events, all of which are well defined, rather than external events, some of which are ill defined. To the extent the behavior of a subsystem manifests internal events rather than the impact of things in its environment (external events), the events that occur in the subsystem are more predictable. As a result, users will be better able to understand the meaning of events that occur in the subsystems.

The definition of minimum coupling refers to decompositions that have the same and equally cohesive subsystems because coupling and cohesion are related: performing more functions internally reduces a system’s coupling but often reduces its cohesion. A good decomposition balances the two criteria. Rather than show the relationship between cohesion and coupling as an explicit interaction (e.g., in Figure 1), the GDM accounts for it only by defining minimum coupling in terms of two equivalent decompositions (those that have the same and equally cohesive subsystems). For equivalent decompositions, the more loosely coupled one is preferred. Weber (1997, p. 160) notes, however, the need for more work on this issue.

## A Framework to Assess the Representation Theory Program

Having described RT, we now propose a framework to answer the questions raised earlier: How good is RT? How should RT research proceed? Initially, we expected to answer both questions by referring to well-known criteria on theory evaluation (Bacharach 1989; Dubin 1978; Edwards and Berry 2010; Shoemaker et al. 2004; Weber 2012). Such criteria reflect a product-oriented perspective on theory: the theory is viewed as a symbolic construction explicated at a point in time (just as we described RT above).

Because RT has been applied, tested, and critiqued for over 30 years, however, we concluded we needed a processoriented perspective to evaluate RT. From this perspective, a theory is a social construction that evolves through the interpretations and actions of those who engage with it over time (Rescher 1996). Accordingly, a good theory does more than just explain specific phenomena; it also supports a research program (e.g., by surviving refutations and revealing new phenomena) over time (Lehoux and Foster 2012).

## Development of the Framework

When we searched for frameworks for guiding theoretical programs, one of the few we found was Gray and Cooper’s (2010) work on the pursuit of failure. Motivated by Popper’s (1959) view on falsification, they argue a theory benefits if scholars pursue its failure because more faith can be placed in robust theories. In addition to recommending a general direction of pursuit—toward failure—Gray and Cooper describe four strategies that researchers can use to pursue failure: (1) identify important tacit assumptions in a theory that are questionable; (2) delineate a theory’s boundaries; (3) seek out competing theories; and (4) rather than assume away findings that contravene a theory, try to explain them.

Extending Gray and Cooper, we argue effective theoretical programs do not pursue failure alone but instead engage in a dialectic underpinned by the pursuits of success and failure. Preference should not be given to the pursuit of success or failure alone, because scientific progress involves both accepting and challenging theories (Kuhn 1996). The dialectic between them can spark ideas, interest, and momentum. The dialectic reflects a productive tension between breaking up systems of thought (transcendence) and building them up (synthesis) (Alvesson and Sköldberg 2009), developing novel ideas and maintaining old ones (McKinley et al. 1999), and engaging in convergent and divergent thinking (Kuhn 1991).

Motivated by these ideas, we sought a framework to characterize the pursuit of success that could complement Gray and Cooper’s framework. We could not find one at the same level of abstraction. Nonetheless, at a higher level of abstraction, Kaplan’s (1998/1964) framework is complementary. He argues researchers can pursue success by improving a theory’s intension (the explanation it offers), extension (the phenomena it explains), or both. Kaplan’s ideas suggest four strategies for the pursuit of success. Improving a theory’s intension involves refining its constructs to achieve a given explanation (e.g., removing unnecessary constructs). We refer to this strategy as intension (reduction). Improving a theory’s extension involves explaining new phenomena (adding new constructs to represent new phenomena) or improving explanations of phenomena the theory purportedly covers (increasing the explained variance in the phenomena). We refer to these two strategies as extension (addition) and extension (precision). Finally, improving both a theory’s intension and extension involves jointly altering its explanatory constructs and the phenomena it covers.

## Elements of the Framework

Gray and Cooper’s and Kaplan’s frameworks each propose an overall direction that researchers should pursue (toward failure or success). Each also suggests strategies to use. Because Kaplan’s ideas are at a higher level of abstraction, we use them to provide a unified framework for pursuing both success and failure. Figure 2 shows the different directions our proposed framework covers.

Figure 2’s axes reflect Kaplan’s ideas of intension and extension. By setting an arbitrary point in the figure to reflect a theory’s current state, we can depict pursuits of success and failure by showing how researchers’ actions move the theory in any given direction.

The diagonal line H-D reflects the meeting point of the two types of pursuit. Consider a move to H, which reflects a reduction in the theory’s intension and extension. For instance, researchers may decide a theory does not provide a good explanation of one of its outcome constructs. They may drop the construct from the theory as well as the constructs used to explain it. The result is a more parsimonious theory covering fewer phenomena.

Compared to a move to H, a move to the region between A-H achieves greater extension for any given decrease in a theory’s intension. Gains in parsimony come with less loss in explanatory power. We see this outcome as a success. In contrast, compared to a move directly to H, a move to the region between H-G loses more extension for a given decrease in intension. Gains in parsimony come with greater loss in explanatory power. We see this outcome as a failure. At the extremes, a move to A is a success because the theory can be simplified with no loss of explanatory power, whereas a move to G is a failure because the theory as it stands cannot provide the explanatory power it purports to offer.

Contrasting region A-H with region H-G helps clarify Gray and Cooper’s arguments. We believe they see moves to H as ideal. The outcome is more parsimonious, circumscribed, and refined theories. Figure 2 shows, however, their call for researchers to pursue failure also means they were admonishing researchers to pursue success. When researchers move a theory to H, success and failure are two sides of the same coin.

The same arguments apply to Kaplan’s ideas, but in the opposite direction. Consider a move to D. Such a move increases a theory’s intension and extension. For instance, researchers could add constructs and associations to a theory to help it explain new phenomena. The result is a larger, more powerful theory. Compared to a move directly to D, a move to the region between C-D achieves a greater increase in explanatory power with less loss of parsimony—a success. In contrast, a move to the region between D-E achieves a smaller increase in explanatory power for greater loss of parsimony— a failure.

At the extremes, a move to C is a success because researchers gain explanatory power with no loss of parsimony. This outcome could be achieved by increasing the precision of the theory’s constructs and associations and/or the procedures for testing it empirically (Kaplan 1998/1964). A move to E is a failure because researchers have to add constructs to the theory to maintain the same explanatory power (e.g., reflecting efforts to shore up a theory to explain mounting anomalies; Kuhn 1991). As the line to D adjoins these regions, studies moving to D can be viewed as engaging in the joint pursuit of both success and failure.

Having described regions A-G and C-E, the success and failure of points B and F are self- evident. A move to B reflects the ideal of simplifying a theory and gaining explanatory power. A move to F reflects the worst-case scenario in which researchers show a theory cannot explain what it purports to explain even with additional constructs.

In sum, Figure 2 can be used to describe any move to progress a theoretical program, whether toward success (the upper-left diagonal), failure (the lower-right diagonal), or both (the line H-D). The different directions are emergent results of the strategies researchers pursue. As Kaplan’s framework is at a higher level of abstraction than Gray and Cooper’s, their four strategies do not map neatly to individual points in the figure. While we believe they saw H as ideal, their strategies could result in moves across the bottom-right region (D-H), with the direction in any given instance of research depending on how researchers enact the strategy.

## Using the Framework

The framework in Figure 2 provides a comprehensive account to assess theoretical programs in terms of (1) the eight strategies researchers can enact (Kaplan’s four strategies plus Gray and Cooper’s four strategies), and (2) the resulting direction they pursue (the eight directions in Figure 2). Together, they underpin the four general approaches to describe and assess theoretical programs that we propose in Table 2.

By examining the results of the strategies used in specific papers, researchers can learn about a theory’s specific strengths and weaknesses (Table 2, Cell A). By looking at patterns of results over time, they can also learn about promising strategies for future work (Table 2, Cell B). Researchers can also focus on the overall directions that emerge from these strategies. For instance, they can learn about a theory’s merits by seeing how well it withstands pursuits of failure and supports pursuits of success (Table 2, Cell C). Likewise, they can look for patterns in pursuits of success and/or failure. Effective programs are likely to show evidence of a dialectic between the pursuits of success and failure (Alvesson and Sköldberg 2009, p. 206; Kuhn 1991, pp. 139-140; McKinley et al. 1999, p. 637) (Table 2, Cell D).

## Literature Review Procedures

Based on our framework, our literature review method drew on several approaches (Bandara et al. 2015; Boell and Cecez-Kecmanovic 2015; Paré et al. 2015; Rowe 2014; Vessey et al. 2002; Webster and Watson 2002). It involved four steps (see Appendix B for details):

1. We identified published research that referenced the seminal papers that introduced RT (Wand and Weber 1990, 1993, 1995). We identified 334 relevant papers.

2. With two independent coders, we categorized the 334 papers broadly, examining their focus and intent (Vessey et al. 2002), type of reference to RT (e.g., theory application, critique, advancement, or empirical test), element of RT addressed (building on the classifications used in Recker 2011; Wand and Weber 2002), research method (Chen and Hirschheim 2004), and evidence obtained (GRADE Working Group 2004). We sought to gain an appreciation of the literature as a whole so our detailed coding was couched within an understanding of each paper’s context. During this process, we found 22 papers that were ancillary to our goals. Thus, 312 papers were categorized.

## Outcomes to Date of Pursuing Success and Failure with RT

In this section, we discuss what we have learned from literature that has pursued success and failure with RT. Specifically, we address the first question that motivated our review: How good is the theory? Recall, our assessment of “goodness” is based upon (1) the extent to which particular strategies for pursuing success or failure in the program reveal specific strengths or weaknesses in RT, and (2) whether RT withstands efforts to pursue success or failure in a particular direction (Table 2). In this light, we structure our assessment around the strategy and direction of the pursuit the literature has undertaken (Figures 3a and 3b).

## Literature Manifesting Pursuit of Success Only

Figure 3a (four leftmost bars) shows most strategies used to pursue success focused on improving RT’s extension rather than its intension. Indeed, we found no paper pursuing intension by reduction. Instead, all 38 papers that pursued success did so through extension, either through addition (8 papers) or precision (11 papers), or through a combination of intension and extension (19 papers).

Literature that shows the application of RT to new phenomena (extension by addition) includes Wand and Wang’s (1996) data-quality metrics, Heales’ (2002) system replacement outcomes, Soffer and Wand’s (2007) system design metrics, and Burton-Jones and Grange’s (2013) system use metrics. Overall, we judge these papers to show successful extensions of RT through addition. Nonetheless, while a wide range of outcomes was evident in absolute terms, these papers were few in number relative to the 312 papers in the full sample. Moreover, none of the new phenomena examined were later examined by other researchers. In sum, while these extensions by addition are signs of success, they have not led to major shifts in RT.

We also found some literature attempted to improve RT’s extension by enhancing research procedures and measurements (extension by precision) (e.g., Opdahl and Henderson-Sellers 2004; Rosemann and Green 2000, 2002; Parsons and Cole 2005; Recker and Rosemann 2010; Recker et al. 2011; Rosemann, Green, and Indulska 2004; Rosemann et al. 2009). As we reviewed this literature, we noticed that researchers rarely adopted innovations made by others. Instead, each innovation was usually limited to one paper or set of papers by a single research team. For instance, the procedures and constructs developed by Gemino and Wand (2003, 2005) are not found in the work of Rosemann and his colleagues (and vice versa). In short, for some reason the successes achieved through extension by precision have not diffused widely.

A third type of pursuit of success involved attempts to improve RT’s explanations by adding constructs from other theories (combined intension and extension). This was often achieved by using new mediating constructs to improve theoretical explanations, such as (1) Gemino and Wand’s (2003, 2005) and Rockwell and Bajaj’s (2004) use of theories of learning and cognition to explain how ontological clarity affects individuals’ understanding of real-world phenomena, (2) Recker et al.’s (2006) use of theories of technology acceptance to explain how ontological clarity affects user acceptance of a grammar, and (3) Bowen et al.’s (2006, 2009) use of cognitive-load theory to examine how RT might explain task performance. Overall, we judge this work to improve both the intension and extension of RT to be limited but successful.

The absence of literature showing pursuit of success by intention (reduction) can be interpreted in different ways. For instance, no attempts might have occurred. Alternatively, any attempts that occurred were unsuccessful (and therefore faced difficulties being published). The absence of major changes to RT’s intension could also be a manifestation of the theory’s success, in that its constructs and associations have proved robust over time.

Figure 3b shows the direction of the pursuit of success in the region between H-D, moving clockwise from H. Overall, we found the directions pursued in the literature were unbalanced. For example, Figure 3b shows we found no papers pursuing direction H (smaller intension explains less extension) but 20 pursuing direction D (larger intension explains larger extension). It also shows we found 18 papers pursuing direction C (same intension explains greater extension) but none pursuing direction A (smaller intension explains same extension) or direction B (smaller intension explains greater extension). These patterns suggest most attempts to pursue success focused on RT’s extensions, consistent with our earlier observation about the strategies undertaken to pursue success.

Finally, most literature we examined pursued success in relation to the RM and not the STM and GDM. We found 4 studies that examined RT as a whole and 22 that examined the RM. We found none that examined the STM, however, and only two that examined the GDM (see Appendix B, Table B6, for details). On the one hand, the larger number of papers pursuing success via directions C and D (greater extension) most likely were motivated by early research that showed how RT and, in particular, the RM could be used to account for conceptual modeling and structured analysis phenomena. Researchers then saw how RT and the RM could be used to explain other phenomena (e.g., data modeling, object-oriented modeling, process modeling, web services, and activity-based cost modeling). On the other hand, to date the STM and GDM have not provided a fertile basis for further research.

## Literature Manifesting Pursuit of Failure Only

Figure 3a (four rightmost bars) shows all four strategies for pursuing failure included in our framework were evident in the literature. Moreover, unlike the pursuit of success, the distribution of papers across different types was balanced. This finding shows that over time the RT research program has explored a range of circumstances under which the limits of RT might become apparent. For instance, researchers have attempted to identify weaknesses in RT through challenging its assumptions, identifying contexts where RT does not work, and undertaking contests with competing explanations. They have also sought to understand outcomes that prima facie do not fit RT’s predictions. Their work has led to a better understanding of auxiliary hypotheses and the methodological and empirical settings in which boundary conditions emerge. They have also unearthed contradictory evidence and to some extent provided alternative explanations.

Nonetheless, the extent to which the literature has pursued failure does not allow us to reach clear conclusions about RT’s limitations. Specifically, many types of pursuits of failure operate on the level of rhetoric and not theory development or empirical evaluation. This situation was most evident in the pursuit of failure associated with challenging tacit assumptions. We found eight papers that sought to identify and critique RT’s tacit assumptions. All argued RT (or the RM specifically) could not support the explanations it purported to offer. Some were strongly worded—arguing that RT’s assumptions are vague (Wyssusek 2004), inappropriate (Allen and March 2006a), or a dead-end (Klein and Hirschheim 2006). Others critiqued assumptions of sufficiency (Gehlert and Esswein 2005; Guizzardi 2005; Strong and Volkoff 2010) or totality (Mendling and Recker 2007; Recker et al. 2010). Yet none proposed solutions in the form of alternative assumptions, let alone empirically tested alternative assumptions.

Similarly, we found the extent to which failure through competing theories was pursued was limited. The literature reporting this type of pursuit mostly contained preliminary conceptual arguments. In this regard, Table B6 shows 13 papers identified competing theories that originate from fields such as semantics (Rosemann and Wyssusek 2005; Wyssusek 2006), pragmatism (Ågerfalk 2010), cognition (Allen and March 2006a; Evermann 2005; Veres and Mansson 2005), and alternative ontological benchmarks (Allen and March 2006b; Guizzardi 2005; Milton 2007; Milton and Kazmierczak 2004). Few competing theoretical explanations and predictions were developed in detail, however. Moreover, we know little about how robust comparisons of RT to other theories could be undertaken. Only two papers we examined (Recker, Rosemann, and Krogstie 2007; Wand et al. 1995) suggest ways to compare RT against competing theories. Also, only two papers (Allen and March 2006b, 2012) engaged in an empirical test of a competing theory. Each yielded equivocal results (Allen and March 2006b; Shanks and Weber 2012). In sum, the pursuit of failure with RT has produced neither conclusive results nor strong support for an alternative theory.

Figure 3b shows the direction of the pursuit of failure in the region between D-H, moving clockwise from D. Overall, Figure 3b shows we found the literature varied substantially in the direction it pursued. A small number of papers took directions E (7) and F (2) (larger intension for same or less extension), a larger number took direction G (22) (same intension for less extension), and none took direction D by pursuing failure alone (larger intension for less extension) (i.e., 31 papers in total).

At first glance, the result for direction D may seem confusing. To clarify this issue, note that D lies on the border of pursuing success and failure. When researchers pursue research in this direction, they can therefore frame their research in one or both ways. Table B6 simply shows the 20 papers coded as direction D all pursued both success and failure, or success alone (i.e., none pursued failure alone).

Recall, we argued one indicator of strong pursuit of failure would be an extensive literature that fell into the region D-E. This literature would show RT achieves small increases in explanatory power at a cost of lost parsimony. We did not find this outcome. Several papers suggested more constructs be added to RT to enhance its explanatory power (e.g., Herrera et al. 2005; Rosemann and Wyssusek 2005), but none tested their assertions empirically.

We also argued strong pursuit of failure would manifest in literature that pursued direction F in Figure 2. This literature would show RT cannot explain what it purports to explain even with more constructs and associations. Figure 3b shows we found only two papers that pursued direction F, however (Mendling and Recker 2007; Wand et al. 1995). Both present conceptual arguments, but neither provides empirical evidence to test its assertions.

By contrast, Figure 3b shows we found most literature pursuing failure followed direction G (the existing explanation offered by RT is smaller than expected). Most were papers describing empirical work. As expected, they reported tests of boundary conditions and instances of counterexamples. They did not describe systematic attempts to pursue failure, however. Rather, they pursued failure in the form of boundary conditions and counterexamples primarily as a byproduct of failed empirical tests or post hoc explanations of results. Some reported counterexamples without detailed explanations, such as where researchers failed to find support for predictions about the negative effects of construct overload (Shanks et al. 2010; Shanks et al. 2008) or construct redundancy (Davies et al. 2004; Green and Rosemann 2001, 2002; Recker et al. 2010). Some were papers in which failure to support a proposition led to delineation of boundaries, such as those that found support for RT when testing actual but not perceived ease of understanding (Burton-Jones and Meso 2006) or complex but not simple models (Weber 1996).

As with the sole pursuit of success, most literature that pursued failure alone focused only on RT and, in particular, the RM. We found 6 papers that examined RT as a whole and 24 that examined the RM. We found none that examined the STM, however, and only one that examined the GDM (see Appendix B, Table B6, for details). Perhaps the STM and GDM have not yet been developed sufficiently to warrant pursuit of failure. Perhaps, also, failures in the STM and GDM have not been pursued because both have been of little interest to researchers.

In sum, much like literature that pursued success alone, literature that pursued failure alone does not reflect a cumulative body of programmatic work. Rather, it manifests diverse studies, each of which raised doubts about parts of RT, but it is not a cohesive body of work that allows researchers to draw firm conclusions about RT’s limitations.

Nonetheless, the literature that pursues failure alone provides insights about RT’s potential limitations that can underpin further research. This is most evident in work on construct redundancy (Davies et al. 2004; Green and Rosemann 2001, 2002; Recker et al. 2010). To date, the research consistently has obtained inconclusive results, which indicates a need for more systematic work on the effects of construct redundancy—for example, to determine if construct redundancy can be eliminated from RT (and hence its intension can be reduced).

## Literature Manifesting Joint Pursuit of Success and Failure

In Figure 2, the joint pursuit of success and failure can be viewed as the line between H and D. Figure 3b shows we found no papers pursuing direction H but 20 pursuing direction D. As we noted earlier, based on how researchers framed their work, only 10 of the direction D studies jointly pursued success and failure—a small subset of the 312 studies. Of these 10, eight examined the RM as a whole, one examined RT, one examined the GDM, and none examined the STM (see Appendix B, Table B6 for details).

Two aspects of these 10 studies are noteworthy. First, nine engaged in the joint pursuit of success and failure using a strategy of delineating boundaries (pursuing failure) and introducing constructs to explain phenomena across the boundaries (pursuing success). They typically added moderator constructs to explain why RT worked well only in some contexts. For instance, some argued pragmatic factors had to be taken into account—for example, users’ knowledge of the phenomena shown in a script (Bera et al. 2014; Burton-Jones and Weber 1999; Milton et al. 2012), users’ cognitive load (Bowen et al. 2006, 2009), the complexity of the phenomena represented (Milton et al. 2012), and the modeling purpose, tool, and conventions used as well as modelers’ roles and experience (Recker et al. 2010; Recker et al. 2006).

Second, while the literature manifesting joint pursuit of success and failure is smaller than the two sets of literature that addressed success or failure alone, it contained interesting papers that pushed RT in new directions. For instance, Strong and Volkoff (2010) showed the value of extending the three structures proposed by RT to include latent organizational structures, Hadar and Soffer (2006) showed how the RM could be used to produce different sets of guidelines with different ontological benchmarks (each yielding different conclusions), and Green et al. (2011) reported counterexamples and then carried out a study to test possible explanations of these counterexamples.

## Overall Assessment of the Literature: How Good is RT?

Using a programmatic perspective, what conclusions can we draw about RT’s “goodness” as a theory based upon our review of literature that has pursued its success and failure (detailed in Tables B4 and B5)? What have we learned about RT? Table 3 summarizes our assessment.

Using our earlier framework (see Table 2, column 1), does this assessment indicate RT provides a robust (strong) account of the phenomena it purports to explain? Moreover, has it supported a program of research through surviving refutations or revealing new phenomena?

From the perspective of pursuing success through intension, the results are mixed. The RM appears to be a robust, parsimonious theory. It has been used extensively to account for phenomena in the conceptual modeling field, and little change has occurred to its constructs and associations. Over time, it has underpinned a large number of research efforts. A similar outcome has not occurred, however, with the STM and the GDM. They have been little used, which may reflect that they are uninteresting, difficult to operationalize and test, or both.

The literature pursuing success with RT’s extension, and the RM in particular, suggests RT accounts for a wider variety of phenomena than first conceived. Because empirical tests of the proposed extensions are lacking, however, RT’s potential remains uncertain. Similarly, over time, empirical procedures that can be used to test the RM have improved. Nonetheless, given the length of time that has elapsed since the RM was first proposed and its extensive use, these advances are scarce. Moreover, they have not had widespread adoption.

If the pursuit of failure had resulted in failure, we would see RT refuted or a more precise theory—one that accounted for a smaller, better-defined set of phenomena. Again, the results are mixed. Some literature suggests RT as a whole, and the RM in particular, may have problematic assumptions. Some literature also suggests alternative theories provide better accounts of RT’s focal phenomena. In summary, in our view, despite many years of research, RT’s relative merits and failures remain unclear. The work to date suggests that RT can account for phenomena of interest to researchers and that it can provide an effective account of the usefulness of representations, within the bounds of the assumptions guiding such research. On the other hand, the work to date has also pointed out many limitations. Such pursuits of failure, however, have been inconclusive. RT has not been unequivocally refuted, nor has compelling evidence been provided to show its tacit assumptions and boundary conditions are problematic, nor have robust competing theories emerged, nor have a large number of counterexamples accumulated.

## Generating Further Theory Development: How Should Research on RT Continue?

Our account of the pursuit of success and failure of RT to date shows much work remains to be done before a verdict about its merits can be reached. In this section, therefore, we provide examples of how RT might be refined, extended, and evaluated through the pursuit of success and failure. On the one hand, our goal is to show both RT’s overt and latent potential. On the other hand, we believe RT must be scrutinized further to determine if its ideas should be retained, modified, or discarded. We thereby address the second question that motivated our literature review: How should the research continue?

We propose that RT research should proceed more systematically by actualizing our proposed framework. To achieve concreteness, the following sections provide examples to illustrate how researchers can achieve this outcome. Following Table 2 (column 2), two principles informed our examples. First, we provide examples that cover the different strategies in the framework and are motivated by clues from the strategies in past work. Second, we have sought to highlight some nontraditional and novel uses of RT (different from those found in our review, in Tables B4 and B5) to encourage more researchers to engage in pursuing its success and failure and to provide more opportunities to engage in a productive dialectic.

## Promoting the Pursuit of Success

In the subsections below, we describe how researchers might use each of Kaplan’s (1998/1964) strategies for pursuing success—via intension, extension, or both.

## Pursuing Success Through Intension

One way to pursue success through intension is to seek greater parsimony. How might this outcome be achieved? Consider RT’s predictions that (1) instances of construct redundancy in an information system grammar will cause confusion among its users about which construct they should use to represent a given phenomenon, and (2) instances of construct redundancy in scripts will undermine their semantic clarity.

Several studies have found, however, that the existence of construct redundancy in grammars does not always cause problems for users (e.g., Green and Rosemann 2001; Recker et al. 2010, Recker et al. 2011). Perhaps users cope with redundant constructs simply by working with only one of the redundant constructs. Perhaps they are not troubled by instances of different redundant constructs because each still represents clearly users’ interpretation of the focal real phenomenon (Clarke et al. 2016, Supplementary Appendix). Future research that pursues the success of RT through intension could investigate whether eliminating construct redundancy from the RM can be achieved without substantially undermining its explanatory and predictive power. If deleting one of RM’s constructs has little impact on its ability to account for variations in the semantic clarity of scripts, the pursuit of success through intension (reduction) would be achieved.

Before construct redundancy and its association with a script’s semantic clarity are removed from the RM, however, the robustness of earlier research results showing construct redundancy has little effect on users should be evaluated carefully. Earlier work has relied on Bunge’s (1977) highlevel ontology as the ontological benchmark in the RM. Other ontologies, such as Searle’s (1995, 2006, 2010), could be used as a benchmark to see if the effect of construct redundancy in a grammar depends on the ontological benchmark used.

Similarly, construct redundancy may have deleterious effects only when the number of constructs representing a particular ontological construct or the number of ontological constructs that have redundant constructs exceed certain thresholds. The possible existence of such boundary conditions needs to be investigated systematically through a program of research before determining whether to delete construct redundancy from the RM.

## Pursuing Success Through Extension

The pursuit of success through extension can occur in two ways: (1) explaining new phenomena not previously explained by the theory (addition), or (2) improving explanations of phenomena the theory already purports to explain (precision).

Pursuing Extension (Addition): Our review showed researchers have not been interested in the STM. We suspect one reason is that its predicted outcome (maintenance of a faithful representation) appears overly academic rather than practically useful. In this light, we propose that a program of research on the STM might progress successfully if researchers focused on whether its four conditions could explain other relevant outcomes.

For instance, consider auditors’ perceptions of risk. We expect the extent to which financial information systems comply with the four state-tracking conditions will be associated with auditors assessing the risk of material misstatements in an entity’s accounts to be lower. If the systems enact the four STM conditions, the accounts are more likely to provide a faithful reflection of their focal phenomena (International Accounting Standards Board 2010, p. 18).

One way in which researchers might investigate an association between compliance with the four state-tracking conditions and auditor perceptions of risk is to employ process mining logs (van der Aalst 2011). These logs can be used to examine whether an instantiated process complies with a documented process (Jans et al. 2014; Rebuge and Ferreira 2012). For instance, in a program of research on the STM, the logs could be used as follows:

Compliance with the Mapping Condition: Does each process state manifested in the event logs map to a lawful state that might occur in the real-world phenomena? If not, the information system process is not a faithful representation of the properties of the phenomena or the state laws that constrain its property values.

Compliance with the Tracking Condition: Does each internal event manifested in the event logs map to a lawful internal event that might occur in the real-world phenomena? If not, transformations (activities) that occur in the information system do not faithfully represent transformations that exist in the phenomena.

Compliance with the External-Event Condition: Providing a history of external events in the real-world phenomena can be accessed, does each external event manifested in the event logs represent an external event that occurred in the real-world phenomena? Similarly, does each instance of an external event in the real-world phenomena map to an external event in the information system? If not, external events that occurred in the phenomena have not been faithfully reported to the information system.

Compliance with the Sequencing Condition: If a history of external events in the real-world phenomena is available, does the sequence of external events manifested in the event logs correspond to the sequence of external events that occurred in the real-world phenomena? If not, external events in the information system may have triggered internal events that do not faithfully represent internal events in the real-world phenomena. As a result, any analysis of the mined process (as instantiated in the system’s event logs) would produce flawed conclusions, which would provide evidence the information system as implemented is not useful.

Using event logs, the proportion of violations of each of the four state-tracking conditions in a set of events can be calculated (e.g., based on a sample of events from the logs). Across different information systems, the proportions then can be evaluated to determine whether they are statistically significant predictors of auditors’ assessment of the level of risk associated with the systems. The relative importance of violations of each of the four state-tracking conditions as a predictor of assessed risk can also be determined.

Pursuing Extension (Precision): The RM predicts that increases in the levels of construct excess and construct overload in a grammar will undermine the ability of the modeling grammar to generate clear scripts (Figure 1). The RM predicts only the sign of the association, however, and not its functional form.

One way to pursue success through extension would be to articulate more precisely how construct excess and construct overload affect a script’s clarity. For instance, researchers might hypothesize the associations follow a sigmoid (Sshaped) form. If this functional form were to hold, low levels of construct excess and construct overload would have only a minor impact on a script’s clarity (perhaps because users can cope with any confusion that occurs). As the levels of these two constructs increase, however, at some point a script’s clarity would begin to decrease markedly. Finally, at high levels of the two constructs, further losses of clarity would be small (because a script already causes substantial confusion among users).

Refining the two associations in this way is an example of pursuing success through extension (precision). RM’s original constructs and associations have been kept, but the associations have been articulated more precisely.

## Pursuing Success Through Intension and Extension

RT says little about the contexts in which information systems might be used. We believe, however, it has an implied focus on only certain kinds of context. For instance, much research on user comprehension of information systems scripts employs contexts that involve cognitive tasks such as problem solving or decision making.

We see opportunities to apply RT in other contexts—contexts that we suspect were not envisaged when RT was developed. Achieving this goal, however, is likely to require a more refined notion of representational faithfulness. Refining the notion of representational faithfulness so it applies to different contexts presents opportunities for pursuing success through intension. At the same time, new contexts also present opportunities for pursuing a program of success through extension.

One way to categorize the contexts in which information systems are used is via the Cynefin framework (Kurtz and Snowden 2003; Snowden and Boone 2007):

Simple: These contexts are characterized by stability and clear cause–effect relationships. Decision makers are advised to sense the facts of the situation, categorize the facts using known taxonomies, and respond using wellestablished decision rules.

Complicated: These contexts are characterized by multiple possible solutions and cause–effect relationships that are somewhat unclear or clear only to experts. Decision makers are advised to sense the facts of the situation with expert advice, analyze the facts with expert advice, and respond based on expert advice.

Complex: These contexts are characterized by uncertainty, unpredictability, and unclear cause–effect relationships. Decision makers are advised to probe the situation to identify patterns, sense those patterns that are most compelling, and respond based on the patterns that are most compelling.

Chaotic: These contexts are characterized by high turbulence, unclear cause–effect relationships, and acute time pressures. Decision makers are advised to act to try to reestablish some order, sense what seems to work rather than seek right solutions, and respond based on what seems to work best.

Disorder: This context exists when it is not clear which of the other four contexts dominates a situation.

Within RT, we suspect the concept of representational faithfulness was conceived implicitly with a simple and perhaps a complicated context in mind—for instance, operational control and management control contexts. In these contexts, the real-world phenomena to be represented by an information system seem reasonably evident. Moreover, in these contexts, the potential benefits of having information systems that possess clear, complete representations seem apparent.

The nature of representational faithfulness and ultimately usefulness in complex and chaotic contexts, however, is unclear. Indeed, the nature of useful information systems in complex and chaotic contexts is uncertain. In complex environments, presumably information systems would be deemed useful if they support probing the situation and the environment, identifying patterns, and evaluating patterns. In chaotic environments, presumably information systems would be deemed useful if they support fast action to assist with the reestablishment of some order, sense-making activities, and action taking on the part of decision makers.

A program of research to pursue success through intension would refine the construct of representational faithfulness so it remains meaningful in complex and chaotic contexts. This would lead to an understanding of the phenomena information systems must represent in these contexts and thus the nature of useful information systems in these contexts. Concurrently, research to pursue success through extension would (1) identify, define, and operationalize what “usefulness” means in complex and chaotic contexts (such as users abilities to probe the real-world phenomena, pattern-match, engage in sense-making, and take fast actions), and then (2) test whether higher levels of representational faithfulness are associated with higher levels of the usefulness of information systems designed to support these contexts. Both of these pursuits would benefit from interpretive methods that encourage researchers to engage in an “intimate dialogue” with practitioners (Corley and Gioia 2011, p. 23) and understand how they may interpret representational faithfulness and usefulness differently depending on the context.

## Promoting the Pursuit of Failure

Gray and Cooper’s (2010) key argument is that the pursuit of failure is fruitful not only to identify when theories should be abandoned but also to improve and develop theories. In the subsections below, therefore, we use their four strategies for pursuing failure to provide some examples of how RT’s merits might be evaluated aggressively.

## Pursuing Failure Through Examining Tacit Assumptions

Pursuing failure through tacit assumptions involves teasing out unstated assumptions that appear to underlie RT and subjecting them to theoretical evaluation and empirical testing. One outcome could be rejection of RT in toto if the assumptions are deemed pertinent and do not hold; another could be clearer articulation of the boundaries of RT.

A tacit assumption that appears to underlie RT is that the usefulness of information systems increases monotonically with increases in their representational faithfulness. Such a relationship might not always hold. For instance, in some cases, information systems might rarely, if ever, provide useful support for stakeholders who undertake unstructured tasks, regardless of stakeholders’ perceptions of the level of representational faithfulness. In other cases, information systems might still provide useful support for stakeholders who undertake unstructured tasks, but characteristics other than representational faithfulness could be far more important to their usefulness. For instance, a more important characteristic might be whether an information system allows stakeholders to better understand the unstructured task they are undertaking through exploring alternative scenarios (e.g., Arnott 2006).

The effect of task structure as a potential moderator on the relationship between faithfulness and usefulness could be explored in several ways. For instance, survey work could be done that elicits participants’ perceptions about (1) the level of structure of the primary task they undertake, (2) the representational faithfulness of the primary information system they use in this task, and (3) the perceived usefulness of the information system in this task. In such surveys, the measure of task structure developed by Lynch (1974), the measures of representational fidelity developed by Burton-Jones and Grange (2013, p. 655) or perceived deficit, redundancy, overload, and excess developed by Recker and Rosemann (2010), and the measure of perceived usefulness developed by Davis (1989, p. 340) could be used.

RT also appears to assume tacitly that the associations between constructs in the RM, STM, and GDM (Figure 1) will hold irrespective of the quality of the ontology used to evaluate a grammar’s ontological expressiveness. For instance, in the RM, a grammar may be deemed ontologically clear and complete but relative to a poor-quality ontology. The scripts produced also might be deemed ontologically clear and complete (again, relative to the poor-quality ontology). Because the scripts have been evaluated based on a problematic ontology, however, their ability to faithfully represent the focal real-world phenomena from the perspective of stakeholders may be limited. The predicted positive benefits of ontological expressiveness and ontologically clear and complete scripts might be swamped, therefore, by the negative effects that arise from relying on a poor-quality ontology. Similar considerations apply to the STM and GDM. For instance, applying the STM presupposes the use of an ontology that minimally covers things, states, and events.

Still another tacit assumption that RT appears to make is that instances of ontological constructs (and therefore instances of grammatical constructs that represent the ontological constructs) can “faithfully” represent stakeholder perceptions of meaning. RT does not provide a theory of meaning, however, to support such an assumption. Nonetheless, theories of meaning based on the notions of sense and reference (see Speaks 2016) seem relevant.

For instance, Bunge’s (1974a, 1974b) two-volume work develops a theory of semantics of conceptual languages (a subset of natural languages that include languages generated via information system grammars). These languages use instances of constructs to represent human perceptions about a domain. In conceptual languages, meaning can be viewed as a property of a construct—its sense and reference (Bunge 1974b, pp. 45-50). Bunge (1974b, p. xi) defines a construct’s sense as the “collection of its logical relatives,” and a construct’s reference as the set of “individuals occurring in its domain of definition.”

Such a theory of meaning could be used to better understand those circumstances under which instances of constructs in information systems scripts capture the meaning stakeholders ascribe to focal real-world phenomena. For instance, to the extent a script better represents the sense of each instance of a construct (via the network of connections among instances of constructs), presumably stakeholders should deem the script more faithfully represents the meaning they ascribe to the focal real-world phenomena. Similarly, to the extent each instance of a construct in a script is defined more precisely (the phenomena covered by the instance are clear and thus its reference is clear), presumably stakeholders should also deem the script more faithfully represents the meaning they ascribe to the focal real-world phenomena. If the ability of instances of constructs to represent the meaning of real-world phenomena is not supported during empirical tests, however, RT’s merits (and the merits of information systems modeling more generally) are undermined.

## Pursuing Failure Through Examining Boundaries

We noted that many boundaries identified in the RT literature to date are byproducts of empirical work. One way to tease out RT’s boundaries further would be to investigate whether RT’s explicit assumptions hold under a wide range of circumstances. Another way is to investigate how potential moderators affect its associations (Edwards and Berry 2010, p. 676). These strategies are notably scarce in RT’s research program to date.

Recall that RT explicitly assumes that stakeholders can agree on and articulate their perceptions of the meaning of the focal real-world phenomena. RT does not enunciate a theory of meaning, however, that predicts or explains why or in what circumstances such agreement could be reached. Instead, it appears to adopt a pragmatic approach to stakeholders resolving the meaning of some phenomena—one that is based upon “a theory of commonsense, practical, everyday semantics” (Stamper 1987, p. 57) whereby stakeholders negotiate and eventually reach a consensus (perhaps only temporarily) about their views of some phenomena (e.g., Habermas 1984, 1998; Wittgenstein 1968, p. 20, Section 43). If this assumption were not to hold in certain contexts, an important boundary condition exists.

We encourage researchers to seek out such boundaries. For instance, in novel or complex domains, stakeholders may be uncertain about the meaning of the focal real-world phenomena and unable to reach a consensus view. Also, when perceptions of meaning underpin how stakeholders believe power is distributed in an organization, agreement about meaning may be difficult to obtain (Markus 1983). Consider hospitals, in which power and institutional differences often undermine attempts to reach agreement on how an information system should be designed and used in hospitals (e.g., Doolin 2004; Lapointe and Rivard 2005). It would be valuable to test RT’s boundaries in such a context. For instance, members of different professional groups (e.g., nursing, medicine, allied health, billing) are likely to see a patient’s “problem” differently and these differences of opinion may lead to disagreements about whether an implemented information system is able to provide an accurate and complete representation of the patient’s problems (Dunn and Jones 2010; Kuhn et al. 2015).

The identification and testing of RT’s boundaries could be investigated via a program of field-study research. For instance, we might predict that dysfunction among stakeholders will reduce their ability to agree on the meaning of their focal real-world phenomena. To test this prediction, cases could be selected to achieve good variations in the levels of stakeholder dysfunction in different contexts. Data could be collected via stakeholder interviews. Interpretive analyses of the data (Klein and Myers 1999) could then be used to evaluate whether the level of dysfunction moderates stakeholder perceptions of relationships between the representational faithfulness and usefulness of the information systems they employ.

## Pursuing Failure Through Examining Competing Theories

Gray and Cooper (2010, p. 629) contend “[t]he most dramatic way to pursue failure is via a contest between competing explanations.” Such tests are difficult to conduct because different theories are seldom directly comparable (e.g., due to having different assumptions and boundaries) (Cooper and Richardson 1986). These difficulties might be why our review revealed few instances of well-developed competing theories. The most common attempt in prior work was to compare other ontological benchmarks to Bunge’s (e.g., Allen and March 2006b), but this is not a test of competing theories because RT is not tied to a particular ontological benchmark (e.g., Wand and Weber 1993, p. 221).

To encourage better tests of competing theories, we outline three tests that researchers might pursue. The first and purest test would involve keeping all elements of RT constant and just hypothesizing a different sign on a relationship. For instance, RT predicts that increases in representational clarity will increase an information system’s usefulness, but one could predict a competing (opposite) prediction. Indeed, some evidence supports such a prediction, namely, that cogni tive conflict can help individuals when learning new conceptualizations (Kang et al. 2004). If scripts containing unclear representations stimulate cognitive conflict, this circumstance could enable stakeholders to construct improved knowledge about unknown or changing real-world phenomena. Researchers could investigate this proposal by conducting experimental tests of the effect of faithfulness on usefulness in different contexts and using measures of cognitive conflict to explore the competing theoretical account.

A second test could pit RT against a theory that takes a similar representational assumption but that does not overtly rely on ontological analysis. For instance, Clarke et al. (2016) argue that because any ontology presupposes an underlying logic, some characteristics of representations can be explained more parsimoniously and powerfully through logical analysis than through ontological analyisis. To demonstrate this idea, Clarke et al. noted that researchers had tried to use the RM to explain the problems caused by optional properties in CM scripts, but a satisfactory solution had eluded them. Clarke et al. then showed how a satisfactory explanation could be offered through logical analysis alone. From a competingtheory perspective, researchers could take the analysis in Clarke et al. further to identify phenomena for which logical analysis is sufficient, not requiring additional ontological analysis. One way to carry out such work would be to use the the new grammar (ERM-R) in Clarke et al. ERM-R is a modification of the standard entity-relationship model (ERM) grammar that has the property of always creating scripts that meet certain logical criteria. Researchers could then analyze the results of prior research that used RM in the context of the standard ERM grammar and try to replicate them using ERM-R. If the replication is unsuccessful, one possibility is that the phenomena can be accounted for by logical analysis without need for further ontological analysis. That is, the RM may offer no value over logical analysis when representing such phenomena.

A third test could pit RT against a theory that agrees with the value of representation but argues that representational perspectives alone are insufficient. Such a view is taken by rulebased approaches to modeling (Hirschheim et al. 1995). Rule-based approaches do not deny the value of analyzing representations or engaging in ontological analysis (Hirschheim et al. 1995, pp. 215-218), but they broaden the focus to include pragmatics. Whereas RT views pragmatics as outside its scope (and merely used to clarify its boundaries), rulebased approaches view pragmatics as primary (and without which semantics cannot be understood) (Hirschheim et al.

1995, pp. 202-203; also see Ågerfalk 2010). To understand pragmatics, and hence semantics, rule-based approaches focus on identifying the rules for using language in specific user contexts to facilitate communication and action (Hirschheim et al. 1995, pp. 198-209). From a competing-theory perspective, researchers could test the RM against a rule-based approach by conducting experiments in contexts in which pragmatics are likely to be salient. Researchers could first conduct two analyses: an ontological analysis of the domain (e.g., using the RM and Bunge’s 1977 ontology), and a pragmatic analysis of the use of language in that domain (e.g., using speech act theory; see Searle 1969; Wand et al. 1995). Researchers could then conduct a two-group experiment, with participants from the domain randomly assigned to each group. The common task in each group could be to construct a model of the domain that is considered to be (1) a faithful representation of the domain, and (2) useful by those who use an information system in that domain. The first group would be required to construct the model using guidelines based on the RM and the preceding ontological analysis; the second group would be required to construct the model using guidelines based on rule-based modeling and the preceding analysis of language. The models would then be rated for their faithfulness and usefulness by two independent raters with expertise in that domain. The resulting ratings would shed light on the relative power of RT and rule-based approaches in such contexts.

## Pursuing Failure Through Examining Counterexamples

Gray and Cooper (2010, p. 630) argue “[t]he ruthless pursuit of failure should also entail understanding exceptions—cases that do not fit the theory.” In this regard, for the most part empirical studies have supported the RM’s predictions about the deleterious effects of construct overload, excess, and deficit in a grammar. Nonetheless, exceptions have occurred. For instance, Recker et al. (2011) obtained good empirical support for the RM’s predictions about the detrimental effects of these three characteristics, but also noted that some participants in their study had no adverse experiences with grammars that possessed these characteristics.

These exceptions in Recker et al.’s (2011) study allude to contexts in which construct overload, excess, and deficit are not problematic—for example, instances of construct excess may allow users to devise workarounds (e.g., using unstructured text) to mitigate the detrimental effects of other kinds of deficiencies in a grammar. If the nature of these contexts could be better elucidated, RM’s boundaries could be articulated more precisely. We believe the most useful way to pursue exceptions is via a program of field study work where the nature of the exceptions can be explored in depth to determine if they can be resolved or, alternatively, if they remain persistent in multiple contexts. Recker et al.’s (2011) study provides clues about contexts in which evidence is likely to be found that contradicts the RM’s predictions.

## Some Overall Implications

In the preceding sections, we provided an overview of RT, proposed a framework for assessing what can be learned of RT’s merits, and offered suggestions for how RT research should proceed more systematically by actualizing the framework. In this section, we outline several overall implications.

First, our analyses show that researchers who studied RT have pursued neither success nor failure systematically enough. This may reflect institutional or ecological factors. For instance, work on RT has progressed in fairly isolated ways via a number of research teams geographically dispersed, each of which followed one particular stream of research (typically pursuing either success or failure rather than both). Our review should help these researchers see how they can draw on each others’ work and proceed more systematically in the future.

Second, we have seen a lack of programmatic work involving the pursuit of both success and failure, in a productive dialectic (as per Table 2, cell D). In this light, note that science often benefits when researchers pool resources and form heterogeneous teams to conduct systematic programs of work (Kahneman 2003; Weick 1989). Accordingly, researchers who primarily have pursued failure in their use of RT could join forces with researchers who primarily have pursued success. A joint endeavor could wring the best from both types of research. For instance, together they could design studies to clarify whether RT is coupled to a specific theory of meaning and what ramifications, if any, this might have, putting to rest some of the debates on this issue. Researchers pursuing success and failure would both benefit from such an investigation.

Third, we scoped our paper by focusing primarily on theoretical issues rather than methodological issues. By focusing on a specific theory (RT), we may have inadvertently prioritized theory (and hence deductive research) over equally important, more inductive forms of research (Locke 2007). For this reason, several of our examples for future research involved inductive strategies. Whether the findings from inductive work lend themselves more to the pursuit of success or failure is unclear. This unpredictability could work to the advantage of the program, however, by reducing the effect of a priori biases. To date, few inductive studies of the representational aspects of information systems have been done (Patel et al. 1998).

Fourth, researchers need to be mindful of the possibility of other types of pursuits. Although we believe the joint pursuit of success and failure will help with RT’s evaluation, other approaches could be taken instead, or as well. For instance, another approach would be to jointly pursue fundamental understanding while striving to help practice (Corley and Gioia 2011; Stokes 1997; Tushman and O’Reilly 2007). For this pursuit to occur, more work with practitioners is needed to determine where RT is interesting and valuable. For instance, researchers could hold workshops with industry representatives who could scrutinize examples of the types of research conducted to pursue success and failure to date. They could also determine if and how such work has relevance for them (Rosemann and Vessey 2008). The RT literature contains only one such example (Recker, Indulska, and Green 2007).

A final, supplementary implication arises from the construction of our framework to assess pursuit of success and failure in research programs. We are not aware of a similar framework. Many research programs exist within the IS field. We believe our framework offers a useful means for assessing and improving other, perhaps even competing, research programs (e.g., Allen and March 2006a, 2006b, 2012).

## Some Limitations of Our Review

Our work has several limitations. First, the RT literature may be reviewed using different strategies (Paré et al. 2015; Rowe 2014) and objectives (Rivard 2014; Webster and Watson 2002). In our identification and classification of papers, we used a broad, inclusive scope. In our analysis of papers, however, we focused on one issue—namely, the pursuit of success and failure. This allowed us to pinpoint and classify research that spoke most directly to RT’s strengths and limitations and make sense of the work from the perspective of an overall evaluation of RT. Nonetheless, other types of reviews should be conducted, such as meta-analysis (Saghafi and Wand 2014), to examine RT from different angles, such as the strength of its predictions, the validity of operationalizations, and methodological confoundings.

Second, our assessment of RT and its research program is an interpretive inquiry in which we socially constructed our shared understanding of RT and the research it motivated. Hermeneutics reminds us that theories can take on a life of their own over time and escape the limits of any one interpretation (see Sarker and Lee 2006, pp. 134-135). Our interpretations are likely to be affected by biases stemming from our engagement with RT over many years (Greenwald 2012). In executing our review, therefore, we tried to use principles of interpretive research (Klein and Myers 1999). For example, we used principles of dialogical reasoning in our joint discussions to establish a shared understanding of RT. Likewise, we used principles of suspicion to question each of our viewpoints and to tease out biases and distortions in constructing the tables that summarize our interpretations. Finally, we attempted to undertake a credible analysis by providing a traceable, documented justification of our research processes and hiring external researchers to assist us with independent coding.

Third, some researchers might criticize the entire basis for our review. Our arguments are based on the assumption that long-term programs of research can benefit if they proceed systematically. Some researchers oppose this view and advocate a laissez faire approach (e.g., Feyerabend 2010; Law 2004). While we believe we are in good company in recommending some level of systematicity (Lakatos 1970; Laudan 1977; McKinley et al. 1999), we accept the merit of this approach is ultimately an empirical question.

Fourth, some researchers might criticize how we carried out our review. Our coding sometimes required an interpretation of the intent and outcomes of a reported study and the way the authors framed it. In some instances, the direction of a type of pursuit of failure or success was difficult to determine because the intent and study framing were unclear. We discussed these cases extensively until we arrived at a shared understanding.

## Conclusions

In this paper, we undertook a focused, thematic, and cumulative review of RT—a theory proposed over 30 years ago to account for the nature and purpose of information systems. We have undertaken this review because we believe RT is potentially important. It focuses on phenomena that many researchers believe reflect the essence of an information system. It has also motivated a considerable, sustained research program—initially within a small community of researchers who focused on relatively narrow topics, but now increasingly within a larger community of researchers who see RT as useful in more diverse contexts.

In carrying out our review, we were motivated to develop a concise description of RT, as we perceive it to stand today, to provide a context for our review and in the hope it will prove useful for researchers interested in taking up research on RT.

We also developed a new framework for evaluating programs of research. We believe we obtained insightful results by applying this framework to RT. In light of our experience, we believe other researchers will find merit in applying our framework to assess other research programs in IS and elsewhere.

Have we reached a final verdict about the value of RT? Should we encourage colleagues and students to engage with RT? Much like our assessment of RT, we believe the answer to these questions must be given as a dialectic. On the one hand, our view is that a conclusive verdict on the overall value of RT cannot yet be reached. We found the research done to date shows signs of success as well as failure; but both types of research remain too limited to reach clear-cut conclusions. On the other hand, we have reached a clear conclusion about how future research on RT can be conducted systematically, independent of the attitude scholars bring to the research. We have provided a framework for researchers seeking to contribute to the evaluation of RT, irrespective of whether they wish to pursue success or failure. If researchers adopt the framework and our suggestions for using it, we believe the field will be better placed to reach a more conclusive verdict about the merits of RT.

## Appendix B

## Literature Review Procedures

## Step 1: Paper Identification

We started our review by identifying published research that referenced RT. We first debated which papers were the seminal papers that described the original theory. We agreed upon three: Wand and Weber (1990, 1993, 1995). Using Harzing’s (2010) publish-or-perish tool, we obtained a total of 1,022 records of papers citing these three papers by July 2013.

To scope our review, we decided each paper in our sample should pass a minimum level of quality. We used two heuristics. First, we determined that each paper should itself be cited at least once according to Harzing’s publish-or-perish tool. Eliminating uncited papers resulted in a list of 770 paper records, which still included duplicates for those papers citing more than one of the three seminal works. The removal of duplicates (53) and the removal of records of papers written in a language other than English (42) resulted in 675 records. Second, because some years may elapse before papers are cited (Allen et al. 2009), we followed suggestions to apply different thresholds for papers outside a five-year window (Straub and Anderson 2010). By consensus, we decided that any paper published over five years earlier (i.e., before 2009) required a minimum of 10 citations to be considered impactful. In contrast, we did not place a minimum limit on citations for papers published during or after 2009 (other than the original requirement of at least one citation).

We identified 202 records of papers published before 2009 with less than 10 citations. To ensure this threshold would not lead to Type-II errors, each of us reviewed these 202 records. We felt some of these papers might still be impactful in the years to come. Through a voting process in which a paper required at least one vote to be considered relevant, 29 of these 202 records were deemed relevant, while the rest were removed. We also added some papers that at the time of data collection were forthcoming or in-press (and have since appeared as publications). These steps resulted in 502 records of potentially relevant papers. We then sought to obtain the 502 papers. We were unable to locate six papers. The remaining 496 papers were included in our analysis.

All papers were processed to enable full-text search. The full-text search allowed us to identify the part of each paper that cited the theory and to determine whether the paper was actually engaging with RT or simply citing it in a cursory manner or for purposes unrelated to our study (e.g., referencing the foundational papers simply to support definitions of “ontology” but not engaging with the RM, STM, or GDM in any way). Following this process, one of us identified 162 papers that cited the seminal works in a cursory manner or for purposes completely unrelated to our aims. Another of us then analyzed the 162 papers and confirmed their lack of relevance. We thus considered 334 papers for categorization.

## Step 2: Paper Categorization

We next categorized the 334 papers using the categories shown in Table B1. We hired a postdoctoral researcher who had completed a Ph.D. using RT to complete the coding. We explained the categories to him and conducted two pilot tests to evaluate the quality of his coding. With each pilot test, we compared his coding with our own. Where inconsistencies arose, we continued to clarify the categorization scheme ove three iterations until his coding and ours indicated we had consensus.

During categorization, the coder identified some papers he viewed as irrelevant to our study (e.g., papers citing the seminal papers but referring to RT simply as an example of an ontology). One of us subsequently reviewed these papers to check for lack of relevance. As a result, 22 papers were removed, which resulted in a final set of 312 papers to code.

To ensure coding reliability, we employed a second postdoctoral researcher familiar with RT to independently categorize a random sample of 52 papers (just over 16% of the 312). We then compared the reliability of the two coders’ work based on percentage agreement and Cohen’s (1960) Kappa. Kappa understates agreement when a specific category is used rarely. This problem occurred with two coding categories (where Kappa was <0.05). In both cases, however, the percentage agreements remained high (> 92%). Moreover, when these two categories were excluded, the overall average Kappa was 0.69. Values of 0.61 to 0.80 are considered substantial (Landis and Koch 1977). Thus, the coding appeared to be reliable.

## Step 3: Paper Coding

With the benefit of our broad categorization of the papers, we coded the 312 papers that used RT to determine the extent to which they engaged in the pursuit of success or failure. To identify pursuit of success, we started by listing all papers that our external coders identified (via the categories in Table B1) as reporting theoretical and/or methodological advances or extensions. To identify pursuit of failure, we started by listing all papers that our external coders categorized (again via the categories in Table B1) as referring to RT as conceptual foundation, test of theory, critique of theory, or reporting empirical evidence.

We then conducted two checks of the combined list. First, one of us reviewed each paper in the list to determine if he/she agreed that each paper reflected a pursuit of success or failure. Second, two of us reviewed the whole set of 312 papers to ensure other papers that pursued success or failure were not missing from the list. We held regular coding meetings to discuss and defend decisions until we reached consensus on the final list. The final list contained 69 of the original 312 papers. Such a small subset was expected because our aim was to assess how RT has been advanced (whether in the direction of success and/or failure). Papers that simply applied RT as is, without extending it, lay outside our review paper’s scope.

Once we had finalized the list of 69 papers, we developed 16 codes to classify each paper alongside two dimensions—eight directions of pursuit and eight strategies of pursuit. Table B2 and Table B3 summarize the coding criteria. Much like the rest of the coding process, we developed these codes iteratively through group discussion until consensus was reached.

With agreed codes established, two of us independently coded all 69 papers and met to discuss, defend, and revise the coding until we reached consensus. This process took three rounds. After the first round, the average percentage agreement was 0.85 and the average Cohen’s Kappa was 0.28. After the second round, the average percentage agreement was 0.93 and the average Kappa was 0.66. As noted before, Kappa is low when some categories are seldom found in the data (such as direction “B,” which had a percentage agreement of 99%, but a Kappa value of 0.00). Even when these categories were included, however, the average Kappa was 0.66 (i.e., within the 0.61–0.80 range considered “substantial”) (Landis and Koch 1977). Given this high level of agreement, the two of us who acted as coders then conducted one more round and resolved the remaining differences through discussion.

We highlight three aspects of our coding that have a key bearing on our results. First, as Figure 2 shows, coding the direction of pursuit requires knowledge of the theory’s starting point. The starting point was not always clear, however, in each paper we reviewed. Rather than articulating what explanatory concepts RT contained, what outcomes it explained, and how it was being extended, authors frequently took the first two for granted and focused on the third point alone. This approach sometimes made it hard to code the direction of pursuit. As noted earlier, one reason for this difficulty could be that descriptions of RT have not been summarized comprehensively in one place (with the exception of Weber’s 1997 out-of-print monograph), something our summary of RT above should help remedy for future research. Nonetheless, for the purpose of reviewing each paper, we simply coded the theory’s starting point based on our understanding of RT and our interpretive reading of each paper.

Second, to code those papers that we believed pursued the theory’s extension, we placed greater weight on papers that identified new phenomena outside conceptual modeling. The reason is that conceptual modeling has always been RT’s primary field of application. As in assessments of research programs in other fields (Kilduff et al. 2006), we wished to weight more heavily those outward-looking papers that increased the program’s empirical scope beyond its original application. Our approach is not meant to deprecate papers that advanced RT within the conceptual modeling field. Indeed, progress in this field has been substantial. We were simply more exclusive when coding strategies of extensions within the conceptual modeling field and more inclusive when coding papers outside conceptual modeling.

Finally, when coding papers that sought to propose an alternative (competing) theory in direct preference to RT, we only coded extensions made to RT and not potential extensions made to competing theories. Our main reason was that such papers did not seek to advance RT primarily but rather the competing theory they discussed. We generally coded such papers in terms of direction “G”and “tests of competing theories” to reflect the basic argument in these papers that RT offered a poorer account of the focal phenomena compared to the alternative theory they used or developed.
