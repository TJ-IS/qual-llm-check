## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Firm action User engagement Firm performance Mediating effect Information processing

## A B S T R A C T

As firms’ essential stakeholder, users can be evoked by the information from firms’ action. Users interpret relevant cues from the information and respond to it through engagement behavior such as online word of mouth through social media. Drawing on the information processing theory, this study develops a model of how user engagement behavior mediates the relationship between a firm’s actions and performance. The theoretical model is tested in two empirical studies. One uses actions of six representative Internet service firms and their users online word-of-mouth behavior over a 50-month period. The second is a two-stage controlled laboratory experiment designed to establish causal linkages between firm actions and user engagement. The study provides support for its hypotheses and offers several key findings. First, the firm’s actions influence user engagement behavior. Specifically, when a firm launches a larger number of actions, users are engaged in a higher volume of activities and deeper activities. When a firm employs actions that are simpler and easier to understand, users are engaged in deeper activities but not necessarily a larger volume of activities, Second, user engagement is a missing link between a firm’s actions and performance. Specifically, engagement depth positively mediates the effects of both action volume and action simplicity on performance. By contrast, engagement breadth positively mediates the effect of action volume but not the effect of action simplicity on performance. Collectively, the study contributes to a clearer understanding of the role of users in firms’ actions and provides key insights regarding how firms benefit from user engagement.

## 1. Introduction

Social media, such as blogs, wikis, and Twitter, engage users in new forms of response to firms’ actions [1]. When Amazon deleted thousands of book reviews from its website in 2012 without offering any public explanation, the action triggered intense debate among Amazon users everywhere on the Internet about the meaning of the reviews and the firm’s action [2]. The number of similar cases continues to increase. Through extensive use of social media to exchange brand- and product-related information, users have engaged in active discussions on the functional connection between eBay and PayPal, Expedia’s ser vice, Yahoo’s legal issues, and YouTube’s new capacities (see Appendix B for more examples). In a survey of 72,000 people in 60 countries [3], 47 % of respondents had commented on firms online.

How does user engagement behavior triggered by firm actions affect firm performance? Two relevant research streams may shed some light. One stream of literature is the competitive dynamics research on firm strategy that primarily addresses the linkages between firm actions and performance. The main argument is that specific and detectable actions initiated by a firm can help defend or improve its competitive position [4]. Competitive dynamics scholars have examined how a firm’s actions influence investor valuations [5], turnover in market leadership [6], or return on assets and sales [7]. Recent research suggests that the effect of firm actions on performance is a complex phenomenon that goes beyond simple, direct effects [5,8,9]. Nevertheless, the intermediate factors that channel the effect of firm actions are underinvestigated (see Appendix A for details).

The other stream of literature concerns linkages between user engagement behavior and firm performance. Recent research suggests that engagement behavior on social media creates a ripple effect that extends beyond the close social network of the users, through a wide group of users, affecting the firm’s performance [1]. Firms should ac count for users’ online word of mouth as representative engagement behavior in response to their actions [10–12] that have an important impact on performance [1,13–15]. Although anecdotal evidence has indicated clear linkages between firm actions, user engagement, and firm performance, the competitive dynamics and user engagement literature, however, is largely separated and neglects the broad linkages between these three variables.

Drawing on the information processing theory, this study develops a model on how user engagement behavior mediates the relationship between firm actions and performance. Specifically, this study examines how the breadth and depth of user engagement behavior mediates the relationship between a firm’s action volume/action simplicity and per formance. The theoretical model is tested in two empirical studies. In the first empirical study, we analyze a sample of six representative Internet service firms and 3846 of their actions in a 50-month period from September 2007 to October 2011. In the second empirical study, we conduct a two-stage $2 \times 2$ controlled laboratory experiment to establish causal linkages between firm actions and user engagement.

The study finds support for our hypotheses and generates the following findings. First, we find that a firm’s actions influence user engagement behavior differently. Specifically, when a firm launches more actions, users are engaged in a higher volume of activities and in more in-depth activities. When a firm employs actions that are simpler and easier to understand, users are engaged in more in-depth activities, but not necessarily more activities by volume. Second, our study sug gests that user engagement is a missing link between firm actions and performance. Specifically, we reveal that engagement depth positively mediates the effects of both action volume and action simplicity on performance. By contrast, engagement breadth positively mediates the effect of action volume but not the effect of action simplicity on performance.

This study offers several contributions. First, it contributes to engagement literature by theorizing and testing how firm action pat terns influence user engagement behavior, particularly online behavior, in response. Although users are active observers of and commenters on firm actions [2,16], scant research has scrutinized how firm actions influence user engagement behavior. This study improves the under standing of how engagement behavior may be triggered and influenced by firm actions and suggests that firms may manage their actions to provide strategic signals to the user market.

Second, the study complements strategy literature by providing a missing link between firms’ resource-related actions and their external market performance [6,17]. The findings reveal that firm actions enabled by its internal resources influence performance through user engagement behavior, such as online word of mouth, and that this behavior is an influential external resource for the focal firm. The mechanism is consistent with the stakeholder literature, which argues that favorable interpretations made by key stakeholders are critical to firms’ establishment of market advantage [5]. Our findings also suggest that user engagement behavior, such as online word of mouth, has strategic importance to firms.

Third, the study contributes to the existing research by showing a strategic way in which user engagement behavior benefits firms. The existing research has argued that favorable interpretations made by key stakeholders are critical to firms’ establishment of market advantage [5, 18]. Our findings suggest that for users, as key stakeholders, an effective way for them to express their opinions is user engagement behavior; and this behavior may allow firms to better realize the strategic impacts of their actions on their online performance. This effect further justifies the strategic importance to firms of influencing user engagement behavior.

## 2. Theory and hypotheses

Although scholars have highlighted the importance of examining the consequences of firm actions through the perspective of stakeholders [19], such as competitors in the market [20] and external investors [5], the interpretation of the process from firm action to performance from the perspective of users has long been missing. Users are firm’s essential stakeholders and are easily evoked by the properties of firm actions. They interpret relevant cues to form impressions about a firm. Firm stakeholders are individuals or groups that can affect a firm’s perfor mance or are affected by the actions and achievements of the firm [21]. Stakeholders may be involved in formal (e.g., employees, suppliers, users, and public agencies) or informal relationships (e.g., media and special interest groups) with the firm. They often pay attention to the focal firm’s actions because the well-being of the firm may affect the benefits they receive and their relationships with the firm [5,19]. Users are crucial external stakeholders because the focal firm’s strategic movements and performance eventually influence users’ usage and consumption decisions [16,22].

The importance of users has frequently been underscored by research on legitimacy, which emphasizes that favorable interpretations and evaluations by key audiences is critical if a firm wants to seize temporary advantages over rivals [18,23]. This argument is consistent with ob servations from practice that users commonly engage in the discussion of firm actions and their products and services. For example, news of firm actions on popular media websites such as WIRED News, Yahoo! News, and the online Washington Post can quickly reach hundreds of shares and dozens of comments by viewers. However, the role of the user in the process from firm action to performance has been largely over looked. This research vacuum is likely due to the underestimation of the value of user responses to firm actions or a lack of micro-level infor mation on user engagement behavior.

Our research framework is grounded in the information processing theory, which suggests that users process critical information from environmental stimuli [24,25]. We integrate this view to link the rela tionship between firm action and user engagement through theoretically arguing how firms’ action patterns evoke users’ attention to interpret relevant cues and stimulate engagement behavior such as online word of mouth.

## 2.1. Information processing theory

Information processing theory suggests that individuals’ judgments and behavior are typically based on limited information that is used at the time of decision-making [24,25]. Information that is top of mind is most likely to be used by individuals and to result in their responsive actions [26]. This accessibility of information can be influenced by a pattern of how the information was activated or used in the past [25]. For example, frequently encountered information continually appears in individuals’ memory. In this way, the frequency of repeated information activation enhances the accessibility of information in individuals memories and is more likely to influence their decision-making [27,28].

A firm’s actions, such as new product development, market expan sion, and strategic alliances, often convey critical information to users, and such information can be used by users to understand the firm’s strategic goals, evaluate the firm’s prospects [4,29], form opinions about the firm, and make consumption decisions and other responsive actions [5]. In this regard, using their actions to deliver useful infor mation to users, firms essentially invest in alternative communication channels to connect with end users, attract their attention, and build brand awareness in the market [30].

However, information processing problems often occur when users evaluate firm actions for two main reasons. First, some actions require a long-term resource commitment [31], which may make the implications of the actions less foreseeable to users [32]. Second, users often have limited resources and capacities to process related information [24].

When users have difficulties processing and understanding the infor mation carried in actions, they are less likely to respond to these actions through engagement activities and aid the firm in realizing perfor mance. By contrast, users will talk about firms’ products more frequently and evaluate them more favorably when receiving more cues from firms [33]. Thus, the focal firm must consider how to better deliver information through actions to users and aid users in processing this information.

When users engage in online discussion, firms that are top of mind and more accessible in their memory are most likely to become topics of discussion, and more discussions are likely to be generated about these firms [34]. If a firm intends to trigger more user engagement, it can use strategies to make its brand name top of mind and readily accessible to users. Users also differ in terms of their capability to understand the meanings and implications of a firm’s actions. Users with more insights and knowledge can become opinion leaders [35] and influence other users’ information processing through word of mouth, which is consis tent with the stakeholder view of users mentioned earlier. As word of mouth becomes a critical communication channel facilitating user pro cessing and understanding of information on a firm’s actions, the opinions generated by leaders ease the interpretation of actions and make them more comprehensible to other users.

## 2.2. Hypothesis on action volume and user engagement

Firms usually employ portfolios of various actions [6]. By controlling certain aspects of their action portfolios, firms can influence information delivery to users as well as the information processing performed by them [4]. This influence can make related knowledge of firms more accessible to users. For example, when a firm initiates actions more frequently, it conveys more information to users and produces more public visibility than if it does so less often. Higher public visibility makes the firm more familiar to users and produces more accessible knowledge among users [33]. Additionally, more frequent actions often convey more repeated and related information to users. Information repetition and relatedness assist users in understanding the underlying meanings and implications of information and improve the information accessibility of a firm to users. With improved accessibility, a firm can stimulate more user engagement behavior [34,36] and leverage the roles of users to realize performance in the end market.

Action volume reflects the competitive aggressiveness of a firm [7]. Firms can increase the volume of their actions to compete more inten sively for market advantage [37]. The literature suggests that the maintenance of competitive aggressiveness is critical to a firm’s estab lishment and preservation of market advantage [6]. From an informa tion processing perspective, an increase in action volume also means that firm information is released more frequently and intensively to an external audience, such as users.

From this perspective, the increase in information release triggered by a higher volume of actions also increases the frequency with which knowledge of a firm is updated and enhanced within users’ minds. The accessibility of knowledge of the firm is enhanced and, as a result, users are more likely to use their knowledge of the firm in engagement behavior and generate more discussions about the firm [36]. In this regard, action volume is likely to increase engagement breadth by improving information accessibility for users. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1a. (H1a): Competitive action volume has a positive impact on user engagement breadth.

Action volume is also likely to increase engagement depth by enhancing information accessibility for users. Users continue to expe rience and learn from various information sources, building improved knowledge that is accessible for future consumption decisions [38]. More than simply remembering information obtained from firms, engagement activities allow users to conveniently be involved in more in-depth discussion with others, digest information on firms’ actions, and assimilate their experiences with and understanding of firms [39]. With regard to this aspect, a firm’s action intensity is a vital approach through which users can improve their information accessibility and engage in more intensive interactions. Subsequently, higher action volumes can result in not only the receipt of more information on focal firms but also engagement in deeper interactions regarding the firms. Hence, we develop the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1b. (H1b): Competitive action volume has a positive impact on user engagement depth.

## 2.3. Hypothesis on action simplicity and user engagement

Simplicity is another key feature of a firm’s action portfolio [6]. The range of actions that firms implement varies. Although some firms may choose to focus on a narrow range of actions, others may pursue a broad range of actions. Some have argued that a firm should select a few key strategic processes with straightforward rules to succeed in a high-velocity environment [40,41]. These processes often consist of few simple and innovative actions to which decision makers can quickly respond in a fast-changing environment [42]. From an information processing perspective, maintaining a simple portfolio of actions allows a firm to make its actions more understandable to stakeholders, partic ularly external stakeholders [5]. Conversely, a diverse set of actions may negatively affect the perceived clarity of actions.

The theoretical perspective of information accessibility suggests that the accessibility of knowledge in memory is also influenced by the strength of its association with situational or informational features that exist at the moment of decision-making [25]. Because users often have limited cognitive resources devoted to understanding firm actions, the more explicit the cue that information can provide, the higher the degree of information accessibility decision makers can achieve (Wyer and Hartwick, 1984). Thus, for a firm with a simple set of actions, more users are likely to understand and possess accessible knowledge of the firm. Such accessible information allows users to engage in online discussions about the firm and generate more word of mouth. Thus, we expect that greater action simplicity increases engagement breadth as follows:

Hypothesis 2a1. (H2a1): Competitive action portfolio simplicity has a positive impact on engagement breadth.

In juxtaposition to H2a1, we also take into consideration the negative effects that a simple action portfolio might have on user engagement breadth. A simple portfolio of actions means there is less variety of in formation that the users can access about the firm, hence fewer topics that the users can talk about. Thus, for a firm with a simple set of actions, less variety of accessible information makes the users discuss in a narrow topic range and makes the actions less appealing to a broad audience as well [34], both of which will lead to less volume of word of mouth about the firm. We, therefore, develop a competing hypothesis to H2a1.

Hypothesis 2a2. (H2a2): Competitive action portfolio simplicity has a negative impact on engagement breadth.

Action simplicity can also assist users in developing deeper knowl edge that can then be used in engagement activities. When a firm focuses on a narrow range of actions, the information released in these actions is more relevant in terms of meaning and implication. From the informa tion accessibility perspective, relevant information repeatedly and spe cifically reminds users of the firm and enhances their understanding of it [27,28]. With more accessible knowledge of the firm, users are able to engage in more in-depth online discussions about the firm. In this re gard, we expect that greater action simplicity also increases engagement depth. Therefore, we develop the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2b. (H2b): Competitive action portfolio simplicity has a positive impact on engagement depth.

## 2.4. Hypothesis on user engagement and firm performance

User engagement activities are empowered by interactive Web 2.0 technologies, such as blogs, wikis, and Twitter, which have dramatically changed the landscape of information acquisition and processing. In the Web 2.0 era, users can easily share their experiences with and opinions of a firm with a potentially large number of peers [43]. This sharing of information may attract the attention of other prospective users and eventually influence the entire market response to firms [15]. User engagement, usually in the form of word of mouth in an online context, has become of strategic importance to firms. In practice, large com panies, such as Starbucks, have been aware of such trends and made users the core audience of their strategy [16].

Researchers have also increasingly emphasized the importance of user networks, groups, and communities [44]. As key firm stakeholders, users are considered influential participants in value cocreation with a firm [45]. The relevant literature indicates a positive association be tween a firm’s marketing performance and word-of-mouth activities (e. g., [46,47]). In this study, we consider a general aspect of a firm’s online performance: users’ online interest in the firm. This performance aspect is measured using users’ online visits to the firm’s website and page views. Higher numbers of online visits and page views indicate that the firm is more attractive to users online. The existing literature has sug gested that it is appropriate to use this performance indicator in research, because the attainment of online attractiveness is a necessary way for online firms to realize other forms of operational and financial performance, such as sales and profit [37].

We expect that both engagement breadth and depth contribute to a firm’s online performance. High engagement breadth indicates that more users are engaged in discussing and disseminating firm informa tion in the online space. This effect facilitates the development of long term reputation and brand recognition for the firm and enhances the firm’s online attractiveness [48]. As more information sharing and interpretation regarding the firm occurs among users through word of mouth, users perceive word of mouth to be more helpful [13] and easier to process [49]. Furthermore, increases in engagement depth increase the perceived cognitive effort that users devote to word-of-mouth communication, resulting in enhanced perceived word-of-mouth help fulness [50]. Given the same engagement breadth, increased engage ment depth has a stronger influence on users, because it aggregately enhances the attractiveness of a firm’s product or service to users [45] and makes users both more interested in the firm and more willing to accept the firm’s services. Therefore, we propose the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 3a. (H3a): Engagement breadth has a positive impact on firm online performance.

Hypothesis 3b. (H3b): Engagement depth has a positive impact on firm online performance.

## 2.5. Hypothesis on the mediating role of user engagement

User engagement is likely to mediate the effect of a firm’s actions on the firm’s online performance. A key reason for this mediation is that to improve performance, the firm first needs user reactions to their actions. Word of mouth is a crucial means by which users engage with and respond to firm actions, and these responses eventually influence the outcomes of firm actions in the market. When users are unfamiliar with the products or services offered by a firm, some users’ word of mouth aids others in accessing relevant information and making critical con sumption decisions (Ellison and Fudenberg, 1995). Additionally, when actions have implications that are not clear to users [31], some users engagement in the form of word of mouth aids other users in better understanding the firm and assembling knowledge that can be accessed during subsequent decision-making. Overall, user engagement mediates the effect of firm actions on firm performance by better engaging users in

the firm’s strategic processes [12].

The effect of action volume on firm performance can be mediated by engagement breadth and depth. An increase in action volume conveys more information about a firm to users. Engagement breadth facilitates the dissemination of information and promotes an awareness of the firm among a broader audience base. Engagement depth enables users to better digest and use information to influence others’ knowledge [45], causing the network effect to have a higher marginal effect. As a consequence, the effect of action volume is likely to be amplified through either engagement breadth or depth. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 4a. (H4a): User engagement mediates the impact of action volume on firm performance.

Similarly, engagement breadth and depth can mediate the effect of action simplicity on firm performance. A simple portfolio of actions, makes it easier for users to understand a firm. More users can then engage in information dissemination through engagement breadth, making the firm more appealing to others. Additionally, a better un derstanding of the firm allows users to generate more content outputs through engagement depth and attract others to the firm more effec tively [51]. Thus, the effect of action simplicity is also likely to be enhanced through engagement breadth and depth. Therefore, we develop the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 4b. (H4b): User engagement mediates the impact of ac tion simplicity on firm performance.

## 3. Study 1

## 3.1. Sample selection and data

Upon the causality evidence from firms’ action to user engagement behavior, we selected a sample of six representative firms, Google, eBay, Amazon, Expedia, Yahoo, and YouTube<sup>1</sup>, in the e-commerce and online information service industries to test the hypotheses. These industries are often characterized by rapid technological innovation and prevailing hyper competition [52]. These firms are top players in corresponding Internet submarkets, including search engine, auction, retailer, travel agent, web portal, and video sharing, measured by their web traffic by Alexa<sup>2</sup> . As users must be aware of firms’ actions before they can respond to the stimulus by action patterns, we focused on top Internet players in Study 1 to ensure enough market awareness to firms’ actions. Later in a lab experiment, we validate the causality from firm actions to user engagement behavior by using a less well-known Internet firm in another submarket as example.

We used three sources to collect longitudinal data on firm actions, user engagement, and online performance of these firms. To collect data on firms’ actions, we searched the headlines and abstracts of public news reports from September 2007 to October $2 0 1 1 ^ { 3 }$ . Following previous research, we defined actions as externally directed, specific, and observable new movements initiated by the firm to enhance its market advantage [20,53]. Based on this definition, we used Google News to search for actions that were reported in the public media and observable to end users, not including trivial actions that cannot be observed by the third party. Google News is a news aggregator of 4500 English language mainstream news sites including those of The New York Times, The Washington Post, Bloomberg, Reuters, Forbes, BBC News, Wall Street Journal, USA Today, CNN News and etc. Using this source, we identified 3846 unique news records of these six firms over the observation period.

Using the data on actions, we calculated each firm’s action volume (ActionVolume) and action simplicity (ActionSimplicity). ActionVolume was measured using the total number of actions of each firm in each month. ActionSimplicity was measured using a Herfindahl index (HI) of actions in each month, defined as $\begin{array} { r } { H I = \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { n } \left( x _ { i } \right) ^ { 2 } } \end{array}$ , where $x _ { i / x }$ was the proportion of the firm’s total actions in the ith category in a given month [6]. A low (high) level of HI means that the firm employed a broad range of different actions. In this case, ActionSimplicity is low (high).

User engagement behaviors was collected from Google Group (http: //groups.google.com), an online communication service provided by Google Inc. Google Group supports both Usenet newsgroups<sup>4</sup> and non-Usenet online discussion groups and is a valuable platform of user engagement as it covers a broad range of topics through many-to-many communications [55]. Google Group also includes an archive of Usenet newsgroup postings dating back to 1981 and currently contains more than 8 million online groups (including both Usenet newsgroups and non-Usenet online discussion groups)<sup>5</sup> . It has become a major platform for online discussion. Past research has suggested the value of Google Group in studying engagement behavior despite its limitations (e.g., [55, 56]). Using Google Group, users can join any discussion groups and participate in any threaded conversations using e-mail or web. Users can also set up mailing list archives for e-mail lists. An online discussion post contains the name of a newsgroup to which the post is sent, the author’s nickname, a subject line, date of the post, and message text. Posts are organized into threads that contain posts on the same topic. A thread is an analog of a many-to-many conversation among users.

We retrieved all newsgroups whose names contained the names of our sample firms. Posts in these newsgroups are mostly related to our sample firms. The final dataset contains 116, 888 distinct conversations (topics) with 509, 604 postings by 210,401 authors. On average, there were 176 active online groups every month. Using Google Group data, we constructed measurements for engagement breadth and engagement depth. Prior studies have used the number of newspaper articles mentioning a focal firm in a certain time period as the proxy for the firm’s media coverage and attention [57,58]. We followed these studies to use the total number of topics about a firm in each month (denoted as TopicCounts) to measure the breadth of engagement about the firm, as this measure captures the general scope and volume of discussion [55]. Also, prior literature has used the average frequency $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ intensity) that a firm is mentioned in a newspaper article to capture the depth of dis cussion about the firm [59]. We followed this approach to measure engagement depth using the average number of posts per author about a firm in each month (denoted as AuthorPosts). Higher AuthorPosts means that the firm was discussed more in-depth by users.

We used online traffic data of the firms to measure their online performance, which has been adopted in the existing literature [37,60]. The performance of Internet firms is dependent largely on the number of users who visit their websites to receive products/service. As a market-based performance measurement, Internet firms’ traffic data are an important nonfinancial performance indicator and has also been commonly used in practitioner research to measure and predict firms market advantage and potential revenue [56]. Besides, the traffic mea sure is a good alternative to financial performance measures given the nascent market nature that Internet firms are usually facing, commonly private nature of these new ventures, and significant relationship be tween traffic data and firms’ sales revenue in Internet business [37]. Following the literature [37], we collected online traffic data from Alexa.com, a major third-party company that tracks online traffic. We chose two variables to measure a firm’s online traffic: Reach and PageViews. Reach is defined as the number of Internet users visiting a firm’s website, out of every million Internet users. PageViews is defined as the number of times that a firm’s web pages are viewed, out of every million views of all Internet pages. Higher PageViews often contributes to the financial performance of the firm through advertising revenue.

## 3.2. Controls

We included two sets of control variables that may influence the relationships among firm actions, user engagement, and firm online performance. The first set of variables controls firm characteristics, including FoundingYear, TrafficRank (as reported by Alexa.com), and NewsRedundancy. A lower TrafficRank means the online service is more popular. We used one-period lag TrafficRank to capture any potential lag effect of online service popularity. NewsRedundancy measures the average number of times a published news report appears on different news agencies’ websites. We used one-period lag NewsRedundancy to capture any potential lag effect of news report. The second set of vari ables control both firm fixed effects and time fixed effects in the data analysis. To further control the firm-oriented effects, we incorporated the one-period lag variables for both outcomes (Reach and PageViews) and mediators (TopicCounts and AuthorPosts). ## 3.3. Analysis and results

## 3.3.1. Empirical methodology

To test whether user engagement mediates the relationship between a firm’s actions and its performance, we follow the multiple-step pro cedure suggested in Baron and Kenny [61] as well as its more recent developments [62–64]. In the first step, we test the relationships be tween predictors (ActionVolume and ActionSimplicity) and mediators (TopicCounts and AuthorPosts). In the second step, we test the relation ships between mediators and outcomes (Reach and PageViews), with the effects of predictors on outcomes controlled.

According to recent studies on mediation testing (e.g., [64]), the only condition required for the mediation argument is that the indirect effects $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . ,$ , the effects of predictors on outcomes through mediators) are sig nificant, and a significant relationship between the predictors and the outcomes is not considered a necessary condition for mediation. In contrast to the original work in Baron and Kenny [61], more recent research suggests that other tests in Baron and Kenny [61] are useful primarily for classifying mediation types, not for testing the existence of mediation [64].

To test the statistical significance of the indirect effects of strategic actions on performance through engagement, we use a bootstrapping method adopted in the literature (e.g., [65]). This method is analogous to the commonly used Sobel test [66]. But, it is considered as a better approach as it does not require the assumption on parameter distribu tion (e.g., normal distribution) [64,65]. To test the significance of esti mation, the bootstrapping method uses random samples repeatedly drawn from the original dataset with replacement to calculate the sample means and deviations. We repeat the sampling 5000 times to estimate the indirect effects through the mediators (i.e., the mediating effects).

We consider different performance variables in the four models, to ensure the robustness of our results. The performance variables in Model 1 and Model 3 are Reach and PageViews , respectively. In model 2 and model 4, we use one-period lead performance variables $( R e a c h _ { t + 1 }$ and $P a g e V i e w s _ { t + 1 } )$ to capture the potential time lag in the effects of strategic actions. ## 3.3.2. Results

The results of hypothesis tests are in Table 5a and 5b. Table 5a reports the results of the four regression models, and Table 5b reports the total, direct, and indirect effects of strategic actions on the perfor mance. As Table 5a indicates, ActionVolume has a significant positive effect on TopicCounts and on AuthorPosts in all models. These results reveal that the volume of actions helps generate more and in-depth postings, supporting H1a and H1b. We also find that ActionSimplicity has a significant positive effect on AuthorPosts , but an insignificant ef fect on TopicCounts , in all the models. Therefore, the results support H2b but not H2a.

In Table 5a, the rightmost column for each model reports the effect of engagement on firm performance, controlling for the effect of actions. We find a positive impact of TopicCounts (i.e., breadth of engagement) on performance in all the models. The data also yield strong support for the proposed positive influence of AuthorPosts (i.e., depth of engage ment) on performance in all the models. Overall, these results suggest that engagement positively influences the online performance of firms; hence, H3a and H3b are supported.

Table 5b reports the results of our tests for the total, direct, and in direct effects of actions on performance. The total effects of ActionVo $\boldsymbol l u m e _ { t - 1 }$ and ActionSimplicity on performance are both significantly positive in all models, implying that actions play important roles in determining the online performance of firms. This result is consistent with the literature on actions [5,40]. The total effect of actions consists of the direct effect and the indirect effect. We find significantly positive direct effects of both ActionVolume and ActionSimplicity on perfor mance in all models, controlling the mediating effect of user engagement.

The total indirect effects of ActionVolume on performance through user engagement (including both TopicCounts and AuthorPosts ) are both significantly positive in all models. In addition, the total indirect effects of ActionSimplicity on performance through user engagement (including both TopicCounts and AuthorPosts ) are significantly positive in all models as well. These results provide strong evidence that user engagement behavior mediates the effects of firm actions on firm per formance. Furthermore, the mediating effects of user engagement are mainly partial mediation as the direct effects of actions on performance are still significant. Therefore, these results provide partial support for H4a and H4b.

Comparing the magnitude of the direct effect with the total indirect effect of actions on performance, we find that their magnitude are comparable in the relationship between action volume and perfor mance, but the magnitude of the indirect effect is much smaller than the direct effect in the relationship between action simplicity and perfor mance. This observation implies that the magnitude of the mediating effect of engagement depends on the patterns in firms’ actions.

For both ActionVolume and ActionSimplicity , their total indirect effects on performance are a sum of an indirect effect through Top icCounts (i.e., engagement breadth) and an indirect effect through AuthorPosts (i.e., engagement depth). Results of all models in Table 5b reveal a significantly positive indirect effect of ActionVolume on performance through TopicCounts , suggesting a partially mediating ef fect of TopicCounts between ActionVolume and performance. We also find that TopicCounts<sub>t</sub> does not mediate the impact of ActionSimplicity<sub>t-</sub> on performance as indirect effect of ActionSimplicity through Top icCounts is insignificant in all models. Overall, these results suggest that the breadth of engagement partially mediates the impact of action vol ume on performance, but does not mediate the impact of action simplicity.

These results, in conjunction with the abovementioned significant direct effects of ActionVolume and ActionSimplicity , suggest that the depth of engagement partially me diates both the relationship between action volume and performance, and the relationship between action simplicity and performance.

Overall, the results reveal distinct mediating effects of user engage ment for different features of actions. Specifically, the breadth of engagement partially mediates the impact of action volume on perfor mance and the depth of engagement partially mediates both the impact of action volume and that of action simplicity on performance.

The results on control variables are largely consistent across different model specifications. TrafficRank has a significantly negative effect on both TopicCounts and performance. This is reasonable because firms with higher ranks are less popular firms in terms of discussion and online visits. The results also reveal that a firm’s founding year has a positive effect on TopicCounts . This indicates that newly established firms tend to attract a broader range of discussion.

## 4. Study 2

To further address the potential for endogeneity and investigate the causal relationship between firm actions and users engagement behavior, we conducted a follow-up two-stage 2 × 2 controlled labora tory experiment to analyze the changes of user engagement intentions, stimulated by the treatments of firm’s actions.

The treatments in the experiment were firms’ action volume captured by the amount of firms’ news reports and action simplicity captured by the diversification of firms’ action portfolios. Same as in Study 1, action volume was measured using the total number of actions of a firm in each month. Action simplicity was measured using a Herfindahl index (HI) of actions in each month. A low (high) level of HI means that the firm employed a broad range of different actions. In this case, action simplicity is low (high).

In the experiment, we use intention to engagement as a proxy for user engagement behaviors. The measures for an individual’s intention to engagement breadth and intention to engagement depth were based on a person’s communication with other persons like his or her friends and colleagues [36]. We also designed questions to measure the dependent variable intention to adopt a firm’s service as a proxy of users’ future adoption of the firms’ product. The study also control for individuals demographics (gender and education), Internet usage experience, and experience with engagement. For the analysis involving intention to adopt a firm’s service, we further controlled for individual attitude to the firm. All measures are five-point scaled. Appendix C describes the measures in detail.

## 4.1. Experiment design and procedures

The process of our lab experiment is two-stage. In the first stage, we introduced to participants the purpose and process of the experiment and the background about the focal firm, i.e., LinkedIn. Then they watched a two-minute commercial video of LinkedIn (titled “LinkedIn for Students- Your Career Starts Here” at http://www.youtube.com/w atch?v=YWp6AN00D\_c). As our experiment subjects were university students, the video was relevant to them and was used to attract their attention. Finally, participants filled up the first round of survey ques tionnaires about their background information and engagement inten tion, to discuss about the firm and to adopt the firm’s service, after knowing the firm (LinkedIn) from the video.

In the second stage, we provided news reports about the firm in paper format to the participants and asked them to read and make notes during reading. All news reports were real reports as we collected them from Dow Jones’s Factiva historic database. We manipulated the treat ment by varying the number of news and the number of action cate gories reported in the news. The coding was conducted according to Ferrier et al. [6] approach: we used keywords appearing in headlines and content to classify the news into different categories. Table 1 shows examples of key words and headlines for each of these action categories.

We kept news’ original format exported from the database. News content included news agency name, logo, reporter name, release time, word count, abstract, and main text. To facilitate the frequency effect under action volume, we compressed the news release time to be in one month. After reading the news reports, participants were asked again to report their intention for engagement and adoption of the firm’s service, along with other questions. Table 6 shows treatment manipulation on action volume and simplicity.

We chose LinkedIn as the focal firm to control for the respondents prior knowledge about the firm. First, when we designed and conducted the experiment in early 2014, LinkedIn had not entered China. Most Chinese were not familiar with the firm (supported by our experiment findings). Thus, individual participants’ engagement intention was triggered mainly by the news information we provided in the experi ment, and the noise of prior familiarity with the firm was largely avoi ded. This setting is important for testing causality relationships.

## 4.2. Data and analysis

After conducting a pilot test with 20 postgraduate and undergradu ate students in a Chinese university, we recruited 113 undergraduate students from the same university and conducted the experiment in February 2014. Prior to the experiment, these participants were told to receive \$5 as an incentive for participation. Also, we asked respondents about their familiarity with LinkedIn in advance, and only used those indicating that they “never heard this firm before” or “only heard the firm name before” for postexperiment analysis. Ten participants (8.8 % of the original sample) indicated that they “knew something about the firm before”, “has registered as a user” or “very familiar with the firm and the service” were therefore filtered out. We further removed 3 participants with missed response to important measurement questions. Finally, we used 100 valid questionnaires, with 25 for each treatment. In the process of data analysis, we used anonymous aggregated data, not involving individual-level data. Besides, this study was approved by the Institutional Review Board.

## 4.3. Manipulation check

We assessed the manipulation of action volume by asking partici pants’ feeling about the frequency of firms’ action and their estimation about the amount of news they read (refer to Table 6). The answers by high action volume treatment group are significantly greater than an swers by low volume group $( t = 4 . 1 9 / 4 . 4 9 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , respectively. We assessed the manipulation of action simplicity by asking participants feeling about the focus of the firm’s actions after they read news. The answers given by the high action simplicity treatment group are significantly greater than the answers given by the low simplicity group (t = 2.77 and p < 0.01). Thus, the manipulation of the two independent variables is successful.

## 4.4. Main tests

We used MANOVA [68] to test the effect of actions on individuals intention for engagement. We assessed the changes in participants intention for engagement breadth, engagement depth, and the adoption of the firm’s service by comparing between participants’ prereading and postreading answers. Such an approach helps rule out multiple alter native explanations. As results in Table 7 show, firms’ action volume has a significant impact in enhancing intention for engagement, both in breadth and in depth. Action simplicity has an impact in enhancing intention for engagement depth but not engagement breadth. Action volume and action simplicity do not have a significant interaction effect.

## 5. Discussion and conclusions

This research contributes to the existing strategy literature by iden tifying the missing link between firms’ actions and performance. Drawing on the perspective of information processing, we develop a research model to explore how firms’ action patterns influence user engagement and response in terms of word of mouth, which eventually influences a firm’s online performance. We conducted two studies to test the model: an empirical test using a longitudinal dataset of six Internet firms and a two-stage 2 × 2 controlled laboratory experiment. The findings suggest that user engagement plays a prominent role in medi ating the effect of firm actions on performance. Specifically, our results suggest that engagement depth mediates the effect of action volume and action simplicity on firm performance, whereas engagement breadth mediates the effect of action volume but not the effect of action simplicity on firm performance.

In the current social media era with its rich Web2.0 technologies, online word of mouth provides a key engagement approach for users to generate online influence by expressing their opinions [69] and sharing and interacting among themselves [70]. When users have their own

understanding and opinions about a firm, they can engage in word of mouth activities to aid other consumers in understanding the firm and thus influence other consumers’ opinions. This influence can affect the firm’s brand image, other users’ decisions, and eventually the firm’s performance in the market [45,50].

This study provides a better understanding about how a firm’s ac tions may improve performance through the aggregate influence of user engagement. Our results regarding engagement breadth are consistent with previous literature (e.g., [55]) and indicate that the breadth of engagement plays a key role in the overall market response. However, we do not find an indirect effect of action simplicity on firms’ online performance through engagement breadth. From an information pro cessing perspective, one plausible explanation is that although simple actions deliver much comprehensible information to users, the simplicity and the narrow range of actions may also make these actions less appealing to a broad audience [34]. Action simplicity is, therefore, less likely to trigger and enhance engagement breadth. Consequently, user engagement is less likely to act as a channel that mediates the effect of action simplicity on a firm’s online performance. Future research can further explore the impact of action simplicity on engagement breadth under different conditions. We also reveal that action simplicity can influence online performance indirectly through engagement depth. Action simplicity enables firms to better leverage the limited cognitive resources of users and trigger in-depth discussion. In-depth discussion should lead to an improved understanding of a firm among users and thus improve its online performance. This result is consistent with the literature regarding the benefits of adopting a simple repertoire of ac tions [5]. These two findings jointly reveal that different aspects of engagement may produce distinct mediating mechanisms between firm actions and performance.

This study has several theoretical and practical implications. Although the usefulness of firm-influenced user engagement has been discussed in the literature [35], insufficient attention has been paid to the role of users as stakeholders in the understanding of firm actions and how different characteristics of competitive firm actions may influence user engagement behavior. As advances in information technology allow users to be more active in making sense of and responding to firms’ actions [56], our study shows that information released on a firm’s actions triggers user engagement in understanding the firm, which further influences firm performance. The implication is that firms may better understand how the features of their action portfolios (e.g., action volume and action simplicity) can indirectly influence their on line performance through distinct aspects of engagement (e.g., the breadth and depth of engagement). Firms can also better plan their ac tion portfolios to benefit more from user engagement.

This study considers firm actions to be exogenous antecedents of user engagement behavior. Actions that are announced in public media are valuable information sources for users [35], and users are likely to use such information sources to generate word of mouth [71]. Thus, firms may proactively facilitate the creation of favorable word of mouth by using certain aspects of their actions, such as by arranging strategy execution more aggressively or by focusing on a few straightforward rules for their actions. This strategy is consistent with the existing marketing belief that firms should attempt to be more active in the strategic creation of word of mouth among potential and existing users [35].

This study also contributes to the existing research by demonstrating a strategic way in which user engagement benefits firms. The existing research has mainly investigated how user word of mouth may improve marketing campaign performance for specific products in the market (e. g., [46,47]). Our study provides support to strategy literature that ar gues about the critical role of users for market advantage establishment $[ 5 , 1 8 ]$ . By showing how user engagement has a mediating effect be tween a firm’s actions and its online performance, our study illustrates the value of word of mouth for a firm’s strategy execution and demon strates that user word of mouth is a valuable tool for realizing effective firm actions. As an important way for users to express their opinions, word of mouth also allows firms to better realize the strategic effects of their actions on their online performance. This effect further justifies the strategic importance for firms of influencing user engagement. These findings also support the theoretical perspective of user engagement (e. g., [12]) by portraying word of mouth as a means by which users in fluence the strategic outcomes of a firm.

By elucidating how firms can plan more effective action portfolios to evoke user response, our research shows that firms’ action portfolios can generate substantial and in-depth user word of mouth if these actions are more accessible to users. In addition, as resource-based commitments or signals to competitors, firms’ action portfolios have been demonstrated to influence user engagement behavior in the form of word of mouth, which subsequently affects the efficiency of firms’ action, execution, and performance. Consequently, managers should consider their action portfolios with regard to market responses and seriously consider how to plan their action portfolios to engineer user word of mouth and brand themselves. For example, in electronic markets, particularly when firms are associated with different levels of ambiguity in the market, a firm’s action portfolios can affect user interpretations and valuations of the firm, which consequently determines to a large extent the firm’s access to resources [56]. Our results further demonstrate how this effect can be applied and reveal the mechanism of user information accessibility. By increasing action accessibility through action portfolio intensity and simplicity, firms’ actions can strengthen their links to user engagement and performance. It is worth noting that firms cannot just take any ac tion, but rather actions with an established level of importance to be considered as actions by news agencies.

This study has several limitations that warrant future research ef forts. First, field data were based on firm events connected to six Internet technology firms. Future researchers may consider generalizing the findings to other types of firms and other contexts. Second, we controlled user attitudes toward the firm in the lab experiment and found these attitudes had a positive effect on the users’ adoption intention regarding the firm’s service. Future research is encouraged to investigate other engagement characteristics other than engagement breadth and depth to improve our grasp of how users understand the implications of firm actions. Besides, the lab experiment only reveals that firm actions have significant influence on user engagement, but cannot prove the mediation effects (H4a and H4b). Extended experi ments can be conducted to verify the mediation effects of user engage ment in future research. Third, in addition to the theory testing of the two action features in this study, action volume and simplicity, future research may consider other important features and implications of actions and examine the value of user engagement for other action types. Fourth, we did not distinguish between positive and negative firm ac tions, which may have a different influence on user engagement and firm performance. Future research is encouraged to investigate the impact of positive and negative firm actions on user engagement senti ment and firm performance. Fifth, we measured firm performance by the number of visits and page views on the firm’s website. The two in dicators are important nonfinancial performance indicators for Internet firms and have also been commonly used in practitioner research to measure and predict firms’ market advantage and potential revenue [56], but may not fully reflect the firm’s performance. Future research may consider more dimensions of firm performance measurement, such as financial performance indicators like return-on-equity or return-on-asset.
