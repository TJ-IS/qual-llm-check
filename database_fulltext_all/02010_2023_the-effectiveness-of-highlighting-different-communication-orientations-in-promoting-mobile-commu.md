# The Effectiveness of Highlighting Different Communication Orientations in Promoting Mobile Communication Technology at Work vs. at Home: Evidence from a Field Experiment

Chee Wei Phang,<sup>1</sup> Zheng Fang,<sup>2</sup> Chengcheng Liao<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Business School, University of Nottingham Ningbo, China, cheewei.phang@nottingham.edu.cn <sup>2</sup>Business School, Sichuan University, China, 149281891@qq.com <sup>3</sup>Business School, Sichuan University, China, 823912149@qq.com

## Abstract

With the development of mobile communication technologies, people can now engage in seamless communications with family members and coworkers at both home and work. When promoting a new mobile communication technology (e.g., the 5G network), firms may be tempted to emphasize how the technology can strengthen communication both within and across the two domains with the hope of improving purchase rates. Yet research has suggested that people may perceive mobile communication differently depending on whether those they are communicating with others who belong to the same domain. Thus, the promotion of the technology to potential users should perhaps consider users’ location domain and their communication targets. Through a field experiment, we show that when promoting mobile communication technology in the home domain, highlighting prevention-focused communication promotes greater purchase rates. However, at work, when coworkers are the target of communication, highlighting promotion-focused communication works better. These findings can not only help practitioners design more effective promotional messages in promoting mobile communication technologies but also contribute to the understanding of nuanced differences in the nature of mobile communication that make it more appealing to users in different within- and cross-domain communication scenarios.

Keywords: Mobile Communication Technology; Cross-Domain Communication, Communication Orientation, Contextual Targeting, Mobile Advertising, Randomized Field Experiment

Jason Bennett Thatcher was the accepting senior editor. This research article was submitted on October 25, 2019 and underwent three revisions.

## 1 Introduction

Mobile communication technologies are technologies that allow the transmission of voice and multimedia data along with their associated applications via a mobile device without being connected to a physical or fixed link.<sup>1</sup> They have made communication possible anytime and anywhere. As succinctly described by Kwon et al. (2016, p. 919): “the portability, convenience, and ondemand accessibility offered by mobile devices make perpetual contact possible; users maintain a constant sense of camaraderie and kinship with the people in their social circles by sharing daily routines.” The development of mobile technologies<sup>2</sup> has also enabled faster and richer communications that cross the boundaries of home and work (Green, 2002; Wajcman et al., 2008). For instance, people at work can use the same mobile communication technology to text colleagues about work issues and talk to family members using mobile voice or video calls. Likewise, people at home can use the same technology to check in with family members, keep in touch with extended family or distant friends, and discuss workrelated matters with colleagues. Therefore, when a new mobile communication technology appears on the market (such as the recent 5G and associated communication applications), it makes sense for firms to target users in both work and home domains and promote the technology by highlighting how it can enhance their communication with others in both domains.

However, it is important to note that the ease of communications facilitated by mobile technologies—in particularly cross-domain communications—is not without controversy. For instance, mobile technologies are considered to be revolutionary devices (Fortunati, 2002) “at the interface of the private and public spheres of life” (Prasopoulou et al., 2006, p. 277). They can “problematize the work-life boundary” (Golden & Geisler, 2007, p. 520), as they result “not only in work being performed at home, but also in shifting nonwork activities to the work domain” (Tennakoon et al., 2013, p. 112). Along these lines, research has highlighted how mobile communications may intrude into home and work domains. As noted by Tams et al. (2020, p. 1): “mobile technologies are particularly problematic, exposing employees to an endless stream of … instant messages, … and other interruptions, all mediated via smartphones and tablets that constantly beep, buzz, and blink.”

Some researchers have also taken a pragmatic view, suggesting that cross-domain communications are inevitable and that mobile technologies allow people to manage communication when needed (Edley et al., 2004; Golden & Geisler, 2007). In this study, we assume that people want to communicate with family members while at work and with coworkers while at home for various personal and work-related reasons (Halford, 2005; Wan et al., 2019), despite the potentially intrusive nature of such communication (Golden & Geisler, 2007; Tams et al., 2020). However, we further posit that people want different types and orientations of mobile communications depending on whether such communications are within-domain or cross-domain in nature, and whether they occur at home or at work.

We build on regulatory focus theory (Higgins, 1998) and border theory (Ashforth et al., 2000; Clark, 2000) to develop our hypotheses. Specifically, we employ regulatory focus theory to distinguish two broad orientations of mobile communications, i.e., promotionfocused versus prevention-focused communication. A person’s regulatory focus is a specific motivational orientation that is adopted during a goal pursuit (Higgins, 1998). A promotion focus emphasizes advancement, growth, opportunity, and accomplishment, whereas a prevention focus emphasizes safety, security, stability, and protection. These two regulatory focuses denote people’s orientation in a focal behavior, including engaging in mobile communication. Communicating with others may serve the goal of obtaining instrumental outcomes (a promotion focus) or a sense of security (a prevention focus) (Dimmick et al., 1994). For instance, in the work (home) domain, a promotion focus in communication may be related to an orientation toward strengthening social connections in order to perform work (family) roles or activities better, whereas a prevention focus may be concerned with an orientation toward avoiding social disconnection in order to feel secure in work (family) roles or activities. Based on these two mobile communication orientations, we then build on border theory to hypothesize people’s preferences for promotion-focused vs. prevention-focused communications, for within-domain (i.e., communicating with coworkers while at work, or with family members at home) vs. cross-domain (i.e., communicating with coworkers while at home, or with family members while at work) communications.

To test our hypotheses, we conducted a randomized field experiment. Randomized field experiments can control for consumers’ unobservable heterogeneity and thus avoid the endogeneity and causality biases that might confound estimation results (Aral & Walker, 2014). To increase the realism and external validity of our research findings, we collaborated with one of the world’s largest mobile operators and conducted a randomized field experiment promoting a new mobile communication technology—namely, the 4G network technology that had recently been rolled out at the time of investigation. The firm was keen to explore ways of increasing and speeding up its take-up rates, given the high capital investments made to implement the related infrastructures. We leveraged the firm’s location-based targeting capability to accurately target users at work and at home. The mobile operator randomly assigned mobile promotional messages to its subscribers according to our manipulations and tracked their actual purchase behaviors.

We adopted a message-framing design to test which orientation of mobile communications people were more attracted to when presented with different scenarios of within-domain and cross-domain communications. The effectiveness of using a message-framing approach to investigate the relationship between users’ psychological states and behaviors has been well-demonstrated in the literature (Angst & Agarwal, 2009; Maheswaran &

Meyers-Levy, 1990; Smith & Petty, 1996). Messageframing designs work by attracting recipients’ attention to the message content and increasing their motivation to process the information contained in the message. If the regulatory orientation framed in the message is in alignment with the regulatory orientation of the targeted person performing a behavior (which can be a trait or a state), the targeted person tends to be more sensitive to and more easily persuaded by the message content, making positive outcomes more likely (Cesario et al., 2004; Higgins, 2005). Specifically, a promotion focus increases sensitivity to the presence or absence of positive outcomes (e.g., whether connections with others can be strengthened), whereas a prevention focus increases sensitivity to the presence or absence of negative outcomes (e.g., whether disconnection from others can be prevented) (Higgins, 1998). This approach can minimize response bias when respondents are asked about what they want (e.g., regarding their mobile communication orientation in a particular situation) in a survey or interview. Our collaboration with the mobile operator also allowed us to objectively and accurately quantify the focal outcome, i.e., actual purchase adoption of the mobile communication technology.

The findings from our study offer several contributions to both research and practice. Although mobile communication technologies enable “anytime, anywhere” communications that span both work and home domains (Green, 2002; Wajcman et al., 2008), our study shows that the nature of mobile communications that appeals to people varies in different within-domain and cross-domain communication situations (e.g., calling family members vs. coworkers when at work vs. home). Specifically, our study provides new insights by introducing promotion vs. prevention regulatory focuses to understand people’s orientation in within-domain and cross-domain mobile communications. A key finding from this study is that in the context of cross-domain mobile communications, prevention-focused communications are preferable to promotion-focused communication, whereas in within-domain mobile communications contexts, preferences depend on whether users are at home or at work. We also contribute to the technology acceptance literature on mobile technology. A common approach in this literature stream is to highlight the usefulness of a technology, a key antecedent in the technology acceptance model, in a promotion-focused way—for example, showing how a mobile technology can strengthen connections for better task performance. We show that the usefulness of a technology can also be highlighted in a preventionfocused manner—e.g., by showing how a mobile technology can help prevent disconnection and thereby improve adoption outcomes.

Our research also has important practical relevance. For the mobile communication industry, our findings can inform mobile communication technology promotion strategies. We focus on an important class of mobile communication technologies, i.e., 4G technology, which forms the basis of mobile communications by affording high-speed connections and multimedia communication support. Promoting users’ take-up of such a technology is important, given the very competitive market and the large investment of time and money needed to develop new infrastructures and services that meet user demands, such as the latest 5G services (Deloitte, 2017). It is important that there is a quick return on investments enabled by the rapid adoption of the technology by consumers. In addition, mobile communication technologies are constantly evolving, and every new development is accompanied by a sea change for a large number of related industries, such as multimedia, entertainment, gaming, and the artificial intelligence (AI) chatbot industries.<sup>3</sup> Thus, when a new generation of the technology is introduced (such as 5G, which offers faster and richer multimedia connections than 3G and 4G), it is important to promote rapid adoption to ensure a smooth transition and facilitate the development of related industries as well as the whole service ecosystem.

Our study highlights the need for firms to develop a customized strategy to promote mobile communication technologies for people in work vs. home domains. These two domains are associated with different psychological orientations toward communication and are also commercially important since they represent the two domains in which people spend most of their time (Verkasalo, 2009; Wajcman, 2015). Thus, these two domains offer excellent opportunities for mobile targeting promotions. Specifically, the promotional strategy can be customized by highlighting different orientations of mobile communications in promotional messages. Our findings show that sensitivity to such customizations can increase users’ purchase rates by as much as 354% for the home context and 278% for the work context.

## 2 Literature Review

Mobile communication technologies that enable “anytime, anywhere” communications have broad implications for both users and corporate providers. For users, they enable “perpetual contact” (Katz & Aakhus, 2002). For corporate providers or firms, they enable the contextual targeting of users based on their location and the time of day (Balasubramanian et al., 2002). Yet for effective targeting, it is important to understand users’ psychological needs and desires in particular contexts (Luo et al., 2014). For this study, we reviewed two streams of literature that are relevant in this regard:

mobile targeting and mobile communications. These two streams of literature informed our study and also offer important areas for contributions.

## 2.1 Literature on Mobile Contextual Targeting

In general, research on mobile contextual targeting (mobile targeting henceforth; Luo et al., 2014), has attempted to reveal how mobile technology’s “anytime, anywhere” capabilities can be leveraged to send information content to users based on their context to obtain better outcomes (e.g., higher purchase rates). A review of the literature on mobile targeting shows that the bulk of the research in this area focuses on utilizing the location and time aspects of user context. For instance, earlier research examined location-based targeting and found that better promotional outcomes, such as higher purchase rates, can be obtained when mobile messages are sent to consumers near retail stores or shopping areas (Banerjee & Dholakia, 2008, Spiekermann et al., 2011). Subsequent research has considered other factors that revolve around location proximity. For instance, Daurer et al. (2012) highlighted the need to consider the search cost of purchasing at a focal store with respect to its location. Hui et al. (2013) showed that sending real-time mobile promotional messages to consumers when they are inside a grocery store can stimulate their spending on unplanned purchases. Fang et al. (2015) revealed the dynamic aspect of location-based mobile targeting effectiveness, showing that it can increase not only immediate but also future purchases. Fong et al. (2015) further highlighted that targeting one’s own customers when they are near or inside a store is effective but poaching nearby competitors’ customers is also viable.

A number of studies have also examined the use of the time factor for mobile targeting. For instance, Bang et al. (2013) showed that the use of mobile technology to communicate product information is particularly effective in stimulating time-sensitive purchases. Luo et al. (2014) examined the time-urgency factor in addition to location. Their research investigated how sending movie discount coupons at different times prior to a movie and at different distances away from the movie theater impact sales. Ghose et al. (2018) investigated the opportunity to target consumers commuting between home and work and found that commuters are more likely than noncommuters to redeem mobile coupons. They attribute this to the stress that people experience while commuting and suggest that responding to mobile coupons can relieve stress. More recently, Phang et al. (2019) showed that highlighting the utilitarian aspect of a product in the morning and its hedonic aspect in the afternoon can engender higher purchase rates. They argued that because energy levels are lower in the afternoon, people are more interested in pursuing enjoyment in the afternoon and more interested in accomplishing tasks in the morning.

In the above research, the contextual factors they address are generally confined to individual-bound contextual factors, e.g., how distance and time influence individuals receiving a mobile promotional offer. However, being a “social object” (Srivastava, 2010), mobile technology is typically not used in isolation from others, and consideration of the social-communicative environment may thus lead to fruitful outcomes. For instance, Provost et al. (2015) highlighted the possibility of targeting users who visit the same place based on the interests of one of the users in this “geosimilarity” social network because users who frequently visit the same place likely share similar interests. Andrews et al. (2015) showed that targeting users when they are on a crowded subway train can yield better responses to a mobile promotional message. Specifically, when people are surrounded by a large number of people, they tend to adaptively turn inward and become more immersed in the mobile promotional message received.

Although the above studies are valuable for highlighting the potential value of social context (i.e., a context beyond the individual that includes social others), we argue that such contextual factors remain understudied regarding mobile communication. Mobile communication technology is known for its ability to enable cross-domain communications—for example, communications that cross work-home domains and involve the social actors of coworkers and family members (Katz & Aakhus, 2002; Sarker et al., 2012; Wajcman et al., 2008). Work and home are two major domains of contemporary human life (Green, 2002; Wajcman et al., 2008). The importance of these domains for mobile targeting is also noted by Ghose et al. (2018), who highlight the opportunity to target consumers when they are commuting between home and work. However, their study neither directly addresses the home and work domains themselves, where people likely spend most of their time nor does it consider the mobile-enabled communications within and across these two domains. In this study, we demonstrate how companies can target users in the home and work domains to promote a mobile communication technology, informed by the relevant literature on mobile communication technology use in and across these two domains, as discussed next.

## 2.2 Literature on Mobile Communication Technology Use in and across the Work and Home Domains

Given the “perpetual contact” (Katz & Aakhus, 2002), enabled by mobile communication technologies, research on the use of these technologies has investigated how they impact work and family activities within and across the work and home domains. Figure 1 below depicts the different mobile communication scenarios involved. In the work domain, earlier research focused on highlighting the capabilities of mobile technologies in facilitating employees’ work and improving organizational efficiency (Barnes et al., 2002; Jarvenpaa et al., 2003; Sheng et al., 2005). For instance, Jarvenpaa et al. (2003) noted that mobile technologies can help strengthen employees’ interpersonal relationships and increase coordination flexibility by fostering communication and knowledge sharing.

However, research has also noted that the constant connectivity enabled by mobile technologies has increased the prevalence of work-home cross-domain communications and thus work interruptions (Iqbal & Horvitz, 2010; O’Leary et al., 2011). Specifically, mobile communications allow for intrusions from the home to the work domain (e.g., calls or instant messages from family members during work hours), resulting in intermittent, disruptive information flows in the form of interruptions (Wan et al., 2019). A number of studies have explored the implications of such interruptions at work. For example, Jarvenpaa and Lang (2005) note that distractions arising from cross-domain communication may prevent people from concentrating on work-related tasks and duties. Smit et al. (2016) argue that attending to family communications during work can consume people’s limited executive control resources that are needed for effective job performance, thus resulting in lower job performance. In general, these studies suggest that communicating with family members through mobile technologies at work can negatively impact employees’ work performance by interrupting work tasks.

In the home domain, the bulk of the research has focused on how mobile communications disrupt family life, although some research has also acknowledged that such communications can help maintain family bonds (e.g., conveniently making calls to fulfill family duties) (Wei & Lo, 2006). As Chen & Karahanna (2018, p. 1024) note, “the increasingly permeable and flexible boundaries between work and nonwork suggest that after-hours work-related interruptions have ramifications beyond the work domain and impact one’s nonwork domain as well.” It has been suggested that using mobile technologies to communicate with coworkers after work can distract people from their roles and activities at home (Ferguson et al., 2016), thereby causing work to intrude into the home domain (Mazmanian et al., 2013). The alwaysconnected nature of mobile technologies makes this issue particularly prevalent, as people now have to pay attention to work-related matters that may arise at home. For example, people may frequently check for instant messages or calls from coworkers on their mobile phone when spending time with their children, preparing meals, or doing other activities at home.

In general, the above studies have largely indicated that mobile technologies may bring about positive communication outcomes when they are of a withindomain nature (e.g., facilitating instant knowledge sharing among employees at work for more efficient job completion, maintaining relationships with remote family members when relaxing at home) (Jarvenpaa & Lang, 2005; Wei & Lo, 2006). However, the outcomes are less positive for cross-domain communications (i.e., from work to home or home to work). Cross-domain communications are generally seen as unfavorable due to their intrusive nature and the interruptions they introduce for domain-specific activities (Boswell et al., 2007; Hemp, 2009; Iqbal & Horvitz, 2010; O’Leary et al., 2011). Indeed, recent research has shown that interruptions due to cross-domain mobile communications can reduce people’s intention to use mobile technologies (Tams et al., 2020).

In addition, noting the importance of considering both within- and cross-domain mobile communications, a few studies have tried to address the issue from both sides (Chen & Karahanna, 2014; Lutz et al., 2020; Wan et al., 2019). Among these studies, Chen & Karahanna (2014) found that interruptions due to work-to-home communications can lead to perceived work-life conflict and, in turn, undermine people’s fulfillment of their personal life responsibilities. In contrast, they found that interruptions due to home-to-work communications directly affected people’s fulfillment of work responsibilities but did not increase work-life conflict.

Lutz et al. (2020) found that the pressure to be constantly available because of mobile cross-domain communications increase people’s negative affect both at home and at work regardless of their levels of stress resilience. Both of these studies highlight the negative effects of mobile cross-domain communications on people’s work and family lives. In contrast, Wan et al. (2019) took a more neutral view, arguing that while crossdomain communications can indeed introduce workfamily conflict, they are not always harmful because they can also provide work- or family-related resources when needed (e.g., allowing one to perform pressing work tasks at home or attend to urgent family matters at work). Thus, mobile cross-domain communications may not always be undesirable.

Our study builds on and extends this stream of literature in three ways. First, although our study does not directly address the negative implications of mobile cross-domain communications (e.g., interruptions), we build on related findings associated with border theory (Ashforth et al., 2000; Clark, 2000), to hypothesize differences in the nature (specifically motivational orientation) of mobile communications that can trigger the adoption of a mobile technology. Second, in line with Wan et al.’s (2019) argument that mobile cross-domain communications are not always undesirable, we adopt regulatory focus theory (Higgins, 1998) to conceptualize two kinds of mobile communications, i.e., promotion- and preventionfocused <sup>4</sup> mobile communications, which likely differentially appeal to people in different within-domain and cross-domain communication scenarios. Third, while previous research such as Tams et al. (2020) suggest that the interruptions introduced by mobile cross-domain communications may cause people to reduce mobile technology use, our study shows that there may be another way to look at the issue. In particular, we show that when people’s attention is focused on an appropriate mobile communication orientation, they become more likely to purchase and adopt a mobile communication technology. In the next section, we discuss regulatory focus theory and border theory, which are employed as the theoretical bases for our hypotheses.

## 3 Theoretical Foundation and Hypothesis Development

## 3.1 Regulatory Focus Theory

We build on regulatory focus theory to understand and conceptualize the major orientations of people’s mobile communications and how the orientations may be shaped by people’s mindset and activities in particular situations.

Regulatory focus theory is a goal pursuit theory that highlights the motivational orientation of people’s behavior during a goal pursuit, i.e., regulatory focus (Higgins, 1997, 1998). There are two types of regulatory focus, namely promotion focus and prevention focus. A promotion focus emphasizes advancement, growth, opportunity, and accomplishment, whereas the prevention focus emphasizes safety, security, stability, and protection (Higgins, 1997; Higgins & Spiegel, 2004). Regulatory focus can be a chronic personality trait or situationally induced (Crowe & Higgins, 1997). Specifically, regarding the latter case, a context can activate a specific regulatory focus due to factors such as the nature of activities pursued or the cues, goals, or needs of a particular context (Boesen-Mariani et al., 2010; Crowe & Higgins, 1997; Fay et al., 2019).

People’s regulatory focus determines their orientation in pursuing a behavior— in this study, engaging in mobile communications. As mentioned above, in the work and home domains, a promotion focus in communications may be related to an orientation toward strengthening social connections (Molden et al., 2007). Such communications may be geared toward obtaining resources and opportunities for better task accomplishment. A prevention focus, on the other hand, may have to do with an orientation to avoid social disconnection (Molden et al., 2007). Such communications may be geared toward obtaining a sense of reassurance or security from knowing that social disconnection can be avoided. The constant connectivity enabled by mobile technologies, even when they are not actively in use, can offer psychological reassurance that communication with others is at least possible (Campbell & Ling, 2008). Indeed, research has shown that apart from the fundamental purpose of socialization, communicating with others may serve the goals of attaining instrumental outcomes (promotion focused) and obtaining a sense of reassurance (prevention focused) (Dimmick et al., 1994).

Previous studies have shown that when promoting a product or service in a way that is in consonance with the receiver’s regulatory focus, i.e., attaining a regulatory fit, its persuasion effectiveness increases (Cesario et al., 2004). Regulatory fit intensifies responses, such as the perceived value of a chosen object or a persuasion (Kruglanski et al., 2007). Promotional messages usually involve highlighting a goal (e.g., communication with others) and the means to achieve it (e.g., a new mobile communication technology with better communication capabilities, such as faster speed and richer media support) as well as a certain strategy of argument. The argument may be presented with a promotion focus or a prevention focus in line with the target’s orientation to induce a feeling of regulatory fit and thus increase its persuasiveness.

In this study, we employ the view that people’s regulatory focus can be situation induced or context triggered (Crowe & Higgins, 1997) in line with our focus on social communication contexts (i.e., work and home). In particular, regulatory focus theory suggests that situations affect people’s regulatory focus through situational cues and the activities people perform in specific situations or contexts (Higgins, 1997). The situational cues and the activities that people pursue in certain contexts differ in terms of the types of goals they are exposed to, which may then activate different types of motivational orientations (Liberman et al., 2001). For example, at work, office environments and work activities likely expose people to cues primarily associated with accomplishment and achievement (Fay et al., 2019; Locke & Latham, 1990; Myers & Johnson, 2004) and are thus more likely to induce a promotion orientation vs. a prevention orientation.

## 3.2 Border Theory

Border theory, also known as boundary theory, proposes that individuals create and maintain physical, behavioral or psychological “borders” or “boundaries” around their work and home roles (Ashforth et al., 2000; Clark, 2000). The terms “borders” and “boundaries” are conceptually similar and often used interchangeably (Mathews & Barnes-Farell, 2010). Borders are conceptualized by Clark (2000, p. 756) as “the lines of demarcation between domains … used to define the point at which domain relevant behaviour begins or ends.” Similarly, Ashforth (2001, p. 474) defines boundaries as “physical, emotional, temporal, cognitive, and/or relational limits that define entities as separate from one another.” Thus, it is important to note that while people may try to enact a boundary with the physical location as a base or starting point, the boundary enacted may extend beyond the physical location and entail psychological aspects such as thinking patterns and the relational nature appropriate to that boundary. An example noted by Ashforth et al. (2000) is that as people leave work and drive home, they may use this time to clear their mind regarding their work role and start embracing their family role by thinking about what needs to be done at home. This “home” psychological boundary may be enacted before people actually reach their homes.

According to border theory, individuals tend to create and maintain boundaries as a means of simplifying and organizing their environment (Ashforth et al., 2000; Clark, 2000; Nippert-Eng, 1996; Zerubavel, 1991), or what Ashforth (2001) terms “mental fences” (p. 262). Two notable boundaries of contemporary human life are work and home. Work is marked by financial incentives and rules that structure who is expected to do what, when, and for how long (Oldenburg, 1991; Steinkuehler & Williams, 2006). It thus shapes people’s mindset about work as a place that provides people with opportunities to use their skills to obtain instrumental rewards (Locke &

Latham, 1990; Warr, 1987). In contrast, home is a place where people live their private lives and signifies a context in which people rest and obtain a sense of comfort, safety, and security (Dovey, 1985; Kidd & Evans, 2011; Moore, 2000). This is consistent with how Shumate and Fulk (2004, p. 56) describe people’s mindset about home, i.e., as a “place of refuge.” The two domains are also associated with a different expected set of roles and activities—at work, people assume the primary role as an employee performing task-based activities according to the organization’s requirements; at home, people often assume family roles such as “parent,” “spouse,” or “family member,” and their activities may range from taking care of parents and children to cleaning the house and doing laundry (Shumate & Fulk, 2004).

Although clear work-home boundaries have been extensively identified in the literature, mobile communication technologies have made the boundaries permeable (Tams et al., 2020). Permeable boundaries imply that people can be involved in one role (e.g., talking to a colleague) while being in another domain/role (e.g., doing laundry at home) (Allen et al., 2014; Ashforth et al., 2000; Shumate & Fulk, 2004). While this blurs the boundaries between work and home in terms of their physical and temporal aspects (i.e., work can now be performed at home after work hours, and family issues can be dealt with at work), it is important to note that people may still prefer to maintain and protect these boundaries to make their environment more psychologically manageable (Methot & LePine, 2016). Psychological boundaries define what thinking patterns, behaviors, and emotions are deemed appropriate within a particular domain (Ashforth et al., 2000; Clark, 2000).

Indeed, boundary theory is grounded in the idea that human agency is central in shaping people’s work-home boundaries, and setting up psychological boundaries is an important way for people to manage their environment when maintaining physical and temporal boundaries is less viable to (Clark, 2000; Hislop & Axtell., 2011). For example, even though people may not be able to avoid work at home entirely, as they can be easily contacted by colleagues, they may consciously or subconsciously communicate (e.g., that they need to perform household chores) in a way that signals that they are generally unavailable for work tasks (Shumate & Fulk, 2004) in order to protect and safeguard the psychological boundaries associated with their current domain. Indeed, previous research has highlighted that while different people may deem boundary permeability to be tolerable to different degrees, a natural tendency is for them to protect and maintain psychological boundaries to minimize psychological strain and transition costs (Ashforth et al., 2000). This is likely to be applicable to cross-boundary communication, which unavoidably introduces psychological boundary intrusion and interruptions (Chen & Karahanna, 2018; Wan et al., 2019).

## 3.3 Hypothesis Development

We build on regulatory focus theory and border theory to develop our research hypotheses. Regulatory focus theory is not only useful in highlighting that mobile communication can be promotion or prevention focused but also salient in informing what regulatory focus is likely to be prevalent in a given communication context based on the situational cues and people’s primary goals and activities in that context. Border theory complements regulatory focus theory by enlightening what mobile communication orientation is likely to be more appealing to people where within- and cross-domain communications are concerned based on their boundary maintenance and protection inclinations.

In general, we posit that when people engage in withindomain communications (i.e., communicating with coworkers while at work and with family members while at home), their orientation of communication is likely to be in line with the prevailing regulatory focus induced by the situational cues and the primary activities associated with these respective domains. In contrast, when engaging in cross-domain communication (i.e., communicating with family members while at work and with coworkers while at home), their orientation of communication is likely to be more prevention than promotion focused, which can be attributed to people’s boundary protection inclinations. Below we develop our hypotheses separately in the work and home domains regarding within-domain and cross-domain communications.

## 3.3.1 Promoting Mobile Communication Technology in the Work Domain

Previous research has highlighted how mobile communication technologies can facilitate communication and knowledge sharing among employees, thereby enhancing their job efficiency and performance (Barnes et al., 2002; Jarvenpaa et al., 2003; Sheng et al., 2005). This capability of mobile communication technologies is well-aligned with people’s work activities that involve efficient communication with coworkers. As previously noted, work is a domain that provides people with opportunities to use their skills to obtain instrumental rewards (e.g., salaries) and achievements (e.g., job promotions) (Locke & Latham, 1990; Warr, 1987). Thus, at work, when people are assuming their employee role, their communication with coworkers is likely to be focused on activities such as completing tasks and solving problems (Fritz, 1997; Myers & Johnson, 2004). Such environments also typically primarily signal the need for accomplishment and advancement (Myers & Johnson, 2004) and are thus likely to induce a promotion focus in people (Higgins, 1997). Accordingly, people likely welcome the connectivity features of mobile communication technologies because they help them strengthen their connections with coworkers. The strengthened connection can promote knowledge sharing and the clarification of work issues, which aligns with a promotion-focused orientation (Higgins, 1997).

Also, border theory highlights that people tend to maintain psychological boundaries as a means to simplify and organize their environment (Ashforth et al., 2000; Clark, 2000; Nippert-Eng, 1996; Zerubavel, 1991), which includes relationship aspects (Ashforth, 2001), i.e., the social actors with whom one communicates and interacts with are part of the psychological boundary to be maintained. As coworkers are the key social actors in the work domain, communicating with coworkers in this domain is consistent with people’s effort to maintain psychological boundaries. Communicating with coworkers can provide people with useful information and tips for task accomplishment (Greenhaus & Powell, 2006; Miller, 2009), primarily aligning with a promotion focus. Therefore, people are likely to embrace communications with coworkers at work in accordance with their prevailing regulatory focus at work, which is shaped by the situational cues and primary activities associated with this domain. Taken together, promoting a mobile communication technology with a promotionfocused approach, i.e., highlighting that the technology can help strengthen connections with coworkers (Molden et al., 2007), would likely make promotion messages more persuasive (Cesario et al., 2004) and thus induce higher purchase rates.

H1: Regarding within-domain communications in the work domain, using a promotion-focused approach to promote a mobile communication technology (highlighting that the technology can improve connections with coworkers) yields a higher purchase rate than using a preventionfocused approach.

In contrast, communication with family members while at work is less aligned with the workplace emphasis on task completion. Research has repeatedly highlighted how cross-domain communications at work may introduce interruptions that can undermine employees’ work performance (Iqbal & Horvitz, 2010; O’Leary et al., 2011; Wan et al., 2019). According to border theory (Ashforth et al., 2000; Clark, 2000), people generally seek to psychologically protect their work boundaries (Methot & Lepine, 2016), even though this may not be entirely possible (e.g., family members may nevertheless contact them). The inclination to protect work psychological boundaries also arises because people are expected to focus on their work activities while at work (Shumate & Fulk, 2004). Thus, communication with family members, i.e., social actors outside of work psychological boundaries (Ashforth et al., 2000), may be deemed inappropriate per workplace norms and social expectations. As Hislop and Axtell (2011, p. 54)

explain: such calls [referring to communications with coworkers], “due to the work-related element to them that exists (such as seeking advice in solving problems, or making a routine administrative call) mean that calls to work-colleagues during working time have more legitimacy than calls involving family.”

The inclination to protect psychological work boundaries when one is at work is in line with a prevention focus, whereby people are motivated to attain security and stability through protection against negative influences (Higgins, 1997), such as potential interruptions from communications with family members while at work. Indeed, it is a natural inclination for people to avoid impacts on desired end states (in the case of the workplace, satisfactorily fulfilling and performing one’s role as an employee) (Higgins et al., 1994; Liberman et al., 1999). Thus, instead of actively anticipating communication with family members at work, people may only want such communications when they are essential. In other words, they may use mobile communication technology merely as a means to avoid disconnection from family members (e.g., avoiding missed calls about urgent matters from family members) to minimize impacts on their activities in the work domain. Therefore, using a prevention-focused approach to promote mobile communication technology—e.g., highlighting that the technology can help prevent disconnection from family members (Molden et al., 2007)—is more likely to yield a higher purchase rate than using a promotion-focused approach.

H2: Regarding cross-domain communications in the work domain, using a prevention-focused approach to promote a mobile communication technology (highlighting that the technology can avoid disconnection from family) yields a higher purchase rate than using a promotion-focused approach.

## 3.3.2 Promoting Mobile Communication Technology in the Home Domain

The connectivity afforded by mobile communication technologies is also valuable in the home domain. Research has noted how communication technologies can help individuals maintain family relationships (Anderson et al., 2007; Wei & Lo, 2006). For example, people can fulfill their family responsibilities by checking in on family members they don’t live with while relaxing at home.

The home domain is both psychologically and behaviorally associated with individuals’ familyrelated roles and responsibilities, such as being a good parent or a caring son/daughter (Voydanoff, 2005). It is also associated with activities such as cleaning, doing laundry, taking care of children/parents, and making home repairs (Schneider, 2012; Soest & Stancanelli, 2010; Starrels, 1994). These roles and activities often involve a focus on keeping the household safe, and thus fit largely a prevention focus whereby people are motivated to avoid negative outcomes (van Dijk & Kluger, 2011). For example, housecleaning and doing laundry removes germs and other hazards that could harm family members’ health.

Along these lines, communication with family members, who are the relational part of home psychological boundaries (Ashforth et al., 2000), is likely to follow a prevention focus as well. From the perspective of border theory (Ashforth et al., 2000), people are motivated to maintain consistency in the boundaries to simplify and better manage their environment psychologically and behaviorally. Thus, they are likely to feel comfortable with such communications with family members while in the home domain, as this is well-aligned with the prevailing regulatory focus of their primary role expectations and activities in this domain (Kahn et al., 1964).

Additionally, the primary purpose of communication with family members is less likely to focus on obtaining instrumental opportunities and advancements (Greenhaus & Powell, 2006; Miller, 2009). Previous research has shown that the instrumental utility of using communication tools to communicate with family is less emphasized by people in nonwork contexts (Zhou & Feng, 2017). Instead, typical communication with family members involves checking on their safety and well-being as a means of fulfilling their family responsibilities (Anderson, 2007). Such communication with family members fosters a sense of security and peace of mind (i.e., knowing their family members are well), which primarily aligns with a prevention focus (Higgins, 1997). Consequently, people are likely to engage in communication with family members mainly when they feel needed. This is reflected to some extent in the finding by Cao et al. (2010, p. 157): “Although communication with family had become an integral part of their lives, participants considered it secondary to other daily routines. They typically would not change their own schedule in order to accommodate communication with remote family, except for special occasions.” Thus, promoting a mobile communication technology with a prevention-focused approach, i.e., highlighting that technology can help prevent disconnection from family members (Molden et al., 2007), is likely a better match with what people want from communication with family members in the home domain. Therefore, in this context, we hypothesize that promoting a mobile communication technology using a prevention focus would be more persuasive (Cesario et al., 2004) and thereby induce a higher purchase rate.

H3: Regarding within-domain communications in the home domain, using a prevention-focused approach to promote a mobile communication technology (highlighting that the technology can avoid disconnection from family) yields a higher purchase rate than using a promotion-focused approach.

We expect people to be more prevention focused in their communication with coworkers at home as well. As noted previously, cross-domain communications involving work have made significant inroads into the home domain due to the ubiquitous nature of mobile technology (Chen & Karahanna, 2018). Such communications are often seen as intrusions of people’s home psychological boundaries (Mazmanian et al., 2013). People follow different norms and engage in different activities in the home domain (Shumate & Fulk, 2004) and consciously or subconsciously seek to protect the boundaries of this domain. One indication of this is that people often follow certain rituals when moving from the workplace to home. As described by Shumate and Fulk (2004, p. 68): “At the end of their specified work time, they travel home … consume an alcoholic beverage or other cold drink, and change into ‘comfortable clothing.’ These rituals aid mental transitions.” As these rituals are enacted over time, they become normalized and help people mold their mindset and cognitive schemas of appropriate behaviors and expectations for themselves in this domain. For example, at home, “taking care of a sick child or doing laundry would be considered … within the boundaries of the role of ‘parent,’ ‘spouse,’ or ‘family member.’” (Shumate & Fulk 2004, p. 58) In contrast, communications with coworkers are typically viewed to be inconsistent with the norms and activities within the home psychological boundary (Mazmanian et al., 2013; Shumate & Fulk, 2004).

Furthermore, although people may be asked to temporarily focus on work activities at home, the home’s psychological mindset (as a place to find rest and obtain a sense of comfort and security—Dovey, 1985; Kidd & Evans, 2011) emphasizes that “I am working at home, not work,” and may thus induce mental conflict. Greenhaus & Beutell (1985, p. 76) describe the work-family conflict as: “a form of inter-role conflict in which the role pressures from the work and [personal]/family life domains are mutually incompatible in some respect.” Peeters et al. (2005) also point out that people naturally want to recover at home from the effort they expended at work. The need for this recovery process would suggest a prevention-oriented mindset and mood (Baas et al., 2008) even when it is necessary to fulfill work duties at home.

In general, the inclination to protect the home psychological boundary when one is at home aligns with a prevention focus, whereby people are motivated to attain security and stability through protection against negative influences (Higgins, 1997), such as potential interruptions from communicating with coworkers when at home. Thus, rather than looking forward to communications with coworkers at home, people may primarily want such communications when they are needed (e.g., to avoid missing important calls about urgent work matters due to disconnection) in order to minimize interruptions to their home activities or to reduce the experience of mental conflict (i.e., between the work they are doing and their family roles and the home psychological mindset) arising from the communications. Therefore, using a prevention-focused approach to promote a mobile communication technology in this context would be more likely to result in consonance and thus induce a higher purchase rate.

H4: Regarding cross-domain communications in the home domain, using a prevention-focused approach to promote a mobile communication technology (highlighting that the technology can avoid disconnection from coworkers) yields a higher purchase rate than using a promotion-focused approach.

## 4 Research Methodology

We tested our hypotheses in a setting where a mobile operator sent a promotional message (SMS) to promote a new mobile communication technology service (4G) to its customers at the time of study. For our study’s purposes, we conducted a field experiment, which affords several advantages. First, it allowed us to realistically assess the outcome of interest, i.e., purchases made in the actual work and home contexts (vis-à-vis a lab experiment lacking real location context or a survey relying primarily on perceptual measures). Second, the field experiment allowed us to rule out the influences of unobserved confounding variables (e.g., individual heterogeneities), which are crucial factors to consider when establishing causality. Unobserved confounding variables could introduce complexity into the measurement and mask nuanced effects, making it difficult to establish causality. In addition, bias arising from endogenous processes can be resolved through randomized field experiments (Aral & Walker, 2014).

## 4.1 Field Experiment

We conducted our field experiment with a mobile operator who prefers to remain anonymous. In the experiment, the company sent an SMS message to its subscribers promoting a new mobile communication technology, specifically 4G mobile network service (henceforth referred to as “4G service” for brevity). 4G service represented a significant improvement over its predecessor, i.e., 3G, by offering faster, more reliable mobile network connection to users. The dependent variable of our study, i.e., purchase rate, was tracked by whether consumers purchased the mobile service promoted in the SMS message sent to them and was computed as a ratio of the number of actual purchases made over the number of promotional messages sent. The occurrence of actual purchases was indicated by whether consumers replied to the message received with a special code sent by the mobile operator (“reply XXX to subscribe”); a positive reply indicated they agreed to purchase the service, with the payment charged directly to their monthly phone bill beginning the following month. This allowed us to directly and accurately measure the purchases of the promoted mobile communication technology (e.g., compared to perceptual measures such as purchase intention).

We employed a message-framing design to frame the messages promoting the 4G service with a promotion or a prevention focus. Research has shown that message framing effectively influences user behavior by tailoring messages as a function of the salient motivational orientation of the relevant behavior (Chernev, 2004; Higgins, 1998). The rationale is that people’s decisions are sensitive to how information is presented in a persuasive message—when the information presented is in consonance with one’s motivational orientation, behavioral change (such as deciding to adopt a service promoted in the message) is more likely (Higgins, 2005). The approach (assessing whether a promotion- or a prevention-focused promotional message would lead to higher purchase rates in a field setting) may also yield more objective results compared to, say, directly asking respondents whether they have a promotion- or a prevention-focused communication orientation and associating this with whether they intend to purchase a product or service, which may be prone to response bias (Furnham, 1986).

In our study, we designed two variants of message framing in accordance with people’s regulatory focus, i.e., promotion focus or prevention focus (Higgins, 1998). Our experiment design is a 2 framings (promotion vs. prevention focus) × 4 mobile communication situations (varied by work vs. home domain, cross- vs. withindomain communications) between-subjects design. We also included two control conditions (sent to the work and home domains, respectively) without any framing or target of communication specified as comparison baselines. Thus, in total, there were 10 conditions in the experiment. The basic (control) SMS reads: “20 times surfing speed with the newly launched 4G! Support photo shoots, send HD video, and download big email attachments in the blink of an eye. Reply “KTSJLL50” to subscribe to the 600M/month package at ¥50. You will get a complimentary 600M/month for the next 3 months. This promotion is only valid for 60 minutes.” In the SMS, our corporate partner offered an additional 600M data usage for free for the first three months to incentivize responses. We carefully made the offer valid for 60 minutes because we wanted to make sure the subjects remained at the targeted home or work location when they made a decision to purchase the mobile service. If valid for longer, the probability of the subjects moving across domains would become higher, which may have confounded the results. In this sense, this is a cleaner and more conservative test of the results. Also, all message versions had the same “valid in 60 minutes” content, thus ensuring no bias in our results.

The messages were sent out simultaneously to all condition groups by the company at 2 pm on a Saturday in mid-April 2014. The company is one of the largest mobile operators and had the technological capability and big data computing power needed to send the messages out simultaneously. As further discussed below, we selected hospital employees as the target population for our study. We chose to send the mobile promotional messages at 2 pm for the following reasons: (1) it is not near a shift-change time; (2) it is right after lunch, when hospital employees are more likely to have time to read and reply to mobile messages; and (3) night-shift employees will have typically woken up by then.

For the promotion-focused framing, the message emphasized the mobile technology service’s ability to strengthen communication with coworkers/family members. Specifically, when family members were specified as the target of communication, we added this statement to the basic SMS: “share every wonderful moment and connect with family members, loved ones, and special people in your family life anywhere you go.” Similarly, when coworkers were the target of communication, we added this statement to the basic SMS: “share every wonderful moment and connect with co-workers, colleagues, and special people in your work life anywhere you go.”

For the prevention-focused framing, the message emphasized the mobile technology service’s ability to prevent disconnection from family members/coworkers. Specifically, when family members were specified as the target of communication, we added this statement to the basic SMS: “avoid disconnecting with and alienating from family members, loved ones, and special people in your family life anywhere you go.” Similarly, when coworkers were the target of communication, we added this statement to the basic SMS: “avoid disconnecting with and alienating from co-workers, colleagues, and special people in your work life anywhere you go.”

Our design approach of the promotion- and prevention-focused communication framing is consistent with previous studies (e.g., Alhouti et al., 2019; Chowdhury et al., 2015; Kim, 2006) and similar to actual message design in practice noted in Melnyk et al. (2013).<sup>5</sup> As manipulation checks, we conducted pilot tests with a separate sample of 286 mobile users from the mobile operator firm (a short survey was sent with the company’s help), which confirmed that the promotion- and prevention-focused communication framings were indeed perceived in line with the promotion and prevention orientations theorized by Higgins (1997). That is, the communication framings of “share every wonderful moment and connect with family members, loved ones, and special people in your family life anywhere you go” and “share every wonderful moment and connect with co-workers, colleagues, and special people in your work life anywhere you go” scored significantly higher on the Higgins (1997) promotion-focused scale than on the prevention-focused scale (p < 0.01). In addition, the communication framings of “avoid disconnecting with and alienating from family members, loved ones, and special people in your family life anywhere you go” and “avoid disconnecting with and alienating from coworkers, colleagues, and special people in your work life anywhere you go” scored significantly higher on the prevention-focused scale than on the promotionfocused scale (p < 0.01), as expected.

We selected hospital employees as the targeted population; hospital employees have often been used as subjects in studies involving the workplace (Konovsku & Pugh, 1994; Krausz et al., 2000; Organ & Konovsky, 1989; Yoon & Lim, 1999). This population allowed us to minimize the possible issue of reaching subjects on the weekdays vs. weekends, which is important when studying the work and home domains. For example, if we had selected other work populations, it may have been difficult to reach subjects from the same organization in both the home and work domains at the same time. Since hospitals need to be staffed at all hours of the day, hospital employees such as doctors, nurses, and administrative staff (all randomly assigned in our field experiment), routinely work shifts that occur on both weekdays and weekends. Regarding our home domain, our general arguments that people go home to find rest and recovery and change their mindset to focus more on the comfort and security of home (Dovey, 1985; Kidd & Evans, 2011; Peeters et al., 2005) apply to this population, as does the expectation that employees may need to occasionally manage work-related tasks at home. Thus, the hospital employee population is appropriate for our study.

With the cooperation of the mobile operator, we were able to accurately pinpoint the location context of the targeted users. To obtain basic mobile services, each mobile user must connect to a cellular base station in the mobile network that identifies and records their location. Our corporate partner identified the hospital employees based on hospital VPMNs (virtual private mobile networks). A VPMN is a mobile phone network for connections by businesses rather than individuals. Hospital VPMNs are dedicated networks providing connectivity to hospital employees, i.e., by connecting to the VPMN, hospital employees can obtain basic access to mobile connectivity. The VPMN is consistently available for hospital employees (our subjects) at both work and home. This ensured that the subjects we examined consisted exclusively of the hospital’s employees. Note that there was no relationship between the promoted 4G service package and these VPMNs, and the two are orthogonal to each other. To obtain faster mobile network connections, users needed to personally subscribe to the 4G service. Our corporate partner had 20 major hospital VPMNs covering over 100,000 hospital employees.

We further limited our targeted population to those who had neither previously subscribed to the offered service nor received a similar offer from our corporate partner. This enabled us to rule out carryover effects from similar prior campaigns or subjects’ prior experience. Shortly before the promotional message was sent, <sup>6</sup> a total of 2,000 of the company’s real customers from hospital VPMNs were randomly selected to receive the promotional message; 1,000 were identified as being at work, and 1,000 were identified as being at home. Subjects at work (i.e., the hospital) were identified based on both GPS information (i.e., hospital locations) and their connection to the hospital VPMN (ensuring they were hospital employees and not just random individuals at the hospital locations). Subjects at home were identified by tracking the locations (determined to be residential areas by GPS) where the hospital employees had been for at least 10 nights over the previous 30 days. We selected 10 nights to account for hospital employees who frequently work night shifts and thus spend relatively fewer nights at home.<sup>7</sup>

Our corporate partner randomly assigned the 2,000 customers to the eight experimental conditions and two control conditions, and sent the different versions of the promotional messages accordingly. Random assignment was performed using SAS software’s random number generator and the RANUNI function, which returns a random value from a uniform distribution (Deng & Graz, 2002). We sorted the random numbers in sequence and extracted a sample from the sequence. We then integrated each of these randomization steps directly into an algorithm in our corporate partner’s information system when sending the mobile promotional SMS messages. While government regulation prevented us from obtaining consumers’ demographic information, we did have access to anonymized consumer mobile usage records. These mobile behavioral records included individual users’ monthly phone bills (ARPU), minutes used (MOU), short message services (SMS) in terms of the number of text messages sent and received, and data usage (GPRS) in byes of communication data used. Table 1a reports the summary statistics of these data.

## 4.2 Results

Of the 2,000 subjects who received the SMS message, 233 participants made a purchase. This robust 11.7% overall purchase rate is relatively high but is not surprising because our targeted users were equipped with a 4G-ready mobile handset and would immediately be able to enjoy the fast mobile data service enabled by their phone by purchasing this new 4G service package. Also, 4G service was newly available in the target market and was widely mentioned in the news at that time; thus, we anticipated a relatively high purchase rate a priori.

## 4.3 Additional Survey

To provide more confidence in our findings, we conducted a survey to further validate the theoretical arguments made in our hypothesis development. The survey was conducted with the help of a professional market research company, which maintains a large panel of general consumers from diverse backgrounds. The company also employs quality assurance mechanisms, including verification of consumer demographic information, checking the quality of survey data, and identifying potentially fraudulent acts. These mechanisms ensured access to a sample that spanned the general consumer population (versus student samples, for example), which is important to differentiate consumers at work from consumers at home.

The professional market research company invited a panel of respondents to participate in the survey when they were at home or at work. A screening question was first presented to check their current location (home, work, elsewhere). If the respondents did not report being at home or at work, the survey was terminated. In the survey, we asked the respondents questions related to the following: (1) regulatory focus of the primary activities associated with the current domain, and (2) perceived role of family members and coworkers. These questions were related to the key assumptions underlying our arguments for the different within-domain and cross-domain communication situations in our study.

Specifically, we argue based on regulatory focus theory that people’s motivational orientation can be shaped by the key activities associated with a particular context, i.e., it can be induced by situational cues from these activities (Boesen-Mariani et al., 2010; Crowe & Higgins, 1997; Fay et al., 2019). Thus, it is important to understand people’s regulatory focus regarding the primary activities associated with a context, such as the work and home domains examined in this study. The regulatory focus should shape people’s preference for communicating with those within the same domain (i.e., within-domain communication) based on border theory, which theorizes that people tend to maintain consistency in their mindset and thinking patterns and thus seek to stay within psychological boundaries in order to simplify their environment (Ashforth et al., 2000). In addition, whether communication is considered consistent may also be related to the perceived role of the communication target, which determines the goal of the communication. Crossdomain communication is likely to introduce inconsistency because the goals and regulatory focus of the primary activities associated with a boundary and the goal of communicating with someone outside the boundary are likely to be different. Consequently, crossdomain communication leads to the perceived intrusion of the current psychological boundary and motivates people to protect the boundary (Ashforth et al., 2000; Clark, 2000; Nippert-Eng, 1996; Zerubavel, 1991), which fits a prevention orientation. Thus, understanding people’s perceived role of their co-workers and family members is important to understand whether communications would be perceived as inconsistent and introduce perceived intrusion to a current psychological boundary. We contacted consumers when they were at home or work to obtain more accurate responses to the items concerning their regulatory focus regarding the primary activities associated with the respective context. Since they would have been exposed to situational cues from the corresponding environment at the time of the survey response, we would expect them to be sensitive to the nuanced differences in the motivational orientations described in the survey items. The market research company obtained responses from 817 consumers; 408 responded to the survey when they were at home and 409 responded when they were at work.

## 5 Discussion and Conclusion

Given the ubiquitous nature of mobile communication technologies and their pervasive impact on human communications in work and home domains (Matusik & Mickel, 2011; Wajcman & Rose, 2011), it is important to understand how people approach both within-domain and cross-domain mobile communications in these two domains. While previous research has noted the innate human drive toward social connections and described how mobile communication technologies can better fulfill such needs, the negative impact of these technologies in terms of introducing intrusions from one domain to another is also well-noted (e.g., Chen & Karahanna, 2014; Jarvenpaa & Lang, 2005; Tams et al., 2020). Given their interruptive nature, one may be tempted to deduce that mobile cross-domain communications should not be highlighted in the promotion of mobile communication technologies (since this may remind people of the interruptions that such technologies can introduce) and that such promotion should focus solely on how the technology can enable better within-domain communications (e.g., with its faster speed and richer media support, as in the case of the transition from 3G to 4G). However, our study shows that there is another angle that should be examined regarding this issue.

Specifically, our study shows that when promoting a mobile communication technology in the work domain, highlighting that the technology can enable promotion-focused communication with coworkers can result in higher purchase rates. However, regarding communications with family members in this domain, prevention-focused communication is more effective and, in our study, resulted in a substantially higher purchase rate than not mentioning any target of communication (e.g., see Figure 2). This study extends and further clarifies the understanding of how people approach cross-domain communications. Although communications from family members introduce work interruptions and may negatively impact work performance, we show that such communication is nonetheless still desired despite its potential negative impact. In particular, our findings suggest that people are interested in facilitating prevention-focused communication with family members at work (to avoid disconnection), and highlighting this need can improve purchase rates.

Our findings are similar regarding cross-domain communications in the home domain, i.e., concerning communications with coworkers at home. Specifically, we found that highlighting the prevention focus of communications generated higher purchase rates, compared to highlighting a promotion focus and compared to mentioning no communication target at all. This again shows that despite their potential negative impacts, people are nevertheless willing to invest in cross-domain communications facilitated by mobile communication technologies as long as the orientation is prevention focused. These findings have nontrivial implications for both research and practice, as discussed below.

## 5.1 Implications for Research

The findings from this research primarily contribute to three areas of literature: mobile communications, mobile targeting, and mobile technology adoption. Mobile communication technologies are considered a “revolutionary device” (Fortunati, 2002), not only because of the pervasiveness and richness of the communications they afford but also because such communications blur the public and private spheres of human life, leading to the need to consider not only the within-domain but also the cross-domain communications they can afford (Jarvenpaa & Lang, 2005; Prasopoulou et al., 2006). However, with few exceptions (Chen & Karahanna, 2014; Lutz et al., 2020; Wan et al., 2019), there is a lack of research that considers both forms of mobile communications together. Our study fills this research gap by showing how people’s approach to within- and cross-domain mobile communications can be understood through regulatory focus theory (Higgins, 1997, 1998), which highlights that mobile communications can be either promotion or prevention focused. In particular, concerning mobile cross-domain communications (i.e., communicating with family members at work or with coworkers at home), prevention-focused communication is more appealing than promotionfocused communication. Our findings also unveil the nuanced differences in within-domain communication preferences—i.e., promotion focused in the work domain and prevention focused in the home domain.

In addition, our study contributes to the literature on mobile targeting. While existing research on mobile targeting has made good progress in uncovering user contexts that can be leveraged to target consumers, the focus has primarily been on contexts related to locations such as cinemas and shopping centers (e.g., Fang et al., 2015; Fong et al., 2015; Luo et al., 2014), neglecting the plausibly larger domains of mobile technology used in daily life, i.e., the work and home domains (Verkasalo, 2009; Wajcman, 2015). People generally spend much more time at work and home than at places like shopping malls. There is thus a need for research investigating and understanding how mobile targeting can be conducted in these contexts. Our study thus not only highlights the relevance of regulatory focus theory in conducting mobile targeting in the work and home domains but, through the additional survey, also unveils people’s psychologies and perceptions regarding the activities and key social actors in these domains. In so doing, our research complements the research by Andrews et al. (2015) and Ghose et al. (2018) that addresses the commuting context but ignores the work and home contexts by including the work and home domains within the repertoire of mobile targeting contextual variables, thus adding an important piece of the puzzle in this research area to make the picture more complete.

Furthermore, our research also relates and contributes to the research on mobile technology adoption (e.g., Boakye, 2015; Hong et al., 2008; Kim et al., 2011; Kuo & Yen, 2009; Kwon & Chidambaran, 2000; Wang et al., 2006). In our research, purchasing the mobile communication technology service (4G) implies that the technology was adopted for use in one’s daily communications. Our study adds to this stream of literature by showing that apart from traditional technology acceptance factors such as ease of use (e.g., Kuo & Yen, 2009; Wang et al., 2006) and factors associated with mobile technology features such as mobility and ubiquity (Boakye, 2015; Hong et al., 2008; Kim & Garrison, 2009), paying attention to the different nature of mobile communications (in particular, whether they are promotion or prevention focused) may also help to promote the adoption of a mobile communication technology.

Specifically, highlighting that a mobile technology can help communication with social others is essentially related to highlighting the usefulness of the technology. In this regard, previous research has customarily focused on the promotion-focused aspects of the communicative usefulness of a focal mobile technology, e.g., how the technology can help promote communication in more efficient and effective ways (Yoon et al., 2015) to accomplish a wider variety of tasks more quickly and effectively (Kim & Garrison, 2009; Kuo & Yen, 2009; Hong et al., 2008; Kim et al., 2007; Han et al., 2006). Our research shows that there is another way to frame the communicative usefulness of a mobile technology, i.e., in a prevention- vs. promotion-focused way. The implication of this is nontrivial because highlighting the communicative usefulness of a mobile technology in different ways according to the particular social context can lead to better adoption outcomes. For example, managers could present an information security technology in a promotion-focused way (such as emphasizing how it can promote the secured sharing of important data) or a prevention-focused way (such as emphasizing how it can prevent the loss of important data) according to different contexts (e.g., highly collaborative vs. highly independent work situations).

## 5.2 Implications for Practice

For practice, the insights provided by our research are most directly applicable to the promotional strategies of mobile communication technologies—in particular mobile network service, such as the latest 5G technology. As mentioned, the development of mobile communication technologies such as 5G can have major impacts on a large number of related industries (e.g., gaming, live video streaming, virtual reality, augmented reality, and other newer forms of multimedia). Yet, take its predecessor, 4G network service as an example— while its subscriber base was forecast to reach 1,812 million worldwide by 2020, <sup>8</sup> it experienced slow uptakes in many markets.<sup>9</sup> Thus, careful consideration is needed to understand how to better promote such technology services to enable quick recovery of investments and facilitate growth in industries depending on the adoption of such technology. Our research provides actionable guidance on how to promote a new mobile communication technology in the work and home domains, where users can be reached at most times during the day.

In particular, our findings show that highlighting an appropriate communication orientation is generally more effective than just sending a generic promotional message stating the benefits of the technology (e.g., faster network speed) and that highlighting crossdomain communications with the appropriate motivational orientation can lead to favorable outcomes as well. This adds to the repertoire of message designs that firms can utilize in promoting mobile communication technologies. Given newer mobile network services such as 5G (and 6G, 7G, etc. in the future), our research is timely. In addition, we believe our research findings may be applied to the promotion of other mobile communication technologies in general, given that people’s motivational orientations in different communication situations (communicating with family members vs. coworkers at home vs. at work) are likely to hold when they use mobiles apps such as instant messaging and social networking tools.

Our research also extends the contextual dimensions that practitioners can employ for mobile targeting beyond typical consumption contexts such as supermarkets, shopping malls, and cinemas. Targeting consumers where they spend most of their time, i.e., work and home, is not only promising but also viable, as our research shows. With respect to mobile communication technologies, the key is to be sensitive to people’s regulatory focus in their mobile communications in the two pertinent domains of contemporary human life.

Furthermore, our findings may provide non-piecemeal but directional guidance to practitioners in incorporating and recommending features of mobile communication applications that suit users’ orientations in different communication situations involving work and home. Specifically, practitioners such as app designers could incorporate and recommend features that focus on preventing disconnection for use in the home domain, as well as in the work domain when communicating with family members. For instance, Chen and Karahanna (2018) describe such features regarding the iPhone (although they are not formally conceptualized as prevention-focused communication features): “while iPhone users can block all incoming communications with the ‘Do Not Disturb’ feature, they can differentiate more important communications (e.g., emergencies and those from specific groups in one’s contacts) from the rest with the ‘Repeated Calls’ and ‘Allow Calls From’ settings” (p. 1039) These iPhone features are prevention focused in that they allow users to maintain communications with specific contacts (e.g., family members when at work) to avoid disconnection from them. Chen & Karahanna (2018) also advocate such features for cross-domain communications. In contrast, for communications with coworkers at work, practitioners might incorporate and recommend features geared toward strengthening connections. For example, the online status of coworkers could be prominently shown (such as “available to talk”), and online meeting functions with rich media support could be provided for easy communication and the sharing of information and resources.

Lastly, the promotion- vs. prevention-focused communication differentiation could also help inform the design of emerging social communicative tools that do not necessarily involve interpersonal communication, such as social robots. <sup>10</sup> Social robots are becoming increasingly popular (Nocentini et al., 2019: Shourmasti et al., 2020); they are “designed to support and service humans through physical and social interactions” (Ivanov et al., 2017, p.3), and are aimed at establishing a natural interaction with humans (Nocentini et al., 2019). Due to their nature, it is believed that they will potentially “influence developments in interpersonal communication” (Abendschein et al., 2021, p. 1) by mimicking human communication to achieve interpersonal communication objectives. An example of a social robot is Pepper, which was developed to communicate with humans and provide services in various settings. For example, in the retail setting, Pepper can greet customers coming to a store, provide product recommendations, and offer assistance when needed. However, it may be important to discern whether users want services from social robots in a promotion- or prevention-focused way. For example, when communicating and interacting with customers, a key benefit highlighted for Pepper is its proactive engagement ability: “Our robots call shoppers’ attention, initiate interaction by voice and animated expression, or proactively go towards people to start conversations.” <sup>11</sup> Instead of such a promotion-focused approach, users may sometimes prefer a prevention-focused approach of communication, such as just having the robot quietly accompany them and let them feel assured that assistance is available when needed. To do this, users’ momentary regulatory focus should first be assessed (e.g., through asking a few questions when they first enter a store) so that social robots can provide the kind of communication and interaction desired, with the aim of achieving better acceptance and use effectiveness.

## 5.3 Limitations and Future Research

There are some limitations of this study that need to be recognized. First, our research employs hospital employees as the subjects in our field experiment, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to some extent. However, this employee population has commonly been employed in research involving the workplace (Cunningham et al., 2002; Konovsky & Pugh, 1994; Krausz et al., 2000; Organ & Konovsky, 1989; Yoon & Lim, 1999); one of the advantages of studying this population is that they routinely work on both weekdays and weekends, which may help minimize the possible issue of reaching subjects at home/work on weekdays/weekends. In addition, research has shown that findings based on this work population can be generalized to other occupations and work contexts (e.g., Al-Abrrow & Abrishamkar, 2003; Matta et al., 2020; Moorman, 1993; Neves, 2009). Nonetheless, future research should investigate other employee populations and conduct multiple field experiments on both weekdays and weekends to test the generalizability of our findings to other conditions (such as firms sending mobile promotional messages on weekends).

Second, our findings may not hold for individuals who work primarily from home (e.g., Ahrentzen, 1987; Felstead et al., 2021; Gurstein, 1991; Hislop et al., 2015; Prügj & Tinker, 1997). For this group of individuals, home and work become one and home may have different meanings for them as a result. For example, Gurstein (1991, p. 174) notes: “With the emergence of the home as a ‘virtual workplace’ the home is no longer a buffer against the external pressures of the market place … it loses its refuge nature as work-related stresses become associated with the home.” Ahrentzen (1987) also corroborates that for these individuals, home loses its character as a personal expression of private life and becomes an expression of the individual as a professional working person. Future research could focus on this group of individuals and investigate what different considerations are needed in promoting mobile communication technologies to them.

Third, we do not consider the potential role of the time of day in influencing users’ motivational orientations in mobile communications. Previous research suggests that people’s psychological states may vary across different times of day (Golder & Macy, 2011) and that different values may be more appealing when receiving a mobile offer in the morning vs. the afternoon (Phang et al., 2019). To keep the scope of our study manageable and to control for the possible time effect, we sent the promotional message simultaneously during daytime hours, as is typical of mobile targeting practices (Danaher et al., 2015). Future research could incorporate the consideration of time of day to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the effects of user contexts on their mobile communication orientations. For instance, it is possible that people may become more prevention oriented in communicating with coworkers in the evening when they are at home and in a relaxed mood. Notwithstanding the above-mentioned limitations, we believe our study offers a solid foundation for promising future research.
