# Mobile phones during work and non-work time: A case study of mobile, non-managerial workers

Donald Hislop <sup>a,</sup>⁎, Carolyn Axtell <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Loughborough University Business School, Ashby Road, Loughborough, LE 113TU, United Kingdom

<sup>b</sup> Institute of Work Psychology & Management School, University of Sheffield, United Kingdom

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 17 December 2009 Received in revised form 5 January 2011 Accepted 5 January 2011 Available online 1 Febuary 2011

Keywords: Mobile phone Work/non-work boundary Boundary management

## a b s t r a c t

This paper examines how some non-managerial/professional workers (mobile service engineers) used mobile phones for work, with a central focus on phone use during working hours. During working hours the engineers not only used their phones as work-related communication tools, but also as a tool to manage the work/non-work boundary. By examining non-managerial workers, and boundary management during work time the paper addresses a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant gap in knowledge. The data presented shows that while the boundary between work and non-work time at the start and end of the working day was clear, and that work demands rarely intruded upon non-work time, the situation with regard to working hours was quite different. Fundamentally, the way the engineers' used their mobile phones during working hours resulted in the work/non-work boundary regularly becoming blurred and unclear. A key reason why the engineers use their phones as a boundary management tool during working hours relates to the character of their work, where the engineers spent much of their working time alone, driving to client sites, and also due to the unpredictable opportunities the engineers had for face-to-face interaction with clients or colleagues.

## 1. Introduction

While much research suggests that the use of mobile communication technologies<sup>1</sup> facilitates the blurring of the work/non-work boundary and makes it easier for work to intrude upon non-work time (Middleton & Cukier, 2006; Prasapolou, Pouloudi, & Panteli, 2006; Schlosser, 2002; Towers, Duxbury,

Higgins, & Thomas, 2006) some research has questioned the inevitability of this (Golden & Geisler, 2007; Wajcman, Bittman, & Brown, 2008), as well as questioning whether a blurred boundary is necessarily an indicator of work-life balance problems (Cousins & Robey, 2005). However, most of this research has focussed narrowly on managerial and professional workers. This paper contributes to knowledge in this area through examining the relationship between mobile communication technology use and the work/ non-work boundary of a cohort of non-managerial/professional workers (of<sup>fi</sup>ce equipment service engineers).

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study highlight how mobile communication technologies can be used as a boundary management tool not only during non-work time, but also during work time. It also highlights how the character of people's work can play an important role in shaping the extent to which work intrudes upon people's non-work time. The paper also suggests that the relationship between mobile communication technology use and the work/non-work boundary is shaped not only by the actions of those who use these technologies (see for example Golden & Geisler, 2007; Wajcman et al., 2008) but is also shaped by the nature of people's work activities.

In looking at work-related mobile technology use and how it can shape the work/non-work boundary the paper draws on boundary theory (Ashforth, Kreiner, & Fugate, 2000; Kreiner, Hollenbse, & Sheep, 2009; Rothbard, Phillips, & Dumans, 2005) as this highlights the often complicated relationship that can exist between people's work and non-work domains and which also emphasizes the role of human agency in shaping the nature of the work/non-work boundary, and how people act to manage activities and demands in and across these domains.

Section 2 reviews relevant literature on mobile technology use in work, and on the role of these technologies in boundary management activities. This section of the paper highlights the existing gaps in knowledge that this paper addresses. Following this Section 3 outlines the research and analytical methodology utilized to collect and analyze the qualitative data that is presented here. In Section 4 the empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings are presented, while Section 5 analyzes the empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings to highlight the key conclusions, <sup>fi</sup>ndings and conceptual implications of the data presented.

## 2. Mobile communication technologies and the work/non-work boundary

Two related areas where the use of mobile communication technologies appears to have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on work are in reshaping work-related communication patterns, and in blurring the work/nonwork boundary. In relation to communication patterns, Fortunati (2002)<sup>2</sup> potentially revolutionary devices. This is because, she argues, the ‘anytime, anyplace’ functionality they possess, which (theoretically) offers people the potential of ‘perpetual contact’ (Katz & Aakhus, 2002; Perry, O'Hara, Sellen, Brown, & Harper, 2001; Wiberg & Ljungberg, 2001), means they can radically change the way people construct and experience time and space through the way they allow people to manage their ‘absent presence’ in contexts where they are not physically located (Gergen, 2002; Licoppe, 2004).

With respect to the relationship between the work-related use of mobile communication technologies and the work/non-work boundary the vast majority of studies have focussed on the use of these technologies outside of normal working hours, and have in general terms concluded that the use of these technologies results in the work/non-work boundary becoming blurred through allowing work to be more easily carried out during non-work time. For example, Towers et al. (2006), Boswell and Olson-Buchanan (2007) and Fenner and Renn (2010) found this to be the case with regard to of<sup>fi</sup>ce-based workers who used a range of technologies to work extra hours away from the of<sup>fi</sup>ce. Further, a number of studies reached similar conclusions regarding the use of mobile phones (Chesley, 2005; Jarvenpaa & Lang, 2005; Townsend & Batchelor, 2005), mobile email devices such as Blackberries (Middleton, 2008; Middleton & Cukier, 2006; Orlikowski, 2007; Schlosser, 2002) and personal organizers/PDAs (Peters & Allouch, 2005). Two illustrations of the general <sup>fi</sup>ndings from these studies can be provided by Prasapolou et al.'s (2006), study of mobile phone use by some Greek professional workers which found that taking calls from work colleagues after working hours, ‘allow[ed] work-related activities to penetrate time segments devoted to other spheres of life’ (p. 280) and the conclusion from Schlosser's (2002) study of blackberry use, where people's use patterns led to the ‘blurring of traditional family–work boundaries’, (p. 414).

However, the paradoxical (Jarvenpaa & Lang, 2005), or ‘janus-faced’ (Arnold, 2003) character of mobile phones, and mobile communication technologies in general means that while they can produce such effects, they can also be used as a tool for managing the work/non-work boundary (Golden & Geisler, 2007; Kreiner et al., 2009). Thus a number of studies emphasizing the agency of mobile technology users in shaping the impacts of these technologies on their work/non-work boundary have challenged the <sup>fi</sup>ndings outlined above. Wajcman et al. (2008), based on a web-based survey of mobile phone use among Australian households argued that, ‘individuals exert control over the extent to which calls invade their personal time’ (p. 635). Secondly, Golden and Geisler's (2007) study of PDA use among professional workers in the USA found that people could use them in different ways to support the particular styles of work/non-work boundary they preferred. Thirdly, Cousins and Robey (2005), based on a qualitative study of four nomadic ‘loan of<sup>fi</sup>cers’ emphasized the active role of these workers in using a range of mobile technologies to manage their work/non-work boundary. Thus, the work-related use of mobile communication technologies does not produce an inevitable blurring of the work/non-work boundary, and equally, a blurred work/non-work boundary may re<sup>fl</sup>ect people's preferences in this regard, rather than being an indicator of work/non-work balance problems (Cousins & Robey, 2005).

Despite the growing number of studies on the work-related use of mobile communication technologies and how they in<sup>fl</sup>uence people's work/non-work boundary, there are a number of limitations to this body of work. Firstly, virtually all of these papers focus narrowly on managerial, professional and ‘white collar workers (see Table 1). Secondly, many of these studies are centrally focussed on examining spillover from work to non-work time via examining work-related device use during non-work hours, and some of those which examine the use of these technologies during work time do so briefly. and provide few detailed insights into mobile technology use patterns during these times (such as Chesley, 2005; Jarvenpaa & Lang, 2005; Peters & Allouch, 2005; Wajcman et al., 2008). Thus, only a few papers provide substantial details on mobile technology use patterns during working time (such as Cousins & Robey, 2005 and Orlikowski, 2007). Thirdly, few of these studies examine workers who are mobile or nomadic and whose work requires them to frequently travel (exceptions being Cousins & Robey, 2005, and the real estate agents examined by Townsend & Batchelor, 2005). Finally, these studies also typically neglect to account for the role of the organizational context, and people's job characteristics in shaping the mobile technology use patterns and the character of their work/non-work boundary. Through presenting data from a study of mobile of<sup>fi</sup>ce equipment engineers, which takes account of the nature of the organizational context, and the engineers job characteristics, and which examines their mobile technology use patterns during both work and non-work hours, this paper addresses all of these gaps in knowledge.

In examining mobile, or nomadic workers, it is useful to acknowledge the diversity of forms that mobile working can take, with mobile workers potentially having a static base in their home, a static office base, or no static base at all (Hislop & Axtell, 2007). Research suggests that an increasing number of workers are required to be spatially mobile to carry out their work (Felstead, Jewson, & Walters, 2005; Hislop, 2008), and there has been a corresponding growth in the number of studies of such workers (Cousins & Robey, 2005; Hislop & Axtell, 2009; Laurier & Philo, 2003; Perry et al., 2001; Sorensen & Pica, 2005; Vartiainen & Hyrkkänen, 2010; Wiredu, 2007). With respect to the relationship between mobile technology use and the work/non-work boundary of such workers, most papers on this topic typically focus on the negative spillover of work into non-work time (for example Middleton, 2008). Thus, few studies of mobile workers provide detailed insights into how they use mobile communication technologies to manage the work/ non-work boundary during working hours.

While studies of mobile workers have in general not examined how these workers use communication technologies to manage relationship and interactions with work colleagues during work-time there is some literature on home-base teleworkers which has. These literatures suggest that the reduced opportunities home-based teleworkers have to interact face-to-face with colleagues may cause problems of social isolation (Baruch, 2000; Mann & Holdsworth, 2003). However, it is also suggested that such feelings are in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the extent to which people work at home, with full time home-based teleworkers likely to experience the greatest feelings of isolation (Baruch, 2001). Halford's (2005) study of hybrid home-of<sup>fi</sup>ce based workers also found that feelings of isolation while working at home were compensated for by maintaining communication with colleagues via phone calls and email. Thus, one speci<sup>fi</sup>c issue considered here is whether and how the engineers examined maintained contact with colleagues while working alone.

The way in which mobile communication technology use (during working and non-working time) affects the work/non-work boundary of mobile workers represents an interesting topic to examine for a number of reasons. Firstly, Hill, Ferris, and Martinson (2003) in a study comparing home-based, of<sup>fi</sup>ce based and mobile workers, found that the mobile workers had the poorest work/non-work balance, and so we might expect them to compensate for this with more non-work communications during work time. Further, the spatial mobility of these workers means that much of the communication with colleagues occurs not through face-to-face interaction, but via mobile communication technologies. Thus for mobile workers, communication via such devices may represent an important communication medium which allows them to manage their ‘absent presence’ from other mobile or of<sup>fi</sup>ce-based colleagues.

There are a diversity of ways through which to conceptualize the relationship between the work and non-work aspects of worker's lives. While the work–family con<sup>fl</sup>ict/enrichment perspective is arguably the most dominant, the perspective utilized here is boundary theory, which has been developed via, and grown in prominence through works such as Nippert-Eng (1996) and Ashforth et al. (2000). This perspective is utilized here for a number of reasons. Firstly, boundary theory is not only sensitive to, but is grounded in the idea that human agency is central in shaping people's work/non-work boundary. Thus, from this perspective, the work/non-work boundary is enacted via people's day-to-day choices and behaviours. Secondly, it is also sensitive to the fact that it is over-simplistic to conceptualize ‘work’ and ‘life’ as two separate and distinct domains as for a large proportion of workers the relationship between the work and non-work aspects of their lives is complex, with the boundary between these domains often being indistinct (Warhurst, Eikhof, & Haunschild, 2008). Thirdly, unlike the work–family con<sup>fl</sup>ict/enrichment literature, the non-work domain of people's lives is conceptualized as being wider than the family through taking account of other aspects of people's non-work lives, such as people's non family social lives, which includes activities such as socializing with friends, or undertaking a hobby.

A fundamental assumption of boundary theory is that people may have different preferences with regard to the nature of their work/non-work boundary, and may act in ways aimed at achieving their desired boundary style (Golden & Geisler, 2007; Kreiner et al., 2009; Rothbard et al., 2005). In general terms the boundary theory literature suggests that people's boundary style preferences can be conceptualized as existing on a spectrum, with a segmented boundary at one extreme, and an integrated one at the other. A segmented boundary is where the boundary between work and life is clear and strong, with work and life existing as separate and distinct domains, with little ‘leakage’ occurring between them. In contrast, an integrated boundary is where the boundary between work and life is permeable and blurred, and where work and life domains are closely inter-related and overlapping. Recent work has developed this framework in a number of ways including via the suggestion that people's boundary style preferences may vary during work and non-work time (Bulger, Matthews, & Hoffman, 2007; Hecht & Allen, 2009).

Another way in which boundary theory has been developed has been through the acknowledgement that the policies and practices of people's employers, and the characteristics of their jobs may also shape the character of people's work/non-work boundary, and that there may be a disjuncture between the type of boundary that people aim for, and that which they are able to achieve. For example Rothbard et al. (2005) examined how the <sup>fi</sup>t between the type of boundary style that people desired and that which they are able to achieve via access to particular types of family friendly policies affected their levels of commitment and job satisfaction. Further, de Man, de Bruijn, and Groeneveld (2008) found that the extent to which people worked at home, or had access to <sup>fl</sup>exitime affected the extent to which the work/non-work boundary was permeable and blurred. Other research, while not explicitly using boundary theory. reinforces these findings. Thus Baldry et al. (2007) found that a combination of personal and organizational factors meant that the work/non-work boundary of the call centre and software designers they studied were quite distinct, and Henninger and Papouschek (2008) found that occupational factors, such as the extent to which people had control over the hours they worked, were important in explaining the extent to which they were able to prevent the negative spillover of work into their non-work time.

In conclusion therefore, this paper utilizes boundary theory to understand how some mobile workers made use of mobile communication technologies as both a work-related communication tool, and to also manage their work/non-work boundary. Further, it looks at their technology use and boundary management practices during both work and non-work time, and also takes account of how contextual factors such as the character of the work undertaken by the engineers also shaped the character of their work/non-work boundary.

## 3. Methodology

The empirical data presented here is taken from a study of some of<sup>fi</sup>ce equipment service engineers. The broad objective of the research was to provide rich, qualitative insights into the lived experience, work patterns, communication dynamics and work context of the engineers (Hartley, 2004).

A multiple case study methodology was utilized for this research, with engineers in three separate organizations being examined. Carrying out case studies on three separate organizations allowed the similarities and differences between them to be revealed. The three companies that participated in the research were all based in the same South Yorkshire city, and all had a client base which was located in the same geographic region (broadly South Yorkshire and the East Midlands). The day-to-day work of these engineers involved visiting on average between three and <sup>fi</sup>ve customers per day to install, service and repair their of<sup>fi</sup>ce equipment (copiers, scanners, faxes, etc.). All three organizations were recruited to the research project via cold calling. In total 17 interviews were conducted, with 13 being engineers and four managers (see Table 2). This interview sample was chosen to gain insights into both the experience of mobile workers, and the managerial challenges involved in managing mobile workers. Finally, in all three organizations a representative sample of engineers were chosen to be interviewed.

The main data collection method used in the study was semi-structured interviews. All interviews were conducted by the paper's authors and lasted an average of 50 min each. All interviews were tape recorded and transcribed. An explicitly interpretative and qualitative approach was utilized, with the interviews concerned with developing an in-depth understanding of the engineer's and manager's work experiences. A semi-structured interview format was utilized as this allowed for both the collection of data on issues of interest to the authors, while also allowing the interviewees scope to introduce and discuss issues they considered relevant (Kvale, 1996). The interview schedule that was utilized asked questions on a number of broad topics including their general work and travel patterns, the extent to which and the way in which they utilized any static of<sup>fi</sup>ce space, the type of mobile communication technologies they had, the general character of their work-related communication patterns, the general character of the organizational structure and culture, and whether they experienced work-life balance problems.

In broad terms, the analytical methodology utilized was what Berg (2006) characterizes as qualitative content analysis, with the objective being to identify patterns in the data. The analysis began with the open coding of the interview transcripts, in order to identify general themes in the data. This involved author 1 reading each transcript in detail and labelling quotations thematically. From this process a number of themes emerged as important, and certain patterns also emerged as being potentially useful in explaining differences across the transcripts. One of the key themes that were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed via this process was the way in which the engineers used their mobile phones to achieve particular goals, and communicate with a diverse range of others. Further, in relation to this topic, one key pattern that was identi<sup>fi</sup>ed was a difference in the communication patterns of the engineers from Region 1 from that of the engineers from Local 1 and 2. Subsequently a process of axial coding was undertaken (Berg, 2006, p. 320), which focussed narrowly on the ways in which the engineers used their phones, and which took account of factors such as the purpose of calls, whom calls were made to, and the time at which calls were made. The interpretation of the data that was made by author 1 from this process was checked via conversation with author 2, who had independently read and coded the transcripts.

## 4. Empirical data

The central focus in this section is on describing the character and purpose of the engineers' workrelated mobile phone use patterns. However, to allow account to be taken of how these phone use patterns were shaped by factors such as the character of the engineers' work, it is also necessary to give details on the way work was organized within the case study companies, as well as how this shaped the way the engineers communicated with clients and colleagues. This section thus begins by examining the way work was organized within the case study companies. Secondly it presents data on the extent to which the engineers studied were able to interact face-to-face with colleagues and clients while carrying out their work. The third and largest topic examined is their phone use patters during working hours, with the <sup>fi</sup>nal section examining their work patterns and their work-related communication patterns during non-work hours.

## 4.1. Organizational context, job characteristics and work-related communication patterns

The day-to-day work of the engineers in all three companies was very similar, with them travelling independently by car to a range of different client sites to repair, service or install of<sup>fi</sup>ce equipment such as copiers, scanners and fax machines. Further, each engineer typically worked within a designated geographic area. Despite this, there were some differences between the companies that impacted on the engineers' communication patterns (see Table 3 and Section 4.3 that follow).

In organizational terms, the main difference between the case study companies was that while Local 1 and Local 2 were small independent, locally owned and managed companies Region 1 was part of a national corporation. This difference had some important implications for how work was organized within these companies. One of the most signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences was that while Local 1 and 2 had a local of<sup>fi</sup>ce base at which management and administrative staff were located, in contrast, in Region 1, there was no local of<sup>fi</sup>ce base that engineers could use, with management being located in a remote regional of<sup>fi</sup>ce, and administrative staff being located in a national call centre.

## 4.2. Opportunities for work-related face-to-face interactions

While the engineers spent the majority of their working time alone, they had a number of opportunities to have work-related face-to-face interactions with colleagues and clients. While customer-related interactions were similar for the engineers in all three companies, the extent to which they interacted faceto-face with colleagues varied, this being shaped by the fact that the engineers from Local 1 and 2 had a local of<sup>fi</sup>ce based, while those from Region 2 did not.

The existence of a local of<sup>fi</sup>ce base gave the engineers at Local 1 and 2 two primary opportunities to interact with colleagues, <sup>fi</sup>rstly at the start of the day, and secondly when they returned to collect parts. Such interactions didn't happen on a daily basis and typically weren't planned in advance, but when they occurred they provided the engineers an opportunity to communicate with each other about both work and non-work issues simultaneously. For example, Karl, the manager at Local 2, talking about early morning meetings said,

‘they might sit in here [of<sup>fi</sup>ce] for half an hour in the morning. OK you might be having a laugh and a joke, but the main part of it is about the jobs they did yesterday and the problems they came across, but it is done in a very loose social way’.

Further, in relation to the collection of parts, Duncan (Local 1) said,

‘we will come in [to the of<sup>fi</sup>ce] for parts and if there is any engineer in you will sit have a chat with them. So, we don't have designated times that we will meet up, it's just as and when we bump into people’.

While the lack of any local of<sup>fi</sup>ce base meant such opportunities didn't exist for the engineers at Region 1, they still occasionally managed to interact face-to-face with colleagues when collecting parts. Thus Jason, a manager at Region 1 said,

‘we tend to get little pockets of gatherings and usually it gathers round where the parts get dropped off. At Sheffield we have a parts drop and several engineers attend, probably first thing in the morning to see each other and have an informal chat’.

In terms of interactions with clients, as outlined, these were the same for the engineers in all three companies. As the engineers visited on average between three and <sup>fi</sup>ve clients per day, this provided a regular opportunity for face-to-face interaction. As many of the engineers had been responsible for clients in the same geographic areas for extended periods, this often resulted in them developing good relationships with some client contacts. Thus Barry talked about how

‘[with] most of the customers we are all on first name terms, they all know us and we know them’.

However, not all client visits provided opportunities for meaningful interaction. This was typically when the engineers didn't know people at a particular client well, or where the client ignored the engineers and didn't communicate with them except for minimal dialogue necessary to allow them to do their job. These two types of variable client experiences are summarized in the following quotations,

‘You know the first names [of some clients]. You know where they've been on holiday that year and things like that because you get on with them. But at other times in respect of certain companies you don't really know anybody’, Doug (Local 1).

‘There is two extremes [in clients]. There are a lot of people who are very sociable and chatty and offer you a cup of tea and then there are some people that would step over you to make a cup of tea and not acknowledge that you are there … then the engineer does not have any interaction at all, apart from saying ‘hello’ and ‘goodbye’ basically’, Kevin (Region 1).

Thus, while all the engineers had some opportunities to have face-to-face interactions with clients and colleagues while working, the number and quality of these interactions varied from day to day Further, the engineers didn't have much control over when such interactions occurred. Thus, face-to-face interactions, while providing the engineers with some opportunities for social interaction and support, didn't provide the engineers with a reliable social communication and support system.

## 4.3. Phone use patterns during working hours

This section provides details on the engineers' phone use patterns during working hours, examining not only who they called, and the frequency of these calls, but also the purposes for which calls were made. Because their work involved a lot of spatial mobility, and also because for the vast majority of the engineers' work time they were physically isolated from their colleagues, they typically made extensive use of their mobile phones. The most common calls they made were to customer call handlers, other engineers, or to their managers.

Overall the engineers regarded their work mobile phones as invaluable work tools, a sentiment that Jason, a manager from Region 1 summed up by saying, ‘you can't live without it, basically.’

For the engineers in Local 1 and 2, the people they called most were the administrators who were based in the local office. This was because not only did the engineers and administrative staff have td constantly share information related to the organization and scheduling of each job the engineers worked on, but also because the companies' computer systems were located at the local of<sup>fi</sup>ces, the engineers were required to call the of<sup>fi</sup>ce with information on each job once it had been completed. Such requirements are summarized in the following two quotations from different engineers,

‘they [administrative staff] are just for allocating calls to us and signing the calls we have done off — ordering parts that we need …’, Andrew (Local 1).

‘If you are stuck on the motorway and it is basically stand still I will phone the office and just say, “look do you realise there has been an accident and I am in a jam. I don't know how long it is going to be and I will let you know when I get moving again”’, Duncan (Local 1).

However, while these calls served an important work function, they also provided the engineers with an opportunity for some social interaction. Thus, Duncan (Local 1) elaborated on the above quote by saying,

‘phoning work control gives you a bit of rapport, a bit of social interaction with the girls’.

As outlined earlier, the allocation of work was organized differently for the engineers at Region 1. For them most communication with administrative staff occurred via the sending and receiving of automated text messages via their PDA phones. Thus for them there were signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly fewer phone calls with administrative staff. The following two quotes highlight this and also show that while the engineers recognized the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of this way of communicating, they also missed the social interaction they used to have when they had to make more phone calls to administrative staff,

‘we used to have to call in maybe three or four times a day to pick up calls, but now you speak to them [customer call handlers] three or four times a week. It was not just a case of getting the calls, you could also have a chat with them and get to know them and it [PDA phone] has certainly taken more of that away’, Mike (Region 1).

‘It is efficient [using PDA phones] and it fits with the modern style of doing it. [But] it cuts down a lot of human contact. When you did used to call into an office in a morning you would phone in and speak to someone, have a natter …’, Kevin (Region 1).

Another common type of call that the engineers from all three companies were regularly required to make were calls to their managers and/or colleagues for problem solving advice. While most of the jobs done by the engineers were relatively straightforward they also regularly came across problems which were uncommon, and which required them to seek advice. Two quotes that illustrate this are outlined below,

‘There are times when you would maybe ring other engineers for advice … or perhaps ring the service manager for advice’, Chris (Local 1).

‘So if I had any problems or technical issues say I was having a problem with a machine and I was struggling how to repair it really I could speak to Jason [area manager] or there are engineers in the area as well …’, Steve (Region 1).

However, as with the calls the engineers from Local 1 and 2 made to administrative staff, while there was generally an important work-related function to these calls, there was also typically a social element to them. This is illustrated in the following three quotations,

‘you know you can ring up and it's like ‘hello Ed. How you doing? Are you well?’ you might have a 30 second chat but then it's a case of you can get straight into ‘right I've got a problem with this machine. What do you think is doing this?’, Doug (Local 2).

‘I get on quite well with Darren he is a good lad. I get on well with them all to tell you the truth, but I get on really well with Darren. So now and again we will phone for a ten minute chat — “I have not seen you for a couple of days how is it going” and stuff like that,’ Andrew (Local 1).

‘Sometimes you need to phone up and blow off some steam or something if you've had a bad day or whatever and talk to somebody about it’, Ed (Local 2).

Thus these calls typically had a dual function, allowing the sharing of important work-related information, and providing a mechanism via which the engineers could maintain social relationships with their colleagues.

The <sup>fi</sup>nal issue to highlight here is the absence of calls made by the engineers during working hours to family members and non-work friends. Opportunities to make or take such calls during working hours were limited. For the engineers, they spent most of their working day either driving to clients' sites, or working at clients' sites which made it very dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to take or make non-work-related calls. The only time during the working day when the engineers made such calls was during their lunch hour, which the engineers typically spent alone, sitting in their vans.

## 4.4. Work patterns and work-related communication patterns during non-work hours

This section presents data on the engineers' work patterns, and how they dealt with work-related calls that occurred outside of working hours. In all three companies working hours were relatively <sup>fi</sup>xed, with the engineers working Mondays to Fridays from approximately 8.30/9 am until 5/5.30 pm. Thus Darren from local 1, in describing his working hours, said, ‘there is no overtime it is 9 to 5.30 and there is not the opportunity to earn more money.

The main area of <sup>fl</sup>exibility in their working hours was with their start and <sup>fi</sup>nish times. Fundamentally, these were shaped by how far their <sup>fi</sup>rst and last customer was located from the engineers' homes/local of<sup>fi</sup>ces. This <sup>fl</sup>exibility is outlined by Andrew (Local 1), who is describing his working hours,

‘9 to 5.30 apart from on Friday, which is 9 to 5. But they are not that strict with us about it … If I am working in the Northern area and you don't finish until 5 o'clock and you have to get round home and you are not home until 6 or half past … But I don't think anyone can complain about that because we are all finishing early a lot of the time. When I say finishing early they will say, ‘it is half four and there is nothing to do so get off home.’ They usually don't give you a new call after 4. If it takes you 10 minutes to get home, brilliant. If it takes you half an hour, not bad.’

Further, pay was not affected by the speci<sup>fi</sup>c hours that the engineers worked, with pay given on the basis of a nominal 37.5 hour week. Thus if they had to leave home earlier than normal to get to a distant client, or they returned home late, they were not paid overtime, with it being assumed that, on balance, this would be compensated for by other occasions when they were able to get home early. Finally, while bonus payments were available to the engineers in all three companies related to various performance metrics such as their ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency in using spare parts, these bonuses were very small compared to basic pay levels.

The most fundamental reason that the engineers rarely had to work outside of their normal daily hours was that their customers typically only required a daytime service. Thus Kevin, an engineer at Region 1, said,

‘most of the time it's not that practical [to work late], because people [customers] go home. And a lot of places aren't keen on leaving you there … a lot of other commercial premises you know, they're off at five, and you're out, and they're locking it up and they don't want people loitering around.

One difference between the companies examined was in the types of mobile communication technologies they had (see Table 3). The PDA-phones used by the engineers in Region 1 were supplied by the company, and were used during work hours only. In Local 1 and Local 2 most engineers had one phone, which they used for both work and personal purposes, with a few possessing two phones, one of which was a dedicated work phone. What was common across all three case study companies was that there were no formal policies regarding how the engineers used their phones outside of working hours, with their being no organizational expectation that they would be available to deal with work-related calls outside of work time.

While it was rare for the engineers to receive work-related calls outside of their working hours, in all three organizations, the engineers acted in a range of ways to ensure that during non-work time the domains of work and non-work were kept separate, and that any work-related calls they did receive did not intrude on non-work time.

For the engineers who had work speci<sup>fi</sup>c mobile phones (the engineers in Region 1 and a few of the others), this boundary was maintained simply by switching their phone off after work. Thus, Martin, an engineer with Local 2 said,

‘when I am at home … I walk out of the car. It is either left in the car overnight or it is switched off and charging up.’

Jason, one of the managers with Region 1 expressed similar sentiments when he said the following about his PDA/phone,

‘you can't live without it basically. But having said that, once work is done I don't have a mobile phone, so it is a purely work thing … [My phone is on] From 8 to 6 and then it is off. Work hours are from 8.30 to 5.30 but I could get a phone call from 8 o'clock’

For the engineers in Local 1 and Local 2 who had one phone for both work and personal use, switching the phone off at the end of the working day was less of an option. One way of dealing with this was through using call <sup>fi</sup>ltering, to transfer work related calls to voicemail. Doug, an engineer with Local 2 articulated this sentiment in relation to holidays as follows,

‘if I am on a day off ….. I have got it set on my phone so all my friends and everything still ring me, but anything work related just get bounced straight to the answer machine straightaway.

When asked whether he was ever tempted to check his voicemail during holidays he said, ‘god no! …. When I am on holiday I am on holiday.

## 5. Discussion

The central focus of the paper is on the way in which the service engineers used mobile phones during their working hours. Before engaging in a detailed discussion of the key issues that emerge from the data just presented, it is useful to make two observations which give an indication of the focus the discussion will take. Firstly, as with the studies of Cousins and Robey (2005) and Kreiner et al. (2009) during the course of their working day the engineers used the mobile communication technologies at their disposal as both work-related communication tools and tools for managing the work/non-work boundary. This meant that when the engineers used their phones during work-time the work/non-work boundary often became somewhat blurred due to the dual work and non-work-related purposes of many of the calls the engineers made. Secondly, in contrast to the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of most studies on the use of mobile communication technologies for work (such as Schlosser, 2002, and Prasapolou et al., 2006; Orlikowski, 2007), the workrelated use of mobile communication technologies by the engineers did not result in work intruding upon non-work time to any signi<sup>fi</sup>cant extent. Thus, the central focus of this discussion will be to explain both of these <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

## 5.1. The boundary between work and non-work time at the start and end of the working day

Of the various studies of mobile technology use and boundary management that have been published, the most comparable to the engineers studied here, due to the spatial mobility involved in their work, is Cousins and Robey's (2005) study of mortgage loan of<sup>fi</sup>cers. When the focus is on the boundary between work and non-work time at the start and end of the day, and work-related phone use patterns during non-work hours (as is done in Section 4.4), a stark contrast emerges. Thus, while the workers studied by Cousins and Robey (2005) had a blurred boundary, the service engineers had a clear and distinct boundary. Further, while the workers studied by Cousins and Robey (2005) frequently made use of mobile communications technology for work during non-work hours, such as at evenings and weekends, for the service engineers this typically never happened, with the instances of when they had to deal with work issues during non-work time being virtually non-existent. Arguably, these differences stem from signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences in the nature of their work and work cultures.

For the service engineers the reason why work very rarely intruded upon their non-work time was that their working hours were relatively <sup>fi</sup>xed, and their employers had little need for them to work outside of their contracted hours. Effectively the engineer's employers provided a daytime, of<sup>fi</sup>ce-hours service only, and the clients they had did not require any out of hours support for their of<sup>fi</sup>ce equipment. Thus, at the end of each working day the engineers were able to switch their phones off and were typically not required to switch them back on until work started the following morning. In contrast, for the workers studied by Cousins and Robey (2005), work was quite different with them having to deal with clients at a wide range of times and not just during daytime ‘of<sup>fi</sup>ce hours’, and also with the nature of their work making it possible to carry out work tasks outside of formal working hours. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings therefore reinforce Baldry et al.'s (2007) argument that in understanding the work/non-work boundary of any type of worker it is necessary to take account of key features of their jobs such as the nature of their work tasks and the character of their working hours and organizational culture.

A subsidiary reason why work-related calls rarely interrupted the engineers' non-work time was in the way the engineers behaved during non-work hours. In Wajcman et al.'s (2008) study of mobile phone use in Australia they found that people were able to make choices and decisions about how and whether to deal with work-related calls during non-work hours and talked about how ‘the flow of workplace demands passing through the walls that separate home and work can be filtered’, (p. 646) and that, ‘people may erect a boundary in order to maintain a distance between home and work’, (2008, p. 645). This is how the engineers behaved on the rare occasions when they received work-related calls during non-work time, for example, in the way Doug (Local 2) reported dealing with work-related phone calls while on holiday. Thus in terms of how they protected non-work time from occasional work demands, the engineers could be characterized as ‘segmentors’ in boundary preference terms (Ashforth et al., 2000).

This paper thus adds to the growing body of literature which argues that the use of mobile communication technologies for work does not result in an inevitable blurring of the work/non-work boundary at the start and end of the working day, or during non-work hours (Cousins & Robey, 2005; Golden & Geisler, 2007; Wajcman et al., 2008). Further, the relationship between the use of mobile communication technologies and the character of people's work/non-work boundary was shaped not only by the agency of the device user (as was found by Wajcman et al., 2008 and Golden & Geisler, 2007), but also by structural factors such as the character of people's work activities.

## 5.2. Phone use and boundary management practices during work-time

The character of the engineers' mobile phone use patterns during working hours challenges the idea that in boundary management terms they can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed purely as ‘segmentors’. In contrast to the clear, strong boundary that existed between work and non-work activities during non-work time, during working hours the boundary between work and non-work activities was more complex and often blurred Further, the engineers adopted a quite different approach to the management of the work/non-work boundary during working hours. Fundamentally, it was the way the engineers used their mobile phones during working hours that regularly created a blurred work/non-work boundary, with their phone-based communication behaviour patterns arguably being shaped by the nature of their work. Thus, in contrast to other studies of the work-related use of mobile technology, which found that their use contributed to a blurring of the work/non-work boundary during non-work hours (Orlikowski, 2007; Prasapolou et al., 2006), this study found that such a blurring could occur during working hours.

The blurred work/non-work boundary that existed during working hours was not a consequence of calls with family and non-work friends, as such calls were typically not made, but was instead due to the character of calls made to work colleagues. Many of these calls typically had a dual function, one being work-related, and the other being social. For all the engineers, but particularly for the engineers at Local 1 and 2, regular work-related mobile phone calls had to be made to both other engineers (to get advice on solving uncommon problems) and to of<sup>fi</sup>ce-based administrative staff (to give and receive information on the processing of client calls). However, the interviews highlighted how these calls also served an important social function for the engineers, for example in maintaining contact and friendship with colleagues, and sharing frustrations regarding particular clients. The importance of the social aspect of these calls was arguably due to some key features of their work, including the fact that the engineers spent a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant amount of work time on their own, driving to client's sites, and that opportunities for good quality face-to-face social interactions with either colleagues or clients were often limited (see data in Sections 4.1 and 4.2). Fundamentally, in this context the engineers managed their ‘absent presence (Licoppe, 2004) from work colleagues by using the opportunity provided by making work-related calls to colleagues to maintain social contacts with them. Effectively, the engineers often used work-related phone calls as a means to compensate for the lack of face-to-face social interaction with colleagues they typically had.

The difference in the number of calls made by the engineers from Region 1 and those from Local 1 and 2 also revealed the importance of this social aspect of such calls. As was outlined earlier, differences between Region 1 and Local 1 and 2 in both the type of mobile phones they had, and how work was organized, meant that the engineers in Region 1 didn't need to make as many work-related mobile phone calls as the engineers from Local 1 and 2 as for them much work-related communication was done via sending and receiving text messages. The two interview quotations by the engineers from Region 1 (in Section 4.3) show how they lamented the reduced opportunities they had for phone-based social interaction with colleagues as a result of switching from using mobile phones to their current PDA phones

These <sup>fi</sup>ndings also link back to the telework literature outlined earlier, as the engineers combining work and social elements in calls with colleagues is similar to the way the home-of<sup>fi</sup>ce hybrid workers examined by Halford (2005) used phone and email to maintain social relations with colleagues when they worked at home. However, as the engineers in Region 1 had less scope to make work-related phone calls than the engineers in Local 1 and 2, the interview evidence tentatively suggests that they generally experienced a greater sense of isolation. Using social relations with work colleagues to ameliorate potentially negative features of work is something that has also been found in other sociological studies. For example, Pettinger (2005) found that young women working in shops used friendships with colleagues as a way to reduce feelings of boredom, while Korczynski (2003) found that people doing call centre work used social relations with colleagues as a way of coping with the negative feelings they regularly experienced from dealing with dif<sup>fi</sup>cult customers.

The engineer's preferences with regard to the character of their work/non-work boundary during working hours are hard to characterize using the segmentation/integration continuum. Thus, in relation to family and non-work colleagues they adopted a segmented approach, not dealing with or making such calls during working hours. However, they simultaneously adopted an integrationist approach with regard to work colleagues, typically regarding colleagues as friends, and with much of their work-related phone based communication with colleagues having a dual work and social purpose. A key reason for the differential approach to having non-work related social interactions with colleagues but not family during working hours is arguably that calls with colleagues are both easier to take and make than calls to family members, and that such calls, due to the work-related element to them that exists (such as seeking advice in solving problems, or making a routine administrative call) mean that calls to work-colleagues during working time have more legitimacy than calls involving family or non-work friends.

Overall therefore, the data presented here, with regard to the engineers phone use patterns during work and non-work time reinforces the research which suggests that it is somewhat simplistic to characterize people as either being consistent segmentors or integrators, as they may adopt different boundary management strategies at different times (Golden & Geisler, 2007; Hecht & Allen, 2009). Further, the paper has also highlighted the importance of not assuming that the non-work domain only refers to people's domestic, family context (Ransome, 2007), as for the engineers, communication with work colleagues constituted an important source of non-work support and communication during work time.

## 6. Conclusion

A key <sup>fi</sup>nding of the paper is that to fully understand the way in which the engineers made use of work-related mobile communication technologies both during work time and non-work time it is necessary to take account of the character of their work. Thus, the clear boundary between work and non-work that existed at the beginning and end of the working day, and the fact that the engineers did not receive work-related mobile phone calls outside of their formal working hours was fundamentally due to the fact that their employers provided a daytime, of<sup>fi</sup>ce hours service. Further, the blurred work/non-work boundary that often occurred when the engineers made mobile phone calls during working hours was also shaped by the fact that their work-related travel and work patterns meant that opportunities to have meaningful face-to-face interactions with clients or colleagues were both limited and unpredictable. Thus, in these circumstances, the engineers managed their ‘absent presence’ (Gergen, 2002) from colleagues by using mobile phone calls to both share important work information, and to also keep in touch with colleagues socially.

This paper contributes to the literature on the work/non-work boundary implications of mobile communication technology use for work in a number of ways. Firstly, in examining non-managerial workers, the paper examines a relatively neglected type of worker, as most studies of mobile technology use in work have focussed primarily on managerial and professional workers (see Table 1). Secondly, another contribution to knowledge is the paper's detailed focus on work time, compared to the more common focus on non-work time adopted by most studies (see Table 1). In relation to boundary theory, the paper has also contributed to knowledge by highlighting the limitations of characterizing people as simply ‘segmentors’ or ‘integrators’ through showing that it is possible for workers to adopt different boundary management practices in different domains, at different times of the day, and with different people.

In conclusion it is worth highlighting some limitations of this study and areas it suggests may represent fruitful directions for future research. In terms of limitations, while the paper has examined some mobile workers, it is problematic to suggest that they are representative of all mobile workers, as mobile work is heterogeneous in character (Hislop & Axtell, 2007). Thus, many diverse occupations involve some degree of spatial mobility, and the patterns of spatial mobility involved may vary greatly, from that undertaken by the engineers examined here, to those who regularly travel overseas for work. Thus, future research could examine the work-related mobile phone use patterns of other types of mobile work. Further, while the focus here on non-managerial/professional workers provides a useful antidote to the general focus in the literature on mobile technology use in work on white collar work, there are many other forms of nonmanagerial work, which is different to that examined here, which could also be examined. Finally, the insights provided here by the central focus on the work-time use of mobile communication technologies again suggest that this represents another area where more research could be done which may provide valuable insight into the ways workers use mobile communication technologies as they work.
