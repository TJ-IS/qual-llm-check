# Considering the social impacts of artefacts in information systems design science research

Gerard De Leoz<sup>a</sup> and Stacie Petter<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Business Information Technology, University of Tampa, Tampa, FL , USA; <sup>b</sup>Department of Management Information Systems, Baylor University, Waco, TX, USA

## ABSTRACT

Design science research (DSR) is a distinct research paradigm in the information systems (IS) field that encourages the creation and evaluation of artefacts as a means for research. Yet, design science researchers often create and evaluate artefacts that tend to be techno-centric. By also considering the social impacts of a design science artefact throughout the creation and evaluation process, the researcher develops an artefact with a greater potential to thrive when the artefact is instantiated and implemented. Furthermore, the purposeful consideration of social impacts in the design science creation and evaluation process supports the researcher in developing a more complete contribution to the IS field, a field whose core foundation relates to information, technology, and social interactions. In this paper, we discuss the importance of identifying social impacts of artefacts as well as an expanded notion of artefacts in DSR. We provide guidelines for design science researchers on how to incorporate the social impacts of artefacts throughout the DSR process. Using vignettes, we demonstrate how researchers have incorporated social impacts and demonstrate the value of considering social impacts as design science researchers seek to create and evaluate artefacts to address a research problem.

## 1. Introduction

Information systems are “complex organizations of hardware, software, procedures, data, and people, developed to address tasks faced by individuals and groups, typically within some organizational setting” (March & Smith, 1995, p. 252). The core of the information systems (IS) discipline is the information technology (IT) artefact, which is embedded in a context, shaped by people, and is constantly changing (Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001). The study of the IT artefact and the artefact’s impact on social interactions is quintessential in the IS discipline and in our theoretical understanding of information systems.

Design science research (DSR) is a distinct research paradigm within the IS discipline (Kuechler & Vaishnavi, 2008; Lee, 2001; Winter, 2008) that encourages the creation of artefacts as a means for research (Pefers, Tuunanen, Rothenberger, & Chatterjee, 2007). These artefacts can take on forms such as constructs, models, methods, instantiations, and design theories (Gregor & Jones, 2007; Hevner, March, Park, & Ram, 2004). The purposeful creation and evaluation of DSR artefacts enable researchers to address relevant problems that impact IS research and practice (Gregor & Jones, 2007; Iivari,

2003). Yet, DSR artefacts are embedded within contexts and are not “independent of people or the organizational and social contexts in which they are used” (Hevner et al., 2004, p. 83). In the quest to emphasise the IT artefact, many DSR contributions in the IS field have narrowly focused on creating DSR artefacts that are more technically oriented (Henningsson, Rukanova, & Hrastinski, 2010; Lee, Thomas, & Baskerville, 2015). Researchers that have focused exclusively on the IT artefact (or the more technical components of an artefact) fail to consider how an artefact created via DSR may impact social interactions. The implemented DSR artefact may consequently create or redefine social structures or relationships among individuals. Therefore, we suggest that the social aspects of DSR artefacts and the impact that an implemented DSR artefact has on social structures deserve equal attention as the IT artefact itself (Lee, 2001; Lee et al., 2015). In this article, we provide recommendations to IS researchers regarding how to attend to the social aspects of a DSR artefact throughout the artefact design and evaluation process. This paper is organised as follows. First, we explain the DSR process and expand the concept of artefacts. We discuss how a DSR artefact may be mindfully and purposefully designed for specific social contexts and evaluated with a consideration of the potential impacts an implemented DSR artefact may have on social structures. In the section that follows, we ofer guidelines to explain how to incorporate the consideration of social impacts at each stage of the DSR process. Finally, we discuss the value of our guidelines and share concluding thoughts.

## 2. Conducting IS DSR

The DSR process is typically described as a series of activities that produce an artefact to solve a problem (Hevner et al., 2004; Takeda, Veerkamp, & Yoshikawa, 1990). Traditionally, DSR has defined artefacts in the context of Simon’s (1996) idea of “artificial things” by relying on the concept of “devising artifacts to attain goals” (p. 65). For example, March and Smith (1995) describe artefacts as a construction that “apply information technology to organizational tasks” (p. 52). Hevner et al. (2004) draw their definition of artefacts from Denning (1997) and Tsichritzis (1998) by describ ing artefacts as “created innovations that define the ideas, practices, technical capabilities, and products through which the analysis, design, implementation, management, and use of information systems can be efectively and eficiently accomplished” (p. 76).

The DSR methodology is described as a series of steps to create and evaluate an artefact to address a problem (Baskerville, Pries-Heje, & Venable, 2009). While there are many processes for explaining the activities of DSR, we leverage Pefers et al.’s (2007) DSR methodology because it provides a process model that synthesises multiple generally accepted DSR models to explain the activities associated with conducting DSR. The methodology may be conveniently partitioned into two sets of activities: artefact creation and artefact evaluation. During artefact creation, researchers first seek to understand the problem to be studied by identifying a need (Takeda et al., 1990) and ensuring the problem is worthwhile of study (Hevner et al., 2004). Next, researchers identify the objectives for the solution, and as appropriate, refer to literature, theory, practice, or other domains for suggestions and inspiration as to how to create an artefact to address the problem (Hevner et al., 2004; Kuechler & Vaishnavi, 2012). In the third step, an artefact, in the form of a theory, construct, model, process, and/ or instantiation (Hevner et al., 2004; March & Smith, 1995), is designed and developed. The artefact, which is considered a DSR artefact, embodies a proof-of-concept of the design created by the researcher and serves as one output of research in design science (Nunamaker, Chen, & Purdin, 1990).

After creating a DSR artefact to solve a problem, a researcher demonstrates the feasibility and value of the DSR artefact (Hevner et al., 2004). Next, via evaluation, the researcher chooses among multiple methods for evaluation, including mathematical proofs, laboratory experiments, observations, simulations to assess the success or failure of the artefact in meeting the design goals. (Gregor & Hevner, 2013; Hevner et al., 2004). The problem, objectives, artefact, and evaluation are then communicated to disseminate the findings to research and practice (Gregor & Hevner, 2013).

If during evaluation, a problem is found due to a mismatch in the actual characteristics of the design and the chosen criteria, a new design cycle emerges to solve the revised problem (March & Smith, 1995; Takeda et al., 1990). This design cycle may occur in a series of iterations as the researcher seeks to find a solution for the problem (Hevner et al., 2004; Markus, Majchrzak, & Gasser, 2002). It is the cyclic process of DSR that makes DSR ideal to consider not only the technical, but also the social aspects of an artefact in its creation and evaluation.

Typically, the ultimate assessment of an artefact has largely focused on the utility or value of the artefact (Hevner et al., 2004; March & Smith, 1995), which tends to emphasise the artefact’s technical components. The challenge to successfully integrate the social and technical components together persists in the artefact creation and artefact evaluation activities within DSR. Researchers have supported calls for the DSR process to include a fuller consumption and production of knowledge that incorporates a richer understanding of relationships between DSR and human knowledge throughout the research process (Gregor & Hevner, 2013; Henningsson et al., 2010), and considering the social impacts of the DSR artefact is one approach to address this call.

## 2.1. Artefact creation: considering social impacts through an expanded notion of artefacts

The design science paradigm originally used the term “artifact” as the name for the output of the creative activity within the design science process, yet other designations for artefact, such as “IT artifact”, “ensemble artifact”, “IS artifact”, and “sociotechnical artifact” have elevated the confusion among IS researchers (Alter, 2015). There is a plethora of “artifact” terms used in DSR (i.e., IT artefact, IS artefact, design science artefact, socio-technical artefact, among others), and there is a need to explicate the term “artifact” along with its relevant components.

Artefacts are objects (conceptual or tangible) created by humans for an explicit purpose or goal (Simon, 1996). IT artefacts are special cases of technical artefacts or human-created tools used for solving problems (Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001) that consider the role of information as part of the technical design (see Figure 1). In the traditional way of creating an IT artefact, there is often some acknowledgement of social aspects that can influence the IT design or IT artefact. For example, when a researcher identifies the social component during the artefact creation activities, the researcher is acknowledging that interactions among individuals, groups, organisations, or societies could influence the IT design or IT artefact. This consideration of the social aspects of the problem or objectives informs the social component. As the researcher creates the artefact, the focus then shifts from a consideration of the social component to the technical component. The needs and requirements of the social component are translated into the information and technology aspects of the artefact, thus, comprising the IT artefact’s technical component. During the artefact evaluation activities, researchers then consider and evaluate the IT artefact as a tool purposed to process information (Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001), sometimes with little to no understanding and acknowledgement of the social aspects that influence the IT artefact. As Figure 1 shows, the social component influences the IT artefact, but remains a separate entity.

We encourage IS design science researchers to think beyond the creation and evaluation of the technical components of the IT artefact and also to consider the social impacts of the IT artefact. Social impacts are efects or consequences that are likely to be experienced by a social group due to an influencing event (Schirmer, 2011). The social impact of an artefact can be explained as the efect an IT artefact has on the interpersonal relationships and interactions within an environment at an individual, group, organisational, or societal level of analysis.

One means to enable the consideration of social impacts is for design science researchers to create and evaluate a broader type of artefact: the IS artefact. The IS artefact is comprised of the IT artefact and also includes a purposeful consideration of the social factors that influence the artefact’s receiving environment into the IT design (consistent with Lee et al., 2015). Consistent with the notion of socio-technical systems, the diference between an IT artefact and an IS artefact is that all three constituent aspects – the information, technology, and social aspects – purposefully make up an IS artefact. Jointly considering the information and technology aspects of a problem and solution, an IT artefact may be created with some recognition of intrinsic social aspects, but the evaluation tends to focus on the techno-centric aspects of the artefact. An IS artefact, on the other hand, is an artefact that is “enabling, interacting with, and even changing one another where, in forming an information system, they can ultimately serve to solve a problem or achieve a goal for individuals, groups, organizations, societies, or other social units” (Lee et al., 2015, p. 6). Compared to IT artefacts, IS artefacts are broader as they consider the information and technology aspects of a problem and solution more fully with the social aspects. The IS artefact purposefully considers social influences that act upon the IT artefact; furthermore, the IS artefact can be separated into technology, information, and social sub-artefacts which allow for the identification of boundaries within the artefact. The IS artefact and its sub-artefacts are then embedded and examined within a larger social context. Each sub-artefact in the IS artefact holds properties, attributes and meanings within the IS design and is presupposed to enmesh well with the other sub-artefacts as well as the IS artefact as a whole.

The information sub-artefact is the verbal or written instantiation of information communicated directly (i.e., person to person) or indirectly (i.e., person to person via computer mediated communication) within a context. Recognising that information only becomes “information” when it is understood within a context (Boell & Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2014), design science researchers must at the very least recognise the social component that is part of information (i.e., Figure 1) or must fully integrate the information sub-artefact with the technology and social sub-artefacts (i.e., Figure 2). To develop the information sub-artefact, meanings (McKinney & Yoos, 2010) or attributes (Boell & Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2014) of information guide researchers in understanding how functional (or less functional) information sub-artefacts are defined as part of the implemented design (Lee et al., 2015). In the evaluation of an information sub-artefact, researchers can consider various attributes of information in its ability to be used to achieve certain values or goals (Boell & Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2014).

Figure 2. Visualisation of an IS artefact – an implemented IS design.

The technology sub-artefact is a tangible or intangible tool created to solve a problem (Lee et al., 2015). For the creation and evaluation of a technology sub-artefact, one may consider a technology sub-artefact’s material properties (e.g., digital or non-digital, electronic or non-electronic, physical or non-physical) and other attributes such as utility and fitness (Gill & Hevner, 2013), as well as editability, distributedness, and interactivity (Kallinikos, Aaltonen, & Marton, 2013). Depending on the design objectives, one or more of these attributes may serve as criteria for analysing the adaptability of a technology sub-artefact.

More broadly, the social sub-artefact aims to capture all social components of a socio-technical system. The social sub-artefact encompasses relevant social contexts and properties, which include not only relationships and social interactions, but also social change among individuals and groups that will be impacted by the IS artefact directly or indirectly within the context of a problem. The social sub-artefact considers relational factors which consist of the individual (or social unit’s) sense of self in regards to societal roles, responsibilities, and identities; relationships among individuals, groups, or other social units; patterns of interactions occurring through communication; and strength of cohesion formed between and among individuals and groups in a social unit (Becker, 2001; Lopez & Scott, ́ 2000; Schirmer,

2011; Vanclay, 2002). Table 1 identifies potential relational factors to consider in the creation and evaluation of a social sub-artefact.

Unlike Figure 1 in which the social component is considered only in terms of its potential to influence the consideration of the problem, objectives, design, and evaluation, Figure 2 demonstrates a perspective in which the sub-artefacts are fully integrated. To purposefully enmesh the social aspects with the IT artefact, the researcher considers the social impacts of the artefact in the design and evaluation processes. The researcher explicitly identifies how social structures among individuals and groups are likely to be impacted by the artefact’s implementation (Lee et al., 2015). The result is not an IT artefact, but an IS artefact, which is comprised of the technology, information, and social sub-artefacts. As Figure 2 demonstrates, the overlapping aspects (i.e., central shaded region) yield an IS artefact in that each sub-artefact (represented as a gear) in the central shaded region holds a collection of properties that are presupposed to interact purposefully with the other sub-artefacts within the whole IS artefact. The combination of gears also represents an implementation of an IS artefact in a specific organisation or social structure, which depict the interactions among the three mutually exclusive, yet interdependent sub-artefacts. This purposeful consideration of the social sub-artefact as part of the artefact creation process consequently influences many properties and functionalities that may be infused to the resulting DSR artefact. ## 2.2. Artefact evaluation: considering social impacts of DSR artefacts within the environment

Once a DSR artefact is implemented and situated within an environment that has an existing social structure, the DSR artefact likely triggers social impacts within the existing environment (Keen, 1981). Such changes may yield both intended results and unexpected consequences, which are often overlooked in IS research as we seek to support technological solutions (Majchrzak, Markus, & Wareham, 2016). The intended or unintended consequences or outcomes may include the disruption of social structures. Social structures are social networks of relationships and roles that are formed (i.e., a relational structure) as a consequence of the individual’s expectations about others’ behaviours that are influenced by one’s cultural and normative backgrounds (i.e., an institutional structure) (Blau, 1975; Crothers, 1996; López & Scott, 2000). The design space (Gill & Hevner, 2013), from which the design requirements are abstracted, must allow for the creation of a functional artefact that is adaptable and capable of addressing the needs of the social structure. By limiting the focus of design and evaluation to the functional features of the IT artefact, the design and evaluation of artefacts lack the consideration of the interactive and cultural properties (i.e., the processes or work-practices) of the social structures that are impacted by the DSR artefact. Thus, if the design process did not account for how the artefact will be used in the social structure once the functionalities have been tested to work as expected, the design may fail to consider the artefact’s impact on the users and the users’ interactions with each other.

In DSR, understanding the interaction between the DSR artefact and the social structure is important, and the likelihood to more fully understand this interaction occurs during the demonstration and evaluation of the artefact. It is through evaluation that it is possible to identify more fully how the artefact serves its intended purpose for its environment (Simon, 1996). As part of the evaluation process, a researcher could examine an artefact’s fitness, which is described as the ability (1) “to survive at a high level of capacity over time” and (2) “to reproduce – completely or in part – and evolve over successive generations” (Gill & Hevner, 2013 p. 3). The fitness of an artefact potentially impacts the social structures that are receiving the artefact (see Figure 3). The first scenario (upper, right quadrant of Figure 3) illustrates a “thriving” relationship between the IS artefact and the social structure due to their strong fit or ability to adapt to develop a strong fit. While the scenario is ideal, the occurrence of a thriving relationship rarely happens over a short period of time or continuously over time. To allow the adoption of something foreign within an existing social structure, the more likely scenario is explained by the second and third cases. These scenarios illustrate “surviving” relationships between the IS artefact and the social structure because either one (but not both) is willing or capable to evolve to accommodate the other. In one case, the artefact is surviving because the social structures can adapt to the introduction of the artefact (upper, left quadrant of Figure 3). In this scenario, the impacts of the artefact are such that the social structure must be modified for the artefact to remain viable. The other surviving option (lower, right quadrant) also enables the artefact to survive because the artefact can be modified to fit the existing social structures. The last case illustrates a “dying” relationship between the IS artefact and social structure in that neither one is willing or capable to evolve to accommodate the other.

Similar to the concept of double contingency (Vanderstraeten, 2002), the interaction that takes place between artefacts and social structures constantly (and indeterminately) seeks for acceptance and approval of each other to achieve the best fit and maintain a thriving relationship. By considering the role of the artefact in a social structure and the factors that influence changes within the social structure, the artefact can be considered more than a tool, but also as a means for social relations (Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001) as new (or modified) social structures emerge after the implementation of an IS artefact. Consistent with the “emergence” concept, Figure 3 also implies that regardless of how well-planned and intended actions are perceived to cause change, situations emerge that may trigger social changes (Orlikowski, 1996), and social structures continually emerge with no inferable trend (Truex, Baskerville, & Klein, 1999). It is, thus, the quality of the IS design that determines the extent of perturbation in the socio-technical system of an organisation (Pefers et al., 2007). To create an IS design and IS artefact that considers the potential of changes that may occur once the artefact is implemented within a social structure, it is necessary for researchers to consider those factors that may stimulate social impacts.

## 3. Incorporating social impacts in the creation and design of DSR artefacts

Social impacts are consequences experienced by a social group due to an influencing event (Schirmer, 2011), such as the implementation of a DSR artefact within a social structure. To identify and study social impacts, researchers have identified “social impact factors” which are variables or elements that bring about social change or a social change process (Becker, 2001; Schirmer, 2011; Vanclay, 2002). Literature on the topic of social impacts assessment (Esteves, Franks, & Vanclay, 2012) broadly incorporates disciplines within the social sciences, including environmental studies and sociology (e.g., Jay, Jones, Slinn, & Wood, 2007; Lockie, 2001), to understand the relationships between natural and artificial processes that afect development eforts (Desai, 2007; Slootweg, Vanclay, & Van Schooten, 2001). Social impact factors such as relational, institutional, and proximity of impacts (Vanclay, 2002) are assessed across diferent scales of social structures – micro, meso, and micro (Becker, 2001; Schirmer, 2011).

To identify social impact factors that are likely to influence the creation, design, and evaluation of DSR artefacts, we performed a wide-reaching literature review across disciplines to develop a list of potentially relevant social impact factors (see Table 2). Each of the social impact factors listed in Table 2 (or additional relevant social impact factors) can also be considered in conjunction with the relational factors that comprise the understanding of the social sub-artefact (see Table 1).

Design science researchers may be interested in assessing social impacts according to the scale of impact of the DSR artefact – that is, impacts to the artefact’s users (direct) and the artefact’s impacts to other stakeholders (indirect). Typically, evaluations of DSR artefacts focus on the direct users. Yet, impacts experienced by a user may vary from those that are less directly impacted by the artefact. As such, DSR researchers may be interested at considering not only the impacts of DSR artefacts to the users, but also the consequential impacts that may likewise permeate to other DSR artefact stakeholders.

The relationships among direct users and indirect users may change when implementing the DSR artefact within a social structure. As relationships change, so might communication patterns, cohesiveness within the social structure, and one’s sense of self within the social structure. Considering the proximity of impact in terms of the relational factors allow for a deeper understanding of how the implementation of the artefact impacts the social structure.

The scale of the social structure (i.e., micro, meso, and macro) also influences the understanding of the relational factors of social sub-artefacts. We identify three levels of analysis: micro – the individual; meso – groups, department, organisations; and macro – inter-organisation, national, or international levels (Becker, 2001; Blau, 1975; Crothers, 1996). In the IS literature, social impacts assessed at the micro-level are focused on the individual user as a social actor (Lamb & Kling, 2003), the individual’s identity with IT performing some societal roles (Carter & Grover, 2015; Lee, Lee, & Lee, 2006), as well as an individual’s use, acceptance, and resistance to IT in response to social influences (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005; Hirschheim & Newman, 1988). At the meso-level, social impacts examine social inertia, interactions, and behavioural changes in groups and organisations in response to IT appropriation (Keen, 1981; Lyytinen & Newman, 2008; Markus & Robey, 1988). On a macro-level, social impacts consider changes to economic, cultural, and crowd or public behaviours that manifest in social networks, online communities, cross-organisations, and social structures of national and global scales (e.g., Abdul-Gader & Kozar, 1995; Banker, Chang, & Kao, 2010; Tan, Sutanto, Phang, & Gasimov, 2014). A design science researcher that is examining the relational factors of a social sub-artefact would examine which level(s) of analysis are most appropriate for the problem and would identify how the sense of self, relationships, communication patterns, and cohesiveness are impacted at the applicable level of analysis.

Potentially relevant institutional structures include changes to culture, way of life, political systems, and economic systems (Keen, 1981; López & Scott, 2000; Vanclay, 2002), as well as health and well-being (Vanclay, 2002) of a social unit. Institutional structures are deeply engrained in a social structure (also called a deep structure), and changes may take place over long periods of time. Changes in these institutional structures impact the various relational factors of the social sub-artefact. Changes in culture could influence meso-level relationships and communication patterns, for example. The DSR artefact’s impact on the political systems within an organisation could afect an individual’s or group’s sense of self within the organisation. Therefore, considering how the DSR artefact could potentially alter institutional structures in terms of the relational factors allow researchers to have greater insights on the social sub-artefact to be designed and evaluated.

The social impact factors in Table 2, although intended to be comprehensive and recommendatory to increase awareness of a range of social impacts to IS researchers, are not necessarily complete; detailing all social impact factors is impossible because “social change has a way of creating other changes” (Vanclay, 2002, p. 185). Any list of social impact factors may exhibit biases in that researchers are influenced by their own cultures, economic settings, and paradigms for interpretation. Researchers may choose a subset of social impact factors to consider in the design and evaluation of an artefact based on the researcher’s goals (Vanclay, 2002). Therefore, researchers may use a subset of the above social impact factors or may identify additional social impact factors beyond those provided here based on the scope of the problem being addressed and the changes in social structures once an artefact is implemented within an environment.

## 4. Guidelines for incorporating social impacts in DSR

To incorporate social impacts in the creation and evaluation of DSR artefacts, we propose six guidelines that are consistent with the steps of the DSR methodology defined by Pefers et al. (2007). The guidelines identify how the social sub-artefact can be mindfully considered at each step of the DSR process (see Figure 4). In the artefact creation steps, researchers can seek to understand the social structures that impact the problem, develop objectives based on social impacts, and consider potential impacts in the design of the artefact to develop a social sub-artefact that is embedded within the DSR artefact. Through the artefact evaluation activities, diferences identified between the anticipated and unanticipated social impacts may inform the subsequent decisions of the DSR project, such as suggesting a need to redesign the DSR artefact or providing insights regarding how to communicate results in research and professional communities.

## 4.1 Guideline #1: understand social aspects of the problem

Ensure the social aspects of the problem are identified and understood along with the technology and information aspects of the problem

The first step of DSR is to understand the problem to be addressed (Pefers et al., 2007). To create an IS artefact, researchers not only must identify technology or information needs, but also should consider “what relationships and interactions among individuals and groups are afected by this problem?” Using the relational factors identified in Table 1 and considering the social impacts in Table 2, a researcher could ask questions, such as who is impacted by the problem (and at what level of analysis, for example) or what relational social structures (and related institutional factors, perhaps) might be afected by a potential solution to this problem.

By considering the social aspects of the problem using the relational factors within the social sub-artefact, researchers develop a baseline profile of the social structure within which an artefact is likely to be embedded. Understanding the problem, not only in technical and informational terms but also in social terms, ensures researchers are considering the potential impacts an artefact might inflict on the pre-identified social structure. As researchers gain a better understanding of the social aspects of the problem, additional research questions may emerge that ofer a socially related view of the problem.

Many DSR contributions, whether deliberately or unintentionally, have included certain social aspects about a real-world problem that is intended to be addressed through the creation of an artefact. These problems by themselves fall within the socio-technical spectrum, which may range from one that is a purely technical problem, to one that is a purely social problem, or to some extent, has elements of both. Highlighting the social sub-artefact in the artefact’s design requires purposeful consideration of the relationships and social interactions that will be impacted by the DSR artefact once implemented, regardless if the problem to be addressed is an observed problem being faced by an individual acting alone or a problem faced by two or more individuals who are interacting with one another. Whichever case, potential social impacts could be assessed purposefully (using Table 1 and Table 2 in concert) to aid in the development of an IS artefact. In Vignette 1, Aaen (2008) conceptualised the problem and considered not only the technology and its functional attributes (product, project, and process) but also the users of the technology (people).

## Vignette 1: understanding social aspects of the problem

In their Design Science Research Methodology, Pefers et al. (2007) identify entry points for the first four steps of their DSR process at which DSR may be initiated. In Step 1, the entry point is “Problem Centered Initiation”. One way a researcher might enter the DSR process and incorporate social impacts is to identify a problem that could use an IT artefact to solve current problems with relationships, interactions, communication, or cohesiveness among individuals, groups, or organisations. If the problem was explained, in part, by the social aspects and social structures that afect the problem, it would naturally encourage the researcher to include a social sub-artefact in the IS design given that, to understand the problem, one would need to understand the social context and social structures.

## 4.2. Guideline #2: define objectives for the social sub-artefact

Identify how social structures may be impacted and account for these changes when defining specific design objectives

The second step of the DSR process is to develop objectives to solve the problem as defined in Step #1 (Pefers et al., 2007). To move beyond a mere consideration of the social aspects (i.e., Figure 1) towards a purposeful consideration of a social sub-artefact (i.e., Figure 2), researchers must augment the DSR process with a formal consideration of how social structures are likely to be impacted by a solution to the problem. Researchers at this stage of the DSR process would consider factors within Table 1 as well as any other factors that are relevant to their problem to identify how the social structure would be disrupted once the artefact is implemented. In other words, the process of developing objectives for the DSR artefact as a whole must identify objectives related to the technology, information, and social sub-artefacts.

By defining which social factors are likely to be impacted by the DSR artefact, researchers will have a better understanding about the extent of socially related problems the artefact is capable of solving or creating once the artefact is implemented. This process of specifically defining objectives with respect to the social sub-artefact sensitises researchers regarding how to appropriately bind an artefact’s scope of (social) impacts. Identifying specific social impact factors related to the problem and requirements of the solution helps researchers define more holistic and feasible DSR objectives with meta-requirements that consider all aspects of the artefact: technology, information, and social. Vignette #2 is an exemplar of how researchers identified a set of objectives for the social sub-artefact.

In the process of developing their taxonomy, the authors developed objectives related to the business, technology, and interactions for digital service providers. The authors considered if customers could distinguish among brands, suggesting a consideration for the sense of self for the organisation. The entry point for the second step of the Pefers et al. (2007) DSR methodology is an objective-centred solution. For researchers that have already identified the problem (with or without consideration of the social aspects of the problem), it is still possible to consider the social sub-artefact at this stage in the design. The design science researcher could consider the social values or disruptions that may occur as a result of addressing the problem identified in Step #1. By considering the potential impacts of the potential DSR artefact to the social structures at this stage and specifically defining objectives, or meta-requirements, to address these impacts, the researcher still could develop a social sub-artefact to ultimately create an IS artefact.

## 4.3. Guideline #3: conduct ex ante evaluations of the design

Perform ex ante evaluations as part of the design and development process to consider potential social impacts that may occur once the DSR artefact is implemented

The third step of Pefers et al.’s (2007) process occurs as the researcher rapidly cycles between design and development to settle upon a final design of the artefact. In this step, the researcher considers (and potentially creates) multiple designs to satisfy the various objectives associated with the technology, information, and social sub-artefacts. As part of the iterative design and development process, one strategy to understand whether a candidate artefact is feasible towards attaining its design goals is by conducting an ex ante evaluation (Pries-Heje, Baskerville, & Venable, 2008). An ex ante evaluation essentially is an economic feasibility analysis that evaluates an artefact based on the design specifications prior to the development of the artefact, typically through a cost–benefit analysis (Pries-Heje et al., 2008). Although ex ante evaluation is traditionally limited to the economic feasibility of a design, we suggest expand ing ex ante evaluation to include the social feasibility of a design by considering the impact of relevant social factors, such as those listed in Table 1, when creating the design of the artefact. This strategy would consider potential human risks and efectiveness of the design more fully in the expected social structure (Venable, Pries-Heje, & Baskerville, 2016).

To conduct a social feasibility analysis, one approach is for the researcher to consider which quadrant or outcome in Figure 3 is likely to occur when the artefact is received into an existing social structure. The researcher could consider the adaptability of the DSR artefact to the social structure as well as identify the flexibility of the profiled social structure to accommodate the DSR artefact. If the objectives of the artefact’s design (in Step #2) can be accommodated by both the DSR artefact and social structure, this suggests to the researcher that the DSR artefact and receiving social structure are willing (or capable) to change as necessary to accommodate each other (representing the “thriving” region of Figure 3). However, if either the artefact’s design or receiving social structure are unwilling or incapable of changing to accommodate the other, this may indicate that the potential relationship between the social structure and DSR artefact lie in either one of the “surviving” regions of Figure 3. A last scenario involves a situation in which the artefact design and social structure are inconsistent or unaccommodating of one another, which suggests that the potential relationship between the social structure and the DSR artefact is “dying” (see Figure 3). This ex ante evaluation enables the researcher to redefine the design by changing the goals or objectives for the DSR artefact, altering the research question, or refining the design prior to the DSR artefact’s construction.

The process of design and development is a creative, as opposed to an analytical, process (Baskerville et al., 2009). An ex ante evaluation prior to artefact construction may avoid unnecessary iterations in the DSR efort and may better inform researchers on how to refine the artefact design to accommodate the related social structures. Vignette 3 provides an example of how one research project conducted an ex ante evaluation during the artefact design with a focus on the social impacts of the artefact design.

This third step of design and development has an entry point of a design- and development-centred approach according to Pefers et al. (2007). From a social sub-artefact perspective, a researcher who has not followed Guideline #1 and Guideline #2 still has potential to incorporate a social sub-artefact. The researcher can begin the creation of a social sub-artefact by considering the social impacts that may occur due to the implementation of the DSR artefact prior to the development and implementation of the artefact. By performing an ex ante evaluation of the intended DSR artefact from the perspective of the individuals, groups, organisation, and/or society, the researcher is given an opportunity to consider the social sub-artefact in the IS artefact design.

## 4.4. Guideline #4: demonstrate the ability of the social sub-artefact in realising the DSR objectives

Examine how well the DSR artefact addresses each objective identified in Guideline #2 with additional consideration of the social sub-artefact objectives by identifying the profile of the social structure that is afected by the DSR artefact

At this step of the process, the design science researcher moves from activities that are related to artefact creation to activities that support artefact evaluation. The “demonstration” phase proves that the artefact works at a cursory level by instantiating the artefact and informally demonstrating the artefact meets the objectives identified in Step #2 (Pefers et al., 2007). Essentially, a demonstration ofers an opportunity for a peer review of an artefact by others to observe how the artefact performs as perceived by the “users” and deliberate what gaps may still exist in terms of the “client’s” expectation of the artefact and the actual capabilities of the artefact.

If the previous guidelines were followed, the researcher ensures that the properties of the social sub-artefact (that were embedded in the DSR artefact) and the properties of the social structure that will receive the DSR artefact are congruent. In this step, the researcher is examining if the artefact can meet the objectives (including those for the social sub-artefact) identified in Step 2. Upon the creation and implementation of the artefact into a social structure, there are sometimes reasons why the DSR artefact may not embed fluidly into the social structure for which the artefact was purposely created. Researchers at this stage of the process could focus on the social sub-artefact by answering the question “what is the actual social profile (including the social unit’s needs and expectations of the DSR artifact) that defines the social structure that is receiving the artifact?” Identifying the profile of the actual social structure that is receiving the DSR artefact may help the researcher understand reasons for diferences (through a gap analysis) in the expected results, as well as explain why unexpected results, occurred (Von Krogh & Haefliger, 2010). By following this guideline, the researcher performs a gap analysis between the social profile used for the DSR design and the social profile of the social structure where the artefact is implemented. This assessment of the realisation of the objectives from Step #2 in the actual social structure will help researchers examine the artefact’s utility and problem-solving capabilities. Furthermore, in this step, the researcher gains useful information to refine the artefact as needed via another DSR iteration. Identifying the similarities and diferences of the expected and actual social profiles may guide researchers as they seek to perform a more formal evaluation in the next step (Guideline #5). Vignette 4 is an exemplar of how a social sub-artefact can be demonstrated during the DSR process.

## Vignette 4: demonstrate the ability of the social sub-artefact in realising the DSR objectives

The entry point in Step #4 occurs through a client/ context initiated efort; however, since the demonstration may or may not be with the actual users or client, surrogate users or clients can ofer insights to the researcher. If seeking to include the social sub-artefact at this point of the design science process, the researcher considers the receiving social structure for the DSR artefact during the demonstration of the artefact. By recognising the profile of the receiving social structure, and considering the needs and expectations of the DSR artefact, the researcher can either refine the artefact to better fit the receiving social structure or take steps to evaluate the social aspects of the DSR artefact more purposefully in the next step. Therefore, this design step may be an opportunity to demonstrate the ability or inability of the DSR artefact to adapt to a social structure whose social profile was not previously incorporated into the artefact design. The consideration of the social structure could better inform which steps to perform next in the DSR process.

## 4.5. Guideline #5: evaluate the ability of the artefact in addressing the social aspects of the DSR problem

Conduct an ex post evaluation of the artefact and identify any expected and/or unexpected impacts to the constructed DSR artefact in the social structure

In the evaluation step, the researcher “observes and measures how well the artifact supports a solution to the problem” (Pefers et al., 2007, p. 56). This type of evaluation is an ex post evaluation, in which the artefact is evaluated after development (Pries-Heje et al., 2008) and is situated in the context of the users of the artefact (Pefers et al., 2007). The evaluation of an artefact is a critical component of the DSR cycle; evaluation identifies if the artefact addresses the problem, ofers an improvement over the current state-of-the-art, and/ or suggests a need to refine the artefact (Hevner et al., 2004; Pefers, Rothenberger, Tuunanen, & Vaezi, 2012; Takeda et al., 1990).

To further include the social sub-artefact in the eval uation step of DSR, the researcher purposefully examines the ability of the artefact to address all (including social) aspects of the problem. The researcher does not only focus on utility in terms of only the technology or information sub-artefacts, but also considers the utility of the social sub-artefact. The researcher should evaluate how well the artefact addressed the problem identified in Step #1 of the DSR process. If a researcher performed Guideline #1 in conjunction with Step #1 of Pefers et al. (2007), s/he would have a comprehensive understanding of the problem from a technological, informational, and social perspective. After the evaluation is complete, the researcher can compare the actual outcomes of the evaluation (i.e., ex post) with the potential outcomes from Guideline #3 (ex ante). At this step, the researcher may also identify decision alternatives for the next steps of the research project, including the alternative to iterate through more designs of the artefact. By conducting a thorough evaluation of the artefact, which includes the social sub-artefact, researchers will be able to compare the hypothesised social impacts identified in Guideline #3 and the actual social impact to have a more complete understanding of how well the DSR artefact addressed the problem identified in Guideline #1.

Using a more comprehensive and thorough approach to evaluation that includes social impacts enables researchers to conduct theory generation in addition to gaining information to further improve the artefact. Theory generation may be performed by leveraging the results obtained from the evaluation step, comparing the results of the previous design steps, and linking the findings to the research problem being addressed. If researchers continue to iterate and improve the artefact, then new design objectives, including those for the social sub-artefact, may be identified in the next round of DSR iteration. These mappings allow for the creation of linkages that may be used for theorising. Vignette 5 ofers an example of how a DSR artefact can be evaluated to consider the social aspects of the problem.

For those researchers that developed an artefact without consideration of the social sub-artefact, but have a desire to evaluate the artefact with consideration of the social impacts and receiving social structure, the researcher would likely face some challenges to enter the process to create a social sub-artefact at this stage. If a researcher evaluates the artefact and realises that there is an opportunity to refine the artefact to address concerns based on social impacts, the researcher can follow the process to revert to step 1 or 2 in the DSR methodology (and respectively, Guidelines #1 and #2). Alternatively, the researcher may choose to evaluate the artefact in terms of social impacts, but may not reiterate through the design science process to refine the artefact further for various reasons (i.e., the artefact does not require refining, out of scope for the researcher, resource constraints). The researcher might document their evaluation of social impacts of the artefact to support additional research in the form of DSR or new explanation research related to the artefact and its social impacts.

## 4.6. Guideline #6: discuss social impacts

Include social impacts in the theorising and communication of results through scholarly and professional publication outlets

Researchers may continue to identify opportunities to refine an artefact (with or without multiple iterations of the DSR process), meaning the artefact is rarely fully complete. At some stage, there is knowledge to be disseminated to the research community regarding the results of the research efort (Pefers et al., 2007).

When communicating results, the accounted diferences between the characteristics of the developed social sub-artefact (embedded within the DSR artefact) and the social structure that received the artefact for evaluation may be relevant to report to the IS community. These accounts would be most beneficial when there is a discussion of the diferences in the expected and unexpected results, as well as the desired and undesired (social) results obtained after the evaluation of the artefact (Majchrzak et al., 2016). The guidelines enable the researcher to demonstrate traceability as the research progresses from research questions, to design objectives, to created artefact, to realised social impacts. The traceability of the diferences in the expected and actual social impacts can also be communicated to readers to enable IS researchers to understand critical and potential causal relationships when investigating intricacies in any IS phenomena.

Identifying the actual social impacts and their differences with the hypothesised social impacts and examining the similarities and diferences between the meta-requirements (DSR objectives) and research questions enables researchers to develop sound and relevant DSR theories. These insights can then be communicated through publications to aid in the theorising of phenomena. When theorising to capture principles of form and function of the design or to capture principles of implementation (Niehaves & Ortbach, 2016), researchers may account for the diferences of the social sub-artefact versus the receiving social structure or hypothesise potential causal relationships that may be inferred by retracing the relationships among the actual social impacts, design requirements (or meta-requirements), and research questions. Such traceability of relationships may likewise be leveraged to locate potential sources of error in design theorising (Niehaves & Ortbach, 2016). It could be that page constraints or complexities in communicating DSR limit the explanation of social impacts on artefacts in journal articles. It could also be that a discussion of social impacts may appear superfluous or distracting if the primary value of the artefact lies within the technical innovations of the artefact. Social impacts may not be often communicated in publications also because social impacts are not necessarily being examined by design science researchers. It is for these reasons that we encourage researchers to follow the guidelines for to incorporate social impacts of artefacts within DSR.

## 5. Discussion

Design science in IS ofers researchers opportunities to solve problems and achieve socially oriented goals while creating new knowledge. As the IS field develops a stronger interest in research that has broader societal impacts (Majchrzak et al., 2016), there is great promise for DSR to develop solutions to address these problems. It is likely that the social sub-artefact is dependent on the stakeholders that are considered when defining the problem, specifying objectives, and/or evaluating the artefact. The complete social impacts of any artefact are unlikely to be known until the artefact is implemented in an environment with a social structure. However, through the incremental approach of DSR, the problem becomes better defined, and the artefact continues to improve by specifying the social sub-artefact in conjunction with the technology and information sub-artefacts (consistent with Figure 2).

Although many researchers may include a social sub-artefact and consider impacts to social structures in

Our recommended guidelines ofer IS design science researchers the opportunity to consider social qualities embedded into artefacts beyond merely evaluating the utility of an artefact. Having a balanced understanding of the technical attributes and the social aspects of the receiving social structures can help researchers avoid creating artefacts that may eventually be rejected from use, which can arise due to the narrow view of design that focuses more on the technical aspects of artefacts. Likewise, this set of guidelines identifies checkpoints that allow creation of artefacts in a pragmatic manner. By accounting for social impact factors relevant at each checkpoint, researchers can expose and avoid unwanted disruptions or promote desired social change in the process of designing and instantiating artefacts. Therefore, meticulously considering the social sub-artefacts in the design process is crucial in developing relevant, more fit, and useful artefacts. While the guidelines in this paper were developed using the DSR process designed by Pefers et al. (2007), researchers using alternative DSR processes to create and evaluate IS artefacts could still incorporate the guidelines at the relevant stages or entry points with an alternative DSR process.

The presence of a few studies that have embraced and considered the social sub-artefact does not suggest that the social sub-artefact is indeed part of the typical design science process. Some of the problems addressed by IS DSR as exemplified in the vignettes are more social in nature and naturally suggest the need for the inclusion of the social sub-artefact. For researchers addressing problems that are social in nature, the guidelines described are likely followed with little additional efort as the researcher performs the DSR process. Some exemplars are more technical in their focus, but the researchers still identified the value in recognising the potential and recognised impacts to social structures. For problems that require more technical solutions, the application of these guidelines to study social impacts may vary based on how directly the artefact influences the existing social structures.

The vignettes also illustrate how potential changes in one’s research may evolve as a researcher includes social factors while navigating through the steps of the DSR process. Depending on when the researcher decides to consider the social impacts of a DSR artefact, changes may occur within the research questions, design goals, design requirements, or any step of the DSR methodology. Additionally, one may observe that not all social factors suggested in Tables 1 and 2 are applicable to all DSR artefacts. Furthermore, other relational and social impact factors not mentioned in Table 1 or Table 2 may be applicable for a specific DSR endeavour based on the problem context, the stage of the research, and the goals of the researcher.

This research has multiple implications to DSR researchers. As researchers apply these guidelines to their DSR process, researchers have an opportunity expand their view of the problem. By considering not only the technology and information aspects of the problem, but also equally weighting the social aspects of the problem, researchers have a better understanding of the issue at hand. This more complete view of the problem enables researchers to develop objectives that address the full problem with consideration of how the intended artefact will function as part of the artefact design process. Every social environment is unique, therefore, the relationship between artefacts and social structures will always require constant readjustments to thrive within its environment over time. The DSR process allows us to make these readjustments through iterations, and our guidelines support researchers interested in addressing the need for readjustments in DSR artefacts due to social impacts. Hence, by considering the receiving social structure early in the design process, researchers can be more aware of potential problems that may occur during artefact implementation earlier in the design of the DSR artefact. This can lead to less re-work and a more comprehensive solution to the problem.

As researchers create IS artefacts that embrace the technology sub-artefact, information sub-artefact, and social sub-artefact, researchers and practitioners alike are able to realise and gain value due to the creation of solutions that more comprehensively address the specified problem. The consideration of social impacts, particularly at macro-levels of analysis could enable DSR to ofer valuable and worthwhile contributions beyond the information systems discipline by creating IS artefacts that benefit the greater society-at-large (Majchrzak et al., 2016).

We do acknowledge that there are limitations of our guidelines. First, we acknowledge that although the IS artefact is a composition of the technology, information, and social sub-artefacts, we focused our discussion on how the social sub-artefact intersects with the IT arte fact (i.e., the intersection between the technology and information sub-artefacts). Given that DSR researchers currently develop IT artefacts, we sought to explain how design science researchers could more prominently consider social impacts in conjunction with the development of the IT artefact. It is certainly appropriate to more closely examine how the artefact creation and evaluation process is afected when diferent sub-artefact dyads are consid ered (i.e., social and information; social and technology). At each of these dyads or at the intersection of the tech nology, social, and information sub-artefacts, new phenomena may be present. We encourage future research to consider how DSR could shift its emphasis to specific sub-artefacts (or overlapping sub-artefacts) based on the nature of the problem. Next, we note that if researchers seek to develop a design theory based on the resulting artefact, there is value in including the understanding of the social sub-artefact in the creation of the design theory. However, specific guidelines on how to incorporate the social sub-artefact in design theory are not discussed in this paper, but should be addressed in future research. Thirdly, the vignettes and mentioned exemplars are based on the information provided within the research article published by the authors. Given that the vignettes are a secondary case analysis, we note that other social impacts and social structures may have been considered by the authors in the design and evaluation of their artefact; yet, we could only describe those impacts and structures that were discussed within the published article. With more awareness and discussion of the role of the social sub-artefact in DSR, we hope that authors will have the ability and desire to share more context about the social consid erations and social structures that influence the design, development, and evaluation of the DSR artefact.

We recognise that following these guidelines may be perceived as imposing additional efort for conducting DSR in the IS field. This is not our intent; we are not proposing that researchers must always evaluate all possible social impacts of DSR artefacts. Alternatively, these guidelines ofer researchers an opportunity to reframe their current mental framework used to create artefacts by considering potential social impacts that each functional feature of a IT artefact might impose as part of the design and evaluation of the artefact. Like Niederman and March (2012), we too wish to highlight that the design of artefacts “represents a significant contribution in and of itself ” (pp. 10, 11); otherwise, nothing would indeed ever be finished. It is our goal, however, to pull the seemingly afixed pendulum of the practice of design science in IS from its “technically-centered state” back to the middle of the “socio-technical spectrum” by emphasising the equal importance of human relationships and social interactions in the design of DSR artefacts. Thus, in the process of creating and evaluating an artefact created in a DSR, traditional design and evaluation techniques could be supplemented with the creation of social sub-artefacts and evaluation of impacts to social structures as described in this article. By encouraging IS researchers to broaden their perspective on the technology, information, and social sub-artefacts within DSR, researchers have an opportunity to create research that contributes to both the IS design science community and the greater IS research community.

## 6. Conclusion

The interdisciplinary nature of IS makes the IS field vulnerable to the “push-and-pull” forces of many influential reference disciplines – either by technically oriented fields, such as engineering and computer science, or the socially oriented fields, such as sociology and organisation sciences. However, the IS field should stand firm on its focal foundation – the interactions between the technical and the social. We seek to sensitise IS design science researchers to suficiently consider the social aspects of IS artefacts as much as the technical aspects of IT artefacts.

As such, the contributions of this research are fourfold. First, this article provides further explanation of the composition of DSR artefacts by discussing the technology sub-artefact, information sub-artefact, and social sub-artefact, and their interrelationships. Second, we discuss the importance of understanding the receiving social structure when designing, implementing, and evaluating a DSR artefact. Next, this research ofers insights to researchers as to the various social factors that may be impacted when embedding an artefact within a social structure to ofer inspiration on the social aspects that should be considered in the design and evaluation of an artefact. Finally, this article ofers a set of design evaluation guidelines to emphasise the social impacts of DSR artefacts as part of the DSR process.

This article challenges and reminds researchers of the importance of considering the social sub-artefact by recognising social impacts and social structures as part of the practice of DSR in the IS field. Ensuring a complete design of the artefact that includes a purposeful consideration of the social sub-artefact allows IS researchers conducting DSR to ofer richer solutions to problems. This thoughtful and purposeful consideration of social impacts also enables other researchers to better build on DSR in the efort to connect IS artefacts to existing theories, develop new research questions, theorise about IS artefacts and/or social structures, and extend knowledge.
