# Information technology and organizational capabilities: A longitudinal study of the apparel industry

## a b s t r a c t

Business value of information technology (IT) is one of the most important research streams in information systems (IS) research. In this study, we follow the resource-based view (RBV) in strategy literature to study how IT enables <sup>fi</sup>rms to develop new organizational capabilities. Using structured content analysis, we have collected longitudinal data from news media for 49 publicly traded apparel <sup>fi</sup>rms in the U.S. over the period of 1995 to 2007. The <sup>fi</sup>nancial data were collected from Standard & Poor's COMPUSTAT data. In this research, we study how IT assets affect apparel <sup>fi</sup>rms' organizational capabilities such as operational capability, customer service, and ability to innovate. The U.S. apparel industry is largely heterogeneous in its use of IT and organizational capabilities, and therefore provides a particularly interesting case to investigate the relationship between them. Our empirical results show that IT can lead to higher level of organizational capabilities. Firm <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources are important in affecting the relationship between IT and operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency as well as the relationship between IT and customer service. In addition, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency plays a mediating role in how IT affects customer service.

## 1. Introduction

In the past few decades, information technology (IT) has enabled organizational transformation and allowed <sup>fi</sup>rms to lower costs and improve ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency (e.g., [14]). Recently, the focus has evolved from whether IT affects <sup>fi</sup>rm performance to how IT transforms <sup>fi</sup>rms in different industries. For example, some information system (IS) researchers <sup>fi</sup>nd that <sup>fi</sup>rms derive bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from IT through its impact on business processes (e.g., [35]). This is consistent with the resourcebased view (RBV) in strategy literature that <sup>fi</sup>rm speci<sup>fi</sup>c resources are the source of sustainable competitive advantage [4] and allow <sup>fi</sup>rms to develop higher-order organizational capabilities [5, 54]. In this study, we attempt to link the literature in IS with the theories in strategic management on RBV and organizational capabilities. Based on a longitudinal dataset of more than 10 years for the public <sup>fi</sup>rms in the U.S. apparel industry, we examine how IT enables companies in this industrial sector to develop critical organizational capabilities.

Management literature de<sup>fi</sup>nes organizational capabilities as the ability to perform the basic functional activities of the <sup>fi</sup>rm, such as logistics and marketing [1]. Capability researchers <sup>fi</sup>nd that capabilities can be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed using a standard functional classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of the <sup>fi</sup>rm's activities, and therefore can be de<sup>fi</sup>ned as one of the three value disciplines—operational excellence, customer intimacy, and product leadership [25, 55, 56]. Operational excellence is achieved by providing products or services at competitive prices and by delivering such products or services with the least dif<sup>fi</sup>culty or inconvenience. Customer intimacy means cultivating lasting relationships with customers and striving to satisfy their unique demands. Product leadership is achieved by offering leading-edge products and services. Tallon and Kraemer [53] <sup>fi</sup>nd that each value discipline reveals how <sup>fi</sup>rms create value for their customers, which implies a different set of core business processes. In the current hyper-competitive environment, products and services can quickly become obsolete and competitive positions can be rapidly overtaken. Thus, <sup>fi</sup>rms need to be able to respond consistently and quickly to changing markets. Identifying, acquiring, and accumulating critical organizational capability in line with the three value disciplines are critical to <sup>fi</sup>rms' strategic renewal. Our study investigates how IT assets could contribute to the acquiring and accumulation of these organizational capabilities in the apparel retail industry.

First, retailers have seen dramatic changes in their ful<sup>fi</sup>llment oper ations in the last decade. Ef<sup>fi</sup>cient ful<sup>fi</sup>llment integration is quickly becoming an operational standard for the retail industry. Firms without integrated cross-channel retailing could face high costs to deliver their products and can potentially lose revenue growth opportunities. Customers can switch to retailers that are able to meet their demands. Presently, the capability of ful<sup>fi</sup>lling customer demand through multiple channels (e.g. online order, in-store pick up) is a major driver of operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency in the retail industry [43]. Ef<sup>fi</sup>cient order ful<sup>fi</sup>llment, independent of which channel a customer uses, is of critical importance to retailers. According to Forrester Research [21], about 55% of U.S. online consumers shop in multiple channels. Consumer expectations for order cycle time and service levels are extremely high, requiring signi<sup>fi</sup>cant changes in processes and procedures. However, without an integrated IT infrastructure, integrated ful<sup>fi</sup>llment is impossible. Retailers who lack IT resources will have to accommodate consumer demands through inef<sup>fi</sup>cient manual processes and will often leave the customer unsatis<sup>fi</sup>ed.

Second, <sup>fi</sup>rms are eager to develop customer intimacy and to enhance customer loyalty. In an increasingly competitive retail marketplace, the end-to-end customer experience has become a new battleground. The ability to effectively sense and subsequently tailor offerings to match customer demand through a customer's channel of choice is the retailers' new competitive differentiator. According to AMR Research Report [2], cross-channel shoppers typically spend nearly 30% more than single-channel shoppers. They want to ensure that they are satis<sup>fi</sup>ed, regardless of the channel. Leading retailers are harnessing both traditional and online channels to provide customers with new and more <sup>fl</sup>exible ways of shopping. Largely because of rapid developments in IT, segmenting and targeting markets are becoming increasingly accessible and affordable. By providing excellent customer experiences across channels, retailers could develop stronger customer relationships, especially with cross-channel shoppers.

Third, together with other complementary organizational resources, a retailer's IT assets can be a catalyst for IT-related product and service innovations. For example, GAP Inc. has launched new product brands speci<sup>fi</sup>cally for the online channel. In a competitive retail marketplace, more and more companies are leveraging their existing resources to introduce new products.

We collected longitudinal data on the apparel industry from 1995 to 2007 to investigate the following research questions:

• How do IT assets affect retailers' operational capability, customer service, and ability to innovate?

• How do complementary organizational resources in<sup>fl</sup>uence the relationship between IT assets and organizational capabilities?

• How do different organizational capabilities affect one another at the competitive frontier?

The apparel industry provides a particularly interesting case to investigate the above research questions. Consumers often perceive apparel as products that must be seen, touched, and tried on before purchase. Therefore, the industry was initially slow to adopt the online channel. Despite its slow take-off, apparel is now one of the leading product categories sold over the Internet today [57]. Yet within the apparel industry, retailers' use of the online channel is heterogeneous, ranging from full, partial, to no integration with other channels. Organizational capabilities in terms of operations, customer service, and product leadership vary dramatically across various retailers. It will be interesting to examine how companies use IT assets to develop and leverage organizational capabilities to deliver superior customer value.

## 2. Prior literature and theory

## 2.1. Literature review

The business value of IT has long been hotly debated by both practitioners and IS researchers. Prior studies employed several theoretical paradigms to examine the performance impacts of IT, and the results have been mixed (e.g. [11, 14, 39, 47, 58]). Economic theories have been extensively used to provide empirical speci<sup>fi</sup>cations for estimating the economic impacts of IT [14]. These studies investigated IT value at an aggregate level. Recent studies started examining the underlying mechanisms relating IT to <sup>fi</sup>rm performance. One important research stream applied RBV to explore how IT resources change business operations and create value [58]. Powell and Dent-Micallef [39], for example, <sup>fi</sup>nd that IT resources have no effect on <sup>fi</sup>rm performance unless managers use IT to leverage the complementary human and business resources such as <sup>fl</sup>exible culture and supplier relationships. Bharadwaj [11] proposes that <sup>fi</sup>rms can obtain superior performance by combining IT-related resources into a unique IT capability. Santhanam and Hartono [47] also <sup>fi</sup>nd that <sup>fi</sup>rms with superior IT capability exhibit better <sup>fi</sup>rm performance. Ramirez et al. [41] discover a synergistic effect on <sup>fi</sup>rm performance from the combination of IT and process redesign.

From a theoretical perspective, RBV has proven particularly useful in opening the “black box” of IT's affect on <sup>fi</sup>rm performance. According to the RBV theory, resources and organizational capabilities are valuable sources of competitive advantage [5, 32, 54]. They are likely to be heterogeneously distributed across <sup>fi</sup>rms and are rent-yielding when they are valuable, rare, imperfectly imitable, and nonsubstitutable [4, 25]. Most empirical work of RBV, in both strategic management and IS literature, uses overall <sup>fi</sup>rm performance as the dependent variable. However, simply examining the relationship between a <sup>fi</sup>rm's resources and its overall performance could lead to misleading conclusions with respect to RBV [42]. Firms can have competitive advantages in some business activities while having disadvantages in others. Using a highly aggregated dependent variable such as <sup>fi</sup>rm performance may neglect the underlying mechanism of how IT combines with other organizational resources to change business processes and competitive tactics. Several studies in strategic management have addressed this problem by examining the implications of RBV on a <sup>fi</sup>rm's product development ability [28], manufacturing effectiveness [49], and customer service [42].

This study aims to examine how <sup>fi</sup>rms utilize IT resources to develop higher-level organizational capabilities. Our research framework draws upon IT business value (e.g., [11, 35]), RBV of the <sup>fi</sup>rm [4], and organizational capabilities [1, 25, 55, 56]. Our theoretical framework is illustrated in Fig. 1.

## 2.2. Theory development

We introduce an integrative model to tackle the question of how IT helps <sup>fi</sup>rms develop higher-order organizational capabilities. In our model, conceptual distinctions are made between IT assets and organizational capabilities. First, we take the view that IT creates business value by enabling business processes and routines and helps companies perform their functional activities and tasks better than their competitors. Commonly available IT resources cannot, by themselves, create a sustainable competitive advantage [19, 39]. Instead, IT assets, the investments in different types of IT, must be integrated into the organizational processes in order to develop higherorder capabilities, which can, in turn, create sustained performance. Mooney et al. [36] propose three impacts from the effective use of IT. Automational impacts result in ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and cost-reduction bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from the role of IT as a capital asset being substituted for labor. Informational impacts emerge from IT's ability to process information ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently. Transformational impacts come from transforming processes, which result in reduced times to conduct business. Thus, we propose that IT assets provide resources that enable organizations to ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently and cost effectively build their capabilities.

Second, we investigate organizational capabilities from three functional areas of a <sup>fi</sup>rm: operations, customer service, and product development. Operational capability is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a <sup>fi</sup>rm's ability to provide “customers with reliable products or services at competitive prices delivered with minimal dif<sup>fi</sup>culty or inconvenience” [56]. This capability could also be blended with processes and routines to develop other capabilities like customer intimacy or product leadership. Customer service capability is identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as the ability to understand markets and to tailor offerings to match the demand of different market niches. Product development capability is the ability to offer leading-edge products by focusing on innovation. These capabilities are valuable organizational resources that can increase sales, enhance customer service, and foster the brand loyalty of existing customers. They can also help retailers to reduce operations and customer service costs.

## 2.3. IT assets and organizational capabilities

From the RBV perspective, organizational capabilities are a valuable source of competitive advantage [5, 32, 54]. Through their indirect effects in initiating and leveraging organizational capabilities, information systems are able to deliver superior customer value in line with other business processes. Basu and Blanning [7], for example, suggest that IT not only improves individual processes, but also enables process synthesis and integration across disparate physical and organizational boundaries. Process synthesis and integration are essential to achieving operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, customer service, and product development. Rai et al. [40] posit that IT infrastructure can be leveraged to create a higherorder supply chain integration capability and to generate signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and sustainable performance gains. Supply chain integration capability is a major driver of operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency.

Barney [5] refers to capabilities as those organizational characteristics that enable an organization to conceive, choose, and implement strategies. In congruence with Barney's de<sup>fi</sup>nition, we argue that organizational capabilities include three implicit, yet essential organizational characteristics: agility in understanding the competitive environment, motivation to take action, and ability to take action. IT assets provide the resources that enable <sup>fi</sup>rms to quickly sense changes in customer demand, carry out action, and hasten decision-making processes. Firms with higher digitization levels may consistently enjoy advantages over other <sup>fi</sup>rms in initiating and deploying innovative business practices. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, <sup>fi</sup>rms with more IT assets can acquire information necessary to exploit the opportunity and can process information ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently to reduce the time to initiate market strategies. Moreover, IT assets in<sup>fl</sup>uence <sup>fi</sup>rms' awareness. Extensive deployment of analytic (business intelligence) systems, for instance, allows <sup>fi</sup>rms to access and analyze market and customer data. Information systems also allow companies to be quick and responsive. By in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the three essential organizational characteristics, IT is able to support higher-order organizational capabilities. In a similar vein, Kotha and Swamidass [31] suggest that advanced manufacturing technology allows <sup>fi</sup>rms to develop multiple capabilities simultaneously.

Operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, in terms of fast ful<sup>fi</sup>llment across channels, hinges on the ability of cross-channel order management and inventory management. In order to provide ef<sup>fi</sup>cient product/service delivery across channels, retailers must integrate their data so that they can get a holistic view of their customers. A single order repository of all customer information across all channels requires retailers to invest in IT infrastructure, enterprise software, and business intelligence. First, IT infrastructures in both physical stores and websites are necessary for the implementation of channel integration. For example, in-store kiosks can be used to give store customers access to products that are available only online. Second, integrated logistics is pertinent to a retailer's ability to offer in-store product pick-up or return and informational services. Investing in technology that centers on order management, supply chain technology, and transportation management is critical for operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. Thus, we hypothesize:

## H1. The level of IT assets is positively related to operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency.

Customer intimacy requires retailers to selectively market to customer segments, observe their interest level in the marketing effort, as well as tie their interest back to a cause-and-effect impact on purchases made due to the marketing effort. Day [17] argues that “strategies that emphasize creating customer value all depend on building distinctive market-sensing and customer-linking capabilities and using these capabilities to guide the internal processes.” Business intelligence systems are essential for this sake. For example, promotion and pricing actions could be easily coordinated and implemented across all channels by <sup>fi</sup>rms with an integrated IT infrastructure. Moreover, delivering a satisfying cross-channel experience requires data warehouse and merchandise planning systems. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

## H2. The level of IT assets is positively related to customer service.

IT assets facilitate innovation. Internet and the online channel provide a new venue for competitive action. For example, GAP Inc. offers new product lines speci<sup>fi</sup>cally for the online channel. Firms with more IT assets are more aware of emerging market opportunities and are more capable to launch new products tailored to the customer's interests. A <sup>fi</sup>rm's IT assets could spawn new product innovations and affect the <sup>fi</sup>rm's competitive actions. Strassmann [52], for instance, proposes the use of IT-induced competitive actions as a new kind of IT value measure. This relationship is consistent with the competitive dynamic perspective, which provides a theoretical link between RBV [4] and the industrial organization viewpoints on strategy [50]. Built upon IT-related organizational capability, <sup>fi</sup>rms are able to innovate and compete more effectively. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

## H3. The level of IT assets is positively related to product development.

## 2.4. Complementarity between IT assets and organizational resources

The strategic value of one organizational resource, according to RBV theory, may be linked to the presence of other organizational resources. Two assets are complementary to each other if having more of one asset increases the returns from the other. Complementarities give rise to synergy among the complementary activities. Powell and Dent-Micallef [39] demonstrate that IT investments require the existence of complementary resources to confer a competitive advantage. Melville et al. [35] propose an IT business value model where IT resources, along with complementary organizational resources, impact organizational performance via intermediate business processes. We argue that developing organizational capabilities, such as integrated ful<sup>fi</sup>llment, is generally costly. Thus, the extent to which a <sup>fi</sup>rm can implement sophisticated operational excellence, customer intimacy, or product leadership strategies is limited by <sup>fi</sup>nancial assets. Limitations in <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources can reduce the marginal impact of IT assets. Likewise, abundant slack <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources with fewer IT assets will not necessary lead to organizational capabilities. Therefore:

H4. The relationship between IT and organizational capabilities is stronger when organizational <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources are abundant than when organizational <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources are scarce.

## 2.5. Relationship among organizational capabilities

We further explore the relationship among organizational capabilities. According to a “tradeoffs theory” [22, 30], high performance in one capability is necessarily traded off for low performance in others; thus, <sup>fi</sup>rms must specialize in order to remain competitive. Recent research, however, suggests that tradeoffs are irrelevant in light of global competition and advanced manufacturing technologies [20]. Firms do not need to trade off capabilities, but can progress in areas where a foundation has been laid out. Firms trade off capabilities only when constrained to a given competitive frontier, but the frontier can be expanded through investment in new technologies and improvement activities [13, 16, 45, 46].

Accordingly, we propose that IT assets can expand the competitive frontier, providing resources that enable organizations to ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently and cost effectively build their capabilities. As shown in Fig. 2, the investment in IT provides an opportunity to expand the competitive frontier in all directions. Moreover, while <sup>fi</sup>rms may desire to move directly to point C from their current position (point A), it seems less plausible as a <sup>fi</sup>rm approaches its frontier. Few have succeeded in achieving this because there are fewer slack organizational resources and because it is increasingly dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to resolve such capability misalignments [48]. As capabilities are layered upon one another, certain progressions of capability improvement are more effective than others [18, 20], especially at the competitive frontier. Therefore, in this study we propose that a more realistic migration path involves building a solid foundation of operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency (point B), followed by improvements in market service and expand beyond point C.

Fig. 2. IT-enabled capability frontier

First, a <sup>fi</sup>rm with high operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency is more likely to have advantages in satisfying customer demands and to have fewer stockouts. For example, operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency enhances customer satisfaction by ensuring that customer expectations are met, in terms of order ful<sup>fi</sup>llment [10]. As a result, it can lead to higher customer loyalty to the <sup>fi</sup>rm, which enables a <sup>fi</sup>rm to develop long-term relationships with its customers and to maximize customer lifetime value. Thus, we hypothesize:

## H5. Operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency is positively related to customer service.

Similarly, a higher level of customer service allows a <sup>fi</sup>rm to better understand its customers, which may enable the <sup>fi</sup>rm to innovate and introduce new products and services to its customers [37]. Thus, we hypothesize:

H6. Customer service is positively related to product development.

## 3. Research method

## 3.1. Research design and sample description

We have collected longitudinal data for a set of publicly traded apparel retailers. The longitudinal sample enables us to study the changes within companies across different years and to eliminate unobservable <sup>fi</sup>rm heterogeneity. Our sample is limited to public <sup>fi</sup>rms due to the availability of public <sup>fi</sup>nancial information. First, the relevant North American Industry Classi<sup>fi</sup>cation System (NAICS) codes were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed for the apparel retail industry. We then identi-<sup>fi</sup>ed a list of potential sample <sup>fi</sup>rms from COMPUSTAT using the sixdigit NAICS codes. The resulting list was complemented by sources through EDGAR, a Securities and Exchange Commission site that lists all publicly traded companies, Hoover's Company Records, a database providing brief information of companies, and http:// apparelsearch.com, a website with a clothing retailer directory. Firms that became inactive before 2000 were eliminated from our list. In the end, 49 publicly traded <sup>fi</sup>rms were retained.

The data for this study come from two sources: (i) public information from news media, and (ii) COMPUSTAT. First, we searched major U.S. and world publications, web publications, and news wire services using the Lexis-Nexis database. We searched the Lexis-Nexis database using <sup>fi</sup>rm name in combination with the keywords (e.g., launch, unveil, or roll out [product or service]). From the keywords appearing in the text of news reports, we were able to identify and collect articles containing possible actions. Using structured content analysis [60], we obtained IT-related investment initiatives and project data, as well as organizational initiatives related to the three functional areas. Second, to complement the data obtained from news media, we also visited the websites and checked the 10K reports of all the <sup>fi</sup>rms in our dataset in order to learn more about the speci<sup>fi</sup>cs of certain actions. For example, we searched each <sup>fi</sup>rm's website to see if the <sup>fi</sup>rm allows customers to order a product online and pick it up in a store, order a product online and return it to a physical store, or even order a product online in the actual store (i.e., using kiosks). Third, in situations that the news articles and the additional data sources (i.e., Web sites and 10K reports) did not provide detailed information for our coding, we called the respective <sup>fi</sup>rms to clarify the stories (e.g., whether the consumer can purchase products from its website, and if yes, around which year). We tested the reliability of our coding process using Perreault and Leigh's [38] index of reliability. Although Perreault and Leigh [38] did not provide speci<sup>fi</sup>c guidelines as to what value the index should be considered acceptable, they suggested that 0.70 might be a reasonable value. Two academic experts independently coded the sample of actions into each category. This approach yielded an index value of 0.90, indicating that the coding process was considered reliable. Financial data were collected from Standard & Poor's COMPUSTAT data.

## 3.2. Measurement of variables

## 3.2.1. IT assets

IT assets are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the total number of major initiatives on IT investment in a <sup>fi</sup>rm over the period 1995 to 2007. This includes the installation of major IT systems, like supply chain management systems, customer relationship systems, and data mining systems. Markus and Soh [34] categorize IT assets into IT infrastructure and a portfolio of applications. Accordingly, we collect news on IT investments for each <sup>fi</sup>rm from two sub-dimensions: IT infrastructure, which includes Point of Sale (POS) systems, networking, and web infrastructure, and Enterprise information systems, which include enterprise resource software, logistic systems, data mining, and customer relationship management. We use the number of projects accumulated as a measure of the respective IT assets in a given year.

## 3.2.2. Organizational capabilities

Firms pursuing operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency strive for cost effective, rapid, and reliable ful<sup>fi</sup>llment of order requirements with minimal dif<sup>fi</sup>culty or inconvenience [56]. Therefore, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne operational capability in the retail industry as integrated ful<sup>fi</sup>llment capability. For example, customers can order online and then pick up merchandise at the physical stores. Also, online purchases can be returned to store locations. Three elements are proposed with regard to this ful<sup>fi</sup>llment integration: Does the retailer allow online order and instore pickup? Does the retailer allow online order and in-store return? Does the retailer allow online order in the physical store? In Bendoly et al. [9], three items in their measures of cross-channel capabilities are closely related to our operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency capability: the extent to which the <sup>fi</sup>rm allows online order and in-store pickup, the extent to which the <sup>fi</sup>rm allows online order and in-store return, and the extent to which the <sup>fi</sup>rm allows online order in the physical store. We code binary responses to indicate whether a retailer has abilities in each of the aforementioned functional areas, where 1 denotes having the ability and 0 denotes not having the ability [23]. In the same way that Gold et al. [24] de<sup>fi</sup>ne knowledge management capabilities, the operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency capability is calculated as the sum of functions that the <sup>fi</sup>rm is capable of in a given year.

Customer service is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as cross-channel marketing capability, which tailors services to <sup>fi</sup>ne-tuned market niches. For example, can companies send coupons for both online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine stores depending on past customer behavior and inventory levels? Can store employees see the entire customer order history and add an online order to a store purchase? Therefore, the capability is operationalized from two dimensions: cross-channel price coordination and cross-channel promotion. It is calculated as the sum of functions that the <sup>fi</sup>rm is capable of in a given year.

Product development capability is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the total number of new products a <sup>fi</sup>rm offers in a given year [33].

## 3.2.3. Control variables

Financial resources allow <sup>fi</sup>rms to implement a greater number of competitive moves [60]. Therefore, we controlled for this in<sup>fl</sup>uence by including a measure for unabsorbed organizational slack, measured as the ratio of current assets, less inventory, to current liabilities. Firm size is an important determinant of innovation, technology use, and <sup>fi</sup>rm performance [12, 40, 44]. Moreover, prior literature suggests that large <sup>fi</sup>rms are slower in terms of action timing [15]. We therefore use total assets (in billions of dollars) as a measure of <sup>fi</sup>rm size to control for the aforementioned effects. The speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of each equation also contains a time effect, $e _ { t } ^ { ( j ) }$ , that is common to all retailers. ## 4. Empirical results and discussions

One possible approach for our data estimation is pooled ordinary least squares (POLS) which treats observations as being serially uncorrelated for a given retailer, with homoscedastic errors across retailers and individual consumers. However, many retailer-speci<sup>fi</sup>c characteristics, such as the retailer's organizational culture, typically exist in the panel data set and are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to observe or measure. If the unobservable retailer-speci<sup>fi</sup>c characteristic is not taken into account, results from POLS might be biased. The Breusch–Pagan tests $( \chi ^ { 2 } ( 1 ) = 4 5 1 . 9 3$ , pb.01 for Eq. (1); $\chi ^ { 2 } ( 1 ) = 4 2 3 . 3 1$ for Eq. (2), pb.01; $\chi ^ { 2 } ( 1 ) = 7 . 5 6 ,$ pb.01 for Eq. (3)) suggest that there is strong evidence of the retailer-speci<sup>fi</sup>c effects in our dataset. Panel data techniques are employed to <sup>fi</sup>t our dataset. The Hausman test indicates that the retailer-speci<sup>fi</sup>c effects are correlated with the explanatory variables. Thus the <sup>fi</sup>xed effects model is preferred to randomeffects estimation. We ran a number of regressions with <sup>fi</sup>xed effects for individual <sup>fi</sup>rms and years. The VIF statistics [8] in the preliminary estimations indicate that no single variable has a VIF above 5, suggesting that multicollinearity was not a concern.

Table 2 reports the effects of IT assets on operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency capability. The test for autocorrelation in panel data models [59] indicates the presence of serial correlation. A modi<sup>fi</sup>ed Wald statistic for groupwise heteroscedasticity in the residuals of a <sup>fi</sup>xed effects regression model, following Greene [26], suggests the existence of heteroscedasticity. To address heteroscedasticity and the correlation of errors within retailers, we <sup>fi</sup>rst ran static <sup>fi</sup>xed effects models with robust standard errors clustered by retailer. Model 1 and Model 2 report the results of the static models with cluster robust standard errors.

As the results suggest, IT assets are positively related with operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency (b=0.11, pb0.01), therefore supporting H1. Financial resources do not have a direct in<sup>fl</sup>uence on operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. Instead, <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources complement IT in enhancing operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency capability (b=0.02, pb0.05), supporting H4. More <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources, by themselves, do not necessarily help <sup>fi</sup>rms achieve operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. Abundant <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources can increase the marginal effects of IT assets, as <sup>fi</sup>rms are more likely to implement cross-channel retailing when facing fewer <sup>fi</sup>nancial constraints. As suggested by the results in Models 1 and 2, larger <sup>fi</sup>rms (measured by total assets) are slower in achieving operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency.

We also ran a dynamic speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of the models by including a lagged dependent variable. The dynamic speci<sup>fi</sup>cation helps eliminate autocorrelation, and, more importantly, allows us to examine the long-term effects of IT assets. Such dynamic panel-data models include a lagged dependent variable as covariates. By construction, the unobserved retailer-speci<sup>fi</sup>c effects are correlated with the lagged dependent variable, making standard estimators inconsistent. Arellano and Bond [3] derived a consistent generalized method-of-moments (GMM) estimator for the parameters of this model. This estimator is designed for datasets with many panels and few periods, and it requires that there be no autocorrelation in the idiosyncratic errors of the dynamic model. The estimates of dynamic models are shown under Model 3 and Model 4. Both the Sargan test of overidenti<sup>fi</sup>cation and the Arellano–Bond test for zero autocorrelation present no evidence of model misspeci<sup>fi</sup>cation. The results are quite similar to those of static models. The long-term relationship between IT assets and operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency is 0.32 (based on the results of Model 3). As suggested by Model 4, the long-term effect of the interaction term between IT assets and <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources is 0.15. In the long run, IT has a much stronger relationship with organizational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency.

We then investigated factors affecting customer service capability. The speci<sup>fi</sup>cation tests provide evidence for autocorrelation and heteroscedasticity. Therefore, we use static models with cluster robust standard errors and dynamic models to address these two issues. The results are reported in Table 3. In Model 1, we test the impact of IT assets on customer service and <sup>fi</sup>nd the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient to be highly signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. The coef<sup>fi</sup>cient for IT assets and that for <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources are both signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly positive, thereby suggesting signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effects of both IT assets and <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources on customer service. H2 is therefore supported.

Model 2 adds the interaction term between IT assets and slack <sup>fi</sup>- nancial resources to Model 1. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that the relationship between IT assets and customer service capability is positively moderated by <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources. Model 3 introduces the operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency variable. Operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency is positively related with customer service capability in our sample (b=0.75, pb0.01), validating H5. The results also suggest a mediated moderation. Both the effect of IT and the moderating effect of slack <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources are mediated by operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. As shown in Model 3, we only <sup>fi</sup>nd indirect effects of IT assets on customer service. Both the magnitude and the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient for IT asset are dramatically reduced. Moreover, although the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient for the interaction term remains signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in Model 3, the magnitude of the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient is reduced (from 0.07 to 0.05) after the introduction of the operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency variable, indicating partial mediation [6]. We further use the Sobel test [6, 51] and <sup>fi</sup>nd the results to be highly statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant (T=6.36, pb0.01), indicating the mediating role of the variable of operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. We also tried the interaction term between the operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency variable and slack <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources, but did not <sup>fi</sup>nd signi<sup>fi</sup>cant results. The estimation results for dynamic models, reported in Models 4, 5, and 6, provide qualitatively similar results as those in static models. As suggested by Model 6, the longterm effect of operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency is 1.2, which is almost double the short-term effect.

One possible reason is that operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and customer service are not factors of product development, but are a source of heterogeneity among retailers. Accordingly, we cannot introduce them directly into the regression as covariates.

Therefore, we conducted several comparative sample-split analyses by dividing the companies into high- and low-operational ef<sup>fi</sup>- ciency groups using the operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency variable, and into high- and low-customer service groups using the customer service variable based on the mean values. IT plays a much stronger role in the high-operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency sample. In low- and high-customer service samples, the role of IT does not change much; if any, our results seem to suggest that the relationship between IT and product development is smaller in high-customer service samples. Mastering customer service capability seems to take resources away from introducing new products. Unlike the complementary relationship between operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and customer service capabilities, customer service and product development are substitutes for one another. A possible reason for this is that two value disciplines may compete for limited organizational resources, or the capabilities developed for the two value disciplines are hard to accumulate simultaneously.

## 5. Implications and conclusions

A majority of <sup>fi</sup>rms have adopted some form of enterprise systems, yet on average, the <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance of <sup>fi</sup>rms with enterprise systems is not greatly different from that of <sup>fi</sup>rms without [29]. Instead of considering a direct impact of IT on <sup>fi</sup>rm performance, this study investigates the impacts of IT at intermediate processes, emphasizing the extent to which IT enhances critical organizational capabilities. Capabilities play a dominant role in achieving a sustainable competitive advantage. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we asked how IT assets correlate with retailers' capability, how other organizational resources complement the relationship between IT assets and organizational capabilities, and how having one organizational capability affects other organizational capabilities. Based on the diagram of value disciplines [55, 56], we investigated critical organizational capabilities related with operations, customer service, and product development. Using longitudinal data on the apparel industry from 1995 to 2007, this study empirically investigates how IT helps to accumulate capabilities for the three areas in the retail industry. While IT assets are susceptible to duplications by rivals, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings reveal that IT assets do help to accumulate organizational capabilities. Helfat and Peteraf [27] suggest that capabilities are vulnerable to decay and so must undergo reinvestment and renewal in order to prevent a loss of a <sup>fi</sup>rm's competitive advantage. Therefore, to safeguard a leadership position, <sup>fi</sup>rms will need to undertake innovative IT investments to facilitate capabilities accumulation. Organizational capabilities enhanced by continuous investments in IT will not be easily copied by competitors. Second, <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources complement IT in acquiring organizational capabilities. This <sup>fi</sup>nding is consistent with a resource-based view. We also <sup>fi</sup>nd some interrelationship among the three capabilities. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, operational capability is highly related with customer service capabilities. Ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency in ful<sup>fi</sup>lling customer demand also helps <sup>fi</sup>rms to acquire ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency in satisfying unique customer needs. However, product development capability is quite different from operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and customer service capabilities. To a certain degree, product development capability can act as a substitute for the capability for customer service. This may be attributed to a <sup>fi</sup>rm's cognitive and resource constraints.

This paper makes several contributions to the IS literature. First, by using an integrative model that examines IT value at intermediate processes, we show that IT is able to confer a competitive advantage by enhancing critical organizational capabilities. This <sup>fi</sup>nding provides guidance to <sup>fi</sup>rms as they develop strategies and decide how to allocate resources. To the best of our knowledge, our research is one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst attempts to look into the “black box” of the <sup>fi</sup>rm by empirically inspecting the relationship between IT and critical organizational capabilities. Second, we propose that higher-order capabilities could also correlate with one another. Our study indicates that operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency capabilities are positively related to customer service, but that there exists a competitive relationship between customer service and product development capability in the apparel retail industry. Treacy and Wiersema [56] suggest that <sup>fi</sup>rms focus on one value discipline at the expense of others. Our results seem to partially agree with their suggestions. There exist contentions among certain capabilities but complementarity also exists. Therefore, when developing organizational capabilities, <sup>fi</sup>rms must understand the substitutive or complementary relationships among their capabilities.

As with any research, this paper comes with a number of limitations that open opportunities for further exploration in future research. First, our study only focuses on public <sup>fi</sup>rms, which may limit the generalizability of our results to private <sup>fi</sup>rms. This study also only investigates the apparel retail industry. It would be of interest to see what role industrial factors play in affecting the relationship between IT and organizational capabilities. Finally, other factors could be included in our model to extend our understanding of the complementary impact of other organizational resources. For example, top management team heterogeneity is an important factor affecting the accumulation of organizational capabilities. Other possible interesting factors for further research may include IT worker skills and organization structural factors.
