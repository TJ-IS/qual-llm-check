# Effects of internal support and consultant quality on the consulting process and ERP system quality B

Eric T.G. Wang <sup>\*</sup>, Jessica H.F. Chen

Department of Information Management, School of Management, National Central University, Chung-Li, Taiwan 32054, ROC

Received 4 November 2002; received in revised form 12 August 2005; accepted 24 August 2005 Available online 28 September 2005

## Abstract

ERP implementation, as a change initiative, is a challenge facing any organization and requires strong support from top management and users. However, internal support is inadequate to overcome client deficiencies in the resources and abilities essential to ERP implementation, implying that the assistance of outside experts is inevitable. This study presents a conceptual framework to investigate how human inputs (top management, users, and external consultants) are linked to communication effectiveness and conflict resolution in the ERP consulting process, as well as the effects of these factors on the quality of the system implemented. Through a survey of 85 ERP implementation projects in Taiwanese manufacturers, the study demonstrates that competent consultants can facilitate communication and conflict resolution in the ERP consulting process and assist in improving ERP system quality. The findings indicate that top management support indirectly enhances ERP system quality through its positive effect on conflict resolution in the consulting process. The results also show that high user support enhances communication effectiveness; however, communication effectiveness does not influence conflict resolution and ERP system quality. The implications and the limitations of the study are discussed.

Keywords: Enterprise resource planning; Consultant quality; Communication; Conflict resolution

## 1. Introduction

In the recent decade, companies have spent billions of dollars and countless hours implementing Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) systems. ERP systems are comprehensive packages designed to integrate a wide range of business functions to provide a holistic view of the firm from a single information technology architecture [26]. The integrative nature of ERP systems makes their implementation more complicated than that of traditional packages, and firms typically find themselves lacking adequate internal resources to implement an ERP system. Owing to this knowledge gap and resource constraints, mediating entities such as consulting firms play an important role in almost all ERP implementations. External consultants, who provide technical and business expertise, reduce client knowledge or learning burden [45]. Their expertise allows clients to configure an appropriate ERP system and helps train users to fully exploit the technology. When the knowledge gap between a client and consultants is salient, the management of consulting process is at the core of ERP implementation.

Researchers, such as Thong and colleagues [47,46, 45], recognized the importance of external consultants in traditional Information System (IS) implementation. For example, Thong [47] found that high-quality external experts were more crucial to IS effectiveness than top management support in small businesses. However, to transfer the knowledge and business practices embodied in an ERP package into the client organization, consultants must have diverse knowledge and skills, such as system knowledge and industry-specific expertise, making the function and role of consultants in ERP implementation different from those in traditional IS contexts. Thus, more systematic investigation into the role of consultants in ERP consultation and implementation is needed. Equally important is the influence of internal stakeholders, top management and users, in the ERP consulting process, because internal stakeholders must understand and learn to use what is embedded in the system. External consultants, top management, and users are the critical persons that will significantly impact the process and outcome of an ERP implementation [36]. To our knowledge, no other empirical studies have directly investigated the influence of clients, consultants and the consulting process on ERP implementation. Consequently, this study constructs an ERP consulting processing model (see Fig. 1), investigating whether such human inputs affect the consulting process related to effective communication and conflict resolution and whether these factors lead to a higher quality system.

This study contributes to ERP implementation research in two ways. First, while many ERP related studies have centered on client aspects, such as change management [1,48], this study stresses the impact of the consulting process on ERP implementation. Second, even though every ERP package incorporates numerous best practices, <sup>b</sup>[s]tandard software by definition can never include every function every company or industry needs<sup>Q</sup> [38]. Since local organizational exigencies need to be crystallized into the workable ERP solutions, extensive client inputs and efforts are required for any ERP implementation [12,14]. This study highlights the contribution of <sup>b</sup>people<sup>Q</sup>—top management, users, and outside consultants, to the process of ERP consultation.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. The following section discusses the conceptual background and the research model. Section 3 explains the study methods, and Section 4 constructs a structural model to test the hypotheses. Finally, Section 5 discusses the findings and Section 6 presents conclusions.

## 2. Conceptual background and hypotheses

This section discusses the research constructs and derives the hypotheses. Fig. 1 depicts the research model and summarizes the hypotheses derived.

## 2.1. ERP system quality

Theorists are still wrestling with the question of which constructs best represent the success of ERP implementation. Gable et al. [19] proposed a measurement model with four dimensions for assessing enterprise systems success. However, to date, the organizational impact of ERP investment in adopting firm may yet be realized, and the impact of different dimensions may also be context-dependent. Further, whether a system can provide reliable and useful information and be maintained or operated without error will determine its further use and benefits. Without such a characteristic, an ERP system may lose users’ trust, resulting in less usage of and greater resistance to the system. Consequently, instead of assessing the individual and organizational impacts of ERP, this study focuses on the quality of the implemented ERP package perceived by the client, which is termed <sup>b</sup>ERP system quality<sup>Q</sup> and includes the system’s operational reliability, functional reliability, integrity, correctness, and usefulness [41].

## 2.2. ERP consulting process

One key to a successful ERP implementation is to maintain an effective and smooth consulting process. The following discusses the two main factors that are likely to affect the ERP consulting process: communication effectiveness [5,9,14] and conflict resolution [25,30,37], and then derives their effects on ERP system quality.

## 2.2.1. Communication effectiveness

Ford [14] argues that effective communication is a key to reliable client–consultant relationships. Communication effectiveness describes the extent to which consultants and users can understand the expressions of the other side in the consulting process. As an interorganizational collaboration, users must convey their understanding of business practices accurately and completely to the consultants, and the consultants then build those local contingencies in a suitable solution. Failure to identify the problem and specify the dyadic roles may lead to recommending unfeasible solutions by consultant and jeopardize the system quality [14]. Intensive information and knowledge sharing in the consulting process therefore is a prerequisite for effective ERP implementation. Insufficient clarity of need and expectation is at the heart of most poor client–consultant relationships [14].

Bloomfield and Danieli [5] suppose the role of consultants is to use stratagems to make themselves indispensable to clients and to build their identity in the implementation project. Similarly, Clark views a client–consultant relationship as an audience–actor relationship and consultant performance as <sup>b</sup>exercises in persuasive communication<sup>Q</sup> [[9]:118]. Therefore, this study holds that communication effectiveness not only connotes the mutual understanding between the client and consultants but also means users continually adjust and redefine their views through their interactions with consultants. Through more effective communication, the probability of delivering a configuration that fits more closely with client requirements is improved [13]. Effective communication also allows knowledge to be discovered de novo within the client organization [3]. Higher communication effectiveness, thus, should benefit system configuration and client process adjustments, leading to higher ERP system quality.

H1. Communication effectiveness is positively associated with ERP system quality.

## 2.2.2. Conflict resolution

Most ERP implementations take more than six months and the long-period inter-firm cooperation between the client and the consultancy is fraught with potential problems [25]. Given that the client and consultants may not hold similar value, frames, and problem-solving approaches, the mismatch between the client and consultants may cause conflicts and the client–consultant relationship and the quality of the resulting work may both suffer [25,30]. Conflicts, however, are not always dysfunctional. Whether a conflict is functional or dysfunctional depends mainly on conflict management [21], which is a critical aspect of the ERP consulting process.

Successful conflict resolution in an ERP implementation is defined in this study as the situation in which conflicts are fully addressed to produce timely settlements that are acceptable to both parties and consistent with the implementation goals. Conflict resolution is a learning and team development process. Through the process, both parties learn more about how to negotiate over disagreements and situations and come up with mutually beneficial solutions [37]. Effective conflict resolution can enhance the information-processing capacity of the project team by facilitating goal congruence and establishing implementation process standards [39], thereby mitigating <sup>b</sup>the adverse effects of complexity by increasing the quantity of information available to the team<sup>Q</sup> [43: 26]. As Robey et al. [37] pointed out, team-member conflict resolution behavior in system development is directly associated with project success. This relationship is believed to hold true in ERP implementations as well.

H2. Conflict resolution is positively associated with ERP system quality.

ERP consultants need to understand the details of existing client business practices, applicable software modules, and change management strategies [1]. Open and intensive communication can accelerate this understanding. Lee and Kim [28] argued that intensive communication could make parties better informed, thus increasing the perceived value of the relationship to each party and making them more willing to maintain it. Additionally, better communication has been treated as an antecedent of inter-firm trust [33]. Moorman et al. [32] asserted that timely communication fosters trust by helping resolve disputes and aligning perceptions and expectations between organizations. Effective communications thus benefit conflict resolution because of the higher levels of trust and partnership quality between the client and consultants.

H3. Communication effectiveness is positively associated with conflict resolution.

## 2.3. Supports from external consultants

Knowledge and technical know-how could be important barriers to complex technology diffusion [3]. Researchers have highlighted the prominent role of external consultants in technology implementation (e.g., [47,46,45]). Competent consultants are valuable to ERP implementation because significant knowledge asymmetry typically exists between the client and consultants [40]. Knowledge asymmetry can induce client dependence on consultants, increasing the importance of consultant quality for ERP implementation.

Based on the literature on perceived service quality [6,10,34] and Freeman and Dart [15], consultant quality is referred to as the extent of support, help and work reliability that external consultants provide. Competent consultants not only are well trained in ERP implementation methodologies, but also have real system deployment experience [1,4]. Besides, consultants generally are those who actually perform the system configuration. The solutions they offer should directly influence the implemented ERP system quality, independent of their interactions with the client.

H4. Consultant quality is positively associated with ERP system quality.

Successful consultation requires that consultants have sufficient technical and management skills to establish their expert power. Without sufficient expertise and communication skills, incompetent consultants tend to have improper business practices, recommend ineffective solutions, or manage the implementation poorly [31]. In addition to the required technical skills and knowledge, consultant willingness to listen to the users and understand their needs, and resolve conflicts with them are also critical for successful consultation. Through a qualitative study, McGivern [30] finds whether the project goals can be achieved depends on the quality of client–consultant relationship. A relationship is characterized with good quality when consultants manifest high level of personal commitment and can be trusted by clients. When consultants cannot be trusted sufficiently by a client, the client will not give confidential information relevant to the consultants’ task. In this situation, mutual communication cannot go on and conflicts may occur [30]. On the contrary, improved user perceptions of consultants’ devotion to help can increase users’ confidence and trust, leading to better communication and conflict resolution in the consulting process.

H5a. Consultant quality is positively associated with communication effectiveness.

H5b. Consultant quality is positively associated with conflict resolution.

## 2.4. Supports from internal stakeholders

Although competent consultants are crucial for delivering a high-quality ERP system, the role of client organizational factors in achieving a successful implementation should not be overlooked. The main factor is client internal support, which refers to the emotional commitment of the client stakeholders toward the ERP adoption. In this study, client internal support is determined by top management support and user support, since top management provides the necessary resources for the ERP project, while users actually operate and exploit the system. Because front line users may be uncomfortable with the realization that top management now can easily gain access to more detailed data related to their jobs than in the past, emotional support should therefore be emphasized throughout the organization [48]. Further, as the freedom of choice in system analysis/design is reduced, the role of the client in ERP implementation will also be qualitatively different from that found in traditional IS development. Consequently, the attitudes of top management and users toward the ERP system may only affect the implementation outcomes indirectly through smoothing communication and speeding up conflict resolution. Although the roles of the two kinds of internal supports have been studied extensively in the literature on IS development and implementation [e.g., [23,47,45]], systematic investigation of their effects in the context of ERP implementation is yet lacking.

## 2.4.1. Top management support

Top management support describes the extent to which client executives provide the attention, resources, and authority required for ERP implementation. In fact, proactive top management support is critical in IS implementation even when external consultants are involved [16]. Because ERP implementations usually trigger profound changes in organizational cultures and business practices, the role of top management should be emphasized more in ERP implementations than in traditional IS projects. Successful ERP implementation requires top management to help the ERP system meet organizational objectives and provide the resources necessary to integrate the system with business strategies and processes over time [24]. Sufficient resources for ERP implementation allow employees to spend more time clarifying their requirements to consultants, improving the communication in the consulting process. Visible top management support also encourages positive user attitudes toward the ERP system, smoothing the conversion from existing work procedures to new ones [47,45]. This is particularly important when the ERP adoption faces strong user resistance. With top management support, user resistance can be partially mitigated by having top executives encourage, or even mandate, user engagement in the implementation. Consequently, top management support can assist the consulting process by fostering positive attitudes of both users and consultants toward the ERP project, leading to more effective communication and conflict resolution.

H6a. Top management support is positively associated with communication effectiveness.

H6b. Top management support is positively associated with conflict resolution.

## 2.4.2. User support

User support refers to the psychological state of users toward the changes brought about by the new system as well as toward the use of the system for performing their tasks. Although users are less influential in ERP implementations than in developing tailormade applications, the lack of user inputs may still reduce the chance of a successful implementation. ERP implementation often requires reengineering the organizational core business processes to match with those implicit in the software [42], thus demanding users to change the way they work to fully exploit the system [48]. When users are not psychologically ready to change and accept the new ERP system, their attitudes and behaviors will hinder them from working and resolving conflicts with consultants, as well as from absorbing and exploiting the ERP knowledge from consultants [31]. Accordingly, the degree of user support should positively affect the communication and conflict resolution in the ERP consulting process.

H7a. User support is positively associated with communication effectiveness.

H7b. User support is positively associated with conflict resolution.

## 2.4.3. Control variable

It has been argued that the business procedures and reference models embedded in an ERP package are influenced by the business culture of the country from which the system originates [42]. Due to cultural differences, the problem of misfit can be particularly significant when a firm adopts an ERP package developed by a foreign vendor [42]. Therefore, a binary indicator (1 = if the package is developed by a local vendor; 0 = otherwise) is included to control the potential confounding effect caused by the country of origin of ERP packages.

## 3. Methods

## 3.1. Survey procedures and sample characteristics

The initial instrument was developed first based on existing instruments, and then tested for face validity by consulting practitioners. Four experienced ERP project leaders and consultants were asked to examine the content of the constructs. The items used in this study were selected based on their expert suggestions. Two project managers with extensive experience of ERP implementation (implementing the SAP system) then further examined the Chinese version of the chosen measures to assess their face validity. The questionnaire was adjusted based on their suggestions. The final questionnaire, with a cover letter, was sent to the COOs (Chief Operating Officers) of 600 Taiwanese manufacturers randomly selected from the Common Wealth list of the 1000 largest Taiwanese manufacturing firms. The cover letter solicited the cooperation of the COOs in helping identify the ERP project leader if they themselves did not fulfill this role in their firm. This procedure helped identify those individuals who had the most comprehensive knowledge of the ERP implementation process in the companies.

Ninety questionnaires were returned, representing a response rate of 15%. Given the length of the survey, this response rate was thought to be reasonable. Incomplete questionnaires were double checked by e-mail or phone. Five questionnaires were rejected because the responding firms (1) had not installed an ERP, (2) were still installing the system (i.e., missing data of the <sup>b</sup>ERP system quality<sup>Q</sup>), or (3) implemented an ERP without external consultants support. Finally, 85 complete questionnaires were available for analysis.

To check for possible non-response bias, t-tests were used to compare the number of employees and annual sales of the respondents with those of the non-respondents. The results showed no significant difference in between. The sample was considered representative of large to medium-sized manufacturing firms in Taiwan. Table 1 lists the basic information of the responding firms.

## 3.2. Measures

The seven-item scale of top management support measured the extent to which top management involved and provided the resources and authority required for ERP implementation and was adapted from Lee and Kim [27]. The five-item scale measuring user support was designed to capture user attitudes toward the change brought about by and the use of the new ERP system and was adapted from Jiang et al. [23]. The scale for assessing consultant quality was adapted from the measure of consultant quality developed by Freeman and Dart [15]. Although the original SERVQ-UAL scale is gap-based [34], performance-only service quality measures have been demonstrated to be superior to gap-based measures [6,10], and therefore this study adopted a performance-only approach. Communication effectiveness refers to the extent to which the users and consultants can understand the expressions of the other side in the consulting process and was assessed by a four-item scale adapted from Davison [11]. Three items for measuring conflict resolution were taken from Sussman and Guinan [43] and designed to express the extent to which the consulting process resolved divergences and conflicts. Finally, five items measuring ERP system quality were adapted from Shin and Lee [41], assessing the extent to which the ERP system provided the client with accurate data and information and sufficient functions. ## 5. Findings and implications

Our empirical results suggest that both conflict resolution and consultant quality can directly and positively influence ERP system quality (H2 and H4), but the postulated effect of communication effectiveness is insignificant (H1). Even though communication effectiveness is hypothesized to enhance both conflict resolution and ERP system quality, the results presented here indicate no such effect. Of course, this result might be caused by a strong correlation between communication effectiveness and conflict resolution (r = -.60). Owing to the high correlation between these two variables, an even higher correlation between conflict resolution and ERP system quality (r = .69) leaves communication effectiveness little additional power to explain ERP system quality in the model. Therefore, although communication effectiveness might be important for effective ERP implementation, resolving conflicts during ERP implementation could be even more important in delivering a high quality ERP system. User support did not show any significant effect on conflict resolution (H7b), despite the high inter-correlations between the two variables. Again, this finding might result from a very high association between consultant quality and conflict resolution.

Both consultant quality and top management support are positively associated with conflict resolution (H5b and H6b). Consultants who offer high quality service can benefit the consulting process by facilitating both communication and conflict resolution, thus reducing the occurrence of persistent conflicts in an ERP implementation. Moreover, with many organizational units involved in an ERP project, visible top management support should also be critical to inter-unit conflict resolution during the implementation process.

Further, both user support and consultant quality are shown to positively impact communication effectiveness (H7a and H5a). This result indicates that user willingness to support the project and accept the changes brought about by the new system motivates users to express themselves more frankly regarding their thoughts and requirements during implementation. Consultants who care for the client and are sufficiently knowledgeable should also be able to communicate more effectively with users, reducing the potential for misunderstanding and increasing user engagement in system implementation. However, top management support fails to significantly affect communication effectiveness. As leaders and facilitators, top executives appear not to be actively involved with daily system implementation, and thus do not directly influence communication effectiveness during implementation.

As for the control variable, local package, the result shows that ERP system quality tends to be higher when the client adopts a local package than when adopting a foreign one. This may be because foreign ERP packages contain business procedures and models that relatively poorly matched with common business practices in

Taiwan, leaving many client requirements unfulfilled if the required customizations or process changes are too costly. Besides directly affecting ERP software quality, <sup>b</sup>local package<sup>Q</sup> may moderate the effect of the factors affecting ERP software quality and thus reduce their main effects. Though not the main issue addressed by this study, exploratory regression analysis was nonetheless performed to examine the potential moderating effect of the local package variable, as illustrated in Appendix B. The results indicate that the local package variable did moderate the relationships between <sup>b</sup>communication effectiveness<sup>Q</sup> and <sup>b</sup>ERP software quality<sup>Q</sup> and between <sup>b</sup>conflict resolution<sup>Q</sup> and <sup>b</sup>ERP software quality.<sup>Q</sup> The results suggest that in the situation of adopting a foreign package, the importance of resolving conflicts is increased, but effective communication is more beneficial when adopting a local package. Furthermore, adding the interaction terms does not significantly change the results of the main-effect-only model, indicating the robustness of our results. The general support to our research model also provides additional evidence of the construct validity of the constructs proposed in the study.

The findings have several implications. First, they suggest that consultants make important contributions during ERP implementation, probably by transferring a full range of technical knowledge and reifying organizational requirements and exigencies into system configurations [3]. Consultants also can contribute to ERP system quality indirectly by enhancing conflict resolution. Competent consultants can negotiate among cross-functional users, ensuring that the concerns of different functions are fully addressed during implementation. Therefore, practical efforts in hiring the right consultants are essential, especially as the consulting fees are quite high. However, <sup>b</sup>hiring a consultant is just the tip of the iceberg. Managing a consulting firm and its employees is even more challenging<sup>Q</sup> [4: 11]. Given the importance of consultants in ERP implementation, the detailed consulting process and the effect of the process on the outcomes of ERP implementation deserve more thorough and systematic investigation. Second, top management support fails to facilitate communication effectiveness, but can improve conflict resolution, producing an indirect effect on ERP system quality. Senior executives thus must intercede when a project is in trouble, when a person or group refuses to cooperate, or when the implementation team cannot resolve a conflict without assistance. Third, communication effectiveness shows in both user support and consultant quality. As Gable [17] asserted that client learning is vital when engaging external consultants. Key users with a strong computer background may still have difficulty in fully understanding the configuration of a complex ERP system. If strong consultant communication abilities can assist users to absorb the ERP related knowledge and skills more effectively, then users subsequently should be able to maintain and further modify the system without consultant engagement.

Fourth, the Market Intelligence Center, an industrial research institute in Taiwan, reported that as of 2002, 56% of the 1000 largest manufacturers in Taiwan had implemented an ERP system. A number of local ERP software vendors dominated the Taiwanese ERP market, such as Data Systems (accounting for 34.1% of the cases reported here, Table 1), and Proyoung Business Information System (12.9%). However, many large manufacturers in Taiwan still like to purchase ERP systems from foreign vendors, such as Oracle (21.1% of the cases reported here), SAP (5.8%), Baan (4.7%) and J.D. Edwards (4.7%). Because the products of local ERP vendors typically do not support global operations, local ERP vendors usually target their market on small to medium-sized firms [44]. Additionally, local vendors in Taiwan usually provide consulting services by themselves (vendor-only approach [46]), while foreign vendors normally authorize one or more independent consulting firms to provide consulting services for their applications (vendor–consultancy approach). The study of Thong, Yap, and Raman [46] suggested that small companies adopting the vendoronly approach have more effective information systems than those adopting the consultant–vendor approach. Such a result might imply vendors provide better implementation services quality than do independent consultancies. The results of this study further suggest that adopting a local package can impact the quality of the implemented ERP package even with the effect of consultant quality controlled. Finally, the data presented in this study also indicate that Taiwanese firms typically implement many modules, with the frequently implemented modules being Financial Accounting (FI) and Material Management (MM), as in the U.S. [29].

## 6. Conclusions

This study empirically shows the importance of consultants in shaping the ERP consulting process and the delivered ERP system quality. Generally, ERP consultants are critical in influencing the effectiveness of communication and conflict resolution in the consulting process. Despite being less influential than consultants, top management support for an ERP project still impacts the system quality indirectly through conflict resolution. However, user support neither directly nor indirectly affects the quality of the delivered system. We consider the results are generally supportive to the research model and such support also provides additional evidence of the validity of the constructs proposed in the study.

This study has several limitations. The first is the threat of common method bias. Since all data were collected with the same instrument, the methodological nuisance of common method variance might lead to inflated model relationships. To examine the extent of systematic errors, Harman’s one factor test was performed. All scale items were factor analyzed together using the eigenvalue-greater-than-one criterion of unrotated factor analysis. Six factors were extracted and explained over 77.42% of the variance. The existence of multiple factors accounting for a sizable percentage of the variance among the variables indicates that common method bias is not a serious problem in this study.

Second, despite care being taken to ensure proper informant selection, self-reports of ERP project leaders might still be perceptually biased. Some researchers have questioned the ability of key informants to report validly [35]; however, others have argued that a single, reliable informant is preferable to multiple respondents with varying familiarity with the investigated phenomenon [7]. Besides, some studies have shown that high inter-rater reliability exists among project team members [22]. Nevertheless, a multiple-informant approach should be valuable for investigating complex processes such as ERP implementation. Third, the high correlations between the independent variables mean large shared variances between these variables, and may lead to non-significant paths in the model. Therefore, the findings of this study should be treated as preliminary and may not preclude the importance of constructs that do not significantly influence the outcomes of ERP implementation in this study. Fourth, the variances shared between consultant quality and its indicators and the correlation between conflict resolution and conflict resolution are equal, even though these two constructs are different in nature. Conflict resolution focuses on the interaction of the client–consultant dyad while consultant quality assesses the service the consultants provide. This nevertheless makes the discriminant validity between consultant quality and conflict resolution suspicious, thus to some extent limiting the interpretability of our results.

Finally, more refined construct development and theory building of ERP implementation are demanded. Since all measures of this study were adapted from the traditional IS literature and may not be completely suitable for the ERP context, the measures must be developed more thoroughly and rigorously to enhance the content validity of the scales for studying ERP implementation. Although, this study emphasizes the centrality of the client–consultant relationship, justification about the interaction between consultants and client organizations still requires more research effort and stronger theory development. As the sample size of this study is relatively small, future research with larger sample sizes is needed to strengthen the preliminary findings of this study.
