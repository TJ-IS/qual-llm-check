# Drivers, barriers and critical success factors for ERPII implementation in supply chains: A critical analysis

S.C.L. Koh <sup>a,</sup>⇑, A. Gunasekaran <sup>b,1</sup>, T. Goodman <sup>c,2</sup>

## a b s t r a c t

This paper reviews existing literature to determine the drivers of and barriers to Enterprise Resource Planning II (ERPII) implementation. The ERPII literature is then extended through interviews with potential players in ERPII implementations to identify the critical success factors (CSFs) or preconditions required for successful implementation throughout supply chains. These interviews were conducted with leading ERP vendors/consultants and organisations involved in the entire supply chain to gather evidence on the success, or lack thereof, of ERPII implementations. The results were compared and contrasted to existing literature on ERPII, collaborative networks, and the extended enterprise. We found more barriers to than drivers of successful ERPII implementation. This leads prospective implementers to have a pessimistic forecast for ERPII implementation success. Our research reveals that main reason for this negativity is a general lack of understanding and appreciation of the capabilities of the extended enterprise network. Second, the research presents two sets of CSFs: CSFs which apply to traditional ERP and carry forward to apply to ERPII, and CSFs that are tailored to the new needs for successful ERPII implementations. Finally, the research questions the suitability of ERPII in today’s modern business environment, and suggests that technology may have overtaken management’s capabilities to capture the full benefits of such an advanced enterprise system. Future trends in ERPII development are also considered in an attempt to find the next phase in the enterprise system life cycle. Beyond ERPII, the research suggests that infrastructure such as large-scale business intelligence (BI) systems must be heavily incorporated into modern enterprise systems to fully understand how information flows throughout an organisation and to make sense of that information.

## 1. Introduction

Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) is an enterprise-wide information system (IS) that has been used as a tool to effectively plan and manage all resources of an enterprise (Koh et al., 2009; Ketikidis et al., 2008; Jacobs and Weston, 2007; Loh and Koh. 2004) and aims to improve internal efficiency by integrating different business functions in an organisation (Tarn et al., 2002). ERP has been viewed as the most imperative information technology (IT) infrastructure in modern organisations (Park and Kusiak, 2005). Over the years, a number of real-life emerging business issues have led to additional modules in ERP system. Demand for integrated information systems and increased competitiveness in supply chains have made ERP vendors to develop additional modules of ERP such as Supply Chain Management (SCM), Supplier Relationship Management (SRM) and Customer Relationship Management (CRM) systems (Hendricks et al., 2007; Møller, 2005). These systems and further advances in technology such as electronic-business (e-Business) (Weston, 2003), allow firms to operate not only internally but to collaborate with external partners through an electronic medium (the internet).

The largest ERP vendors have recognised the above development of enterprise systems in the market, and started working on the next-phase in the enterprise system life-cycle, referred to as ERPII (Møller, 2005). The aim of ERPII is to pursue interorganisational collaboration, extending traditional ERP which offered intra-organisational process efficiency. Essentially, ER-PII offers a modularised ERP system, which is web-based and provides full collaboration in the supply chain (SC).

Inter-enterprise collaboration provides a number of mutual benefits and opportunities. There are of course a number of limitations to SC collaboration and certain conditions need to be in place before an organisation can commit to a collaborative enterprise system, such as ERPII. Due to the importance of traditional ERP systems in modern business, ERPII has created a wave of excitement and further research is required in a variety of areas to fully understand the potential.

ERPII actually involves four major perspectives; the full SC – suppliers, central SC enterprises (users) and customers, as well as ERP vendors/consultants. ERPII is still a relatively new concept and although the latest most advanced ERP systems may coincide with this ERPII vision, few organisations have a fully functional collaborative-enterprise system. Further research on ERPII would therefore be beneficial to a number of organisations spread across a variety of industries who are interested in investing in such a system. To expand on existing literature, organisations need to thoroughly understand the benefits and limitations of implementing ERPII, in a clear and concise manner. They also need to understand what defines a successful ERPI implementation, and as such what conditions are required to successfully implement an ERPl system.

The aim of this research is to capitalise on the opportunities presented by gaps in the existing literature on ERPII. This will require focusing on all perspectives involved within its implementation; vendors/consultants, suppliers, users and customers. The research aims to gather primary evidence from all four respondent types and compare and contrast these outlooks against existing literature where possible. To help supply chains understand the reasons to invest (or not invest) in an ERPI system, the research will highlight the contrasting views on the drivers and barriers of ERPII implementation, which refers to the benefits and impediments which supply chains face when implementing an ERPII system. Additionally, critical success factors (CSFs) for ERPII implementation will also be identified, which refers to the conditions and prerequisites which supply chains require for successful ERPII implementation. Incidentally, traditional ERP literature is flooded with research on driv ers/barriers and CSFs, hence a comparison of results will be very intriguing. Further emphasis will be based on the future development of ERPII, to determine the industry expectations on the next-generation enterprise system. From the four perspectives previously outlined, the objectives of this research are to: (i) examine the drivers and barriers of ERPII implemen tation in supply chains, (ii) identify the critical success factors (CSFs) for ERPII implementation in supply chains, and (iii) investigate the future development of enterprise systems.

## 2. Background for the research

In the 1970s, manufacturing processes were supported by Material Requirement Planning (MRP), which produced a set of time-phased requirements for subassemblies, components, materials planning, and procurement (Yen et al., 2002). The list of requirements generated the Master Production Schedule (MPS) which was pivotal to the material planning and control process (Chen, 2001). In the 1980s, MRP expanded to Manufacturing Resource Planning (MRP II), which planned and controlled nearly all of a firm’s resources, specifically production, marketing and finance (Jacobs and Weston, 2007). However, MRP II production used the same MRP planned order release logic to produce almost identical outputs (Loh and Koh, 2004). In the 1990s, technology enabled MRP IL to incorporate all the resources of an enterprise (Umble et al. 2003) such as mate: rial planning, product design, HR, finance, logistics and capacity planning to name a few. This was as such referred to as Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP). There are many articles that deal with different approaches for ERP implementation. Nandhakumar et al. (2005) study the ERP implementation process in a large multinational company by examining the emergence of its ERP system and endorsement of changes to the system along with the intentions and actions of the individuals involved in their company perspective. Boersma and Kingma (2005) present a case study of modifying an ERP system within a manufacturing company. Their ERP system integrates MRP and JIT for assembly lines. The main focus of this change is based on the mutual shaping of technology and organisational culture, in particular the virtualisation of the organisation.

ERP provided a number of benefits, such as the automation and integration of business processes to enable a reduction in head count and paper work, and to share common data throughout an organisation, which reduces the costs of maintaining legacy systems (Ketikidis et al., 2008; Nah et al., 2001). However, the introduction of ERPII and SC collaboration has provided scope for a number of additional benefits such as SCM, CRM and SRM which can further assist organisations in managing their resources, in an attempt to create competitive advantage (see Bayraktar et al., 2009; Peteraf, 1993) and ultimately drive shareholder value. As the term suggests. ERPI is a “transformation of ERP into the next-generation enterprise system" (Møller, 2005, p. 483). Bond et al. (2000) define ERPII as:

‘‘A business strategy and a set of industry-domain-specific applications that build customer and shareholder value by enabling and optimising enterprise and inter-enterprise, collaborative-operational and financial processes’’.

Weston (2003) who uses the term Extended Enterprise refers to collaborating enterprises, provides a much clearer definition by arguing ERPII ‘‘includes virtually everything related to front- and back-office system (ERP I) integration, plus all system functionality that is included within either CRM or SCM’’. Weston depicts the all-encompassing ‘ERPII Umbrella’, which highlights a vast number of acronyms relating to a variety of existing enterprise systems, tools and techniques which collectively represent the ERPII vision. Finally, Møller (2005) summarises ERPII as componentised ERP, e-business and collaboration in the supply chain.

Although these definitions are slightly inconsistent, what is consistent is the objective of ERPII to surpass intraorganisational ERP systems, to integrate full supply chains by inter-organisation collaboration, referred to as Collaborative Commerce (c-commerce). Nowadays, companies are trying to ‘‘re-invent their businesses and maintain their competitive advantage through collaboration’’ (Bititci et al., 2004a,b). Zrimsek (2003), again from the GRG, argues the context of an enterprise has to be re-evaluated in order to accommodate the ERPII vision. Zrimsek argues the SC should be viewed as a collaboration of ‘virtual enterprises’, which when combined is responsible for the production of products/services, rather than independent organisations working in isolation, often referred to as the extended enterprise.

Although originated by the GRG in 2000, Møller (2005) argues there has been little research conducted on this ERPII vision. As a result, Møller proposed a conceptual framework for ERPII, in an endeavour to summarise the numerous thoughts and insights into the ERPII vision. Aside from the umbrella theory defined by Weston (2003), Møller’s conceptual framework is the first of its kind to attempt to plug together components which when combined form a proposed ERPII system. The framework is illustrated in Fig. 1, with each component/layer defined in Table 1. The framework is merely a generalised model based on retrospective analysis of previous enterprise systems and industry requirements. However, it is useful in the sense it is the first conclusive framework available in the literature, beyond Weston (2003). The foundation and process layers provide the core ERP web-based system. The analytical layer comprises a number of components which enhance and extend central ERP functions, such as SCM, CRM, SRM, PLM and Corporate Performance Measurement (CPM) systems. The majority of these systems are currently available as ‘bolt-on’ systems to existing ERP packages. More importantly, the model defines an e-business layer to communicate and integrate with external actors – referred to as the ‘‘portal of ERPII’’ (Møller, 2005).

The most advanced enterprise systems today appear to incorporate the majority of functionality associated with ERPII, as the new ERP philosophy of e-business and modularised packages has been gradually integrated into legacy systems (Møller, 2005, p. 488). The largest ERP vendors are keen to capitalise on the ERPII vision, including SAP’s Business Suite (SAP UK), and Oracle's e-Business Suite (Oracle UK). Both of these systems offer a range of componentised modules which almost coincides with Møller’s (2005) framework. However, it is debatable whether these latest ERP systems currently offer the full e-business layer capabilities envision by Bond et al. (2000) and Zrimsek (2003). Despite these latest systems, many believe ERPII is not just about making use of advanced technology. Fornadel (2003) suggested that ERPII is not about newer better, flashier technology, but about extracting real, tangible business benefits from ERP software. Following a number of unsuccessful ERP/SCM/CRM implementations, the industry has now seen a pattern of ERP re-implementations and this trend has now turned to ERPII (Sammon and Adam, 2005).

Before continuing, it is worth clarifying a number of similar terms often associated with enterprise systems and more importantly the e-business layer. Electronic-commerce (e-commerce) is often related to the buying and selling or transaction-making activities of business, using an electronic medium such as the internet (Kownslar, 2002). Similarly, the use of the internet that promotes business collaboration in the SC is known as c-commerce (Kownslar, 2002). The term e-business is often associated with c-commerce and is another umbrella term used to define electronic business collaboration in the SC. E-business and c-commerce will hence be used interchangeably. Cadili and Whitley (2005) explore the interpretative of ERP systems through a case study of a project to implement a hosted system for the central accounting department of a large multinational. Srivardhana and Pawlowski (2007) examine the relationship between ERP systems and innovation from a knowledge-based perspective. They have developed a theoretical framework to model the relationships between ERP-related knowledge impacts and potential/realised absorptive capacity for business process innovation. These articles suggest that the implementation of ERP should support decision making and business process innovation.

## 3. Critical review of research objectives

In this section, the research objectives are discussed with reference to the existing literature on ERPI and ERPII. The underlying framework for the qualitative analysis is based on the analysis of the research objectives of existing literature, viz., ERPI and ERPII drivers and barriers for the implementation for ERPII, critical success factors for the ERPII implementation, and future trends of ERP.

## 3.1. Drivers and barriers of ERPII implementation

Whilst not explicitly outlined as ERPII drivers and barriers, analytical (SCM/CRM/CPM) and e-business application layer benefits and impediments, may represent them. Ndede-Amadi (2004) argues that the biggest driver behind ERPII implementation is the need to transform to e-business, to improve internal efficiency by extending processes outside a firm. Another key driver for ERPII is the capability to see the impact of decisions and activities in real-time (Koh et al., 2006; Møller, 2005; Weston, 2003). By extending processes to collaborating partners, management will be able to measure performance and make real-time strategic decisions accordingly (Zrimsek, 2003). ERPII also presents a number of operational benefits compared to traditional ERP, such as faster cycle times and reduced inventory levels which enables even lower operating costs (Ndede-Amadi, 2004). Loh et al. (2006) argue EEA/ERPII will enable more automation of operations, less day-to-day management of the supply chain, and even the ability to manage exceptions within the enterprise system. Another key value-adding activity of ERPII is improved customer responsiveness (Koh et al., 2008; Weston, 2003). To summarise the main drivers from existing ERPII literature, Loh et al. (2006) indicates that EEA are expected to increase SC information velocity, provide greater product customisation, and also further reduce the costs of doing business.

The main barriers of ERPII can be encapsulated in two distinct categories; issues relating to technological infrastructure, and more importantly general business issues spanning the extended enterprise (Weston, 2003). Consequently, ‘soft’ ERPII business issues are compounded more so from its predecessor.

Due to its collaborative nature, managing people, organisational inertia and change management are even more critical to ERPII implementation (Koh et al., 2008; Møller, 2005; Weston, 2003). Due to the complex nature and extended organisational boundaries of ERPII, social issues from employee resistance to change will become even more problematic when involving external parties. Ash and Burn (2003) argue before contemplating e-business solutions, conflicts between the needs of the organisation and people, have to be addressed and balanced. For such enterprise-wide change to take place, executive commitment, leadership and cultural readiness to accept change is vital for successful e-business change (p.385). In an ERPII context, Weston (2003) and Loh et al. (2006) make reference to issues with collaborative BPR, or Business Process Management (BPM), as a key barrier to implementation. Further business barriers arise from concerns with trust and security, which corresponds with collaborative enterprise literature on trust and information transparency by Akkermans et al. (2004).

ERPII also faces a number of technological barriers to overcome. Each enterprise within a SC often has a number of different IS applications which communicate with one another. For collaboration to take place to enable firms to initiate c-commerce, disparate systems need to be able to freely communicate with one another. The technology required for such unhindered flow of information between disparate systems (interoperability), is known as EAI (Erasala et al., 2003).

Whilst ERPII literature appears to highlight a variety of ERPII drivers and barriers, existing literature does not appear to depict many conflicts between authors, probably due to the infancy of the research. The first research proposition suggests each perspective company will have their own drivers and barriers of ERPII implementation, and following the trend of existing literature, will be largely in favour of the benefits of ERPII. Consequently, the customer would be able to track orders in real-time, automate ordering and documentation and even drive hard bargains via electronic supplier auctions/relations. The research will endeavour to evaluate this proposition further.

## 3.2. Critical success factors for ERPII implementation

There is currently little literature on CSFs for ERPII implementation. Although not explicitly stated as CSFs, nor discussed in great detail, Weston (2003) lists resistance to change, local culture issues, training, testing, and good project management as key to ERPII implementation. Similarly, Loh et al. (2006) expand on this list and indicate BPR/BPM as important for ERPII implementation. However, there is a plethora of research on CSFs for traditional ERP, which may of course apply to ERPII.

It is self-explanatory that many CSEs for traditional ERP will no doubt be carried forward for ERPIL. However, there are even mixed outlooks about the actual CSFs for traditional ERP. As such there are discrepancies over the number of CSFs for ERP, such as Al-Mashari et al. (2003) (12 CSFs), Nah et al. (2001) (11), Umble et al. (2003) (8) and finally Akkermans and van Helden (2002) (10) who base their study on CSFs from Somers and Nelson (2001). Despite the contrast in CSFs, there is a common theme among the majority of these identified lists, many of which use dissimilar names to represent similar CSFs. Strong communication, top management support, data accuracy, BPR and minimum software customisation, legacy systems management and performance evaluation are among some of the most common CSFs highlighted by the above authors.

To aid the construction of theory for ERPI CSEs, literature involving CSEs for SCM and CRM is crucial as these are two of the main components of ERPII (Weston, 2003). Chen and Paulraj (2002) for example, provide a theoretical framework of critical SCM elements, including communication, supplier base reduction, long-term relationships, supplier selection and trust and commitment. Additionally Power et al. (2001) highlight agile SC CSFs, to respond quickly to changes in demand of volume and variety. However, there is less literature available on CSFs for CRM. Although, Croteau (2003) argues the main elements for CRM are top management support and strong knowledge management capabilities, which is reinforced by Chen and Popovich (2003).

It is evident there are mixed outlooks between CSFs and different types of enterprise systems, which may become more apparent when compared in an ERPII context. This may result in a whole new set of ERPII CSFs, relevant to collaboration in the extended enterprise. However, these outlooks may become more divided by comparing results between the four research perspectives. The research will analyse the proposition that each perspective will produce group-specific lists of CSFs. Additionally the research proposes that a high number of CSFs for ERPII will be brought forward from ERP. Finally, with its reliance on collaboration, it seems appropriate to suggest CSFs relating to managing collaborative relationships are going to be vital for ERPII implementation, which will be analysed further.

## 3.3. Future trends

The final objective, the future of next-generation enterprise systems, again has limited literature available. Aside from a holistic view of greater SC collaboration, there are a variety of authors suggest that greater interoperability of dissimilar enterprise systems will be vital to future development (Loh et al., 2006; Helo and Szekely, 2005; Weston, 2003). This will involve further development of web-service technology and EAI to enable systematic and standardised data exchange between disparate systems (Loh et al., 2006). However, this is essential for ERPII, so is not very forward looking. Helo and Szekely (2005) argue there will be the possibility of ERPII packages readily available for different industries. They also make a number of other innovative suggestions such as voice recognised Warehouse Management Systems (WMS), SCEM, and fur ther use of Radio Frequency Identification (RFID) technology within logistics.

## 4. Research methodology

ERPII is a relatively new topic and little research currently exists, hence a grounded theory methodology was deployed in this study. Grounded theory methodology seeks to identify patterns from inductive approach and takes an interpretive view from actuality based on empirical research (Glaser, 1992). As a result, potential new theory emerges. Saunders et al. (2007) argues new research topics with little literature are more suited to an inductive approach using qualitative research. The first objective focuses on drivers/barriers where existing literature is limited to vendor and user perspectives, with little research on suppliers and none on customer perspectives. Therefore, an inductive approach was deemed more suitable.

The second objective focuses on CSFs for ERPII implementation where to date there is currently no existing literature. Again, an inductive approach appeared most appropriate to gather data and attempt to theorise following analysis of this data. The downside to an inductive approach is the length of time it takes to analyse data and theorise (Saunders et al., 2007). However, with adequate planning and a rigorous stakeholder contact phase, this issue has been thwarted.

Robson (2002) highlights that research results in either descriptive, exploratory or explanatory answers, or a combination. This research used a combination of descriptive and exploratory study. Descriptive research was applied to the first objective as greater detail and clarity was applied to the existing research on vendor/user/supplier perspectives. Exploratory research is often used when little research currently exists in an attempt to ‘‘shed new light’’ on a problem (Robson, 2002). Therefore, exploratory research was applied to the customer perspectives of the first objective, as well as applying to the second objective on CSFs. This entailed extending existing CSF literature on traditional ERP. Likewise, the third objective on the future of ERPII used this approach to explore the development of future enterprise systems.

There are three main types of data collection associated with qualitative research; participant observations (ethnography), qualitative interviews and focus groups (Bryman and Bell, 2003). This research favoured qualitative interviews and more specifically semi-structured (non-standardised) interviews. The selection of qualitative interviews for data collection is quite an obvious one. In this research context qualitative interviews are the most direct technique to gather rich and detailed data from industry experts, compared to participation observations or focus groups. Observing behaviour is not feasible with the three proposed objectives. Although focus groups would provide a variety of outlooks from a group discussion, time and stakeholder availability constraints restrict the use of this method.

The semi-structured interviews were face-to-face to enable strong respondent relationships to be formed, to build rap port and ensure high quality data collection. Initially twelve interviews were targeted, consisting of three interviews for each perspective. However, twelve interviews were not attainable and only ten were arranged due to accessibility constraint. Following these ten interviews, it was considered that more than sufficient rich data had been collected to draw adequate conclusions, so further interviews were deemed not necessary. There were three interviews each for the vendor/consultant and user perspectives, and two for both suppliers and customers. The vendor/consultants were targeted first, to hopefully provide a somewhat biased outlook towards ERPII. Secondly, the users were targeted in an attempt to build a rapport and further develop contacts with their suppliers and customers. However, only two interviews were managed to be arranged for the suppliers and customers perspectives. Each of the interviewees was of a senior position directly involved in ERP within their respective company. The companies themselves (see Table 3) were mainly all global firms, renowned in the industry for their expertise. By targeting firms on a global scale, and interviewing individuals within a senior position, the data collection stage was given the best possible chance of obtaining reliable and interesting data. All the interviews were recorded using a Dictaphone to ensure the data was collected reliably and hence the data analysis was consistent (see Appendix A for the sample questions).

## 5. Results and analysis

In this section, the results obtained are analysed to identify the drivers and barriers for the ERPII implementation, critical success factors for ERP II implementation and future trends of ERP.

## 5.1. Drivers and barriers of ERPII implementation

## 5.1.1. Drivers

The overriding results from the four respondent perspectives are summarised in Table 5. Whilst the results clearly indicate four quite unique perspectives on the main drivers of ERPII implementation, it should be stressed that numerous individual outlooks often spanned different perspective categories. However, by using a qualitative grounded theory approach to analysis, the overriding themes became apparent

Vendors/consultants appeared to be more interested in the ERPII capability of a holistic view of the extended enterprise. This is achievable by having an increased seamless flow of information within the extended enterprise, which enables customers to determine demand more accurately and drive out forecasts to suppliers with greater accuracy and speed. The increased velocity of data coincides with Loh et al. (2006), and the ability to analyse real-time information is highlighted in greater detail by Zrimsek (2003). Essentially, real-time information or real-time available-to-promise (ATP) information (as one vendor/consultant referred to it as), is designed to be used at a strategic level for critical decision-making to make faster and more reliable decisions. This driver was also reinforced by a user, to quickly move critical information to managers with the relevant understanding to respond to this information. One vendor/consultant argued ERPII enables more leverage from a system, to provide more ‘‘artificial intelligence than hired intelligence’’, which corresponds with the business intelligence capabilities of the CPM module, illustrated by Geishecker and Rayner (2001). To summarise this perspective, a vendor/consultant stressed, ‘‘ERPII is all about better information, better planning and better deployment of resources’’.

Suppliers seemed to be more influenced by the ability to meet customer demand more accurately and to further reduce operating costs and improve operational efficiency. The ability to meet demand more accurately relates back to the increased velocity of information being circulated throughout the extended enterprise, championed by the vendor/consultant perspective. However, none of the suppliers highlighted ERPII’s ability to reduce the bull-whip effect, or how ERPII could reduce uncertainty by increasing information sharing. Additional operating cost savings by forming collaborative partnerships are also reinforced by Yu et al. (2001), although these benefits refer to SC partnerships, and not specifically extended enterprise benefits. Similarly, operational efficiency benefits are supported by Ndede-Amadi (2004). A supplier summarised ERPII drivers as ‘‘enabling minimal inventory, reducing operating costs and as such, support in maintaining a ‘pull’ model of planning and control’’, which also coincides with Ndede-Amadi (2004).

The user perspectives appeared to respond by focusing more directly on improving individual business performance, thus improving the overall performance of the SC, resulting in individual value creation. However, this ‘individualistic’ attitude opposes the collaborative vision set by Zrimsek (2003), where collaborating firms should behave as a ‘‘single enterprise to maximise the corporate goals of the extended enterprise, thus optimising the performance of each individual enterprise’ (Bititci et al., 2005:p.338). However, without additional research on a much larger sample, it would be wrong to generalise this individualistic view, and could simply be associated with the sample users’ perspective. A number of users made reference to focusing on value-creating activities, and ERPI providing the capability to have more control over an organisations value-stream (see Porter, 1985). Additionally, a user argued that by combining core competencies and capabilities, unique competencies could be created, which is again supported by Bititci et al. (2004a,b). An additional driver outlined was the ability to further enhance lean management of business processes, to improve their efficiency and reduce waste, which does not appear to be highlighted in existing ERPII literature. However, it could be argued that ERPII’s ability to support a pull planning and control model, highlighted by the supplier’s perspective, could contribute towards lean process optimisation.

Additionally, product innovation and customisation was seen to be a major driver to implementation, supported by Loh et al. (2006). ## 5.1.2. Barriers

Whereas analysis of the drivers of ERPII implementation presented four distinct perspectives (with minor discrepancies between them), there were no such overriding themes developed from the qualitative data collected on the barriers to ERPII implementation. Therefore, the results have not been partitioned by the four research perspectives. However, it was apparent that the barriers fell into two separate categories; barriers which were attributed to traditional ERP implementation and compounded in ERPII implementation, and barriers which were unique or more distinctive to ERPII specific implementation. These results confirm Koh et al. (2008) findings that ERPII barriers increase in number and intensity from its ERP predecessor. The results have been tabulated accordingly in Table 6

5.1.2.1. Barriers of ERP and ERPII implementation. A common barrier highlighted by all four perspectives was organisational inertia and the inability of employees to accept or embrace change. In particular, vendors/consultants and users perspectives stressed that people would without doubt be the largest barrier to ERPII, which is carried forward from ERP. This reinforces the outlooks by Weston (2003) that ERPIl would be impacted by more general business issues than technological obstacles. Resistance to change and embracing change were some of the most collective responses, as well as enforcing the reasons for change, promoting the change to employees and hence ensuring they understand the benefits which derive from the change.

Associated with inertia and change, is the difficulty in maintaining and improving business processes. Therefore a number of suppliers and users indicated that BPR is critical to ERPII, especially when involving external parties, which supports Loh et al. (2006). However, a vendor/consultant opposed this view and argued ERP or ERPII is usually introduced as a ‘‘catalyst to change" Therefore, adopting different business processes is “part and parcel of an implementation”, it is not “an impediment, merely a challenge whilst on the project’’. Another vendor/consultant backed this argument and also referred back to the organisational inertia issue that many clients did not want to accept change had to take place.

A variety of responses were also collected involving the cost of ERPII implementation. Five specific interviewees (one supplier, two users, and both customers) were quick to mention high costs as a major barrier, as a business has to invest many resources to manage the project (finance, time and staff). Surprisingly, the vendors/consultants reinforced this barrier, and stressed that the main business had to be run whilst supporting the implementation. A vendor/consultant argued the “cost of finding the right partner, the process of selecting the right partner/software, and finding the resources to support the implementation’’ are major cost barriers. Similarly, a vendor/consultant argued additional ‘fringe costs’ are ignored from implementation costs, such as change management, training and getting user consent. Most of these cost issues relate to traditional ERP implementation (Koh et al., 2009). ERPII specific costs were not perceived as great, as ERP is required as a platform for ERPII so the initial costs of setting up ERPII are already dealt with. However, further costs to add modular enhancements to this ERP platform to form an ERPII system were recognised, which coincides with Beatty and Williams (2006).

5.1.2.2. Barriers of ERPII specific implementation. A vendor/consultant argued that many modern firms simply do not have the knowledge of their own extended enterprise and its benefits out of an ERPII system. The respondent was actually very critical of the positioning of a number of ‘leading’ global enterprises, arguing ‘‘although the theory is in place for ERPII and the extended enterprise, the industry is not in a position to respond to it at this stage’’.

Although often associated with traditional ERP barriers data accuracy and quality of information have been highlighted in more detail within the section, due to the complexity of data spanning different enterprises. Although the majority of respondents highlight data accuracy and information quality as a major barrier, there were discrepancies in their reasoning. A supplier argued that collaboration requires external data consistency between collaborative partners, which would be difficult to maintain despite data cleansing cycles. More importantly, a vendor/consultant claimed that current ERP transactional data is poor in quality and consistency, therefore ‘‘data needs to be standardised in some form between collaborative boundaries for ERPII success’’. A number of additional respondents also commented on the issue of adhering to data standards as a barrier to ERPII. Many respondents were unsure where the ERP industry was heading in terms of data standards development. However, apart from maintaining data consistency, technological barriers and more specifically interoperability issues with external connectivity were generally perceived as less of an issue, as it is assumed ERPII technology (EAI/web services) would handle these problems, opposing existing literature such as Weston (2003) and Loh et al. (2006) who argue connectivity is a major limitation.

Barriers associated with collaborative relationships were also perceived as fundamental. Four interviewees, including one vendor/consultant, one supplier and two users, were keen to highlight that partners often operate under conflicting key performance indicators (KPIs) and therefore have different objectives, which could negatively influence implementation and the performance of EPRII. A vendor/consultant argued that collaboration is only feasible with predefined objectives, although this will require enterprises ‘‘letting their strategic guard down’’ to embrace collaboration, something which many firms will simply not be willing to accept, Similarly, Simatupang et al. (2002) argue collaborators must embrace necessary change, and key initiators need to overcome the layers of resistance. Three interviewees (one vendor/consultant and two users) also pointed out that a clash in cultures between two enterprises could impede ERPII implementation, although one particular customer disregarded this notion insisting collaboration takes place at the business unit level not a corporate level, which coincides with Bititci et al. (2005, p. 342), and as such conflicts can usually be resolved departmentally, whether internall or in an extended context.

## 5.1.3. Summary

The drivers and barriers of ERPI implementation identified from this research are summarised in Table 7, and have been categorised using Shang and Seddon’s (2000) structure of traditional ERP benefits. It must be noted that research takes an inductive approach, and attempts to ground new theory from empirical research. Hence prior conception of deductive thinking was not deployed, rationalising the blank cells in Table 7. The authors did not attempt to fit reality to theory or vice versa, but reporting reality against theory.

The results provide a clear relationship between the drivers of ERPII and the papers four research perspectives. Each perspective appeared to have a unique set of needs and a general understanding of how ERPII could address these needs. The majority of these drivers appear to be strategically or operationally oriented, which opposes Koh et al. (2008) who found numerous drivers spanning all categories, including technological and organisational drivers. However, the results do not provide any clear relationship between the barriers of ERPII and the research perspectives, instead indicating a variety of barriers which concerns all four perspectives. These findings oppose proposition 1 (P1), which suggested each perspective would display unique drivers and barriers for implementation of ERPII. In hindsight, this is hardly a surprising result as there are clearly a number of ERPII drivers which each perspective are interested in, and a number of barriers which ultimately affect all perspectives in some way or another, many of which are carried forward from traditional ERP.

Although the results indicate a relatively similar number of ERPII drivers to barriers, the general impression from the data collection phase was a sense of negativity associated with ERPII. Although this may oppose many vendor/consultant outlooks, for obvious reasons, at this moment in time there appeared to be greater emphasis towards the barriers outweighing the drivers of ERPII implementation, thus opposing proposition 2 (P2), which suggested the drivers would outweigh the barriers. Even a vendor/consultant provided a very cautious approach to ERPII, arguing the industry is still not ready for its full capabilities. This finding opposes the majority of existing ERPII research which is largely favourable of the perceived ERPII benefits (drivers), compared to the impediments (barriers) of implementation (Loh et al., 2006; Weston, 2003; Bond et al., 2000). This negativity could be largely due to the majority of test cases not being in a position to invest in full ERPII implementation at this moment in time, even though a number of them are leading firms. However, their global status would suggest they would be ideal candidates for such a collaborative system. A user was very sceptical of the complexity of ERPII and argued ‘‘if we still struggle to integrate ERP with HR, finance and operations, then a more complex collaborative system currently appears too advanced for modern business’’.

## 5.2.1. ERP/ERPII CSFs

Without achieving the CSFs in Table 8, an ERPII implementation simply is not feasible, which implies an efficient ERP system is a prerequisite or CSF for ERPII itself. When assessing the practicality of ERPII investment, a core ERP system should form part of the business case for investment, and should be critical in the chartering phase of implementation. The results confirm proposition 4 (P4) which suggested a high number of CSFs from ERP will be carried forward for ERPII.

Although the results merely build upon traditional ERP CSFs, they do present two additional significant findings. Secondly, in the onward and upward phase of implementation, many respondents appeared sceptical of current extended enterprise performance monitoring and evaluation measures which were deemed vital for ERPII. Corporate performance in ERPII is controlled by the CPM module within the analytical application layer.

## 5.2.2. ERPII specific CSFs

CSFs directly associated with ERPII implementation are illustrated in Table 9. The results again have been categorised into relevant implementation life-cycle phases, exercised by Markus and Tanis (2000). The results indicate a high concentration of CSFs relating to the ‘chartering’ phase of implementation. This implies that enterprises willing to invest in an ERPII system have a number of additional decisions to overcome in defining the business case and constraints to implementation. However, this is an obvious observation considering the scale and complexity of such a system.

A variety of respondents outlined internal operational efficiency as a CSF to implement ERPII. Four respondents, including two vendor/consultants, one user and one customer, insist that a firm needs to be operationally (and strategically) efficient in controlling supply and demand, and have the capabilities and flexibility to optimise lean operations and core competencies to adapt to an ERPII system.

A vital CSF supported by all respondents was a thorough understanding of the extended enterprise, and understanding the value-adding processes of their individual enterprise and their collaborating partners. By understanding the strengths and weaknesses of an extended enterprise, collaborating members are in a position to exploit additional value-creating opportunities.

The remaining CSFs identified in the chartering phase of ERPII implementation all relate to the relationships between members of an extended enterprise. Consequently, these CSFs may be impacted by a firm’s existing partner relationships, which are traditionally affected by meeting delivery targets and adhering to payment periods. A supplier added ‘‘if adequate forecasts are provided and payments are consistently on time, then more collaboration will be welcomed’’. The respondents however identified a number of specific CSFs associated with relationships between extended enterprise partners, which confirms proposition 5 (P5). One of these CSFs was sharing common partner goals. In order for enterprises to collaborate effectively, they have to work in unison to meet similar objectives. However, objectives may need to be realigned (Bititci et al., 2005) and hence communication between enterprises is vital, especially if objectives are unrealistic between partners. Likewise, similar partner priorities are essential to an ERPII project. A vendor/consultant argued ‘‘collaborative procedures require the same commitment and priority levels from both parties’’.

Partner support was also seen to be a key CSF, which could be a product of both similar partner priorities and common partner goals, and could also form part of the partner contribution assessment. Many respondents highlighted that strategic relationships require mutual support, including support in the implementation procedure, resolving problems spanning across operating boundaries, and in providing the necessary sales and consumption information to assist in supply and demand operations. However, the level of information shared between enterprises and the level of commitment can alter the level of trust within an extended enterprise. A user claimed the ‘‘emotions and perceptions of previous supplier/customer relationships have to be placed to one side for ERPII to work’’. When sharing information between partners, security is a major issue and as such, high levels of equal trust needs to be in place (Akkermans et al., 2004). Similarly, Bstieler (2006) found a positive relationship between trust and collaborative relationships in new product development.

To conclude the discussion surrounding partner relationships in the chartering phase, a comment emerged early in the data collection phase which was supported by the remaining interviewees, to form ‘lean’ partner relationships. This means relationships should be simple with equal high-trust focused on delivering equal benefits for all parties involved. A customer insisted that ‘‘ERPII may bring about a new set of management abilities, requiring the ability to form ‘lean’ relationships between trading partners, resulting in a win–win–win situation for both collaborating parties and end customer’’. This idea was reciprocated by a vendor/consultant who believed that ERPII would provide a distinct set of new challenges for vendors/consultants, who will be stretched to deliver a revolutionary new approach to ERPII implementation, based on supporting lean relationships between collaborating partners. This suggests a revision of the implementation life-cycle by Markus and Tanis (2000).

The results highlight no CSEs specifically for ERPI in the project phase of implementation. However. there were additional CSFs in the shakedown phase of ERPII implementation. The most popular of these CSFs identified was relationship change management. The difference between this and the change management programme CSF (4.2.1) is that change management in a direct ERPII context takes place at the trading partner environment. Similarly, Ndede-Amadi (2004) argues that ‘‘businesses must overcome process and cultural changes associated with spanning the corporate firewall’ (p. 193). A user highlighted that ‘‘the ability to impose change is removed’’ due to the difficulty in enforcing change upon collaborative partners. Therefore, an additional CSF was derived, partner culture similarity. A vendor/consultant revealed that ‘‘high maintenance relationships are not suited to ERPII’’, hence culture similarity would be beneficial to ERPII implementation. However, this CSF has been classified in the chartering phase, rather than the shakedown phase, as it should be a prerequisite for collaborating enterprises to acknowledge before implementation begins. However, a user argued that conflicts in culture could easily be confronted during implementation, so it could also be placed in the shakedown phase after all.

Finally in the shakedown phase, a common theme to combat barriers relating to data accuracy emerged as a CSF for data standard consistency. A vendor/consultant argued ‘‘standards are in place for accounting, but there appears to be difficultly in applying standards to supply chains’’. The majority of interviewees (all three vendor/consultants, one supplier, two users and one customer) stated that to achieve the ‘‘next-level’’ of information which ERPII could potentially provide (real-time information) then more consistent and accurate data is required. Therefore data standards were deemed necessary, partic ularly from a vendors/consultants perspective to uphold data integrity.

## 5.2.3. Summary

The list of CSFs for ERPII implementation are summarised in Table 10, and have been categorised by four implementation phases.

The results did not find any relationships between ERPII CSFs and the four research perspectives. This underlines the difficulty in distinguishing ERPII CSFs at this early stage in its product lifecycle. If firms are not in a position to invest in ERPII, then identifying CSFs associated with specific perspectives is even more challenging. However, the research was successful in identifying numerous CSFs which are the conditions required by an organisation for successful implementation of ERPII.

## 5.3. Future trends

Finally, the third research objective provides scope for discussion surrounding the current version of ERP/ERPII and the contrasting outlooks about these systems. Additionally, future ideas about the evolution of enterprise systems are discussed, and a final summary is provided to conclude the results.

As expected, the results provided two quite different attitudes towards ERPII. The vendors/consultants were generally in favour of ERPII and its capabilities, providing a biased outlook. In contrast, the remaining perspectives provided a quite cautious approach to the development of ERPII, which confirms proposition 6 (P6), that there are mixed outlooks between user requirements and vendor perceptions of requirements of future enterprise systems. A user argued from an IT perspective, ERPII is a great solution, however from a business perspective it falls short. Similarly, a customer claimed ERPII has the potential to manage the extended value-chain, but very few currently have the vision or resources they need to be fully integrated along the full value-chain. A supplier supported these arguments and insisted they could see the value with ERPII, but it simply does not suit their strategy. One user was even more critical claiming, they could not see the demand for ERPII, let alone beyond EPR II. Similarly, a user highlighted it was difficult to imagine how ERPII will evolve further. Despite this negative approach there were a number of useful outlooks how enterprise systems may evolve further, although it should be stressed that the majority of these originate from vendors/consultants. However, with their experience in the industry, they are probably best positioned to provide an interesting and more importantly, reliable insight.

A few respondents (two vendor/consultants and one supplier) insisted further web-based collaboration will be the key to the future, which reinforces work by Weston (2003). Similarly, a vendor argued there would be further collaboration linked to more traditional core financial and inventory modules. There were also some additional comments regarding further levels of integration and improved communication in a real-time seamless manner. A vendor/consultant associated future integration metaphorically with the simplicity of ‘‘Bluetooth’’ connectivity, with small development times and lower costs of integration. Similarly, another vendor/consultant argued their will be a need for a ‘‘plug-and-play environment’’ between extended enterprises.

Perhaps the most common theme amongst respondents was the idea of further business intelligence (BI) to support enterprise systems such as ERPII. A vendor/consultant argued there would be more BI and analytical tools which sit on top of the ERP platform. Another vendor/consultant claimed this would lead to ‘‘grid computing for business’’. Likewise, a user highlighted that the future is in information management and not IT systems, about understanding how information flows around an organisation. A supplier predicted a thin topography which sits above an ERP system and keeps the data organised. Similarly, a vendor/consultant claimed business logic will maintain data consistency. Other respondents were more detailed in their outlooks, with a vendor/consultant claiming the future will see ‘‘a concept of best-in-breed with a consistent open architecture feeding into a very thin data warehouse (DW) that is able to maintain the integrity of core business information and manage it effectively’’. A customer reinforced this idea and claimed that more tailored best-in-breed pack ages will be prominent.

Beyond ERPII, there will be further development of BI systems incorporated into mainstream enterprise systems. Flexible, lightweight, plug-and-play applications will revolutionise collaborative enterprises. The future of cost reductions will be more user oriented systems, designed for user operability. Backend systems will just sit there as big data repositories and a new thin veneer of software will sit on the desktop which integrates all back end systems and allows the end-user to become more functional and efficient.

## 6. Conclusions

ERPII has been regarded as the next-phase in the enterprise system lifecycle, developed from the acquisition of a variety of legacy enterprise applications and further advances in technology. Modern business is steadily becoming aware of how collaborating business processes can result in mutual benefits, spread throughout a supply chain. ERPII is the proposed system to encapsulate these benefits to drive out additional value from an enterprise, and as such the extended enterprise.

There is no doubt its technological prowess to support e-business and promote collaboration within the extended enterprise. However, obstacles associated with traditional ERP are compounded in ERPII, which additionally suffers from limitations spanning the corporate firewall.

This paper has set out to examine in greater detail the drivers and barriers of ERPII implementation, and to identify CSFs for ERPII implementation. The research also provides a discussion surrounding the industries outlook on ERPII and possible trends for the future of enterprise systems. Where possible, these three objectives were analysed against four perspectives directly involved with ERPII: vendors/consultants, suppliers, central SC enterprises (users) and customers.

The research has also identified a number of CSFs required for ERPII implementation, although no unique relationships could be found between the research perspectives and these identified CSFs. The list of new CSFs suggests that if firms cannot meet these basic criteria, an ERPII implementation will struggle to be successful. Although many CSFs are brought forward from traditional ERP, CSFs specifically for ERPII appear to be associated with the early stages of implementation (chartering phase), and the vast majority relate to creating and maintaining external partner relationships. Forming lean relationships and ensuring strategic and operational partner synergy is as such a key component of ERPII success. Without mutual partner contribution and involvement, the benefits presented by the extended enterprise do not appear viable.

To conclude, if managing the extended enterprise is the solution to future business success, the question remains whether or not ERPII is the solution to the extended enterprise. If ERPII is the solution, then managing the strategic and operational synergy between collaborating enterprises will be critical to its success.

## 6.1. Limitations

The research builds on the limited existing literature directly affiliated with ERPII. Without an extensive academic background, completing a thorough literature review and analysing results against existing literature is challenging. However, to counteract this problem, the research endeavoured to incorporate related literature on collaborative networks and the extended enterprise where possible, although recent journals on this new subject area (e.g. 2006 and 2007) were limited.

One of the major limitations to qualitative research is a generalisation of results (Saunders et al., 2007). Therefore, due to the number of interviews carried out for each perspective, it could be argued the results are too generalised. Ideally, more interviews would have been conducted, but time and availability constraints limited the number of interviews. However, in response, the majority of organisations interviewed were leading global firms, all of which have experience in ERP, so their collective outlooks should be representative of the ERP industry. Further limitations can be raised regarding the suitability of organisations interviewed. All of firms interviewed were involved with ERP systems, although none of them were at a position to invest in an ERPII-type system. More reliable data could have been collected by focusing on firms more willing to invest in ERPII. However, this may again have resulted in even more biased results, in favour of ERPII. Similarly, distinguishing which firms are currently in the process of adopting ERPII is unfeasible considering the authors academic position.

An additional limitation could be highlighted regarding the industries of the interviewed organisations. A high number of these organisations were associated with manufacturing within the transport industry, simply because of availability issues. It could be argued the industry sample is too narrow. ERPII may be more suited to certain industries, such as fast moving goods industries to gain maximum velocity of data through an ERPII system, or in contrast, industries with longer lead times to analyse system data in more detail. Further research is clearly needed in more diverse industries.

The results were grouped into four research perspectives used throughout the research. However, it could be argued the vendor/consultant group could be split into two separate perspectives with their own thoughts and outlooks. Finally, CSF results were categorised using existing implementation phases from traditional ERP literature, which assumes ERPII imple mentation stages are equivalent to its ERP predecessor. However, due to its collaborative nature, an innovative implementation life-cycle may have to be devised to coincide with ERPII.

## 6.2. Future research direction

When considering investment in an ERPII system, organisations need to consider more than simply the drivers and barriers of ERPII implementation. This research has identified CSFs which provides a foundation of prerequisites necessary for implementation. Assessing these CSFs against the competencies of an organisation are critical in forming the business case for such a system. However, further analysis, internally and externally, is required before fully committing to the investment process.

This research has identified a general lack of understanding of the extended enterprise. For organisations to adopt a collaborative nature, the implications of the extended enterprise have to be fully explored. There clearly appears to be confusion over the performance measures of the extended enterprise, Without clear tangible performance measurements, convincing a board to invest in such a resourceful project is unfeasible. This research suggests creating and maintaining collaborative partner relationships are critical to the success of EPRII. Therefore, collaboration relies on ‘soft’ business practice between partners, which suggests collaborative performance measurements may have to quantify the unquantifiable. However, there is little literature, if any, which studies extended enterprise performance measurements and its relationship with ERPII. There is clear scope for additional research in this area and to assess the functionality of the CPM module within the ERPII analytical layer. In fact, extended enterprise literature in general needs to specialise more formally with ERPII, to provide a more direct solution, rather than conducting research as two separate entities.

For ERPII to develop further, research needs to focus on developing lean relationships between collaborating partners. Similarly, if striving for strategic and operational partner synergy is vital for ERPII, then additional research needs to focus on BPM spanning the boundaries of the extended enterprise. A plethora of authors have focused on BPR/BPM for traditional ERP, however there remains little research tailored for business processes spanning the extended enterprise. Likewise, there is also scope for additional research on change management strategies between collaborating enterprises. Existing literature has at least highlighted that a ‘big-bang’ approach to ERPII implementation will not suffice and modular upgrades are required to progress from ERP to ERPI (Loh et al 2006: Bond et al 2000) However new research needs to clarify how to go about making these modular enhancements, and to clarify how to implement an agile development process for ERPII, which could build on existing work by Beatty and Williams (2006). Vendors and consultants need to be targeted to compare and contrast their approaches in detail. Similarly, a development plan needs to be outlined to highlight the unique implementation phases which are critical to ERPII, which could be combined with the CSFs identified in this research to provide a more complete list of CSFs. Finally, this research has provided scope for additiona research to measure the effect of the drivers and barriers of ERPII implementation, and more importantly the CSFs fo implementation.

Despite the suggestions for additional research, perhaps the most influential factor to entice organisations to implement ERPII is to learn from examples. It is human nature to learn from mistakes, which traditional ERP investment has developed from. In today’s competitive environment though, a project such as ERPII is too costly to make mistakes and this research indicates that the barriers are too intense and the consequences too high if mistakes are made. However, by following the CSFs identified in this research, an organisation can greatly minimise the likelihood of mistakes and strive for successful implementation. ERPII clearly requires some form of longitudinal research on several case studies before additional organisations will be willing to commit to its implementation. These studies need to be applied to a variety of industries involving a variety of organisations, and incorporating the two largest vendors (SAP/Oracle). Only then will the capabilities of the extended enterprise and in particular ERPII, be realised. Therefore, additional respondent-types also need to be investigated to extend the existing literature, thus including customers’ perspectives as well.
