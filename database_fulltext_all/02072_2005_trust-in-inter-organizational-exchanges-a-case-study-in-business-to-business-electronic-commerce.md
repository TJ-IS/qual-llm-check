# Trust in inter-organizational exchanges: a case study in business to business electronic commerce

Pauline Ratnasingam

Harmon School of Business Administration, Department of Computer Information Systems, Central Missouri State University, 301A Dockery, Warrensburg, MO 64093, USA

Received 1 December 2002; accepted 1 December 2003 Available online 27 April 2004

## Abstract

Given the uncertainties of online transactions, e-commerce encourages the creation of institutional structures for online exchange relationships. Building upon the notion of institutional trust, this research examines how institutional structures develop through governance mechanisms leading to technology trust derived from structural assurances for online dyadic relationships. Technology trust is defined as the subjective probability by which organizations believe that the underlying technology infrastructure is capable of facilitating transactions according to their confident expectations. Using a cognitive process framework, we discuss four perspectives of trust, namely technological, economic, behavioral, and organizational perspectives, to show how technology trust evolves into relationship trust. We examine the impact of technology trust within an inter-organizational dyad between Cisco and Compaq. The findings posit that technology trust contributes and evolves into relationship trust. The study provides evidence on how specific structural assurances in Cisco Connection Online help to build initial online technology trust in B2B marketplaces that in turn promotes relationship trust and builds trustworthy e-commerce relationships. The paper discusses theoretical and managerial implications of this study, while proposing suggestions for future research.

Keywords: Institutional structures; Business-to-business e-commerce; Technology trust; Inter-organizational trust

## 1. Introduction

The Internet, a rapidly expanding global computer and communications infrastructure, has facilitated the emergence of computer-mediated environments that serve e-commerce partnerships [79]. Internet business, in the United States alone, is forecast to increase as high as \$7.3 trillion in 2004 [25]. Previous research suggests that trust in Internet-based business to business (B2B) e-commerce is an important factor for both practitioners and academicians alike [34,36,42].

Alternatively, the spatial and temporal separation among business partners together with an acceleration of technical changes, have generated implicit uncertainties in B2B e-commerce [9,27]. Technical uncertainties arise from the complexities of e-commerce operations that in turn demand technical skills and knowledge. Previous research suggests that the lack of personal contact and anonymity associated with ecommerce followed by increasing competitive pressures by businesses to adopt e-commerce have created uncertainties, thereby creating perceived risks from privacy concerns [40,55,64]. This in turn makes the system vulnerable to fraud and increases the need for security services. Parkhe [66] and Ring and Van de Ven [69] identify two types of uncertainties: uncertainty regarding unknown future events (or environmental uncertainty) and uncertainty regarding trading partners’ responses to future events (or behavioural uncertainty). Similarly, Mayer et al. [52] pointed out the relationship between trust and risks and suggest two sources of risks. First, risks in the partner who will not behave in a benevolent, competent, honest and predictable fashion, and second, risks that stem from factors outside the relationship that make the decisions significant and uncertain [52].

Uncertainties inherent in the current e-commerce environment give rise to a lack of trust and reliability in e-commerce partnerships, thereby reducing confidence and creating barriers to trade [5,42,76]. Handy [32] suggests that virtuality requires trust to make it work, as technology alone is not enough. Keen [42,p,1] noted, ‘‘We are moving from an IT economy to a trust economy.’’ While one group of scholars believe that trust contributes to positive predictable outcomes leading to willingness to belief [2,8,22, 33,59,69], another group of scholars have posited that for trust to occur there must be an element of risks and vulnerabilities [13,14,15,23,29,41,46, 48,52]. Given the uncertain nature of the online environment, we argue that trust is important for B2B e-commerce relationships.

Zaheer et al. [84] discuss the distinction between interpersonal and inter-organizational trust suggesting that organizations are the subjects of trust as individuals make up members of an organization. This study contributes to inter-organizational trust literature, as the context of this study examines an inter-organizational dyad which is governed by specific structures, and policies. Trust is a multi-dimensional concept and has traditionally been difficult to define and measure Hosmer [37]; McKnight et al. [55]; Rousseau et al. [70]; Sako [72]. We examine trust from four perspectives, namely technological, economic, behavioral, and organizational perspectives. These perspectives focus on how institution-based trust at an organizational level provides structural assurances, and security technical solutions in the form of technology trust leading to interpersonal or relationship trust focusing on trading partners’ interactions, behaviors, motives, and intentions. We then discuss how technology trust evolves into relationship trust applying Doney and Cannon [18] cognitive process framework. From an empirical perspective, this study provides experiential support to previous research on initial trust formation [55,74,85]. The managerial contributions come from the study’s findings on the relative effectiveness of technology trust mechanisms that contribute to technical security solutions and provide institutional structures for initial trust formations that in turn increases the awareness of managers.

The purpose of this study is to shed light on the role and nature of institutional structures in the form of technology trust in a dyadic buyer–supplier relationship by providing theoretical and practical insights to the following research question. How does technology trust evolves into relationship trust in B2B ecommerce? Using a cognitive process framework, we discuss the evolution of trust from four perspectives, namely technological, economic, behavioral and organizational perspectives. The rest of the paper is structured as follows. The next section reviews the relevant literature on trust from four different perspectives. We then describe the research method, report and discuss the findings. Finally, we conclude the paper with implications to theory, practice, and suggestions for future research.

## 2. Theoretical development

## 2.1. Trust in business relationships

Previous scholars suggest that trust in B2B ecommerce is relevant and important in exchange relationships. Trust has been related to desirable outcomes including firms’ performance, and reduced conflicts [83], competitive advantage [2], Ganesan [24,28,60] and other favourable economic outcomes [18,69]. Trust also reduces transaction costs of an exchange, resulting in efficient transactions [8,31]. Sociologists argue that trust is embedded in the social context of the buyer–seller relationships that modify economic activities and create social capital [21,30]. For example, Fukuyama [21] claims that national efficiency is highly correlated with an existence of high trust in institutional environments and suggests that there is social embeddness in economic actions. He argues that ‘‘the economic success of a nation as well as its ability to compete is conditioned by the level of trust inherent in the society’’ (Fukuyama, 1995:7). Furthermore, trust operates as a governance mechanism that diminishes opportunism in exchange relations and promotes cooperation [7,60,85]. In order to conduct B2B e-commerce, it is necessary to have trust in the technological infrastructure and environment. Since the early days of trade history, trust has been a key factor for successful long-term trading partner relationships. Trust increases cooperation, encourages open communica tion, and information sharing [18,58,75]. We argue that trust in the context of B2B e-commerce consists of two forms of trust. First, trust in the technology (i.e., situation specific) serving as a transmission medium for conducting e-commerce and includes security services and technical solutions embedded in e-commerce technologies. Previous research re ferred this to ‘situation normality’ believing that the environment is in proper order and success is likely because the situation is normal [46,55]. Similarly, Zucker [85] refers to certification, licenses, and brand names as institutional-based trust. Second, trust in the partner (i.e., person specific) which builds upon relationship trust from a behavioral perspective. Zucker [85] used the term characteristic-based trust which relies on personal characteristics and processbased trust derived from concrete experiences of social and/or economic exchanges where the future transactions are deeply rooted in stable personal relations. In the next section, we expand and discuss these two forms of trust from four different perspectives, namely technological, economic, behavioral, and organizational perspectives.

## 2.1.1. Technological perspective of trust

The technological perspective of trust stems from institutional structures that have its origins in the sociological literature. Institutional structures suggest that beliefs, intentions, and behaviors act as antecedents to trust generated by situations followed by impersonal assurances so that expectations can be fulfilled. Following McKnight et al. [54], disposition to trust is derived from situational normality suggesting beliefs that the Internet has legal or regulatory protection for consumers (institution-based trust), and should influence the trust of a particular e-vendor (interpersonal-relationship trust). Following, McKnight et al. [54,p,474], trusting beliefs refer to the extent to which the partner is likely to behave in a way that is ‘‘benevolent, competent, honest and predictable’’ in a situation (i.e., relationship trust). Trusting intentions refer to the extent to which an individual is willing to make himself vulnerable to the target’s actions (i.e, willingness to take risks). Similarly, previous studies on theory of reasoned actions based on technology acceptance showed a strong correlation between behavioral intentions and actual behaviours [80]. For example, in her seminal study, Zucker [85] suggests that institution-based trust is created in an impersonal economic environment (without familiarity) and similarity (communality), providing evidence that trust was formed not because people knew each other personally, but because of existing institutional structures such as licensing, auditing, laws, and governmental enforcement bodies. Previous research describes institution-based trust as the belief that a party has about the security of a situation because of guarantees, safety nets, and other structures that contribute to structural assurances such as guarantees, contracts, regulations, promises, legal recourse, and procedures that are conducive to situational success [46,47,55,74,85]. This line of reasoning is consistent with Leifer and Mills [45,p,117] who referred to controls as ‘‘a regulatory process by which the elements of a system are made more predictable through the establishment and standards in the pursuit of some desired objective or state.’’ Similarly, Tan and Thoen [78] used the term ‘control trust’ to refer to embedded protocols, policies, and procedures in ecommerce that reduces opportunistic behaviors among consumers and Web retailers. Further, Lee and Turban [44] measured trustworthiness of Internet shopping based on consumer evaluations of technical competence and Internet performance levels (such as speed, reliability and availability). Based on these arguments, we propose the term ‘‘technology trust’ to capture a subset of institution-based trust, drawing from the adherence to technical standards, security procedures, and protective mechanisms that provide technical solutions.

Technology trust is defined as ‘‘the subjective probability by which organizations believe that the underlying technology infrastructure is capable of facilitating transactions according to their confident expectations’’ [67,p,22]. Dimensions of technology trust include security services, such as (a) confidentiality mechanisms that aim to protect e-commerce transactions and message content against unauthorized reading, copying, or disclosure using encryption mechanisms; (b) integrity mechanisms that provide transaction accuracy and assurance that the e-commerce transactions have not been altered or deleted; (c) authentication mechanisms that provide transaction quality of being authoritative, valid, true, genuine, worthy of acceptance or belief by reason of conformity to the fact that reality is present; (d) non-repudiation mechanisms that protect the originator of e-commerce transactions and use acknowledgement procedures applying digital signatures; (e) access control mechanisms that provide authorization mechanisms thereby assuring that transactions are sent and received without interruption; and (f) availability mechanisms that protect transactions against weaknesses in the transmission media and protect the sender against internal fraud or manipulation by using authorization mechanisms such as User IDs and passwords. We suggest that the dimensions of security services contribute towards technical solutions from a capability process that serves to support the privacy, accuracy, authenticity of authorized parties, and accountability of e-commerce transactions [6,11,39,65,50].

## 2.1.2. Economic perspective of trust

Previous research on trust in the marketing and management literature mainly focused on transaction specific investments and firm’s performance [18,24,75,83]. While some scholars [12,49] have focused on information technology as a means of reducing inter-organizational transaction costs. The findings of Kumar et al. [43] suggest the ability to substitute and complement trust and technology in reducing transaction costs in inter-organizational relationships. Implementing e-commerce applications can be high as it includes initial search costs, costs of writing contracts, and paying staff to update and maintain electronic databases and other costs, such as connection costs, hardware, software, set up, and maintenance costs [38,51,62,73].

Most e-commerce technologies are embedded with automated security protocols that enable firms to ensure partner authentication. Implementing encryption mechanisms protects online transactions from being intercepted, manipulated, and deleted, thereby contributing to transaction integrity [50,68,73]. Transaction integrity in turn contributes to economic benefits from savings in time and costs, as timely, accurate and reliable information is received. For example, Mukhopadyay et al. [61] conducted a study of nine Chrysler assembly centers and found that EDI improved the quality of information sharing and reduced inventory, transportation, and administrative costs. Further, authorized login procedures, e-mail acknowledgments, and confirmations provide confidentiality, authentication, and non-repudiation mechanisms derived from a capability process (i.e., the capability of the system to transact correctly and efficiently). Such functional acknowledgments in the form of email feedbacks or other e-commerce protocols provide real-time reliable and timely feedback mechanisms that in turn increase trading partners’ satisfaction [1]. Satisfaction in turn increases partner reputation via intentionality and transference process, as the partner is able to demonstrate skills in producing high-quality goods, products, and services thereby achieving high levels of trustworthiness that can be extended. We argue that the automation, speed, security services, and technical efficiencies of the e-commerce systems contribute to time and cost savings derived from technology trust [10,39,44,63], thereby contributing to an economic perspective of trust that in turn influences trading partner’s intentions and behaviors.

Trust plays an important role in the economic perspective due to interdependencies among trading partners. We argue that trust from an economic perspective primarily involves a calculative process, as trading partners usually undertake a cost-benefit analysis (when calculating the costs and/or rewards of staying in the relationship) when renewing trading contracts on future investments [31,81]. Sambamurthy and Zmud [71] referred to ‘‘economics of scope’’ as benefits arising from sharing appropriate IT enabled expertise, know-how, and investments across the enterprise (p. 266).

## 2.1.3. Behavioral perspective of trust

The behavioral perspective of trust is derived from relationship trust which focuses on the interpersonal behavioral characteristics of trading partners within a

specific situation. Gefen [26] found that disposition to trust influences trust in vendors. Similarly, the theory of reasoned action also supports this link in that beliefs strongly predict corresponding intentions and behaviors [16,80]. McKnight et al. [55] suggest that institution-trust leads to disposition to trust where trading parties exhibit a willingness to rely and depend on ‘‘faith in humanity,’’ which refers to the partner’s integrity, competence, and benevolence (i.e., relationship trust). We support this reasoning and identify three types of trust, namely competence, predictability, and goodwill trust, derived from four cognitive processes adapted from Doney and Cannon [18]. First, competence trust is derived from a capability process of the trading partner and determines the ability, skills, competence, and technical knowledge of the trading partner to transact correctly [52,57,72]. Sambamurthy and Zmud [71] referred to ‘‘absorptive capacity’’ as the ability of a firm’s employees to develop relevant knowledge bases, recognize valuable external information, make appropriate decision, and implement effective work processes and structures. Second, predictability trust is derived from a predictive process that relies on one party’s ability to forecast, predict and make judgments of their partner from prior experiences (derived from repeated interactions and consistent behaviours) [47,74]. Finally, goodwill trust is derived from an intentionality process and focuses on the trading partner’s intentions, motives that allow the trustor to interpret their partner’s words, behaviors, thereby determining their intentions. Doney and Cannon [18] and McAllister [53] suggest that when reliability and dependability expectations are met, trust moves to affective foundations that include emotional bonds such as care and concern. The focus here is on the reliance of a trading partners’ care, concern, honesty and benevolence which provides an avenue for an extension pattern via the transference process (from reputation for fair trading) and allows trading partners to commit and invest further in their relationship. Goodwill trust is characterized by an increased level of cooperation, open communication, coordination, commitment, and information sharing that leads to an increase in ecommerce participation [2,24,60,70,72].

Zaheer et al. [84] distinguish between interpersonal and inter-organizational trust claiming that individuals make up members of an organization. Technology trust draws from e-commerce technologies, third party services, and organizational actors (auditors, security analysts, and top management personnel) who are committed to enforcing best business practices. ## 3. Research method

Studying inter-organizational-trust in B2B e-commerce requires the context of real organizations using e-commerce systems or operating in an ecommerce environment. We applied a multiple case study design exemplified by Eisenhardt [17] in order to discern and induce patterns in two large multi-national corporations. Given that limited research exists on trust in dyadic B2B e-commerce relationships, a case study research method was found to be appropriate for the following reasons. First, previous research suggests that the concept of trust is multi-dimensional and is difficult to measure [37,72]. Case studies allowed us to examine complex e-commerce issues in depth. Second, case studies enabled us to elicit subtle and rich data needed (in the form of explanations and descriptions) and to increase our understanding of trust from four different perspectives [3,82]. Third, this study interviewed multiple participants from each case in addition to obtaining data from other sources (such as document analysis and observations). Fourth, case studies enabled us to undertake an in-depth analysis of trust in a real life situation by answering ‘‘how’’ and ‘‘why’’ types of questions. Yin [82] suggests the impact of a ‘replication logic’ versus a ‘sampling logic’ when applying a single case study versus multiple cases studies. This study examined two cases forming an inter-organizational dyad. ## 3.1. Data collection

The case study was initiated by telephone calls to key representatives of Cisco’s and their customer Compaq. Site selection was undertaken with two main objectives. First, in order to control the effects of environmental variables and to focus on the four different perspectives of trust, it was decided to include firms within a similar industry (i.e., within the data communications industry); and second, in order to increase the number of multiple informants/ participants from each case that will in turn contribute to corporate views and an organizational perspective of trust, large multi-national corporations were chosen (such as Cisco and Compaq).

Table 3 presents the characteristics of the sample, descriptions of the role, size, and types of e-commerce application used by the organizations and details of the participants. The table presents the title of the participants whose age ranged between 35 and 55 years old. Eighty percent of the participants were from the IT and e-commerce departments. The remaining participants were from the accounting and audit departments. The interviewees discussed e-commerce adoption, processes, policies, controls, their trading partners’ interactions, e-commerce applications that they had implemented, and how they perceived their trading partner’s trust from different perspectives. The next section describes data collection and analysis procedures.

Data were collected via face-to-face interviews with senior managers involved in e-commerce operations applying a semi-structured interview protocol.

The questions focused on gaining an understanding of the following. How and why do technological, economic, behavioral, and organizational perspectives of trust impact outcomes in e-commerce participation and trading partner trust development? How do the nature and the role of B2B e-commerce operations impact these perspectives of trust and how does it evolve from technology trust to relationship trust? Interviews continued for three months and five interviews per person on average were conducted with each interview lasting just under one and half hour. Transcripts from each case were generated and returned to them to validate the researcher’s interpretation. Construct validity was supported by employing multiple data collection methods [3,82]. The author had access to the firm’s web site, memos, policies, procedures, standards, financial statements, and documents relating to sales. Further, observations included sitting in at meetings, and noting interviewees’ perceptions. Both qualitative and quantitative data were used with multiple participants from each case, in order to achieve triangulation. Reliability was enforced by using the same semi-structured interview protocol for all interviews. Table 4 illustrates how the four perspectives of trust were examined via a semistructured interview protocol guide.

## 3.2. Data analysis

Systematic analysis was applied during data analysis while the first part indicates conditions or quotes from participants that support the independent variables (in this case trust). The second part discusses the effects of the independent variables from the four different perspectives. Pattern matching and explanation building (as in narrative descriptions and causal explanations) were used to analyze the findings. The data collected were mapped, cross-referenced and compared with the predictions. For example, the findings from competence trust were matched against economic benefits. Similarly, the findings from trading partner predictability and goodwill trust were matched against relationship-related benefits. Whenever a difference in the participant’s opinions occurred, further explanations were sought and examined. The first step identified recurring patterns and themes from the data collected during interviews for each organization [56]. Then the similarities and differences between the participants of each organization were identified as a cross-case analysis that paved the way for analytical generalizations and predictions to be made.

## 3.3. Background information of Cisco

Cisco’s headquarters at San Jose, California, USA has well over 225 sales and support offices in 75 countries, including Cisco New Zealand (NZ). Cisco’s channel partners include Compaq, Logical, Datacom, IBM, Telecom, Unisys, Clear, Fujistu, and Computer Link. In 1997, Cisco NZ implemented their extranet application called ‘‘Cisco Connection Online’’ (CCO).

Cisco embraces the Global Networked Business model which aims to implement tools, systems, and share information with trading partners, such as suppliers, distributors, customers and employees. By using Cisco Connection Online (COO), Internetworking Product Center (IPC), and Partner Initiated Customer Access (PICA), Cisco was able to connect their channel partners to the manufacturing resource planning system. For instance, the Internetworking Products Center (IPC) provided order status information, pricing, configuration assistance, and on-line tracking of orders. Trading partners can access details about their purchase orders, order date, expected shipment date, and shipping carrier without calling Cisco’s NZ office. CCO allows its trading partners to link up directly with the Federal Express tracking web site, while logging inside CCO, to find out exactly where their order is, whether it is in the warehouse, on the truck or plane, or receiving dock. This type of precise order tracking detail prevents all kinds of possible billing and shipping problems in providing accountability and accurate proof of delivery. We argue that the above contributes to the capability process of CCO that in turn creates technology trust.

## 3.4. Background information of Compaq NZ

Compaq is a large company with three hundred employees and their reach is both national as well as global. Compaq buys computer equipments and data communication parts from Cisco. Compaq has five branches in New Zealand: Wellington, Auckland, Christchurch, Hamilton, and Dunedin. Compaq also receives direction from their headquarters in US. As a system integrator, Compaq supplies computer systems and provides computer services. Compaq sells computers, undertakes systems integration, and develops application software, hardware, networks, and database application systems.

## 4. Findings

In this section we report the findings of the interorganizational dyad between Cisco and Compaq from the four different perspectives of trust.

## 4.1. The technological perspective of trust

The e-commerce partnership between Cisco and Compaq began with training given by Cisco to Compaq employees which was found to be encouraging and supportive. Cisco was willing to share information relating to their e-commerce operations. Further, the IT consultants at the Cisco’s Internet Business Solutions Group (IBSG), located in Sydney (Australia), provided the technical support and handled all queries relating to technical clarifications and operations of the Cisco Connection Online (CCO) application. A Compaq network sales specialist described the technical perspective as:

‘‘CCO enables us to check the prices, request a discount if necessary, and electronically receive an estimated time of arrival even before confirming the order. CCO undertakes an automated checking mechanism, thereby contributing to 80% of the fault activities reported on the web. The system automatically gives the number of days as to when the goods will be delivered, thereby enabling Compaq staff to undertake an inquiry on the system in advance. The updated information on Cisco’s website was accurate and reliable.’’

## Cisco’s Accounting manager noted:

‘‘Although, the extranet site maintained by Cisco was fairly secure, there were concerns that Compaq staff could leak out sensitive information (such as price and product information) to Cisco’s competitors.’’

We conclude that the embedded technical security solutions and services in the CCO system contributed to the speed, privacy, accuracy, timely information, accountability, and authenticity of trading parties logging into CCO, via the capabilities of the system that in turn encouraged the formation of technology trust.

## 4.2. The economic perspective of trust

Compaq staff experienced economic benefits from reduced costs, timely quick responses, and accurate information that enabled them to make strategic decisions. The use of CCO also reduced the error rates. In fact, 80% of the non-technical support questions were answered online through Cisco’s convenient, self-service applications that in turn increased Compaq’s satisfaction. Compaq received immediate, reliable, round-the-clock access to richer, more precise service and support information, thereby contributing to strategic benefits. A Compaq sales consultant described:

‘‘We have experienced reduced error rates and improved accuracy of information exchanged, as we can configure our orders and check them first before confirming the placement of the same order. Faster responses to orders and reduced lead time from CCO provided estimated arrival dates of the goods and tracking information which enabled us to inform our end consumers. Cisco staff kept their business promises which assisted Compaq to make strategic decisions derived from a capability process.’’

## Cisco’s accounting manager indicated that:

‘‘Initially, the error rate was 80%, has now reduced to less than 15%, thus contributing to economic benefits from savings in time and cost (via reduced telephone calls, email, and faxes) in correcting the mistakes. CCO provides benefits in the form of real-time order tracking information and estimated arrival dates of goods.’’

One of Cisco IBSG participant indicated that ‘‘without CCO, my staff would have about three times larger to handle the same workload.’’

Cisco’s e-commerce coordinators indicated:

‘‘Economic benefits over a period of time led to positive, consistent behaviors shown by our improved customer service, product quality, and satisfaction. We saved up to US\$800 million dollars per year, as there was no need to re-key the same order information that was entered by Compaq staff. Further, the provision of real-time online tracking information has contributed to additional savings in time and costs previously handled via telephone calls and fax.’’

We conclude that the speed and reliability in providing real time tracking information of the orders placed by Compaq, in addition to order acknowledgement, and error-detective mechanisms led to time and cost savings of e-commerce transactions derived from a calculative process that in turn contributed to economic benefits. Strategic benefits were derived via a transference process (i.e., quality of Cisco’s products, services creating a brand name and reputation).

## 4.3. The behavioral perspective of trust

The behavioral perspective of trust was examined by observing the competence, predictability and goodwill trust of the trading partners. Cisco staff initially noted that the competence trust of Compaq staff was low. One possible explanation for this is that although CCO is a user-friendly package, the process of creating an order for the products and components is a complex one. Cisco’s accounting manager said:

‘‘We have some employees from Compaq who can perform competently, and some that cannot but have learned it the painful way in terms of wasted time and costs, as they need to re-send the same order twice. Some of our trading partners do not have the underlying fundamental knowledge to place complete and correct orders, thereby lacking the intellectual horsepower required to undertake the ordering process.’

## A Cisco Sales Consultant indicated that:

‘‘Compaq staffs are aware of the market coverage and were confident in their capability in resolving standardized issues. Over time Compaq staff had shown an ability to be competent channel system integrators using Cisco computer and communication equipment.’’

Compaq staff will need to become familiar with our products, thereby demanding an ability to configure the orders correctly and completely (i.e., a capability process for examining the competence). In our interview, the Compaq Network Specialist Manager indicated that:

‘‘Cisco staff are the ‘pros’, as they know what they are doing. Their IT support people are excellent, very responsive, and timely in providing complete and accurate information. In addition, their online tools have checking mechanisms that enabled Compaq to detect errors.’’

‘‘Competence trust was shown by Cisco staff, who had the ability to do their job. We are able to say that trading with Cisco is better than trading with most of Cisco’s competitors, particularly when it comes to problem-solving which creates confidence in conflict resolutions and clarifying issues. I guess that’s why Cisco is known for their reputation and goodwill trust.’’

The Compaq Network Sales Specialist indicated that they were dependent on Cisco staff.

‘‘Cisco’s staffs are reliable. We want to continue using Cisco’s communication equipment for two reasons. First, our end customers rely on us as system integrators, and second, our end customers order Cisco’s equipment, which only Cisco can supply. In that respect we are dependent on Cisco.

This exemplifies a predictive process and contributes towards predictability trust as noted by Compaq’s inventory manager:

‘‘We speak of trust in e-commerce systems with regard to actual dealings, but it takes some time for trading partner relationship trust to gradually develop.’’

Goodwill trust was developed from improved communications and cooperation with Compaq staff through constant emails and phone calls made to Cisco’s IT call center in Sydney-the Internet Business Solutions Group (IBSG). According to Cisco’s Accounting manager:

‘‘One possible explanation for this is our high quality services together with the information we provide (that is timely and accurate) and our IT support group staffs are competent, and responsive.’’

We conclude that the behavioral perspective of trust was derived from competence trust (via the trading partner capability), predictability trust (via a predictive process), and goodwill trust (via an intentionality and transference process).

## 4.4. The organizational perspective of trust

The organizational perspective of trust focuses on institutional policies, procedures, standards, top management commitment, and risk management strategies in the form of best business practices. Cisco accounting manager stated:

‘‘Our trading partners typically tell us that doing business with us is better than doing business with our competitors, due to our high quality services, and reputation for our products.’’

## Cisco’s sales consultant indicated that:

‘‘We admit that our number of trading partners has increased. When we first implemented e-commerce on the web we had between 3 to 5 major channel partners, now we have 10 trading partners (channel partners). Our main role is to establish customer preference, and increase the number of channel partners by trading relationship trust, which in turn increases e-commerce participation. In fact, Cisco participants admitted that 35–40% of the revenue came from Compaq.’’

The online support services are based on Cisco’s philosophy to move beyond the traditional business that aims to make all of Cisco’s information services available; provide support to their global customers, partners and employees on demand; deliver faster problem responses; improve user productivity; and significantly lower the cost of doing business, thus contributing to a successful organizational image. The Cisco accounting manager noted:

‘‘We try to resolve issues within twenty-four hours via telephone calls, e-mail and sometimes even face-to-face meetings as Compaq staffs were also located in the Wellington region, thus contributing to relationship-related benefits. We also have explicit trading and procedures agreement with Compaq regarding their roles and responsibilities outlined in the System Integration Supply Contract.’’

‘‘He went on to say, Compaq staff do adhere to policies, terms of contract and the trading partner agreement. We sign a three- to five-year contract with our trading partners,’ which outlines the procedure to be followed from the time we place an order until settlement. E-commerce is very crucial to our organization as it helps reduce the costs and increase customer satisfaction with fewer errors.’’

Further, Cisco considers factors such as commitment in joint revenues and joint investments, commonality of vision and objectives, cultural compatibility of organizations, and the potential breadth and depth of the relationship across the different business units at Cisco when forming an ecommerce partnership.

When referring to best business practices, a Compaq accounting manager stated:

‘‘We have reference numbers that served as unique identifiers thereby providing authentication. We believe that top management commitment is important especially when it comes to the budget. We do have to meet with the standards set out by Cisco (industry and universal) and policies. We are part of the ISO 9000, but not all of the divisions. We have a trading partner agreement, and undertake regular audit checks in order to manage risks. We have a business risk management group of consultants who design contingency procedures for our e-commerce systems and operations.’’

## 5. Discussion

Based on the above findings, we suggest that trust in e-commerce relationships gradually evolves from technology trust to relationship trust. Fig. 1 illustrates the evolution of technology trust to relationship trust applying the four perspectives of trust.

5.1. Stage 1: Technological perspective via a capability process

During the first stage, Cisco staff typically concentrated on training Compaq staff to use their CCO system correctly. The focus is on the capabilities of the e-commerce system and the trading partner (i.e., their competence, skills, and ability to operate the ecommerce system correctly and transmit business transactions completely and accurately). This contributes to technology trust and competence relationship trust derived from the automated technological solutions and security services. Technology trust focusing on the technological perspective of trust was rated high by both Cisco and Compaq as they initially focused on learning and getting trained to use the CCO e-commerce system.

## 5.2. Stage 2: Economic perspective via a predictive and calculative process

During the second stage, consistent positive patterns of the e-commerce system and reliability of the trading partner contributed towards trustworthy behaviors and predictability trust. The predictive process was based on the knowledge of past experiences that in turn contributes to economic benefits from savings in time and cost from the CCO system. Both firms experienced economic efficiencies from automation, speed, real-time tracking of orders and detective mechanisms derived from a calculative process (via a cost-benefit analysis). The economic perspective was rated high by both firms and the focus now gradually evolves from technology trust to relationship trust, which emphasizes on the trading partner’s behaviors (such as reliability, honesty, and predictability).

## 5.3. Stage 3: Behavioral perspective via an intentionality process

During the third stage, goodwill relationship trust develops from the positive consistent behaviors that encourage trading partners to invest further in their trading partner relationships (i.e, renew the trading partners contract), increase e-commerce performance (as in volume, dollar value of goods), and reduce opportunistic behaviors (such as power, control, and the imposition of strict deadlines). The behavioral perspective of trust was derived from an intentionality process that aimed at maintaining good business relationships.

## 5.4. Stage 4: Organizational perspective via a transference process

Institutional (organizational) reputation and brand names were accomplished by enforcing best business practices that included top management commitment, high-quality standards, open communication, information sharing, and long-term trading partner relationships, thereby contributing to strategic benefits. The organizational perspective of trust was rated high as Cisco focused on institutional structures and best business practices that in turn help build their reputation via a transference process.

We would also like to note here that there are unsuccessful business relationships that do not evolve in this manner. Instead, their relationships are terminated in the first stage and their contracts are not renewed due to lack of competence, not keeping up with business promises, exercise of coercive power, poor quality goods and services from poor business practices that all lead to mistrust among the trading parties. ## 6. Conclusions

Recent literature has stressed on the importance of trust for successful e-commerce. This study contributes to a typology of trust from four different perspectives, namely technological, economic, behavioral, and organizational. Further, we discussed how technology trust evolves into relationship trust applying five cognitive processes, namely capability, calculative, predictive, intentionality and transference processes, adapted from Doney and Cannon [18] to fit the context of B2B e-commerce relationships. We argue that initial trust formations are derived from both impersonal institutional structures embedded in ecommerce technologies in the form of security services, and structural assurances from technology trust. Enforcing technology trust contributes to consistent behavioral patterns among the trading partners that contribute to three types of relationship trust, namely competence, predictability, and goodwill trust, that gradually evolved into relationship trust. While the scope of the paper was limited to the relatively narrow domain of trust in the context of B2B e-commerce, the typology of trust perspectives should be useful to researchers studying trust in other situations. By recognizing the different perspectives of trust, the typology highlights different operational, relational, and strategic implications of trust.

## 6.1. Managerial implications

Managers will be made aware of the importance of trust from different perspectives and the need to develop sustainable competitive advantages in exchange relationships. By properly utilizing technology trust, this study paves the way for managers to understand how the adoption of B2B e-commerce can impact their exchange relationships. This study provides a guide for early practitioners of e-commerce technologies by increasing their levels of awareness on the role and impact of e-commerce technologies and best business practices that create technology trust and engender relationship trust via collaborative exchange relationships. Therefore, this study informs managers on how the new character of trust can be strategically used to realize business value beyond transaction efficiencies and cost reduction.

## 6.2. Research implications and suggestions for future research

This study attempts to make a theoretical contribution in the area of trust in B2B e-commerce by integrating the trust literature with theories from security, privacy, and control to propose a new theoretical construct (called technology trust) which describes the importance of trust in the underlying technology infrastructure from four different perspectives. The theoretical foundations discussed in this paper contribute to organizational, transaction cost economics, resource dependence, and social exchange theories, as we extend previous research on trust to explain a process framework within the context of B2B e-commerce partnerships. This study contributes empirically by proposing how trust can be strategically used beyond the traditional dyadic level to contribute to perceived benefits, organizational performance, and B2B e-commerce success. The role of establishing trust in the underlying technology infrastructure has become an important driver for the value realization in today’s B2B e-commerce. Further, Doney et al. [19] and Fukuyama [21] convincingly argue that culture may have a strong moderating effect on trust formation. Therefore, future research could examine whether institutional structures have a different effect in dissimilar cultures. Finally, this research is limited as only two firms participated and we only examined a subset of the many possible relationships between trust and its antecedents and consequences. Future research should examine or propose other trust-building mechanisms available in online marketplaces that are likely to engender trust.

Dr Pauline Ratnasingam, is an Assistant Professor of MIS, Department of Computer Information Systems, Harmon School of Business Administration, Central Missouri State University (CMSU), Warrensburg, Missouri. Before joining CMSU, she was an Assistant Professor at the School of Business Administration, University of Vermont, Burlington, Vermont. Dr Pauline Ratnasingam received her Bachelors in Computing (Information Systems) and

(Honors in Information Systems) from Monash University, Melbourne, Australia. She received her PhD titled ‘‘Inter-organizational Trust in Business to Business Electronic Commerce’’ from Erasmus University, Rotterdam School of Management, The Netherlands. She lectured on topics such as; Project Management, Management of Information Systems, and Electronic Commerce in Australia, New Zealand, Europe and America. She is an associate member of the Association of Information Systems, and is a member of the Information Resources Management Association and Academy of Man agement. Her research interests include Business Risk Management, Internet based business-to-business e-commerce, Organizational Behaviour, Inter-Organizational-Relationships and Trust. She is a recipient of a National Science Foundation Grant and has published several articles related to this area in national and international conferences and refereed journals. Her biography is also published in the 58th Edition of Marquis Who is Who Publications.
