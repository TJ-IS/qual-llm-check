# Segmenting online customers to manage business resources: A study of the impacts of sales channel strategies on consumer preferences

Achita (Mi) Muthitacharoen <sup>a,\*</sup>, Mark L. Gillenson <sup>b</sup>, Nattawat Suwan

<sup>a</sup> Wichita State University, Barton School of Business and Economics, Box 77, Wichita, KS 67260-0077, USA

<sup>b</sup> The University of Memphis, TN, USA

<sup>c</sup> BBBS of Kansas, KS, USA

Received in revised form 21 October 2005; accepted 10 November 2005 Available online 10 January 2006

## Abstract

This study extends our current knowledge in the area of online consumer behavior by examining how a business’s sales channel strategy could influence consumer’s sales channel preferences. It makes a new argument that business strategies could play an important role in consumer sales channel preference development. When a business offers multiple sales channels (a hybrid model) its customers can compare sales channels either within or outside of the same corporate business. Such freedom is, however, diminished when a business employs the Internet as the only transaction medium (pure Internet store). A matrix was developed to demonstrate how business strategies could interplay in the consumer preference formation process and later was used to segment our respondents into three different groups. They were segmented according to the sales channel strategies of their selected Internet store and the brick-and-mortar store that they used to make a comparison. MANOVA and structural equation modeling tests were performed on 435 survey respondents. Four preferential factors, including transaction cost, product, risk, and social experience, were used as examples and tested across three groups of respondents. Results revealed that online users employ different sets of preferential factors when comparing different sets of sales channels. Such results were thereafter used to draw a new set of online strategies that could be used to allocate business resources more effectively. <sup>#</sup> 2005 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Internet; Sales channel strategies; Customer segmentation; Preferences

## 1. Introduction

Research in the area of business-to-consumer electronic commerce has been an on-going topic for IS researchers for more than a decade. Several approaches have been applied to studying consumer behavior in the e-market. A group of researchers applied intention theories to investigate Internet adoptions [13,19,39]. Some investigated website characteristics that render a higher quality Internet store (e.g. [2,29,39]). Many were interested in studying types of Internet usage [26]. Others investigated individual characteristics of consumers that affect online purchasing decisions [6,17]. In addition to these topics, some researchers have devoted their efforts to categorizing products that can be sold on the Internet successfully [27,28,37], while others have investigated how shoppers transform themselves from non-innovators to innovators [41].

Despite this growing number of studies and research topics, total sales generated from online retailers comprises only 1.3% of total retail sales value for the third quarter of 2002 [54]. Such statistics reveal that consumers remain loyal to traditional sales channels (e.g. brick-and-mortar, catalog, etc.) and it implies that our current online business strategies may not be effective enough to change consumer sales channel selections.

Our work is a new approach to the study of online consumer behavior. It combines the concept of the sales channel strategy with the preferential decision as its theoretical framework to investigate how online consumers make comparisons of sales channels. A stream of research provides empirical evidence of the impacts of sales channel comparisons on the decision to purchase online [15,16,56]. The current study strives to extend current knowledge in this area by examining the role of business sales channel strategies in consumers sales channel preferences. Two major strategies (the Pure Internet Store and the Hybrid strategies) were chosen and empirically tested in conjunction with consumer preference development. Such an approach leads us to find that different segments of online customers use different sets of factors to make sales channel comparisons. This finding is later used to produce a new set of guidelines that allow corporate businesses to manage their resources more effectively.

## 2. Literature review: preferential decision and business models

## 2.1. Preferential decision

The current study argues that combining preferential decision knowledge with the concept of sales channel strategy could reveal different patterns that online users employ to compare sales channel alternatives. Preferential decision is a well-developed research area in marketing and other social science disciplines. We adopt a traditional definition of preference, which is ‘‘the setting by an individual of one thing before or above another thing because of a notion of betterness’’ [9]. Its importance to other psychological variables, such as beliefs and intentions, has been witnessed in prior studies (e.g. [3,12]). While preferential decision has been an ongoing research topic in other social science disciplines for the past three decades [50], very little effort has been made to incorporate preference in IS research. Despite its rare appearance, the preference antecedent has been studied as a dependent variable that demonstrates a significant relationship with consumer satisfaction in the electronic market environment [16]. At times, it has been discussed in the context of website comparisons and demonstrates why a website is preferred to a brick-andmortar store by Internet users [48].

There are several approaches in preferential choice studies, one of which is the multi-attributed modeling approach. Several benefits of adopting the multiattribute modeling approach in social sciences studies, such as its potential insights to consumer’s cognitive processes and its pragmatic values to industry, were listed in past streams of research [21,30,44]. This approach has gained increasing significance in the last three decades [7,57]. Within the domain of the multiattribute modeling approach, two concepts of preference development have emerged. They are Attribute-Based Preference and Attitude-Based Preference. The first approach suggests that preference formation involves comparing specific attributes (Attribute-Based Preference), while the second approach signifies the overall evaluation of alternatives (Attitude-Based Preference) [32].

When Attribute-Based Preference is used, individuals compare their alternatives in detail. For instance, a customer, who is engaged in mobile phone selection, might want to compare several features such as a builtin camera, supporting carriers, MP3, and Bluetooth functions, etc. When Attitude-Based Preference is used, individuals employ their general feelings to develop their preference. Such general feelings might be derived from brand, past experience, etc. [58]. Tversky [51] proposed that alternatives are compared directly on each dimension (attribute), and the differences on those dimensions are summed together to reach a decision. In addition, it was articulated that humans somehow combine all dimensional (Attribute-Based Preference) values cognitively and come to an overall evaluation (Attitude-Based Preference) before making their decisions [18]. In other words, these propositions asserted that Attribute-Based Preference (ABP) influences Attitude-Based Preference (ADP) or ADP is a function of ABP. The relationship between ADP and ABP is shown in Fig. 1.

The current study attempts to provide concrete examples of how consumer preferences of sales channels are developed and influenced by business strategies of using sales channels. Attributes that online consumers generally use to make online purchasing decisions were reviewed. Early studies in this area provided insightful information regarding factors that online consumers use to make online purchasing decisions. Several factors – transaction cost, product, social interaction, and perceived risk of credit card – have been repeatedly claimed to be vital factors in the electronic market (e.g. [4,8,15,16,23,49]). With the prior findings in this area, the current study proposes a decomposed version of ABP and its relationship with ADP in Fig. 2.

In this study, we defined the four attributes as followed:

Transaction Cost Preference: The superior setting of a sales channel from its ability to provide its customers with favorable product price and other financial transaction costs (i.e. shipping cost, sales taxes, etc.) occurring during the purchasing process. Product Preference: The superior setting of a sales channel from its ability to provide its customers with a favorable product at the point of purchase, including variety, selection, and availability of product.

Social Interaction Preference: The superior setting of a sales channels from its ability to provide its customers with interactive communications and a supportive sales environment.

Risk Preference: A higher degree of personal risk inherently stemmed from the use of a sales channel to make purchases (see definition of personal risk in a study by Jarvenpaa and Todd [23]).

While the definitions of Social Interaction and Risk Preferences could be considered commonly used in IS studies, we believe that it is important to further explain the definitions and originations of transaction cost and Product Preferences. It is widely acknowledged that using different sales channels would generate different transaction costs to customers. This study however focuses only on the financial transaction cost which could consist of, but not be limited to, product price, shipping fee, handling fee, and sales taxes. In our study, this construct and its instruments were developed and purified through our two pilot studies. Initially, the pilot studies had shipping and handling cost items but they did not converge well with these other items. These were later eliminated from the instrument. In its current form, the Transaction Cost Preference has two items, including product price and saving (see Appendix A). Although the shipping and other financial transaction costs were not retained in the instrument, we believe that the second item ‘‘financial saving’’ inherently captures several characteristics of financial advantages and disadvantages (i.e. the absence of sales taxes, shipping costs, etc.) from using sales channels and therefore was retained in our Transaction Cost Preference instrument (see more detailed discussion of instrument development in the research method section).

The Product Preference, in the study, emphasizes two product aspects, including the accessibilities of product and product variety. One may find that a product may be more readily available in one sales channel than the others. For example, an online user may perceive that Internet stores could be connected to several product warehouses and have more products in stock than those found in brick-and-mortar stores. Thereby, ‘‘product availability’’ is used as one of the items for the Product Preference. The Product Preference construct additionally emphasized the product variety aspect. While product variety could have different meanings, we utilize this Product Preference construct to capture the concept of ‘‘one stop shopping’’. Thus, the product variety in this study entails the ability to provide different kinds of products (i.e. books, CDs, clothes, etc.) that users can add to their shopping carts to satisfy their various needs. We believe that such a definition is broader and could reflect how online shoppers receive more values from their online transaction (see Appendix A).

Thus, the decomposed version of the relationship between ABP and ADP introduces the following hypotheses:

$\mathbf { H _ { 1 } } .$ Attitude-Based Preference is a positive function of Transaction Cost Preference.

$\mathbf { H } _ { 2 } .$ Attitude-Based Preference is a positive function of Social Interaction Preference.

$\mathbf { H } _ { 3 } .$ Attitude-Based Preference is a positive function of Product Preference.

$\mathbf { H } _ { 4 } .$ Attitude-Based Preference is a negative function of Risk Preference.

The above model (Fig. 2) is a part of a bigger model that has been previously tested and validated by using structural equation models [36]. Its instrument was developed by following a guideline from Churchill [14] and Straub [46]—see Appendix A. The current study does not attempt to provide a test of the relationship between ABP and ADP. Such a result was reported in a previous study [36]. On the other hand, the current study strives to provide a richer understanding of online consumer behavior. It hypothesizes that the above attributes could be compared differently in different business settings. In other words, it strives to examine how business strategies could influence consumers comparisons of sales channels.

## 2.2. Impacts of sales channels strategies on online purchasing decisions

In reality, consumers have to make comparisons of their shopping alternatives on a daily basis. Not only do they compare products, they also compare stores and sales channels [34]. The Internet store is a continually growing sales channel for today’s consumers. The present study attempts to examine how online users make comparisons across sales channels and investigates factors that could be significant in different business setting environments. It was generally asserted that consumers are likely to compare Internet stores to brickand-mortar stores, since brick-and-mortar stores are alternatives to which consumers feel most acquainted (see anchoring and adjustment role in decision-making in Kahneman and Tversky [24,25]; Tversky and Kahmeman [52,53]). When consumers experience a new alternative, a familiar alternative could serve as an anchor to make a comparison [43]. Results of a comparison process will in turn determine whether the new alternative (an Internet store) will be selected. Alba et al. [1] acknowledged the importance of sales channel comparisons in consumer decision-making and provided a comparison of the Internet and other sales channels at an early stage of Internet development.

As the Internet becomes a more viable and vital alternative for consumers, managing sales channels has simultaneously become a challenge to businesses and their executives. In the early days of the Internet, most retailers hesitated in implementing online businesses due to their beliefs in the cannibalizing roles of Internet stores to their brick-and-mortar stores [40,45]. Such beliefs have radically and quickly changed in the past decade. It is now imperative for businesses to offer online access to their customers. The Internet store has become a crucial element for starting and maintaining a business. With this new sales channel, corporate businesses have availed themselves of a new opportunity to give better service to their customers. Some have even adopted a Pure Internet Store business strategy in which their customers are allowed to make purchases only through the Internet (i.e. Amazon.com). Some others have adopted a hybrid strategy [38] where Internet and brick-and-mortar stores are concurrently offered to customers (i.e. Wal-Mart and Wal-mart.com). Past streams of research have examined the advantages and disadvantages of these two strategies [11] and many others have investigated their effectiveness and return on investments [20,47]. Despite the growing evidence of benefits from using the Internet as another sales channel for retail businesses, little has been done to investigate how business strategies could influence consumers’ comparisons of sales channels. Such an agenda has become a prime goal of this study.

Research in the area of sales channels comparison has grown significantly as corporate businesses have recognized the power of the Internet. While some researchers have focused on the comparison across sales channels ([1,56], etc.), others have focused on how consumers make compassions between Internet stores [13,48]. It can be said that the latter has received more attention from IS researchers. Prior findings in this area indicated that when consumers compare an Internet store to another Internet store, several website characteristics (e.g. design, content, security, etc.) are taken into account. These findings have been effectively used to produce guidelines and the guidelines have been widely adopted by web designers, rendering a friendlier and more secure environment for consumers to make purchases.

The current study takes a different approach and focuses only on the comparison process between Internet stores and brick-and-mortar stores mainly because it strives to examine the impact of business strategies on consumer decision-making. It does not however neglect the fact that consumers can also make comparisons between Internet stores (e.g. walmart.com versus target.com). While the results of studying consumers comparison process between Internet stores are tremendously beneficial to online communities, it does not allow IS researchers to investigate the role of business strategies in consumer decision-making. In addition, comparison between Internet stores has been rigorously studied and its prior findings have become common knowledge for researchers in this area. This study thereby emphasizes the comparison process between Internet and brick-andmortar stores and further extends the knowledge in this area by investigating such a comparison process.

The current study integrates consumer perspectives about sales channels selection with the concept of business strategies. It adopts the following matrix to demonstrate the inherent impact of different business strategies on the customer preference development process (see Fig. 3). On the vertical side of the matrix, two common business strategies are presented: the pure Internet store and the hybrid strategies. Fig. 3 additionally manifests how business strategies could influence cross channel comparison (Internet stores versus brickand-mortar stores). When a business adopts the hybrid strategy, its customers could compare its Internet store to its own brick-and-mortar stores (cell 1: Internal Rivalry) or to its competitor’s brick-and-mortar stores (cell 2: External Rivalry). Unlike customers in the hybrid model, pure Internet stores’ customers are forced to compare the Internet store to its competitors’ brick-and-mortar stores (cell 3: Enforced External Rivalry). There is no corporate or ‘‘internal’’ brick-and-mortar store to which its customers can compare (cell 4: N/A).

Each cell in the proposed matrix represents different settings that an online store can segment their prospective customers. Customer segmentation is a common practice and well-established research area in the marketing discipline. In general, customer segmentation facilitates one-on-one marketing and can result in higher satisfaction to customers, lower costs, and/or higher profits to the firms. With more advanced database technology, firms are seeking new avenues to perform customer segmentation [5]. Traditional research generally employed user/ customer demographic information (i.e. gender, age, income, education, etc.) to perform the segmentations [33]. In recent years, a new approach of using customers’ attitude, motivation, and preference to segment customers has emerged [31,33]. This study utilizes such an approach together with sales channels strategies and segments online users into three different groups to explore how online customers make comparison of sales channels in different settings. Its proposed matrix illustrates that there is an inherent difference in the way that online users compare sales channels. For example, users who compare online and brick-andmortar stores from the same corporate business may have a neutral preference value toward product price offered by the two sales channels. Similar comparison may result different preference values when stores from different corporate businesses are compared. To provide concrete evidence to such an argument, this study attempts to integrate knowledge in the area of preferential decision with its proposed matrix.

## 3. Research method and data analysis

This study extends our knowledge in this area by examining how the four attributes (transaction cost, product, risk of their credit card, and social interaction)

are perceived in different comparison settings. Each comparison setting is defined in the proposed matrix of Fig. 3. The study examines how consumers compare the attributes when facing the choice of Internet versus brickand-mortar stores (inside or outside corporate business). The online survey is the underlying methodology of this study and survey pages were made available at a privately hosted website. Approximately, 1000 email invitations were sent to prospective participants in the United States and 353 people accepted the invitation. Of 353 consumer participants, 320 responses were usable. To expedite the process of data collection, a number of student subjects at a university in a metropolitan area were invited to participate, as well. Using student data is deemed appropriate for research in this area. A growing number of IS studies employ students to represent online consumers (e.g. [19,35]). Thereby, one hundred and thirty eight students (138) participated and one hundred and fifteen (115) students (graduate and undergraduate) completed the survey. Before combining the two groups of respondents, a MANOVA test was performed to find significant differences in their online experiences. Two survey questions were provided to them to measure how long they have used the Internet. The MANOVA test did not reveal any significant difference (see Appendix B). Thus, four hundred and thirty-five (435) respondents completed the questionnaires, and their data were used to conduct the analysis. The sample group contained 263 males (60.46%) and 172 females (39.54%). None of the participants were older than 49 years of age. A majority of the subjects reported income levels in the range of US\$ 30,001–45,000. The respondent profiles generally matched those of typical web users [55].

The survey questions allowed the respondents to select an actual Internet store. Respondents were then asked to provide the name of a brick-and-mortar store that they could use to compare with their selected Internet store. The respondents then compared their selected Internet and brick-and-mortar stores based on the four representative attributes and also on the ADP. A scale of 1–7 was used to measure preference level. On this scale, 1 indicated that the brick-and-mortar store was highly more favorable, 4 indicated the neutral point where respondents did not perceive a difference between the two selected sales channels, and 7 indicated that their selected Internet store was highly more favorable. The ADP and store’s attributes were multi-itemed variables. ADP, Transaction Cost Preference, Product Preference, Risk Preference, and Social Interaction Preference included 3, 2, 3, 5, and 3 items respectively (see Appendix A). Previous results of the measurement model and structural model testing can be found at our previous study [36].

## 4. Data analysis

Based on the proposed matrix (Fig. 3), 82 respondents were categorized into the group of Internal Rivalry model, where their selected Internet store and brick-and-mortar store were in the same corporate business (e.g. BarnesAndNoble.com versus Barnes and Noble). Fifty five (55) respondents were categorized into the group of External Rivalry (e.g. WalMart.com versus Target’s brick-and-mortar store). Two hundred and ninety eight (298) respondents were categorized into the group of enforced External Rivalry (e.g. Amazon.com versus Border bookstore). Such a division of respondents into different sections reveals an interesting outlook. The majority of the respondents generally think of the Internet stores that adopt the Pure Internet Store strategy (Enforced External Rivalry), when they consider making online purchases (almost 70% of users). Such a finding showed a need for the businesses that adopt the hybrid strategy to urgently promote their visibility in this competitive market.

Descriptive statistics, in Table 1, showed that the average of ADP for all three groups was less than 4.00, which was the neutral point of comparison. This revealed that the majority of respondents preferred their selected brick-and-mortar stores to Internet stores. Such a finding explains the minute level of online sales revenue when compared to the national GDP [54]. Also, it is worth nothing that the respondents in the group of External Rivalry have the average value of ADP close to the neutral of 4.0. More detailed descriptive statistics can be found in Appendix C. Graphical descriptive statistics were additionally manifested in Fig. 4.

Fig. 4 summarizes how each group of respondents compared their selected sales channels. For the purpose of presentation, each bar-chart represents the average values of the items that belong to the different attributes. Based on Fig. 4, it could be said that all three groups of respondents had similar opinions in terms of the social experience attribute, risk to their credit card, and ADP. Not only do they have an overall preference toward brick-and-mortar stores, all three groups of respondents also agreed that there were more social interactions and that there was less risk to their credit cards from brickand-mortar stores. This is consistent with the prior findings in the area [1].

Post hoc analysis of the MANOVA test is provided in Appendix D. An implication of this result will be discussed thereafter.

In addition to the above findings, this study conducted a structural equation modeling test separately for each group of respondents to validate the relationships between attribute variables and ADP. Before testing the structural model, measurement models of the three groups of consumers were tested. Using a correlation matrix as the input, a test of the measurement model generated a strong measure of fitness between the data and the proposed model (Fig. 2). For example, CFI, NFI, and NNFI were found to be between 0.83 and 0.95 and the RMR value was less than 1.00 [10,22,42] (see Appendix E).

## 5. Discussion and implication of research

In terms of theoretical contributions, the current study proposes a new avenue to examine consumer decision-making in the online shopping environment. We are able to provide a richer understanding and empirical evidence of how users’ preferences are formed and what attributes are important to them. Our result confirms the relationship between ADP and ABPs. It additionally indicates that investigating the relationships between decomposed ABPs to ADP is imperative. Contradicting the traditional beliefs in prior research, our results indicate that different attributes may have different relationships to ADP, depending upon the context and group of users.

We found that business strategies play an important role in the consumer preference development process. Two business strategies, the pure Internet store model and the hybrid model, were used together with user’s choices of brick-and-mortar stores to develop a matrix.

The matrix proposes three different circumstances that consumers could experience in the E-marketplace, including Internal Rivalry, External Rivalry, and Enforced External Rivalry. While the past stream of research in this area has led us to believe that the four preferential attributes (transaction cost, risk, social experience, and Product Preferences) are important in all circumstances, we found that they have varying degrees of importance in different situations. Below is a set of new strategies and business implications derived from our findings.

According to our findings, a number of business strategies can be considered. First, it could be said that online consumers are generally price sensitive. For all three groups of consumers, Transaction Cost Preference was found to be a significant factor that influences consumers’ overall comparison of sales channels. Businesses that have multiple sales channels (the Hybrid Model) could adopt a price discrimination strategy. A business can sell their products to online customers less expensively to enhance the popularity and acceptance of its business websites. Such a strategy has been implemented by several companies, such as Comp USA, Northwest Airlines, and T-Mobile. Such a pricing strategy is, however, applicable only to the Internal Rivalry and External Rivalry business settings. It could not be applied to the Enforced External Rivalry group due to the absence of their corporate brick-andmortar sales channels.

The pressure to compete by price for businesses in the Enforced External Rivalry group (pure Internet store) is heightened when taking the rank of the transaction cost attribute into account. Their customers ranked financial transaction cost the most important factor when comparing sales channels. Businesses in this situation could adopt a more frontal assault strategy to compete with their competitors’ brick-and-mortar stores. Such a strategy has also been implemented by highly successful Internet stores such as Letstalk.com, a store that is well known for its competitive prices for cellular products.

Risk Preference appears to be a significant factor only for the Internal Rivalry and Enforced External Rivalry groups. Businesses in these environments should give more priority to allocating their resources toward developing secure websites and a fraudprotection policy to gain their customers’ confidence. Based on the findings, the perceived risk attribute could not, however be considered a significant factor for the External Rivalry group. It could be articulated that customers in the External Rivalry group might be more risk tolerant than those in the internal and Enforced

External Rivalry groups. We do not however imply that businesses in the External Rivalry group should neglect the issue of developing a secure website. Developing a secure website has become one of the fundamental elements of conducting online business. Our guideline mainly suggests that online business could prioritize their resources more effectively if they have a better understanding of their customers preference formation.

Product Preference was found to be a significant attribute only for the enforced External Rivalry group. This study therefore suggests that in order to adopt the pure Internet store business model, one should provide a wider range of product selection. On the other hand, this attribute was found to be insignificant in the Internal Rivalry and External Rivalry business settings. Such a finding could be used to conclude that customers in the hybrid model (Internal Rivalry and External Rivalry) are more goal-oriented. They might have a preconception of what to purchase and therefore do not need wide ranges of product variety.

In sum, the merit of this study lies in the guidance for businesses to prioritize their resources more effectively. To follow the above guidelines, an online customer database has to be implemented and maintained so that a business can identify the location of its target customers. Information, such as zip code, will be beneficial for businesses to segment their customers into different segments. For instance, Wal-Mart may be able to group a customer from the zip code 00001 to the External Rivalry group because the customer may not have convenient access to a Wal-Mart brick-and-mortar store in the nearby area of this zip code, thus rendering a higher likelihood for this customer to compare Walmart.com to its competitor’s brick-and-mortar store. Later, Wal-Mart can follow the above guidelines to identify the use of their resources.

The results of this study demonstrated that consumer behavior in the electronic market is far more complex than we originally thought. Businesses should segment their consumers into appropriate groups so that they can manage their relationships properly. Conversely, consumers could adopt different approaches when comparing different sets of Internet and brick-and-mortar stores. Also, as we have seen, some variables that are considered significant in one comparison setting may not be important in others. The guidelines proposed above could also be used to better allocate business resources. For example, a brick-and-mortar retailer that attempts to entice its existing customers to move to its online store (Internal Rivalry) should acknowledge that its customers are more concerned about personal risk (e.g. risk to their credit card information), product price, and social interaction. A business in this category may not however have to spend as much on resources to provide a wider-range of product variety at its online stores. In sum, online businesses should take the consumer comparison approaches and their current business strategies into account when developing business strategy in this competitive market.

## 6. Directions for future studies

This study examined a comparison process of two sales channels. In reality, consumers encounter more choices of sales channels, such as direct sales, the Home Shopping Network, catalogs, etc. Future studies could adopt a similar approach to investigate how business models influence other sales channel comparisons (e.g. Internet versus Catalog). In addition, future studies could incorporate more attributes, such as trust, product characteristics into the model.

We encourage future studies to investigate the relationship between attributes. The concept of compensatory and non-compensatory attributes could be used to expand our knowledge in this area. A customer who is more price-conscious could be willing to take more risk and/or sacrifice more social experience in the purchasing process. Future studies could also investigate at what degree risk and social preferences could be compensated for by lower product prices. Having such a clear picture of compensatory and non-compensatory attributes could allow online businesses to entice new customers and properly maintain relationships with their existing ones.

Last but not least, we encourage future studies to examine the relationship between preferences and other psychological variables. Incorporating preference to important theories in IS research, such as the innovation diffusion theory, the theory of reasoned action, and the technology acceptance model, could provide an avenue to explain why a user refuses to adopt a proposed technology, which is an on-going topic in IS research.
