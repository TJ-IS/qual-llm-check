# The Effects of IP Address Revelation on Location-Based Name-Calling and User Engagement: Evidence From a Chinese Online Platform

Yuxin Zhang,<sup>1</sup> Dafeng Xu<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Wayne State University, USA, yuxin\_zhang@wayne.edu <sup>2</sup>University of Washington, USA, dafengxu@uw.edu

## Abstract

IP address revelation policies are a novel IT intervention designed to regulate user behaviors through de-anonymity and, in China, are implemented to curb misinformation. However, these policies may inadvertently encourage online attacks due to revealed geographic information. There is limited theoretical and empirical understanding of how these interventions influence user behaviors. Drawing on social identity theory, we introduce the scenario of “multiple identities” and analyze how individuals attack others—ostensibly because of the revealed geographic identity but fundamentally due to another salient and substantive identity of actual interest. We analyzed identities and attacks using data from Hupu.com, a Reddit-type platform. We observed an increase in location-based name-calling—where users attack others based on revealed geographic information—following Hupu’s implementation of the IP address revelation policy. Name-calling occurred specifically as political attacks and operated through a proxy model—instead of a choice model—of multiple identities, where the geographic identity was correlated with a more salient political identity, reflecting region-specific political stereotypes. Users leveraged this model to launch attacks for practical reasons, such as avoiding censorship. Relatedly, the policy led to a shift in content and a reduction in users’ geographic representation, as increased hostility discouraged indepth contributions and deterred participation from users in certain regions. These findings suggest digital interventions revealing one identity (e.g., geographic) may unintentionally affect behaviors associated with another identity (e.g., political), emphasizing the need for careful design of interventions to enhance digital responsibility.

Keywords: Name-Calling, Identity, De-Anonymity, Digital Responsibility, Intervention

## 1 Introduction

As online platforms grow in influence—facilitating information sharing (Zhang & Watts, 2008), opinion exchange (Marett & Joshi, 2009), and emotional expression (Zhang et al., 2013) on a massive scale— they also become environments where misinformation, harassment, and other abusive behaviors can thrive (Chan et al., 2023; Song & Oh, 2018; Wong et al., 2021). In response, platforms face increasing pressure to regulate digital spaces while addressing concerns about privacy, misinformation, and user safety (Mitchell & El-Gayar, 2022; Nguyen et al., 2024; Pan et al., 2023). Enforcing these priorities without stifling engagement has become a key challenge.

One response to these challenges has been to reduce anonymity (Cho & Kwon, 2015; Moore et al., 2012), such as revealing user identity markers like geographic information (Liu et al., 2024), with the intent of helping users assess content credibility and identify potential sources of misinformation. While these measures are designed to build a sense of accountability within the platform, they also carry the risk of encouraging hostile interactions (Chan et al., 2023; Nguyen et al., 2024). Users may reinterpret and repurpose this identity information to reinforce polarized perspectives and exacerbate existing social divides (Guo et al., 2023). In this paper, we examine a specific policy intervention: the revelation of coarse geographic information. We investigate its influence on user behavior in both the short and long term, focusing on its immediate impact on language use and the broader shifts in user engagement over time.

Understanding the immediate shifts in user interactions is crucial, as early behavioral responses provide insight into how users perceive the intervention and how it shapes norms of engagement, which, in turn, can influence long-term patterns of interaction (Burtch et al., 2018). In particular, we focus on a specific form of online attack—the use of location-based name-calling, which is defined as weaponizing geographic identity as a means of attack on other users on online platforms. This behavior leverages geographic markers to reinforce group-based antagonism, attack perceived out-group members, or undermine opposing viewpoints. While existing research showed that revealing geographic information increased the use of location-based name-calling as attacks on Weibo.com, a follower-based platform (e.g., Guo et al., 2023), little is known about the effects on topic-based platforms, whose structure differs significantly from that of follower-based platforms. In this paper, we ask: (1) Does revealing geographic information on topic-based platforms also affect user behaviors, and if so, how? And, more importantly: (2) What are the underlying mechanisms driving behavioral shifts specifically on topic-based platforms?

Beyond the changes in behaviors directly related to location-based name-calling, it is equally important to examine how the revelation of geographic information reshapes long-term user engagement. We specifically analyze two long-term effects: (1) content shifts measured by the text length and the extent of locationbased name-calling, and (2) changes in users’ geographic representation. These factors are essential for the sustainability of user-generated content platforms, as platforms depend on both the continued production effort of existing users and the ability to attract new users (Goes et al., 2016; Pu et al., 2020). The length of individual pieces of content can reflect user engagement, such as the levels of production effort and cognitive investment (Burtch et al., 2018; Pu et al., 2020; Qiao et al., 2024). A decline in content length and an increase in the extent of the use of location-based name-calling may indicate a shift toward less deliberative participation. Additionally, changes in geographic representation can signal whether users feel encouraged to remain active or choose to disengage. Users may withdraw from participation if they perceive the online environment as hostile (Cao et al., 2020), and their disengagement will affect the platform’s long-term success. Understanding these behavioral changes is important, as they reveal the consequences of policy interventions on discourse evolution and user engagement.

Interventions baased on the IP address<sup>1</sup> were introduced in China in 2022 to due to concerns about misinformation and disinformation, particularly that attributed to users outside of China (Reuters, 2022). During this period, Chinese officials and state media emphasized that “foreign forces” were spreading misleading narratives about the Russo-Ukrainian War, COVID-19, and China’s domestic affairs (e.g., Greitens, 2022). This government messaging framed foreign influence as a pervasive threat and strengthened platform users’ perception that online discussions needed active protection against foreign influence. Amid this climate, geographic identity—whether domestic or foreign—may have influenced user interactions by reinforcing in-group cohesion and intensifying hostility toward perceived outsiders, especially in politically sensitive discussions.

Using data collected from Hupu.com, a popular Reddittype internet platform in China, we estimate the effects of the IP address revelation policy on user behaviors under the specific setting of a politically charged online environment, where government narratives on foreign misinformation and disinformation heightened ideological divisions. As a comprehensive platform, Hupu.com provides a large number of posts, each of which contains an original post and numerous replies to the original post or other replies. In addition to the large sample size, three other unique features make Hupu.com an ideal data source for our paper. First, Hupu.com is a topic-based platform where interactions are organized around shared interests rather than personal connections. Unlike follower-based platforms, which foster ties through friends or follow functions, Hupu users form connections through mutual participation in topic-specific discussions. Second, we were able to examine a comparable online community that operates without any IP revelation policy: a Reddit community established by Hupu users. This provided a natural comparison group because it shares similar content themes and website structure with Hupu.com but has never implemented any policy related to geographic identity. This allowed us to consider a broader spectrum of regulatory environments, helping to contextualize the behavioral shifts observed under policy implementation on Hupu. Third, unlike other platforms, Hupu.com publicizes IP information retrospectively, i.e., original posts and replies created before any policy implementation still have observable IP information.

The IP revelation policy was introduced in stages. The initial policy, implemented in May 2022, displayed only the IP region of the original post’s creator (“partial implementation”); the full policy, implemented in January 2023, extended IP region visibility to all users posting replies (“full implementation”). Our primary analysis focuses on the full implementation, as this stage is most relevant for understanding how identity-based cues influence user behavior. This is because discussions primarily unfold within replies, where user interactions often escalate beyond the intended topic of the original post. While the post provides an initial starting point, the most intense exchanges occur between replies, and replies not only drive most user engagement and discussions but also reflect the broader platform environment. Therefore, analyzing behavioral changes in response to full geographical identity disclosure provided comprehensive insight into the policy’s impact. The partial implementation, which offered a limited form of identity disclosure, did not create the same conditions for identitydriven interactions and was therefore examined separately in a post hoc analysis at the end of this paper’s empirical analysis.

Our research yields three primary findings that reveal notable changes in user behavior, content generation, and user engagement following the implementation of the IP region revelation policy in a politically charged online environment. First, the use of location-based namecalling as a means of attack significantly increased after the full implementation of the policy, highlighting a distinct behavioral change. This change was driven by two key factors: (1) the political stereotype, which was revealed to be the most salient among all stereotypes associated with geographic identity, and (2) how users navigated, responded to, and leveraged multiple identities. Second, there was a significant decline in the number of words in text posted by users and a greater proportion of words related to location-based namecalling following the full policy implementation, suggesting that the policy had unintended negative effects on user engagement. Third, the geographic representation of users on Hupu.com became more concentrated after the full implementation, with a marked increase in users with Chinese IPs and a decline in users with non-Chinese IPs. These behavioral shifts did not emerge following the partial implementation of the policy.

Our research makes several key contributions to the literature on online abusive behaviors, digital interventions, and user engagement. First, we investigate the underlying mechanism that drives identity-based interactions when coarse identity information is revealed, within a politically charged digital environment.

Understanding this mechanism is essential for designing digital interventions that mitigate online attacks while maintaining user engagement. Second, our research addresses a significant gap in the literature by examining topic-based online platforms. Prior studies have primarily focused on follower-based platforms, which differ significantly in network structure and user interaction, leaving topic-based platforms largely unexamined. Our study contributes to the literature by highlighting how topic-based platforms shape user interactions and the risks they pose to inclusivity and accountability. Third, our research provides a longitudinal perspective on intervention effects, revealing not only immediate shifts in user interaction but also long-term consequences for user engagement. This offers broader insights into the unintended effects of digital interventions. Fourth, our research deepens the theoretical understanding of the interplay between identity, contextual salience, and platform environments in shaping digital behaviors, providing a foundation for more responsible digital practices that align with sociological and psychological theories of identity and online behaviors.

Our research has critical implications for digital responsibility, particularly in the design of platform policies and the understanding of the trade-offs of identity-based interventions. While prior studies suggest that reducing anonymity can enhance accountability, our findings reveal that such interventions can also escalate abusive behaviors and reshape user engagement in unintended ways. This highlights the complex tradeoffs that online platforms must navigate when implementing identity-related policies. By demonstrating how identity cues influence users’ behaviors and engagement over time, our paper offers valuable considerations for balancing transparency, accountability, and harm mitigation.

Section 2 reviews the background and the setting of our research, Section 3 builds the theoretical foundation and develops the hypotheses, Section 4 introduces data and methods, Section 5 reports empirical findings, and Section 6 presents discussions based on these findings and concludes the paper.

## 2 Background and Research Setting

## 2.1 Hupu.com and IP Revelation Policies

In 2022, many Chinese online platforms decided to display the regions of IP addresses of users who publish their opinions online. This also applied to Hupu.com, a Reddit-type internet forum (its interface will be presented later in this section). The website administrators adopted two different policies. Starting from May 2022, Hupu.com began to display the IP regions of creators of original posts. Starting in January 2023, Hupu.com further began to display the IP regions of creators of all replies to the original posts or other replies. IP addresses were displayed at the level of provincial regions, such as “Guangdong Province” or “New York State.” The idea behind the IP region display policy was to regulate online language by reducing misinformation (Reuters, 2022).

Hupu.com is a prominent, comprehensive Chinese internet forum known for its extensive user base and diverse range of discussion topics, akin to Reddit. Hupu.com is a topic-based online platform on which users connect with others primarily through discussions in posts, rather than friendship formation like on Facebook. Launched in 2004, Hupu.com initially focused on sports, particularly basketball, but has since expanded quickly to include a wide range of topics involving politics, entertainment, and technology, for example. This setting allows users to share information, engage in discussions, and express their opinions on a wide array of subjects. Hupu.com has become a significant online platform in China, attracting millions of active users who contribute to its dynamic discourse. Its popularity and broad scope make it an influential player in shaping public opinion and online culture within the country.

But, most notably, Hupu.com has developed into an internet forum in which politics has become one of the most heated topics. While a similar political forum outside of China, such as the Chinese-language forum on Reddit.com, may contain both international politics and domestic politics in a particular country, Hupu.com (and many other Chinese platforms) has a more visible focus on international politics as a result of censorship (Fang & Repnikova, 2018). Such censorship does not necessarily eliminate all discussions of China’s domestic politics, but it does lead to users’ discussions of international politics that reflect their political opinions on not only foreign but also domestic affairs (Shao & Wang, 2017). This is because (1) the Chinese government plays a key role in international politics, often linking its official foreign policy stances to domestic policies, and (2) users on online platforms have greater freedom to express opinions on foreign affairs. These discussions can indirectly reflect their views on the government’s domestic affairs, as direct comments on domestic policies, especially negative ones, are more likely to face censorship.

Similar to Reddit.com, a post on Hupu.com contains an original post published by a user; other users can reply to the original post or to another reply published earlier under the original post (or another reply). ## 2.2 Location-Based Name-Calling

Since the onset of the Russo-Ukrainian War in February 2022, discussions on Chinese online platforms using offensive language to attack others have surged dramatically. This increase is largely driven by sharp differences in political stances. When users encounter posts or replies that express political opinions divergent from their own, they are more likely to respond with derogatory language aimed at attacking those with opposing views. The highly polarized nature of the conflict has intensified emotions and provoked stronger reactions, leading to a proliferation of hostile exchanges. These aggressive interactions are often fueled by deepseated beliefs and the need to assert one’s political stance, further exacerbating the prevalence of offensive language in online discussions.

The use of offensive language to attack others on online platforms is not exclusively tied to political discourse, and this paper considers all kinds of offensive language. Users frequently employ derogatory terms to attack others with differing non-political opinions as well. Topics such as sports, entertainment, technology, and lifestyle can all provoke heated debates. When disagreements arise in these areas, users may resort to offensive language to demean or discredit those holding opposing views.

We focused on location-based name-calling as offensive language for two practical reasons. The first reason is the censorship landscape in China and on Hupu.com. In general, website administrators rigorously scan the use of derogatory language aimed personally at users, making it difficult to measure changes in person-specific offensive language. Location-based name-calling behaviors are more likely to evade censorship because they do not contain sensitive keywords targeting individuals. As a result, location-based name-calling is commonly seen as a type of attack on Hupu.com. The second reason is that the IP revelation policy includes geographic information as a variable in online discussions, which aligns with location-based name-calling that similarly concerns geographic identity; we found the variables regarding the policy and behaviors to be comparable.

## 2.3 Research Setting

The implementation of the IP revelation policy on Hupu.com took place when Chinese officials and state media actively promoted the idea that foreign entities were spreading misleading narratives about the Russo-Ukrainian War, COVID-19, and domestic affairs. This government messaging reinforced the idea that “foreign actors” were attempting to manipulate public opinion and undermine social stability (Greitens, 2022). For example, in May 2022, the Chinese Ministry of Foreign Affairs published a document titled “Falsehoods Spread by the US on the Ukraine Issue: A Reality Check,” which explicitly accused the US of engaging in information warfare to manipulate global perceptions (Embassy of the People’s Republic of China, 2022).

This government messaging had a profound impact on the sociopolitical climate, particularly on the online environment in China, by intensifying ideological tensions. The IP revelation policies are part of such message conveyance, and these policies may affect user behaviors and engagement. As the Chinese public relies heavily on social media for political information, online platforms can significantly shape public opinions (Chen et al., 2023; Pan et al., 2022)—even a small proportion of strategically positioned influential users on online platforms can significantly influence people’s opinions and beliefs (Ross et al., 2019).

## 3 Theoretical Background and Hypothesis Development

## 3.1 Digital Interventions to Prevent Online Attacks

Online attacks remain one of the most pervasive challenges in digital environments (Nguyen et al., 2024; Wong et al., 2021). Prior studies have demonstrated negative consequences, such as psychological distress and a chilling effect on free expression (Chan et al., 2019, 2023; Lowry et al., 2017; Wright, 2018). These outcomes punctuate the urgent need for effective interventions. Online platforms are the primary arenas for these interactions and thus have a responsibility to proactively address such attacks (Davison et al., 2023).

To mitigate online attacks, many platforms have implemented various interventions. A particular type of intervention involves user information disclosure aimed at increasing accountability (Pu et al., 2020). The rationale behind such interventions is that individuals are more likely to engage in prosocial behavior and refrain from harmful actions when their identities or actions are tied to their real-world persona. For instance, real-name policies on platforms like Facebook are initially introduced to foster civility and reduce harassment, based on the premise that de-anonymity may discourage harmful behaviors (Halpern & Gibbs, 2013). Similarly, South Korea’s Real-Name Verification Law requires users of major online platforms to verify identities using government-issued IDs, thereby creating a sense of responsibility regarding their behaviors (Cho & Kwon, 2015).

However, these policies have faced significant criticism due to privacy concerns (Cho & Kwon, 2015). The disclosure of personal information, such as full names or precise locations, has been associated with the risk of online attacks. This highlights the trade-off between transparency and privacy, challenging platforms to balance the need for accountability with the ethical imperative to protect users. In particular, while real-name policies are widely adopted in the context of personal social networks (e.g., Facebook and LinkedIn), they are rarely seen on topic-based platforms like Reddit and Hupu.

## 3.2 Revelation of Coarse Geographical Information

The display of coarse geographical information at the regional level has gained attention as another type of intervention aimed at fostering transparency and reducing harmful behaviors. By providing a lower level of de-anonymity, the approach can strike a balance between reduced anonymity and privacy. Research shows that users typically perceive coarse IP information disclosure as less invasive and not a significant breach of privacy (Liu et al., 2024; Zhu, 2024).

The display of coarse geographical information has been implemented on multiple platforms in China (Guo et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024; Zhu, 2024). The intent of IP revelation policies across platforms is consistent: to address the challenges of misinformation and disinformation, particularly from users with non-Chinese IPs (Reuters, 2022). Further, displaying coarse geographic information is promoted as a means to reduce online attacks among users by encouraging them to be more accountable for their behaviors.

Prior studies have provided mixed insights into the implications of policies revealing IP regions, highlighting both their potential benefits and unintended consequences. On the one hand, scholars have suggested that users generally perceive the disclosure of coarse geographical information as less invasive compared to other transparency measures, making it a more acceptable intervention that raises fewer privacy concerns (Liu et al., 2024; Zhu, 2024). On the other hand, scholars have shown that this practice can lead to aggressive behaviors, most notably the increase in location-based name-calling on follower-based online platforms (Guo et al., 2023).

Despite these findings, the body of research on IP region revelation remains limited. In particular, there is a lack of investigation within the information systems (IS) field, where understanding the interplay between digital interventions, user behaviors, and platform governance is of critical importance. This gap underscores the need for more comprehensive research to explore the broader impacts of revealing coarse geographical information on user behaviors, engagement, and underlying mechanisms in diverse online environments.

## 3.3 IP Revelation on Topic-Based Online Platforms

While prior research has investigated the revelation of coarse geographical information on follower-based platforms like Weibo (e.g., Guo et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024), the mechanism underlying location-based namecalling following the revelation of IP information on online platforms remains unexplored. Furthermore, prior research has exclusively focused on the impact of IP revelation on follower-based platforms, while its implications on topic-based platforms remain unexplored. Scholars and policymakers highlight the distinction between the two types of social networks in terms of the structure and user interactions (Bogdanov et al., 2014). Follower-based platforms, such as Weibo and Twitter, revolve around personal networks where users choose to follow and engage with specific individuals or accounts. These platforms can amplify social and ideological bubbles, as interactions are more likely to occur within homogeneous networks (Smith et al., 2013). In contrast, topic-based platforms, such as Reddit, organize interactions around shared interests or subjects rather than personal connections. These platforms tend to foster more heterogeneous discussions, as participants are drawn together by topics rather than preexisting relationships (Yu et al., 2024). Given these differences, it is important to investigate whether the revelation of coarse geographical information has similar impacts on user behaviors on topic-based online platforms and, more importantly, the underlying mechanisms driving the behavioral changes.

## 3.3.1 The Salience of Stereotypes

The IP region, as a revealed feature on a platform, represents an aspect of a user’s fundamental geographic identity similar to race and gender. This identity does not inherently convey a user’s other substantive characteristics, such as political stance, economic status, or cultural norms, which can also be viewed as identities (political, economic, and cultural identities, respectively). However, the IP region may be perceived as a proxy for these more substantive identities through stereotypes. This occurs because the geographic identity and personal characteristics might correlate at the population level, leading to generalized assumptions (e.g., individuals from a particular region may be presumed to be “conservative” or “liberal”), even if a stereotype may not necessarily reflect individuals with that geographic identity (e.g., a specific user from the region stereotyped as “liberal” might not actually be liberal).

Multiple identity-related stereotypes may be associated with a single revealed identity (Settle, 2018; Zhang & Xu, 2023), reflecting the complex and multifaceted nature of identities. For instance, a particular region might carry political, cultural, and economic stereotypes simultaneously, each of which can be invoked depending on the context (Higgins, 2012; Xu, 2017). Existing research shows that the activation of specific stereotypes associated with a given identity is dependent on the context in which interactions occur (McGlone & Aronson, 2006). For example, scholars have found that the relevance of a stereotype increases when it aligns with the cues and themes present in the immediate context (Higgins, 2012). This suggests that the same geographic identity may trigger different stereotypes depending on whether the discussion focuses on politics, culture, or economic conditions. Similarly, scholars have found that perceptual salience and cultural cues play critical roles in shaping which stereotypes are activated. This shows that participants exposed to different contexts invoke contrasting stereotypes about the same group (Hutchison et al., 2018).

We propose a different perspective on the activation of stereotypes, arguing that on a topic-based online platform, it is the broader “platform environment” that determines which stereotype is invoked, while the immediate context of the discussion or conversation plays a minor role. The platform environment, shaped by its most polarized or dominant themes, emphasizes certain stereotypes over others and makes them universally salient across discussions. On followerbased platforms, discussions are organized around specific influencers, and participants in these discussions tend to be relatively stable over time due to the “follow” mechanism. As a result, the stereotypes prevalent in these discussions often reflect the influencer’s identity and attitudes rather than the broader platform environment. In contrast, topic-based platforms have less person-specific participation patterns, as users engage with discussions based on shared interest in the topic rather than loyalty to certain individuals. This feature leads to discussions that are more reflective of the stereotypes dominant in the entire platform environment.

Our exploratory content analysis (see Section 4.1 and the Appendix for details) indicates that this platform-wide environmental influence is particularly evident on Hupu.com, prominent even before the implementation of the IP revelation policy. Government messaging had already helped form strong political stereotypes with geographic identity, thereby shaping a broader online environment; thus, users began to anticipate the use of location information to confirm these political stereotypes. Therefore, the nature of Hupu suggests that among all stereotypes that can be potentially linked to location information, political stereotypes are most correlated with the IP region. Based on actual users’ posted content, political stereotypes were indeed the most mentioned topic in discussions of the IP revelation policy.

## 3.3.2 Location-Based Name-Calling as a Means of Attack

Building on the findings (see Appendix) that political stereotypes tied to location are salient on Hupu.com, we turn to the question of when and why these stereotypes lead to location-based name-calling. While the platform’s environment leads users to view location through a political lens, location-based name-calling may not occur universally across all discussions. Instead, it is likely that specific types of posts or replies—particularly those centered around politics— act as triggers for such attacks. Political discussions amplify the relevance of ideological differences, making it more likely for users to interpret location as a marker of political alignment and to direct hostility at other users who hold opposing opinions.

Social identity theory (SIT) provides a foundational framework for understanding why users with non-Chinese IPs who express differing political opinions become targets in the context of political discussions. In this theory, “identity” pertains to how individuals classify and perceive themselves through their membership in social groups. According to SIT, individuals derive their self-concept from these group affiliations, categorizing both themselves and others into in-groups and out-groups based on specific identity markers (Tajfel & Turner, 1985). SIT has been extensively applied in information systems research to analyze behaviors influenced by social identity. For example, Mishra et al. (2012) explored the assimilation of electronic health record systems in physician practices, drawing on concepts from identity theory and SIT; Mueller et al. (2019) explored the complexity of large enterprise resource planning programs, using SIT as a foundational framework for interpretation; Pan et al. (2017) leveraged identity theories to explore how two levels of social self-identity differently influence reinforced and varied social media usage while also examining how inertia moderates these effects.

SIT explains how individuals categorize themselves and others into in-groups and out-groups based on salient identity markers and react based on these markers (Tajfel & Turner, 1985). On Hupu.com, the combination of a non-Chinese location and political opinions different from the Chinese government’s official stance (or “antiofficial” opinions) creates a distinct out-group that stands in opposition to the in-group. This out-group categorization is particularly evident when political discussions are involved. These discussions highlight ideological differences and heighten the perception of opposition. In non-political discussions, these identity markers may not hold the same relevance, and hostility toward users with foreign IPs may thus be weaker.

According to SIT, individuals emphasize the most visible, salient, and distinctive markers of out-group identity during intergroup conflict. On Hupu.com, geographic identity became highly visible following the implementation of the IP revelation policy, leading to the concept of “multiple identities,” a classical topic in social network research.<sup>2</sup> Here, multiple identities include (1) a fundamental identity, i.e. the geographic identity revealed after the IP policy, and (2) a substantive identity, such as an identity shaped by political opinions (e.g., anti-official) that may be revealed through a post’s or reply’s content. Many studies of multiple identities have found that unfriendly online behaviors—such as distrust or defriending—stemming from two users’ differences in one substantive identity may be mitigated by the presence of another shared identity between the two users; a typical example involves the interplay between political disagreements and racial homophily (Paik et al., 2023; Park et al., 2023). Essentially, these studies propose a “choice model,” in which multiple identities are simplified to a single identity based on the user’s preferences. For instance, studies on defriending behaviors, including the aforementioned papers, suggest that sharing the same racial identity decreases the likelihood of potential defriending due to political identity differences. In such cases, racial identity is often prioritized, leading to tolerance for differences in political identity and preventing defriending.

However, it is also theoretically possible that users interpret multiple identities in a way where one identity serves as an indicator or proxy of another through stereotypes, thereby encouraging rather than mitigating behaviors related to a particular identity. This suggests a “proxy model,” because user behaviors (such as attacks) targeting the proxied identity may need to be avoided for practical reasons, such as censorship. In this proxy model, location-based name-calling can act as a form of attack when discussions involve a salient stereotype. For example, if the political identity is most salient, then a user encountering a reply with an unfavorable political opinion may engage in location-based name-calling based on the correlation between the geographic identity and its stereotypical political identity.

In our paper’s context, there are indeed several practical reasons why a proxy for political identity is needed in launching attacks: utilizing this model reduces the risk of violating platform usage policies, which typically impose stricter censorship on personal attacks (e.g., targeting a user’s political identity) compared to non-personal attacks (e.g., targeting a geographic identity).

Convenience and effectiveness, driven by the recognition of stereotypes as common knowledge on a platform, are central factors contributing to location-based namecalling. When a regional stereotype is widely recognized, users can confidently engage in location-based namecalling, knowing their hostility will be readily understood by others. This ease of recognition reduces the cognitive effort required to formulate more complex arguments or engage in ideological debates. Such convenience of using location-based name-calling as a weapon aligns with the dynamics of online platforms, where rapid exchanges and low accountability foster emotionally charged and simplistic responses. At the same time, the polarized environment of Hupu.com amplifies the effectiveness of such behaviors, as attacks on non-Chinese IPs not only express personal hostility but also reinforce in-group values and narratives, strengthening group solidarity.

Together, these rationales provide an explanation for why location-based name-calling would become a preferred method of attack after an IP revelation policy is implemented. These rationales, however, center on the most salient stereotype. Therefore, while our first hypothesis proposes a general increase in location-based name-calling as a result of the IP revelation policy, our second hypothesis also posits a specific increase in location-based name-calling when political identity (i.e., the most salient stereotype in Hupu’s context) is central to the post’s or reply’s content:

H1: Location-based name-calling as a form of attack will increase after the full implementation of the IP revelation policy.

H2: Location-based name-calling as a form of political attack will increase after the full implementation of the IP revelation policy.

## 3.4 Shift in User Engagement

A potential increase in the use of location-based namecalling can indicate changes in sentiments—such as hostility—on the platform (Cheng et al., 2017). The changing sentiments on the platform may further alter patterns of user engagement, including in both the short and long run.

We first focus on content and study two interrelated aspects of engagement: (1) shifts in content length, and (2) shifts in the proportion of words expressing locationbased name-calling within the content. Content length serves as a crucial indicator of how users engage in discussions. First, discussions with more words are associated with more production effort (Pu et al., 2020; Qiao et al., 2024). Second, while a reduction in content length in individual discussions may not necessarily indicate decreased overall effort—e.g., users may compensate by posting more frequent but shorter responses (Pu et al., 2020)—length-related changes still signal shifts in users’ communication style. When users are continuously exposed to online attacks in the form of location-based name-calling, they may feel offended and experience both mental and emotional distress and exhaustion (Cao et al., 2020). Such mental and emotional burden would thereby impose an extraneous cognitive load, impairing users’ ability to process complex information and engage in complex discussions (Sweller, 1988). Initially, users may attempt to participate as before, but as this burden accumulates, they recognize that the environment is changing on the platform. They are thus likely to adjust their engagement strategies accordingly, shifting toward shorter, less cognitively demanding, and more reactive responses over time. As a result, the average content length may gradually decrease.

Driven by this rationale, we further examine the change in the proportion of words related to location-based namecalling within the total word count of the text, i.e., a post or reply. This measures the extent of location-based name-calling. An increase in the extent of location-based name-calling indicates a shift toward more reactive and emotional interactions, where users engage with identitybased attacks rather than substantive discussions. Such an engagement pattern ultimately suggests an adaptation to a less deliberative discussion style on online platforms.

Based on the above rationales, we hypothesize:

H3a: The length of texts (posts and replies) will decline after the full implementation of the IP revelation policy.

H3b: Relative to the total word count, the proportion of location-based name-calling in texts will increase after the full implementation of the IP revelation policy.

An extreme case of user disengagement is the departure from the platform. If changes in user behaviors and engagement patterns create hostility against users with certain identities, then these users may respond by reducing participation or leaving the platform (Cao et al., 2020). In this paper, disengagement may be most strongly associated with non-Chinese IPs, as users with these IPs may consider the platform as increasingly unwelcoming amid increased location-based namecalling. Over time, this selective disengagement may result in a more concentrated geographic representation in discussions. Understanding the shift in user composition on an online platform is critical, as it allows platform administrators and scholars to assess whether interventions are achieving their intended goals or inadvertently reshaping patterns of user engagement in the long run.

Based on this rationale, we hypothesize:

H4: The proportion of users with non-Chinese IPs declines after the full implementation of the IP revelation policy.

## 4 Data and Methods

## 4.1 Qualitative Content Exploration

Before the quantitative analysis, we first qualitatively explored the content to understand user attitudes toward the IP revelation policy and the broader platform environment. This provided the foundation for the subsequent quantitative analysis. We focused on 12 relevant posts on discussions of the policy with the highest number of replies; these posts were published around the time of the policy change. We categorized actual Hupu users’ opinions toward the policies. We summarize the findings of our content exploration here; detailed qualitative data can be found in the Appendix.

First, most Hupu users in our sample supported the implementation of the IP revelation policy because they thought this policy would help them link replies they disliked with IP information, thereby launching attacks based on political stereotypes. Such opinions existed throughout the entire sample, even before the implementation of any regulatory policy on Hupu.com. Second, some Hupu users expressed their concerns about attacks stemming from regional discrimination based on stereotypes. These concerns mainly appeared after the full implementation of the IP region display policy at the reply level.

These two types of behaviors suggest that users have incentives to both increase and decrease location-based name-calling. But, overall, texts from the most widely discussed posts presented in the qualitative analysis suggest that users on Hupu.com are more interested in the first type of behavior: launching attacks using location-based name-calling. In our sample, there were more discussions focusing on attacks than on defenses, and these discussions persisted for longer durations. These insights suggest support for H1 and H2, which we tested in the quantitative analysis, which posit that the IP revelation policy would result in a net increase in location-based name-calling, particularly among attackers focusing on politics and addressing political stereotypes.

Another finding from the content exploration is that users identified changes in user behaviors related to the length and composition of replies. Specifically, some users appeared to rely on location-based name-calling to launch attacks as an alternative to more careful discussions, thereby (1) increasing the proportion of words related to location-based name-calling in texts, and (2) decreasing the overall text length. These findings are associated with H3a and H3b, which address text length and the proportion of location-based name-calling text.

A further question is: Why do users appear to be so willing to launch location-based attacks? Many discussions in this exploratory analysis suggest a possible explanation that stems from geographic representation. Since Hupu.com is a website based in China, its users are mainly from China. Therefore, users are likely to be more tolerant of attacks on non-Chinese IPs and, at the same time, are less likely to experience attacks based on their own domestic regions. These experiences are related to H4, which posits that users with non-Chinese IPs may be gradually “driven out” due to location-based name-calling. If this is true, then the IP revelation policy should lead to some users’ disengagement from the platform when their geographic identities are under attack. More specifically, a possible consequence is the decline in the presence of non-Chinese IPs.

## 4.2 Quantitative Data and Empirical Strategies

Following qualitative content exploration, we now introduce the data retrieved from Hupu.com. Our main dataset contained posts (including original posts and replies) from late February 2022 to July 2023. The starting date, late February 2022, marks the eruption of the Russo-Ukrainian War, which subsequently led to the implementation of the full IP revelation policy in China months later. For each post, we collected information about the original post (OP), displayed at the top of the entire post, and all the replies following the OP. For each text (OP or replies), we were able to collect the date the text was posted, the user’s IP at the region (province, state, etc.) level, the order of the reply (e.g., the second reply under the OP), the word count, and most importantly, the content.

We relied on the texts of OPs and replies in the Hupu dataset to identify the appearance of location-based name-calling. In the sample, 99% of texts were replies. The full list of offensive words was coded based on posts and replies published on Hupu.com in 2021, supplemented by a published dictionary of offensive

Chinese words (Tien et al., 2022). In the empirical analysis, we treated every OP and reply as a single observation. The total size of the sample was 190,390 OPs and replies.

Table 1 presents summary statistics of this paper’s main dataset. Among the 190,390 texts in the Hupu sample, 6.6% contained location-based name-calling, identified based on our dictionary of offensive words. Among these cases, approximately 40% were related to political stereotypes: overall, 2.6% of all pieces of text in the sample were political location-based name-calling. The implementation of the full regulatory policy—showing every reply’s IP— occurred on January 1, 2023; thus, 46% of texts were in the pre-full-implementation subsample, and 54% were in the post-full-implementation subsample. The initial policy—showing only the original post’s IP region—was implemented on May 1, 2022, and only 17% of texts were in the subsample of data recorded prior to this initial policy. In the full sample, 90.5% of texts were published by users with Chinese IPs. The average word count of a text was 33.4, while 19.5% of all texts contained less than 10 words. Furthermore, we measured the proportion of words expressing location-based namecalling in the total word count in replies: For example, a 10-word reply containing two words that represented attacks on other users based on location-based namecalling would equal 20% for this reply. On average, 1% of the text expressed location-based name-calling.

Table 1 also presents the statistics of two subsamples split by January 1, 2023, when the full revelation policy—on both OPs and replies—was introduced. Compared to e the post-full-implementation subsample, in the pre-fullimplementation subsample, there were relatively fewer texts containing location-based name-calling (4.4% vs. 8.6%), fewer texts related to politics (1.4% vs. 3.5%), fewer texts published by users with Chinese IPs (89.8% vs. 91.1%), more words in each text (32.258 vs. 34.427), slightly fewer texts with less than 10 words (19.3% vs. 19.6%), and substantially lower proportions of words related to location-based name-calling in the text (0.6% vs. 1.3%). These statistics suggest possible changes in users’ online behaviors and engagement after the extensive display of their IPs.

In the empirical analysis, this baseline was extended in several ways. First, we used various other dependent variables in the model. Specifically, we examined location-based name-calling in terms of political attacks (H2), content shifts (H3a, H3b), and indication of non-Chinese IPs (H4).

Second, we considered a difference-in-differences regression by supplementing the Hupu dataset with a similar dataset retrieved from Reddit.com. We focused specifically on the Reddit community “China\_irl,” created by Hupu users who were also active on Reddit.com. As Reddit.com has never implemented any policy regarding the display of IP information, including Reddit data in the sample allowed us to treat the IP revelation policy as a placebo on Reddit.com and add its data as a control group in our analysis, thereby completing the empirical setting for a difference-in-differences model:

In Hupu’s context, there were two types of policies, one targeting OPs only and another targeting both OPs and replies. ## 5.1 Hypotheses 1: Location-Based Name-Calling

We now present the quantitative analysis of the IP revelation policy implemented on Hupu.com. In our main empirical analysis from Sections 5.1 to 5.3, we first focused on the full implementation of the IP revelation policy: we marked its occurrence with a solid line in bi-week −1, where the coefficient was set to 0 as the reference point. Figure 5 reports the results of testing H1, which examined the overall change in locationbased name-calling. In the figure on the left, we used only the Hupu sample; in the figure on the right, we also included the Reddit sample. The visualized coefficients are presented in Table 2.

The above results reflect three major conclusions. First, there was an increase in location-based name-calling in and after bi-week 0 (January 2023), suggesting a rise in the use of IP information to launch attacks following the IP revelation policy. Second, while the figure on the left focuses only on the Hupu sample, adding the Reddit sample in the figure on the right did not alter the conclusion of the increase in location-based namecalling in and after week 0. These two conclusions support H1, indicating an overall increase in locationbased name-calling.

Third, our previous conclusions rely on the assumption that any changes in location-based name-calling after the policy implementation were not associated with significant pre-trends. Otherwise, the observed changes may be explained by trends in user behaviors that existed before the policy, rather than the policy itself. In both the left and right figures, we observe that the majority of pre-policy coefficients were statistically insignificant at the 0.05 level, and the joint F-test indicates that pre-trends were not statistically associated with changes in location-based name-calling occurring after the full implementation of the IP revelation policy.

## 5.2 Hypothesis 2: Political Stereotypes and Attacks

We now move to H2. We posited that the IP revelation policy would affect location-based name-calling primarily when users were involved in political discussions, based on the rationale that political identity is the most salient identity on Hupu.com. In Figure 6, the figure on the left focuses on politically related locationbased name-calling. After week 0, the beginning of the full implementation of the IP revelation policy on all replies, all coefficients were positive and statistically significant, suggesting a rise in location-based namecalling related to political stereotypes. Most coefficients before bi-week 0 were statistically insignificant. Also, the results were generally not significant for the initial IP revelation policy on original posts only. These results support H2—that location-based name-calling as a form of political attack will increase.

However, Figure 6 presents more mixed, albeit not completely insignificant, empirical results in the figure on the right regarding texts that are not related to political discussions. After the full implementation of the IP revelation policy in bi-week 0, the first 10 post-policy coefficients were all statistically insignificant. In contrast to H2, this result shows that location-based name-calling as a form of non-political attack did not significantly increase. However, three of the next four coefficients, from bi-week 10 to bi-week 13, were positive and statistically significant. This suggests that the IP revelation policy may have led to non-political attacks through location-based name-calling, even though political identity is the most salient identity on Hupu.com. However, this conclusion does not support an immediate change in location-based name-calling as a form of nonpolitical attack after the full implementation of the IP revelation policy, given that the statistically significant coefficients emerged nearly half a year after the policy implementation.

If political stereotypes are the key driver of location-based name-calling, and anti-official opinions are often the target, then one would expect that users with Chinese IPs (who would be more likely to attack anti-official opinions) would be more likely to launch location-based name-calling as a form of political attack. We tested trends in such behaviors among the two types of users in Figure 7. In the figure on the left, we indeed observe a significant increase in location-based name-calling as a form of political attack among users with Chinese IPs. The trends are much less clear for users with non-Chinese IPs, as shown in the figure on the right. In other words, as observed earlier in Figure 6, location-based name-calling as a form of political attack appears to be mainly driven by users with Chinese IPs.

## 5.4 Post-Hoc Exploratory Analysis: Partial Policy Implementation

While our primary analysis focused on the full implementation of the IP region revelation policy, where location information was displayed for both original posts and replies, the policy was initially implemented in partial form. Under this partial implementation, only the location of original post authors was revealed, while replies remained anonymous in terms of geographic identity. This raises an interesting question: Is revealing the location of post authors alone sufficient to trigger identity-based attacks in a politically charged environment, or is full implementation necessary for this effect to materialize? A key consideration is that on Hupu.com, engagement is largely driven by replies, where discussions frequently extend beyond the original post’s intended topic and often escalate into more intense interactions. While the post serves as an initial starting point, aggressive behaviors primarily emerge in replies. These attacks are shaped more by the broader platform environment than by the post itself. Given this, partial implementation may not provide a sufficient context for users to engage in identity-based attacks at the same level as that observed under full implementation. Examining this distinction has implications for platform design, as it shows whether incremental interventions are sufficient in shaping online interactions.

## 6 Discussion

## 6.1 Summary of Findings

This study investigates the behavioral and systemic impacts of revealing coarse geographical information on topic-based online platforms in a politically charged online environment. We uncover the ways in which the digital intervention of the IP revelation policy we examined affected user behaviors and platform dynamics. We found that location-based name-calling, as a form of attack, increased significantly immediately after the full implementation of the policy, driven by the embedded salience of political stereotypes and the broader political context of the platform. However, we found no statistically significant behavioral shift following the partial implementation of the policy, suggesting that the level of the policy played a critical role in shaping the results.

In addition to these behavioral changes, our study identified three long-term shifts in user engagement following the full implementation of the policy. First, we observed a decline in the average length of usergenerated content. Second, the extent of location-based name-calling increased, as reflected by a higher proportion of words related to location-based namecalling in the texts. Third, the geographic representation of users declined, indicating a shift in the platform’s user composition. These findings present the trade-offs of interventions, where the intended goal of promoting accountability may be undermined by unintended negative effects.

Our findings also extend the understanding of how platform environments, rather than specific discussions, affect the activation of stereotypes tied to identity markers like IP information. By demonstrating how these effects unfold in a topic-based network, this study highlights the critical interplay between digital interventions, user behavior, and the platform-wide environment. Together, these insights offer a comprehensive view of the impacts of IP region revelation policies, providing a foundation for more thoughtful and balanced approaches to digital governance.

An interesting paradox behind the IP revelation policy or similar de-anonymization policies is their dual potential. While intended to reduce individual-level online attacks by increasing accountability, they can simultaneously foster group-level aggression and hostility (You & Lee, 2019). Our findings suggest that this paradox tends to primarily be manifested through hostility rather than accountability, when the level of privacy revelation is insufficient to establish true accountability. In the context of the IP revelation policy, the coarse granularity of the disclosed geographic information was not sufficient to reduce individual-level attacks as anticipated. Instead, it facilitated new forms of hostility in the form of location-based name-calling.

This highlights a critical limitation of de-anonymization measures: They can potentially reveal sufficient information to enable identity-based stereotyping and conflict but not to build meaningful accountability that could deter individual-level aggression. Our findings suggest that the effectiveness of de-anonymization policies relies on the degree of privacy revelation, and future research should investigate how the granularity of identity disclosure interacts with platform-specific dynamics to shape both individual and group-level behaviors.

## 6.2 Theoretical Contributions

Our research makes several theoretical contributions. To begin with, our research offers a theoretical perspective on the concept of multiple identities within the social identity theory (SIT). A methodological challenge of directly applying SIT to understand identity-related online behaviors is that users may possess multiple identities. Inside SIT, scholars require additional theoretical frameworks to explore how these multiple identities are perceived and interpreted by other users. Prior research centers on a “choice model,” which describes a scenario where users prioritize one most salient identity while having the option to consider another. This model is widely applied to the analysis of online political behaviors, such as instances where individuals sharing the same demographic identity tolerate political disagreements or where those sharing the same political identity break demographic segregation.

While the choice model reflects scenarios where the two identities are completely independent, it overlooks the possibility that users may equate the two identities, focusing only on one while treating the other as equivalent. Our findings point to a “proxy model,” wherein the two identities are correlated, resulting in their interpretation as interchangeable or reinforcing one another. We also identify the underlying mechanism driving this process: the role of stereotypes. Importantly, we show that users may leverage one less sensitive identity (e.g., IP) as a proxy for another, more sensitive identity (e.g., politics). We then show that the less sensitive identity not only serves as a mechanism for attacks but also reinforces the values and narratives of the in-group based on the more sensitive identities.

The above theoretical contribution regarding identity and stereotypes further connects to two more specific approaches to understanding online user behaviors. First, we demonstrate that when expressing out-group antagonism, the simplicity of the expression contributes to its adoption. Unlike complex ideological debates based on presenting detailed arguments, location-based name-calling requires minimal cognitive effort or information. Our research extends SIT by illustrating how the interplay between identity salience, simplicity, and platform polarization shapes identity-based hostility on online platforms.

Second, our research presents a theoretical perspective of stereotype activation. We demonstrate that on online platforms, the broad platform environment—shaped by both platform-wide norms and sociopolitical contexts— rather than the specific context of discussions, is the key feature that determines which stereotype will become salient and how this stereotype will influence user behaviors and engagement. While our empirical analysis focuses on political identity as a specific example, the theoretical insights on stereotype activation can be extended to platforms with other types of salient stereotypes. This new perspective on stereotypes emphasizes the systemic role of platformwide cues in shaping identity-based interactions, which are particularly pronounced in politically charged environments and prevalent on topic-based platforms.

Finally, our research provides a theoretical explanation for how an online platform’s policy regarding user information can influence user content generation. Prior studies have primarily emphasized the “reputation channel,” based on a straightforward assumption that revealing even partial user information incentivizes better content creation and suggests potential penalties for poor-quality content (Andreoni & Bernheim, 2009; Goes et al., 2016; Pu et al., 2020; Wasko & Faraj, 2005). We focus on a less straightforward yet equally influential channel: identity-based attacks. Our analysis suggests the possibility that a user’s utility from attacking others based on revealed personal information may outweigh the disutility of reputation loss, particularly if the revelation of personal information is considered minor by the user. Our analysis further explains the distinction between these two channels: reputation is tied to the personal information of a user as a content creator, while attacks are linked to the perceived identity of other users observed by that user. This underscores the importance of analyzing both sides of user interactions in understanding online behaviors.

## 6.3 Implications for Digital Responsibility

Our research provides important implications for digital responsibility, presenting the challenges of balancing accountability and obligation in platform governance. While some digital interventions, such as the revelation of user identity, are designed to promote accountability, they can also introduce unintended risks related to online behaviors, content, users’ mental well-being, and long-term engagement. These negative outcomes highlight the need for platform managers to not only design interventions with a single objective but also monitor and refine the interventions to ensure that they do not introduce unintended consequences that may harm users, the platform, or society.

In Hupu’s specific case, our empirical findings reveal that its IP revelation policy failed to foster accountability. Instead, users exploited the visibility of IP information to launch attacks through location-based name-calling, which deepened divides rather than bridging people with different opinions. This conclusion highlights a critical challenge in digital responsibility: accountability cannot be achieved through deanonymization alone, as such interventions may create new avenues for abuse without complementary safeguards. Platforms must consider complementary interventions, such as robust moderation systems, community guidelines, and tools to manage identitybased conflicts, to ensure that accountability is coupled with protective measures against harm.

Therefore, this paper indicates that managers and policymakers have an obligation beyond merely implementing digital interventions; they should also ensure that these interventions do not undermine community well-being and can encourage sustainable user engagement. The decline in geographic representation following the implementation of the IP region revelation policy suggests that certain users disengaged from the platform due to heightened hostility. It reflects a trade-off between addressing misinformation risks and maintaining an inclusive online space and raises an important question for platform governance: How can interventions aimed at improving content credibility avoid inadvertently hurting users’ engagement patterns? To ensure longterm sustainability, platform managers must carefully anticipate how digital interventions may reshape engagement patterns over time and make timely adjustments when necessary to balance the need for accountability with the obligation to foster inclusivity.

In addition, our study suggests that the effects of the revelation of coarse geographic identity were shaped by the interaction between platform governance, individual user behaviors, and the prevailing sociopolitical climate. While the intervention was introduced by the platform, its impacts were not determined by platform design alone. Instead, the user responses were shaped by the broader political climate, which was heavily influenced by, e.g., government messaging. This shaped how users interpreted and reacted to the intervention, reinforcing in-group versus out-group divisions and amplifying hostility. In turn, these user responses determined the effectiveness of the intervention and contributed to its unintended consequences, such as the decline in geographic representation and increased polarization within discussions. This highlights a critical aspect of digital intervention: Platform policies do not operate in isolation but are embedded in larger societal narratives; thus, platforms should consider how external factors may shape user behavior and undermine their intended goals of the intervention.

Taken together, our findings underscore that accountability and obligation are deeply interconnected. While platforms can enforce behavioral norms through digital interventions, these interventions must be accompanied by safeguards that prevent harm. This requires a commitment to a sustained effort to evaluate, refine, and adapt policies in response to evolving platform dynamics. This also highlights the need for platform managers to view accountability not as an isolated goal but as one that is closely linked to their obligation to protect users and promote healthy online platforms.

## 6.4 Managerial Implications

Our research provides several actionable insights for platform managers, administrators, and policymakers seeking to balance transparency with user engagement and community health.

First, our research highlights that the implementation of the IP revelation policy can fuel hostility, particularly through location-based name-calling. Therefore, managers must recognize that such de-anonymity measures may not improve accountability because the information revealed is insufficient to enforce accountability effectively; it is not sufficient to raise users’ awareness of privacy concerns. Instead, it tends to aggravate identity-based conflicts, foster hostility, alter user content generation, and narrow the geographic representation of users. To address these challenges, platforms should explore alternative approaches that balance transparency with protective measures, such as equipping users with context-sensitive moderation tools or restricting IP region visibility to moderators rather than making it publicly accessible.

Second, managers must consider the long-term consequences of interventions like the revelation of IP regions. While such policies may initially reduce misinformation and disinformation, their broader impact on user engagement and community trust can undermine the platform’s sustainability. Strategic planning should prioritize long-term engagement over short-term solutions, ensuring that the platform remains a welcoming space for diverse users and perspectives.

Third, our research emphasizes that platform environments play a crucial role in shaping user behaviors. Managers should be aware that systemic cues within the platform can amplify certain stereotypes and behaviors, particularly in polarized environments. Adjustments to platform design, such as promoting diverse content or reducing the visibility of polarizing cues, could mitigate these negative effects.

## 6.5 Limitations and Future Research

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings.

First, while the analysis highlights significant behavioral changes following the implementation of the IP revelation policy, it can be challenging to disentangle the effects of the policy itself from the impact of its announcement. The announcement explicitly framed the policy as a measure to reduce misinformation and address concerns about out-group actors, potentially influencing user perceptions and behaviors. On the one hand, users may have become more sensitive, leading to a rise in accusations and location-based name-calling as part of a broader narrative of protecting the in-group. On the other hand, the announcement may have discouraged the participation of users from non-Chinese locations. This indicates a limitation of the research design, as we could not fully separate the effects of the announcement from those of the policy implementation. Second, our findings are based on a Chinese platform. While the diverse characteristics of online users in China make it representative of similar platforms, further research should be done to assess the generalizability of these results to other types of networks, such as follower-based platforms, and to comparable platforms in other countries.

## Appendix

## Qualitative Analysis

We explored real users’ attitudes toward the IP revelation policies on Hupu.com. To do so, we analyzed 12 posts (each with 200 replies or more) that discussed the policies implemented on Hupu.com and other internet forums. During the period covered by this paper’s datasets, these posts received more replies than other relevant posts on the same topic. We had three posts around May 2022 (before the partial implementation of the policy displaying IPs of original posts), three posts between June 2022 and January 2023 (after the partial implementation), and six posts throughout the year 2023 (after the full implementation of reply-level display). These posts are labeled by letters, from A to L, in chronological order (rather than the order of their citations in this section). For each post, we report the replies that received the most upvotes, thereby suggesting the most representative opinions about regulation based on IP regions on Hupu.com. In this section, we cluster these opinions into groups and present them as follows.

## Advocacy for Location-Based Name-Calling in Political Discussions

The first group of opinions mainly expresses users’ support for the IP revelation policy, as displayed IPs can reveal the geographic identity and further imply regional stereotypes. One post (post A), published in late April 2022, discusses why many Chinese websites (not including Hupu.com at that time) implemented IP revelation policies at the level of individual replies (or their equivalents) as a result of political turmoil following the Russo-Ukrainian War. In the original post, the user provides an example from Weibo (a Twitter-like forum based in China, where the IP region display policy had been implemented at the time) and says:

I just randomly checked a few posts on Weibo, and all the replies that are trolling have foreign IP locations. (A0)

This opinion is shared by two other posts (B and C) published around the same time (April 2022, before the partial implementation). In B’s original post (B0), the user lists several examples of why Hupu users with foreign IPs are more likely to bring ideologies and concepts prevalent in Western countries to Hupu.com (i.e., “anti-official”), which may be detrimental. Similarly, in the original post of C, the user says:

The forums [several other forums the user mentions] have all become so cleansed after everyone’s IP region was displayed. (C0)

The most upvoted reply to this post (C1) calls for Hupu.com to fully implement the same IP revelation policy, which is agreed upon by the fifth most upvoted reply (C5). More specifically, a large number of highly upvoted replies (e.g., C7-C9) explain that displaying the IP region will make it easier for Hupu’s users to detect foreign IPs, thereby allowing them to attack users not in China based on their stereotypes, and, specifically, political stereotypes. For example:

Many users with foreign IP locations are actually foreign [ideological, anti-China] forces. They are trolling, and we will find it easier to detect them [if Hupu.com also implements similar regulations]. (C7)

C7’s stated theory eventually leads to location-based name-calling involving political stereotypes even without any display of IPs at Hupu.com at the time. For example, within the same post, C9’s author launches an attack based on negative political stereotypes:

## These people are definitely [hateful trolls] from foreign countries X and Y. (C9)<sup>3</sup>

The opinions presented above do not only reflect earlier attitudes on Hupu.com; similar statements have also been prevalent since the implementation of IP-related regulations in China (after April 2022), including both the pre- and post-full-implementation periods on Hupu.com (before and after January 2023). Two representative, widely discussed posts from these periods (D and G) have various highly upvoted replies supporting the IP revelation policy due to attacks on stereotypes of foreign IPs. For example:

There is a possibility that displaying IPs will discourage fewer people from abroad from posting here, because they are spies! [along with a sarcastic emoji] (D5)<sup>4</sup>

Come on, guys! Here is a [politically derogative adjective] person from [country X]! (G2)<sup>5</sup>

Note that D5 is an attack on the stereotype (“spies”) of users with foreign IPs before the full implementation of the IP revelation policy; G2, replying to another reply, is an attack on the stereo- type after the full implementation, when the user in G2 can see the IP region (country X) of the user of the previous reply.

In sum, the above cases of location-based name-calling based on political stereotypes show examples of specific cases of offensive language. These cases confirm the belief among Hupu users that displaying IPs is helpful and makes it more convenient and more effective for them to launch attacks, and they gain utility from such attacks. However, their goal is presented as sublime: to “cleanse” the forum.

Finally, it is important to note that attacks on stereotypes are not limited to foreign IPs; domestic IPs across China can also be targeted by political and social stereotypes. This was particularly common after the full implementation of the IP revelation policy on Hupu.com after 2023. For example, in a post (H) published in January 2023, the original post expresses that the user’s hometown is frequently attacked for political conservatism due to “social disadvantages” (H0). The four most upvoted replies (H1-H4) all focus on using derogatory terms to describe political stereotypes of different Chinese provinces. Similarly, another post (I) in January has two highly upvoted replies (I2 and I3) describing derogatory terms about how their hometown provinces are stereotyped and are purposefully “ignored” and “looked down upon.”

## Worries About Location-Based Name-Calling

One concern widely studied in digital responsibility research is privacy. Post C, published before the implementation of any IP revelation policy on Hupu.com, has many users who mention that privacy should not be a concern even with the policy. Their reasons include, for example, that “province is not as detailed as an individual’s home address” (C2) and “province is not part of privacy” (C4). In post E, published two weeks before the full implementation of the IP region display policy on Hupu.com, the user who creates the post also contributes the most upvoted reply. Both the original post and the reply (E0 and E1) express little concern over privacy, even including a facetious statement: “I don’t even care about displaying my income and educational attainment.” In post C, another user provides a similar, albeit less strong, view on this:

Showing the IP location at the province level does not harm privacy, but Hupu.com cannot show more detailed information. (C3)

The above replies posted on Hupu.com contrast with prior studies, as reported in Sections 1 and 2, which point out people’s worries about the association between deindividualization and the loss of privacy. This is summarized by the most upvoted reply of a post (F) published before the full implementation of the IP region display policy. The original post (F0) discusses the possibility of Hupu.com learning from other online social networks in China, and a user says:

I don’t think showing IP will have any effect on privacy issues among the majority of Hupu users. Why should we care? (F1)

Many replies echo this sentiment by expressing that, e.g., Hupu users are “more lawful than users on other networks” (F3) and are “most harmonious” (F5).

For users on Hupu.com, a more severe concern is not about privacy but about discrimination. This discrimination stems from regional stereotypes expressed through the use of offensive language. This explains why the IP revelation policy is both supported by many Hupu users and raises concerns because users develop public self-awareness. Importantly, the development of such self-awareness is gradual, and it is concentrated in the period of full implementation of the IP region display policy. In post J, published in the full implementation period, the original post observes a rise in regional discrimination and further points out that:

Hupu.com deserves it. At the time they implemented the IP region display policy, they should have realized that it would lead to regional discrimination. (J0)

Another post (K) in 2023 also involves similar discussions. In fact, the original post (K0) refers to the IP revelation policy as “the greatest invention in 2023.” However, this is refuted by the post’s most upvoted reply:

I don’t think so. I think [stereotyping people for] regional discrimination is simply cyberbullying. (K1)

These concerns about regional discrimination across the forum explain why some Hupu users gradually become aware of the disutility of using IP information to launch attacks: their own regions can be attacked as well. However, we found little evidence of concerns about privacy or the reputation of their regions, i.e., their geographic identity.

The above concerns finally lead to a more advanced concern about the quality of generated content. A widely discussed post (L), published in mid-2023, describes in its original post that Hupu.com has experienced a decline in content quality because people “use IP locations to discriminate against others,” thereby making the forum a deteriorating environment for users (L0). The two most upvoted replies (L1 and L2) express agreement with L0, while the third most upvoted reply further proposes word count as a key variable for the declining content quality on Hupu.com:

Other online communities can easily surpass us as long as users there post some words [because we have much fewer]. (L3)

Interestingly, following the above reply, the fourth most upvoted reply uses location-based name-calling (targeting a Chinese province) to launch attacks on a user defending the “decent quality of content” on Hupu.com. The creator of L4 says:

You are so confident about the quality here! Have you been fed too much of your [derogative adjective] hometown [X] food? (L4)<sup>6</sup>
