# ORGANIZATIONAL SENSEMAKING IN ERP IMPLEMENTATION: THE INFLUENCE OF SENSEMAKING STRUCTURE<sup>1</sup>

Barney Tan Discipline of Business Information Systems, The University of Sydney, Sydney, AUSTRALIA {barney.tan@sydney.edu.au}

Shan L. Pan School of Information Systems and Technology Management, University of New South Wales, Sydney, AUSTRALIA {shan.pan@unsw.edu.au}

Wenbo Chen Department of Information Management and Decision Sciences, Shanghai International Studies University, Shanghai, PEOPLE’S REPUBLIC OF CHINA {cwb@shisu.edu.cn}

Lihua Huang Department of Information Management and Information Systems, Fudan University, Shanghai, PEOPLE’S REPUBLIC OF CHINA {lhhuang@fudan.edu.cn}

Organizational sensemaking is crucial to enterprise resource planning (ERP) implementation. This is because it enables the focal organization to gain an understanding of the unique contextual issues within and surrounding the organization, and continuously monitor and reinterpret these issues so that the approach to ERP implementation can be modified or realigned accordingly. Yet, little attention has been paid to studying the nature and implications of this process. Using a case study of the ERP implementation journey of Shangha Tobacco Corporation, a Chinese state-owned enterprise, this paper explores the different ways in which organizational sensemaking can unfold in the context of ERP implementation. Analyses of our data suggest that the process of organizational sensemaking is influenced by a sensemaking structure, which consists of a technical structure (i.e., the technical foundation for sensemaking) and a social structure (i.e., the behavioral norms and relational ties surrounding sensemaking). The sensemaking structure, in turn, is influenced by the contextual conditions surrounding ERP implementation. With its findings, this study contributes a process model of ERP implementation from a sensemaking perspective to complement the existing research, and provides indications to practice on the effective implementation of ERP systems.

Keywords: Enterprise resource planning, organizational sensemaking, systems implementation, sensemaking structure, package-organization misalignment, case study

## Introduction

Enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems refer to configurable software packages “that integrate information and information-based processes within and across functional areas in an organization” (Kumar and Van Hillegersberg 2000, p. 23). The operations of the largest and most successful business organizations in the world today are underpinned by ERP systems (Liang et al. 2007; Morris and Venkatesh 2010). But despite their prevalence, the failure rate of ERP implementation projects remains significantly high. One report suggests that 57% of ERP implementation projects were over budget and over schedule, while only 46% of projects delivered on their expected benefits (Panorama Consulting Solutions 2016). In addition, market research firm Gartner predicts that imminent shifts in the business and technological landscapes will cause 80% of all ERP projects to fail due to the lack of an “application integration strategy and execution ability, resulting in integration disorder, greater complexity and cost” (Gartner Group 2016).

The implementation of an ERP system tends to introduce structural changes within an organization (Morton and Hu 2008). This transformation evokes sensemaking because it disturbs the existing understandings of an organization (Maitlis and Christianson 2014; Mantere et al. 2012). Sensemaking refers to

a process, prompted by violated expectations, that involves attending to and bracketing cues in the environment, creating intersubjective meaning through cycles of interpretation and action, and thereby enacting a more ordered environment from which further cues can be drawn (Maitlis and Christianson 2014, p. 67).

To elicit a collective response to the structural transformation, sensemaking must be enacted at the organizational level. This is referred to as organizational sensemaking, which is defined as a social process in which

organization members interpret their environment in and through interactions with others, constructing accounts that allow them to comprehend the world and act collectively (Maitlis 2005, p. 21).

Organizational sensemaking is crucial to ERP implementation because, as will be discussed in our literature review, it has an overarching influence on all stages of the implementation lifecycle (Gosain 2004). As such, an understanding of the process of organizational sensemaking could hold the key to addressing the high rate of implementation failure. This is because when things do not go as planned at any point during the implementation process, ERP practitioners would be able to shape their responses to problems that arise (i.e., triggers of organizational sensemaking), and manage the development and selection of sensemaking accounts (Weick et al. 2005) to arrive at an appropriate remedial action (Berente et al. 2011; Jensen et al. 2009). Yet, although research on ERP implementation has been one of the most vibrant areas in the discipline of information systems (IS) for over two decades, a review of the literature indicates that the nature and implications of organizational sensemaking in the context of ERP implementation have not been studied to a significant degree. To address this knowledge gap, this study will examine ERP implementation from a sensemaking perspective. Specifically, it seeks to address the following research questions:

(1) How does organizational sensemaking unfold (i.e., its nature) in the context of ERP implementation?

(2) How does organizational sensemaking influence ERP implementation (i.e., its implications)?

In addressing these research questions, the aim of our study is twofold. First, our study seeks to complement the existing discourse on ERP implementation by examining the process of organizational sensemaking and its overarching influence on the various prescriptions for ERP implementation in the literature (see Patnayakuni et al. 2007). Second, beyond its academic implications, our study also aims to provide indications to practice on how to make sense of the salient contextual issues within and surrounding an organization that influence ERP implementation, and more importantly, how ERP systems can be implemented effectively. For instance, one of the most widely recognized causes of ERP implementation failure is package-organization misalignment (see Berente and Yoo 2012). The insights gained from this study may reveal how misalignment arises (e.g., because of suboptimal sensemaking during the implementation process) and, more importantly, how it can be ameliorated (e.g., by forming iterative explanations for misalignment and attempting to resolve them systematically).

This paper is organized as follows. The first section has established our motivation and the research questions we are seeking to answer. In the second section, we review the relevant literatures on our phenomenon of interest (i.e., ERP Implementation) and our guiding theory (i.e., organizational sensemaking) respectively to construct the foundation for our subsequent theoretical arguments. The research method is then presented in the third section, followed by a presentation of our findings in the fourth section, so that the reader may follow the process of theory building (Klein and Myers 1999) and judge the validity of the developed theory based on the cogency and plausibility of the underlying logic (Walsham 1995). The fifth section of the paper presents the key arguments inductively derived from the case study, as well as their theoretical implications, before a discussion of the limitations and practical contributions of our study in the concluding section of the paper.

## Literature Review

## ERP Implementation

ERP systems are designed to facilitate the seamless integration of all the information of an organization held in different business functions; such as finance, logistics, human resources and sales, for the purpose of integrating the business processes of the organization (Brown and Vessey 2003; Davenport 1998). Semantically, they are different from enterprise information systems and enterprise systems. The former is a broader term that refers to enterprise-wide systems developed for the purpose of improving the operational performance of organizations (which can include Supply Chain Management and Customer Relationship Management systems; see Zang and Fan 2007), while the latter is a narrower term that refers specifically to packaged (i.e., off-theshelf) ERP systems (Strong and Volkoff 2010).

The integration of an organization’s business processes through the use of ERP systems is expected to enhance its competitiveness by improving the way strategically important information is created, shared and managed across business functions and geographical locations (Howcroft et al. 2004). In addition, ERP systems are expected to bring about many other significant benefits, which include (1) enhanced data accuracy (by eliminating redundant data entry and concomitant errors, and simplifying data analysis), (2) operational efficiency(by reducing inventory, human resources, operating and administrative expenses), and (3) organizational effectiveness (by facilitating standardized procedures, reduced cycle times, increased customer responsiveness and enhanced organization wide decision support) (Lee et al. 2003; Markus and Tanis 1999).

Despite its immense promise, ERP implementation failures can be devastating, often leading to severe multi-million dollar losses, the erosion of competitive advantage, reduced organizational performance and even bankruptcy (Soh and Sia 2004; Sritharan and Pan 2007). The severity of the consequences of ERP implementation failure, together with the magnitude of the resources, commitment and organizational changes required for implementation (Davenport 1998), have sparked a proliferation of research on ERP implementation over the last two decades. The cumulative body of work on ERP implementation, in turn, can be divided into two major streams.

The first stream is made up of research centered on identifying the critical success factors (CSFs) of ERP implementation. Studies in this stream are centered on identifying the necessary conditions for successful implementation and are typically not aligned with a specific theory. In an extensive review of the literature, researchers have found over 20 CSFs identified in prior studies (Finney and Corbett 2007). The more frequently cited CSFs in the literature include top management support, consultant support, user acceptance, quality of project team, alignment of system with business processes, an appropriate implementation strategy and timeframe, as well as the presence of a project champion, among others (Holland and Light 1999; Kwak et al. 2012; Nah et al. 2001). As CSFs are conceptualized as the key areas where “things must go right” (Rockart 1979, p. 85) for a business to flourish, this stream of research can inform our inquiry by providing indications on how organizational sensemaking is initiated. This is because sensemaking is especially evoked when things go wrong—when new or unexpected situations arise, and there is no predetermined way to act (Jensen et al. 2009; Weick et al. 2005).

The second stream is made up of research examining ERP implementation from a specific theoretical perspective (refer to Appendix A). Mirroring developments in the broader literature on IS development (see Patnayakuni et al. 2007), this research stream is dominated by five theoretical perspectives. The first perspective is based on the agency theory, which views the minimization of the conflicts between the business units of an organization and the developers of the ERP system as the key to implementation success (e.g., Basu and Lederer 2011; Boudreau and Robey 2005). A second perspective is rooted in the principles of risk management. Studies aligned with this perspective tend to emphasize the importance of the proactive management of project risks in ERP implementation (e.g., Aloini et al. 2012; Dey et al. 2013). A third perspective is grounded in the theory of control and suggests that ERP implementation success is a result of effective control over the implementation process (e.g., Berente et al. 2010; Berente and Yoo 2012). This in turn, stems from a judicious application of both formal and informal controls. A fourth perspective is based on the coordination theory, which suggests that effective collaboration between the various entities involved in ERP implementation may hold the key to ERP implementation success (e.g., Lyytinen and Newman 2015; Sasidharan et al. 2012). A fifth perspective is borne out of the principles of knowledge management, which recognizes not only the need for the application of knowledge in the various stages of the implementation process, but the transfer, translation and transformation of knowledge as well (e.g., Pan et al. 2007; Sedera and Gable 2010).

The second stream of research is also important to our study because not only does it highlight the key entities and their potential sensemaking roles (Maitlis and Christianson 2014) over the course of ERP implementation (i.e., the agency theory perspective), but the frameworks to manage or mitigate risks (i.e., the risk management perspective) and enact the appropriate controls (i.e., the control theory perspective) could shed light on the nature of the entities’ mental models (Cornelissen and Clarke 2010) that form the basis of organizational sensemaking. In addition, the mechanisms to enhance collaboration (i.e., the coordination theory perspective) and facilitate knowledge integration (i.e., the knowledge management perspective) could provide indications on how a coherent shared sensemaking account is formed among the entities to guide organizational action (Maitlis 2005).

Yet, despite the relative maturity of research on ERP implementation, there are only a handful of studies that touches on sensemaking, and none of these studies are focused specifically on the nature and implications of organizational sensemaking in this context (see Boersma and Kingma 2005; Gosain 2004; Saeed et al. 2010). We contend that a sensemaking perspective of ERP implementation is important for a number of reasons. First, sensemaking is inevitably and invariably triggered by ERP implementation because it is a complex endeavor that involves pervasive and significant organizational changes (Strong and Volkoff 2010). These changes often invalidate or dramatically alter taken-forgranted roles and routines (Morris and Venkatesh 2010), which coerces or provides the motivation for organizational constituents to “give up their existing accounts of the world and (enact sensemaking) to construct new meanings” (Maitlis and Christianson 2014, p. 77).

Second, not only is sensemaking inextricable from ERP implementation, it also has implications for many aspects of the process. Organizational sensemaking can play a crucial role in shaping package selection (Svejvig and Jensen 2012), stakeholder participation (Jensen et al. 2009), systems assimilation (Berente and Yoo 2012), usage behaviors (Hsieh et al. 2011), as well as the knowledge integration between participating stakeholders (Vlaar et al. 2008). As these aspects essentially span all stages of the implementation lifecycle (see Markus and Tanis 1999) and would be relevant to many of the prescriptions for implementation success in the literature, the influence of organizational sensemaking on ERP implementation is not only profound (Gosain 2004), but overarching in nature.

Third, one of the main challenges and leading causes of ERP implementation failures is package-organization misalignment (Sia and Soh 2007; Soh et al. 2003). Alignment can refer to enablement fit, which refers to the extent to which a package “enable” the business processes of an organization, as well as coverage fit, defined as the extent to which the “coverage” of the package’s features meets the organization’s requirements (Berente and Yoo 2012). The notion of package–organization misalignment and more importantly, how the issue can be resolved is “infrequently studied” (Strong and Volkoff 2010, p. 732), and we propose that understanding organizational sensemaking can hold the key to addressing both forms of misalignment. This is because the process provides the means for an organizational actor to carve meaning from flux and the “infinite stream of events” (Weick et al. 2005, p. 411) that surround the actor, and iteratively test alternative explanations of a new phenomenon (Berente et al. 2011). As such, studying sensemaking in the context of ERP implementation can shed light on how the misalignments between the embedded structures and processes of both the package and the organization can be identified, managed, and resolved.

To address this gap in the literature, the aim of this paper is to examine the nature and implications of organizational sensemaking in the context of ERP implementation, Accordingly, we turn our attention to the literature on organizational sensemaking so as to construct a theoretical lens to guide our subsequent inquiry (Walsham 1995).

## Organizational Sensemaking

The influence of organizational sensemaking pervades all levels of an organizational hierarchy. For top management, sensemaking activities such as environmental scanning and issue interpretation (Thomas et al. 1993) are critical to strategic change initiation (Gioia and Chittipeddi 1991) and organizational decision making (Maitlis 2005). For other organizational stakeholders, sensemaking affects how they manage their organizational identity in the process of organizational adaptation (Dutton and Dukerich 1991; Pratt 2000), respond to pressure (Cornelissen 2012; Gephart 1993), and rationalize their participation in organizational change initiatives (Balogun and Johnson 2005; Rouleau and Balogun 2011).

The need for organizational sensemaking is intensified in situations that demand coherent shared understandings among relevant stakeholders for collective action (Weick 1993). In the field of IS, sensemaking has been primarily applied as a theoretical lens to examine the social aspects of the implementation of various systems. For instance, Jensen and her colleagues (Jensen and Kjærgaard 2010; Jensen et al. 2009) have examined the nature of sensemaking, as well as its influence on the existing practices, perceived identity, and authority of doctors and nurses in the implementation of an electronic patient record system. Likewise, Vlaar et al. (2008) have adopted the sensemaking perspective to examine the sociocognitive acts and communication processes between onsite and offshore system vendor teams in the implementation of a geographically distributed information system.

Organizational sensemaking is triggered by ecological change (Weick et al. 2005), which may take the form of environmental jolts or organizational crises (Christianson et al. 2009), threats to identity (Petriglieri 2011) and planned change interventions (Mantere et al. 2012). As these triggers represent anomalies amidst the flow of events and inputs that surrounds an actor (Weick et al. 2005), the actor would attempt to enact order into flux by extracting cues from the raw flow of activity for closer attention (e.g., Chia 2000). The cues are then interpreted in light of the existing organizational norms (Rouleau and Balogun 2011). If expectation gaps that breach the sense of continuity or disrupt ongoing collective organizational action are detected by the actor (Dervin 1992; Maitlis and Lawrence 2007), it implies that “the current state of the world is perceived to be different from the expected state of the world” (Weick et al. 2005, p. 414) and drives the need for the enactment of sensemaking (Balogun and Johnson 2005).

The enactment of organizational sensemaking is influenced by organizational identity (Weick et al. 2005), which refers to the core, distinctive and enduring traits (Albert and Whetten 1985) of an organization that contribute to how the organizational actors participating in sensemaking define the organization and their role within it (i.e., who the organizational actors think they are; see Gioia and Thomas 1996). In the existing literature, scholars have primarily conceptualized two ways of enacting organizational sensemaking (Maitlis and Christianson 2014). On one hand, organizational sensemaking can be seen to be discrete, taking place within individuals. With discrete sensemaking, intersubjective meaning is constructed when an individual advocates for a particular view and attempts to influence the understanding of others (e.g., Gioia and Chittipeddi 1991). The primary influencing mechanism identified in the literature is known as sensegiving, defined as attempts by an individual or group of stakeholders to influence the way others think or act (Gioia and Chittipeddi 1991; Maitlis 2005). However, the individuals being influenced are typically not passive recipients of meaning, but can engage in sensemaking of their own (Maitlis and Christianson 2014) or enact a variety of responses including sensedemanding and sensebreaking (e.g., Vlaar et al. 2008). Sensedemanding refers to the proactive attempts by an individual or group of stakeholders to ameliorate uncertainty and improve the quality of information available for decision making (Vlaar et al. 2008). Sensebreaking refers to attempts by an individual or group of stakeholders to question the existing understandings of others and induce new ways of thinking or acting (Lawrence and Maitlis 2007). On the other hand, organizational sensemaking can also be regarded as a joint process that unfolds between individuals. With joint sensemaking, intersubjective meaning is co-constructed as individuals mutually engage and develop a shared understanding of an issue collectively (see Meyer et al. 1998). The example of jazz orchestras is commonly cited to illustrate this form of collective sensemaking where “members must listen closely to each other, take turns leading and following, and respond together in real-time to novel or unexpected performance” (Maitlis and Christianson 2014, p. 78).

In any case, the construction of intersubjective meaning results in the creation of multiple sensemaking accounts among the various involved entities, which must be pared down to a shared account in order to enable organizational action (Maitlis 2005). This is gradually achieved through a process of competition and selection (Maitlis and Sonensheim 2010), which entails the mechanisms of elaboration (Klein et al. 2006), negotiation (Balogun and Johnson 2005) updating (Maitlis and Sonensheim 2010), and replacement (Cornelissen and Clarke 2010). These may manifest in activities such as questioning, issue selling and the propagation of ideas in consultative committees (Maitlis and Lawrence 2007). The retained shared account may then be used to inform the next iteration of sensemaking (Weick et al. 2005) and guide organizational action (Maitlis 2005).

The nature of the retained shared account and its capacity for guiding organizational action are dependent on varying extents of control and animation (Maitlis 2005). Control refers to the ability of top management to direct and manage the process of organizational sensemaking. Animation refers to the rhythm and intensity of the flow of information between the involved stakeholders (Stensaker et al. 2008). First, when characterized by high control and high animation, a guided form of organizational sensemaking arises. Consequently, the account that is generated from the process tends to be unitary due to the ability of top management to shape them. Moreover, the account is rich as it incorporates the perspectives of diverse stakeholders. The unitary and rich account enables an emergent series of actions with consistent foci. Second, when characterized by low control and high animation, a fragmented form of organizational sensemaking emerges. This results in a multiplicity of narrow accounts as the divergent perspectives of different stakeholder groups are not integrated or reconciled by the top management. The multiplicity of narrow accounts, in turn, gives rise to an emergent series of inconsistent and contradictory actions. Third, when characterized by high control and low animation, a restricted form of organizational sensemaking eventuates. This leads to the production of a shared account that is unitary but narrow due to a lack of participation from a diversity of stakeholder groups. The unitary but narrow account precipitates actions that are consistent but unamenable to subsequent improvisation and extensions. Finally, when characterized by low control and low animation, a minimal form of organizational sensemaking is the result. Accordingly, the accounts generated from this process are nominal in that they are neither unitary nor rich due to the lack of top management direction and participation from a diversity of stakeholder groups. These nominal accounts preclude coordinated, effective, and sustained action (Maitlis 2005).

## Research Method

The case research method was adopted for a number of reasons. First, it is particularly appropriate for examining processes (Rynes and Gephart 2004) and how research questions (Walsham 1995), and our research questions are “how” questions that delve into the process and implications of organi zational sensemaking in the course of ERP implementation. Second, as sensemaking in ERP implementation consists of a social, a technological and a business dimension (Wagner et al. 2010; Xue et al. 2005), the inherent multidimensional complexity of the phenomenon makes objective approaches to research difficult. As such, it may be more appropriate to examine the phenomenon by interpreting the shared understanding of the relevant stakeholders (Klein and Myers 1999).

Based on our research questions, two criteria formed the basis for case selection. First, organizational sensemaking at the selected case organization should ideally have been enacted in a variety of ways, to varying results, so as to potentially reveal the ways in which the process can unfold and how this variance can influence ERP implementation. A corollary of this criterion was that we needed to investigate multiple iterations of ERP implementation at a single case organization or the variance observed can too easily be attributed to interorganizational differences (Tan et al. 2010). Consequently, our second criterion was that we needed a case with a consistent and highly stable context so as to make comparisons over time (Cornelissen 2012). The ERP implementation journey at STC presents a revelatory case (Sarker et al. 2012) for our purpose as different forms of organizational sensemaking were enacted in the implementation of ERP systems that resulted in distinct outcomes at STC. Moreover, as a Chinese state-owned enterprise, STC is characterized by an internal environment that remained remarkably stable throughout an ERP implementation journey that spanned an extended period. For example, STC’s Chief Information Officer (CIO) whom we interviewed as part of our study was at the helm of the implementation of all the iterations of ERP systems since they were first introduced and has only recently retired. Likewise, many of the informants selected for our study have also been in their respective roles for a greater part of this period. An important benefit of this stability and our single case research design is that many contextual variables are kept relatively constant, which helps to rule out possible alternative interpretations of the data (Tan et al. 2010).

## Case Background

STC is the largest tobacco firm in China. It is under the purview of the China National Tobacco Corporation (CNTC), a government agency established to manage the production, distribution, marketing and sales of all tobacco products in the country. STC manages an extensive network of over 50 tobacco suppliers and 30,000 retailers worldwide. From 22 production facilities across the globe, STC produces an array of different cigarettes, ranging from the internationally renowned Chunghwa and Panda cigarettes, to local bestsellers such as Double Happiness, Peony, and South China Sea cigarettes. In 2018, STC’s net profits were estimated at US\$16.1 billion after contributing US\$14.6 billion in taxes. The firm accounted for approximately 0.6% of the entire nation’s corporate tax revenue alone, making it one of largest corporate contributors of taxes in the whole of China.

The ERP implementation journey of STC began in 1993 with the introduction of BPCS, a manufacturing resource planning (MRPII) package developed by SSA Global. Prior to 1993, most of the business units at STC were still heavily reliant on paper-based information, although a handful of them had developed small systems and applications in isolation. The implementation of BPCS was acknowledged to be unstructured and poorly planned. For instance, it was only after BPCS went live that the business units realized that the system was fundamentally incompatible with its existing processes. The misalignment between the structures and processes embedded within BPCS and the existing structures and processes of STC was so severe that only a small subset of modules from the software package could eventually be launched, and the use of all but one of the launched modules were quickly discontinued within months.

Following the failed implementation of BPCS, the consensus was that it was too difficult to configure a packaged ERP system to suit its complex, formula-based production system (e.g., the production formula of cigarettes has to be constantly readjusted as the quality of each batch of tobacco leaves may differ). Consequently, the decision was made to custom build an ERP system composed of loosely integrated modules that were tailored to its idiosyncratic business processes. These modules would only be aimed at automating and supporting the existing processes as opposed to fundamentally transforming them. This culminated in an era termed “e-enablement” by the organization that spanned from 1995 to 1999.

The efficiency gains brought about by automation provided STC with an indication of the business potential of ERP systems. With a growing realization that the modules developed could play a more important role in improving its business processes and management effectiveness if they were better integrated, the modules were combined into three major subsystems for finance, sales and resource management in a systems integration era that lasted from 2000 to 2005.

The systems integration era was deemed a major success by STC as important improvements to the efficiency and effectiveness of their operations were realized. For the organization, the success of the systems integration era was an indication that they were ready to adopt a packaged ERP system that embodied international best practices and fundamentally transformtheir business processes accordingly. Consequently, the decision was made to implement SAP ERP in 2006. The system was successfully launched and is perceived as an important enabler of the business processes and strategies of STC today.

## Data Collection

Interviews formed the primary source of data (Myers and Newman 2007; Walsham 2006) and a total of 21 unique informants were interviewed (some repeatedly) over two main rounds of data collection that spanned a period of 3 years (refer to Appendix B). Of the 21 informants, 18 were members of STC’s top management, IT department and various business units (refer to Appendix C). These informants were selected on the basis that they were the key decision makers or members of a particular stakeholder group in the relevant periods of interest, and were therefore able to give an adequate and first-hand account of the process of sensemaking (Porra et al. 2014). Additional interviews were also conducted with three informants representing STC’s business partners to corroborate the data obtained from the internal stakeholders. The interviews were conducted with semistructured interview guides (see Appendix D for a sample) that were designed based on our theoretical lens that is presented in the following subsection. This approach is less rigid than an explanatory case study that simply seeks to validate preformulated hypotheses (Ferlie et al. 2005), and balances the generative nature of pure induction with the pragmatism of early structure (Langley 1999).

The interviews took an average of 90 minutes, were digitally recorded and later transcribed for data analysis. The earliest interviews, centered on gaining a broad overview of the phenomenon (Pan and Tan 2011), revealed the four distinct eras of ERP implementation described earlier in our case background. Consequently, the protocol for the subsequent interviews was such that the informants were asked to describe, in an open-ended fashion, their role in sensemaking for each of the eras that were relevant to them. This protocol is widely used in prior studies on sensemaking (e.g., Cornelissen 2012; Rouleau and Balogun 2011), and the descriptions that result are conventionally regarded as sensemaking accounts in that they are “linguistic device[s] employed whenever an action is subjected to evaluative inquiry …. [and that] prevent[s] conflicts from arising by verbally bridging the gap between action and expectation” (Scott and Lyman 1968, p. 46).

Moreover, as STC’s ERP implementation journey spanned an extended period of time that precluded real-time data collection, to mitigate the possibility of retrospective rationalization on the part of our informants (Glick et al. 1990), we supplemented our interview data with a historical analysis of secondary documents (Mason et al. 1997a). With the questions from our interview guides doubling as “focusing questions” (Mason et al. 1997b, p. 312) for our historical analysis, we gathered secondary data in the form of newspaper articles, journal and magazine articles, internal publications, academic theses, white papers, online articles, and information from the corporate website (refer to Appendix E). We then assessed the credibility of each source of secondary data (refer to Appendix F) using the criteria for external criticism proposed by Porra et al. (2014). In addition, as one of the co-authors of this paper had served as a consultant of various systems implementation projects at STC over the last decade, the co-author was also able to independently verify much of the data that was uncovered. Notes from direct observation were also used to corroborate the data obtained. The interview transcripts and the data from secondary sources amounted to approximately 1183 pages of textual data, and were subject to the same procedures of coding and analysis.

## Data Analysis

To take advantage of the flexibility that the case research method affords, data analysis was performed in tandem with data collection (Strauss and Corbin 1998). After gaining an overview of the phenomenon from the initial interviews, we adopted a temporal bracketing strategy (Langley 1999) and divided the events, activities and decisions that transpired at STC based on the four distinct eras of ERP implementation that emerged (i.e., BPCS implementation, e-enablement, systems integration, SAP ERP implementation). The temporal bracketing strategy was also used to conceptually distinguish between four phases of the sensemaking process that unfolded in each era: (1) An initiation phase featuring the juxtaposition of environmental cues and organizational norms to trigger sensemaking (Maitlis and Lawrence 2007), (2) an enactment phase capturing a process of retrospection based on the extracted triggers and the mental models of the primary (i.e., initial) sensemaker for the creation of a basic sensemaking account (Weick et al. 2005), (3) a selection phase featuring the competition between the basic account and the multiple secondary accounts of the other sensemaking entities (Maitlis and Sonensheim 2010), and (4) a retention phase capturing how the multiple sensemaking accounts are pared down into a shared account to shape the nature and process of ERP implementation (Maitlis 2005). The purpose of the temporal bracketing strategy was to create a logical structure and a frame of reference to organize the subsequent data collected (Langley 1999).

Next, we constructed a preliminary theoretical lens (Pan and Tan 2011) based on the literature on organizational sensemaking. As part of this lens, we identified an initial set of four dimensions and 12 second-order themes (Dacin et al. 2010) that were potentially significant to sensemaking in ERP implementation. Specifically, the dimensions and secondorder themes were related to the four aspects and 12 concepts of organizational sensemaking respectively described earlier in our literature review (summarized in Table 1). The dimensions and themes were then used as the basis for the formulation of our questions for the subsequent interviews.

The data collected were organized and coded according to the set of dimensions and themes (Strauss and Corbin 1998) after each interview. If the data used in coding was extracted from a secondary source, the data would be subjected to further scrutiny based on Porra et al.’s (2014) five criteria for internal criticism (i.e., competence, expertness, objectivity, reliability, corroboration) and only used if it satisfied all the criteria. When coding, if an emergent piece of data was closely aligned with an existing dimension/theme, the piece of data would be associated directly with the conceptual category (see example in Appendix G). On the other hand, if the emergent piece challenged an existing dimension/theme (Dacin et al. 2010), then the schema would be modified accordingly (Walsham 2006). For example, existing conceptualizations of sensemaking entities tend to be only restricted to two entity groups (i.e., top management and other organizational members; see Gioia and Chittipeddi 1991; Maitlis 2005) when it is well established in the ERP implementation literature that the key stakeholders involved include the top management, the developers and the various business units of the organization (e.g., Lyytinen and Newman 2015). A finer-grained perspective of the involved entities was therefore required and we found it necessary to introduce a new conceptual category termed sensemaking configuration. Moreover, we realized that there were various sensemaking configurations that were akin to the different approaches to IT governance (see Weill and Ross 2005). Consequently, these approaches were incorporated into our coding scheme and coding was restarted. In general, coding would be restarted whenever new conceptual categories were added, modified or deleted. By “recursively iterating between existing theories, data, and the emergent model” (Pan and Tan 2011, p. 171) in this way, our theory was inductively derived and gradually shaped.

At various points in the process of data analysis where changes to the emergent theory were particularly significant, we would use the narrative and visual mapping strategies (Langley 1999) to summarize and validate our findings. The narrative strategy entailed the construction of a “story” that represented our account of what happened. The visual mapping strategy, on the other hand, involved creating chronological event timelines and documenting the emergent theory in a series of diagrammatic sketches (Figures 1 through 4 are examples of these sketches). After the narrative and the visual maps have been constructed, they were verified with the relevant informants. This was to ensure the validity of both our interpretation of the events, activities and decisions that have unfolded, as well as our theoretical ideas (Klein and Myers 1999). This process continued until each of our findings was supported by at least two sources of data and the state of theoretical saturation was reached (Glaser and Strauss 1967).

## Findings

Analyses of our data suggest that the way in which organizational sensemaking unfolds is influenced by a sensemaking structure. Improvisation is a concept that is closely related with sensemaking in the literature because both processes entail actions taken “on the fly” at the individual level that have implications at the organizational level (the extent of similarity is reflected in the way the same jazz metaphor has been applied in the elaboration of both constructs; see Kamoche and Cunha 2001; Maitlis and Christianson 2014). In their conceptualization of organizational improvisation, Kamoche and Cunha (2001, p. 744) have suggested the need for what they term a “minimal structure” in order for organizational improvisation to occur. The minimal structure refers to a set of basic conditions “conducive to a state of alertness that will facilitate a propensity for action” (Kamoche et al. 2009, p. 661) and consists of a technical structure and a social structure (Kamoche and Cunha 2001). In a similar vein, we define sensemaking structure as the set of basic conditions that must be satisfied in order to engage in collective sensemaking within an organization.

Moreover, resonating with Kamoche et al.’s (2003) conceptualization, our data reveals that the sensemaking structure consists of a technical structure and a social structure. The former refers to the technical foundation (see Van der Meer-Kooistra and Scapens 2008) on which sensemaking is based, and consists of the existing knowledge, mental models and experience in relation to the implementation and application of ERP systems (which we term ERP knowledge), as well as the entities’ belief about their ability to influence the sensemaking process (which we term perceived influence). The latter, on the other hand, refers to the behavioral norms and relational ties (see Varoutsa and Scapens 2015) that surround sensemaking, which include the arrangement of sensemaking roles taken up by the organizational entities participating in sensemaking (which we term sensemaking configuration), as well as the mode of interactions that occur between them (which we term sensemaking exchanges).

The distinction between the two concepts is illustrated by Kamoche and Cunha using the metaphor of jazz improvisation, which likewise demands collective sensemaking (Maitlis and Christianson 2014). In particular, the technical structure in this context refers to the “techno-structural and performative conventions of jazz music” (Kamoche and Cunha 2001, p. 747) that include the “grammar” of music (i.e., keys, chords and chordal progressions), the talent and performative competence of an individual musician, and the musician’s knowledge of instrumentation and music technology. Conversely, the social structure refers to the norms of how jazz musicians should communicate and behave collectively, which include the assignment of performative roles (e.g., a nominal leader that others will look to to control the performance, and a soloist who will determine the style and embellishments to use), and the exchanges that will communicate changes in tempo and other performative nuances (e.g., hand signals and nonverbal cues) when the performance is under way. In other words, the technical structure is a composite construct made up of the individual abilities and willingness of the involved stakeholders to participate in a collective endeavor, while the social structure is a form of shared understanding that forms the basic rules and boundaries for joint action (Van der Meer-Kooistra and Scapens 2008; Varoutsa and Scapens 2015). We illustrate these concepts with evidence from our case study in the following subsections before presenting a process model that elaborates on the influence of sensemaking structure on the process of organizational sensemaking.

## Era 1: BPCS Implementation (1993–1994)

In the earliest era of ERP implementation at STC, the top management saw themselves as the only stakeholders capable of steering the implementation process despite an admitted lack of knowledge on ERP systems. Neither the organizational IT function nor any of STC’s business units saw themselves capable of contributing to the process: The former was not even a formal department within STC at the time, while the latter were lacking in even basic IT knowledge because most of their business processes were still heavily paper-based and manual at the time. This technical structure, influenced by an organizational culture characterized by a deep respect for hierarchy (Lockett 1988), resulted in STC’s top management taking on the role of primary sensemakers (Maitlis 2005), creating a sensemaking configuration that resembled a business monarchy where the top management was solely responsible for making all of the key decisions surrounding ERP implementation (see Brown and Grant 2005; Weill and Ross 2005).

Consequently, the sensemaking cues that were extracted and interpreted in the Enactment phase of sensemaking (Weick et al. 2005) were those that the top management most keenly felt. This consisted of the environmental influences acting on STC at the time, which included pressures from the Chinese government and other firms within the tobacco industry for ERP implementation. After the decision to implement BPCS was made by the top management, it was followed by a unidirectional mode of sensegiving (Gioia and Chittipeddi 1991) with little inputs from the IT function or the business units. In other words, the IT function and business units were “passive recipients of meaning” (Maitlis and Christianson 2014, p. 78) in the selection phase of sensemaking (Maitlis and Sonensheim 2010). The former CIO of STC explained:

The government was strongly encouraging the implementation of MRP II as part of the national drive for informatization. As a result, there was an industry-wide push to implement MRP II and there were seven other tobacco firms that were implementing MRP II at the same time …. The main consideration (for ERP implementation) was that the management wanted it. There was little consultation, and in any case, the IT group and business units were unequipped to provide suggestions.

Based on Maitlis’ (2005) typology, the organizational sensemaking that manifested in this era was of the restricted form. This is because the top management had total control over the sensemaking process while animation was very low due to the lack of involvement from the IT function and the business units. With restricted sensemaking, negotiation was very limited (Maitlis and Lawrence 2007) and there was little competition between the accounts of the involved stakeholders (Maitlis and Sonensheim 2010). Yet, the sensemaking account that eventuated from this form of sensemaking in the Retention phase was not a unitary, narrow account as proposed in Maitlis’ typology. Instead, it was a nominal account that involved only “token understanding or interpretation” (Maitlis 2005, p. 42). The account was not unitary in that the perspectives of the involved stakeholders were not synthesized. Neither was the account rich in that the management lacked the requisite IT and domain knowledge to craft a welldeveloped interpretation, and they had no way of accessing this knowledge due to the unidirectional nature of the sensemaking exchanges. The inability of the nominal account to guide effective organizational action was a primary cause of the implementation failure of this era. ## Era 2: E-Enablement (1995–1999)

In the e-enablement era, our data revealed that sensemaking was triggered by the prior ERP implementation outcome of the previous era. A return to the former reliance on paperbased information was not seen to be an option, but failure taught the top management that it did not possess the requisite IT and domain knowledge to drive ERP implementation unilaterally. In addition, now equipped with the experience and knowledge gained from the implementation failure of the previous era, the organizational IT function wanted to be responsible for rectifying the failure of the previous era because of an organizational culture that views failure as a loss of “face” (Huang et al. 2011, p. 558). These shifts in the technical structure resulted in the organizational IT function taking on the role of primary sensemakers, with a sensemaking configuration that resembled an IT monarchy where key decisions are made by corporate IT professionals (see Brown and Grant 2005; Weill and Ross 2005).

As their main objective was to ensure the success of systems implementation from a technical standpoint, the IT function sought only to automate so as to minimize disruptions to the existing business processes as much as possible. Consequently, the environmental cues that were extracted and interpreted in the enactment phase of sensemaking (Weick et al. 2005) were not the overarching needs of the organization, which would necessitate extensive business process redesign and systems integration (Markus et al. 2000). Instead, they were the existing individual needs of the various business units. Guided by this objective, the Selection phase of sensemaking (Weick et al. 2005) unfolded with bidirectional sensegiving between the IT function and the top management in that the IT function would identify the business processes for prioritized automation and submit them to the top management for approval. There was also a bidirectional combination of sensegiving and sensedemanding (Vlaar et al. 2008) between the IT function and the business units as well in that the IT function had to uncover the individual requirements of the business units, and work with them to shape the ERP modules that would support their operations. The Section Head from the IT function explained:

The top management would work with us to identify some business processes for prioritized automation …. Then based on what we learnt from our failure, we would tell them if it [fulfilling a requirement] was possible or not.

In line with Maitlis’ typology, the organizational sensemaking that manifested in this era was of the fragmented form because the process was primarily channeled through the IT function. Control of the top management was limited to validating and approving the business processes identified for automation, while animation was high for the IT function and moderate for the business units. This form of sensemaking is characterized by the elaboration (Klein et al. 2006) and updating (Maitlis and Sonensheim 2010) of the accounts of top management and the business units by the IT function, which resulted in the production of a multiplicity of narrow accounts (Maitlis 2005) in the retention phase. This was because the accounts primarily reflected that of the IT function, while those of the various business units and the top management were not brought into competition with one another. Consequently, the multiple, narrow accounts ameliorated the enablement misfit (see Berente and Yoo 2012) of the previous phase. However, it also gave rise to an unsynchronized form of ERP implementation in that modules were launched for individual business units in isolation and only loosely integrated with one another. ## Era 3: Systems Integration (2000–2005)

In the systems integration era, our analysis revealed that sensemaking was similarly triggered by the prior ERP implementation outcome of the previous era. In particular, the efficiency gains that stemmed from automation in the preceding era provided STC’s top management with a better understanding of the business potential of an ERP system. But in order to derive more strategic outcomes that went beyond efficiency gains, the top management realized that they had to play a more active role in driving the next iteration of ERP implementation. In addition, the success of the previous era allowed STC’s IT function to gain recognition within the organization. The group was rebranded as an “IT Information Center” and provided with additional resources, manpower, and responsibilities in line with their heightened status. The enhanced status of the organizational IT function, coupled with the renewed conviction of the top management to take the lead in systems implementation, led to further shifts in the technical structure to precipitate a sensemaking configuration that resembled an IT–business duopoly where key decisions are jointly made by IT executives and the top management (see Brown and Grant 2005; Weill and Ross 2005). The Vice Director of IT described this sensemaking configuration:

The management became more confident of leading ERP implementation. At the same time, the IT department had more responsibilities with its new status as an “IT Information Center.” The systems integration era was driven by the top management, together with the IT department.

In this structure, a basic account (Cornelissen and Clarke 2010) was co-produced by the top management and the IT function as primary sensemakers. As the main objective was to derive higher-order outcomes such as business process improvement and enhanced management effectiveness that necessitated an integrative view of STC’s operations (Markus et al. 2000), the environmental cues that were extracted and interpreted in the Enactment phase of sensemaking (Weick et al. 2005) were the overarching needs of the organization. In turn, the selection phase of sensemaking unfolded in a process of joint sensemaking (Maitlis and Christianson 2014) between the top management and the IT function in which the top management would lay out their business objectives and the IT function would formulate a complementary systems development strategy. This led to the decision to integrate the numerous modules launched in the previous era into three major subsystems (i.e., a financial system, a sales system, and a resource management system). After the decision was made, there were also bidirectional sensegiving and sensedemanding (Vlaar et al. 2008) between the primary sensemakers and the business units as well in that the business units were presented with the basic account that identified the ERP modules to be developed or integrated, and asked to provide feedback. The former CIO of STC explained:

The plan for which modules to develop and integrate was developed by the top management and the IT department in close collaboration .… But the business units have to contribute as well because they are the business owners. They had to tell the management what they needed… they had to let the IT department know of their requirements.

## Era 4: SAP ERP Implementation (2006–2015)

Finally, in the SAP ERP implementation era, sensemaking was triggered by the successful prior ERP implementation outcome of the previous era once again. Specifically, the improvements in operational effectiveness as a result of systems integration in the previous era led to the insight that STC was now ready to adopt a packaged ERP system, which would enable the organization to fulfill its strategic objectives by conforming to the best practices embodied in the system. In addition, the accumulated knowledge and experience of the previous phases caused the business units to see themselves as ready and able to contribute to ERP implementation at this juncture, while the success of the previous phase also made the top management of STC cognizant of the benefits of integrating their perspective in initiating and planning for ERP implementation. These shifts in the technical structure induced a transition to a federal sensemaking configuration where “[top management] and business representatives of all the operating groups collaborate with the IT department” (Weill and Ross 2005, p.27) in the making of key ERP implementation decisions. The production manager of STC’s Shanghai factory described this sensemaking configuration:

The management realized that the business units have an important role to play in systems development. Now, the business units are key drivers of systems development as well. The business units would bring their business requirements, the IT department would bring their technical expertise, and the top management would bring their strategic vision. All of these contribute to shaping the overall direction of systems development.

In this structure, all of the three stakeholder groups were involved in the co-production of the basic account as primary sensemakers (Cornelissen and Clarke 2010). Consequently, the environmental cues that were extracted and interpreted in the Enactment phase of sensemaking (Weick et al. 2005) were the strategic objectives of the organization framed by the vision of the top management, the IT expertise of the IT function and the operational requirements of the business units. The decision to implement SAP ERP was eventually made and following this decision, the Selection phase of sensemaking (Weick et al. 2005) unfolded with intense joint sensemaking (Maitlis and Christianson 2014) between the three primary sensemakers. The joint sensemaking exchanges are illustrated in the following quote from the Section Head of IT:

It was a collective approach to systems development .… The management’s vision for ERP implementation would be tempered by operational and IT constraints. The operational requirements are shaped by strategy and IT possibilities. The development of the systems, of course, had to account for strategy and operational requirements. In this way, the top management, the business units and the IT department all provided inputs that influenced one another.

Extending Maitlis’s typology, we label this form of organizational sensemaking as ubiquitous sensemaking because sensemaking accounts could originate and develop from any of the involved parties. Control of the top management over the sensemaking process is lower than before because the responsibility for primary sensemaking is now shared with the IT function and the business units. Conversely, animation for both the IT function and business units are at their highest because both stakeholder groups are actively engaged in every phase of the sensemaking process. Ubiquitous sensemaking is characterized by the highest degree of competition between sensemaking accounts as all the accounts of the stakeholder groups are viewed with importance, resulting in the need for intense negotiations (Maitlis and Sonensheim 2010). This gave rise to a shared sensemaking account with two defining characteristics. First, the account was unitary as the multiple competing accounts were pared down into a single, unified account through competition. Second, the account was rich as it encompassed the perspectives of all the three stakeholder groups that were fundamentally involved in ERP implementation. The unitary and rich account resulted in an implementation approach that was encompassing and integrative, mitigating coverage misfit (Berente and Yoo 2012) to an even greater extent. This in turn, facilitated the successful implementation of the SAP ERP system. ## Discussion and Conclusion

By integrating our findings across the four eras, a process model of organizational sensemaking in the context of ERP implementation can be inductively derived to address the research questions set forth at the beginning of this paper (refer to Figure 5).

Specifically, contextual conditions influence the technical structure by shaping its constituent elements, which manifested in our case study in the form of ERP knowledge and perceived influence. The effect of context on ERP knowledge is both supported by our findings (e.g., in Eras 2–4, ERP knowledge was enhanced by the lessons learnt and experience gained from the prior ERP implementation outcome of the previous era/s) and well-established in the literature (e.g., Scott and Vessey 2000). Likewise, our finding that context affects perceived influence (e.g., the organizational culture that emphasizes respect for hierarchy led to the perception that the top management was the most appropriate stakeholder group to drive ERP implementation in Era 1) echoes the work of Beck and Plowman (2009), who used the example of the space shuttle Columbia disaster to argue that contextual factors such as culture can influence the beliefs of organizational members in relation to their ability to influence the process of sensemaking, to the extent that some would choose to defer to authority even when they possesses the relevant expertise.

In addition, our process model suggests the technical structure exerts a strong influence on the initiation and enactment phases of sensemaking. In the Initiation phase, it is because the knowledge and experience surrounding ERP systems provide both a sense of what is normal and an indication of what is possible, which facilitate the detection of expectation gaps (Maitlis and Lawrence 2007). Similarly, the technical structure plays a crucial role in the enactment phase because it is the prior experience and existing mental models surrounding ERP systems that would shape retrospection (Cornelissen and Clarke 2010; Klein et al. 2006), which is enacted by a primary sensemaker to produce a basic sensemaking account in this phase (Weick et al. 2005).

While the initiation and enactment phases are individual processes, the selection and retention phases that follow as organizational sensemaking unfolds are social processes that require the involvement of other stakeholders (Weick et al. 2005). Accordingly, our process model suggests it is the social structure that plays an important role in shaping the latter phases. The nature of the sensemaking configuration and sensemaking exchanges that constitute the social structure, in turn, are influenced by the technical structure. This is because how each stakeholder group see themselves in terms of their abilities to contribute and influence others would define how they would act in organizational sensemaking (Patriotta and Spedale 2009). In the context of ERP implementation, our findings suggest that these two abilities are reflected by ERP knowledge and perceived influence (i.e., the two constituent elements of the technical structure) respectively. For example, the various business units of STC did not take on a central role in organizational sensemaking until the final era of its implementation journey, because it was only at that juncture that they perceived themselves to be sufficiently knowledgeable about ERP systems and their implications.

Likewise, the influence of STC’s IT function steadily increased across the four eras in line with their growing status within the organization, which likely made them more confident of engaging in sensegiving to shape the views of the other stakeholder groups (Gioia and Chittipeddi 1991).

Evidence from our case study further indicate that the sensemaking configuration could take on a variety of forms that are akin to the different approaches to IT governance (see Weill and Ross 2005). More specifically, the sensemaking configurations that manifested in our case study were the business monarchy, the IT monarchy, the IT–business duopoly, and the federal configurations in Eras 1–4 of STC’s ERP implementation journey. As these forms would have implications for the levels of control and animation in sensemaking (Maitlis 2005), our process model suggests the sensemaking configu ration would determine the nature of sensemaking exchanges as well. At STC, the aforementioned configurations gave rise to the restricted, fragmented, guided and ubiquitous forms of sensemaking exchanges in each of the eras respectively.

The nature of sensemaking exchanges, in turn, has an influence on the selection and retention phases of organizational sensemaking. The reason for this is that the exchanges would entail various combinations of sensegiving, sensedemanding (Vlaar et al. 2008), and joint sensemaking (Maitlis and Christianson 2014) that form the basis for constructing intersubjective meaning (Balogun and Johnson 2005; Cornelissen and Clarke 2010). In other words, sensemaking exchanges would shape the extent and nature of competition between the basic sensemaking account of the primary sensemaker and the secondary sensemaking accounts of the other involved stakeholders in the selection phase, as well as influence the unitariness and richness of the resultant shared account in the retention phase (Cornelissen and Clarke 2010; Klein et al. 2006; Maitlis and Sonensheim 2010).

Finally, our process model suggests that an iteration of organizational sensemaking may be influenced by the outcome of a previous iteration in the context of ERP implementation. A possible explanation for this is that when sensemaking evokes action (Maitlis and Christianson 2014), the sensemakers would be able to use the outcome of the action as feedback to clarify future sensemaking (Weick et al. 2005). For example, in our case study, we observed that the prior ERP implementation outcome of a previous era can activate some aspect of the organization’s culture that provides an impetus for action (e.g., failure perceived as a loss of “face” in Era 2), or alter the ERP knowledge and perceived influence of the sensemaking entities (e.g., success enhancing the status of STC’s IT function in the latter eras). This is supported by the notion that stakeholders “who act in organizations often produce structures, constraints, and opportunities that were not there before they took action” (Weick 1988, p. 306), playing a key role in influencing the environment in which they find themselves.

## The Influence of Organizational Sensemaking on ERP Implementation

In relation to our second research question, our process model also sheds light on the influence of organizational sensemaking on ERP Implementation. In particular, our model suggests that each of the four phases of organizational sensemaking has an influence at different points of the typical ERP implementation lifecycle (see Markus and Tanis 1999; Pan et al. 2007; Parr and Shanks 2000).

Specifically, our model suggests that the initiation phase will influence ERP implementation when the process is first triggered (i.e., when the original idea for ERP implementation arises; see Pan et al. 2007). Organizational sensemaking originates with an individual (Weick et al. 2005) who serves a primary sensemaker, and our findings suggest that it is in the initiation phase where a relevant stakeholder is installed in this role. Moreover, the initiation phase is centered on the detection of expectation gaps (Maitlis and Lawrence 2007), which, as argued previously, is dependent on the existing knowledge and experience of the primary sensemaker surrounding ERP implementation (i.e., the technical structure). In other words, the Initiation phase will influence what gets noticed out of the continuous stream of activities and events that surround an organization (Chia 2000) to provide the spark for ERP implementation. In the context of STC, the triggers of ERP implementation across the four eras all stemmed from the expectation gaps of the primary sensemaker of the era. To illustrate, in Era 2 of STC’s ERP implementation journey, it was the IT function’s desire to rectify implementation failure, coupled with their perception that they were responsible for the failure of the previous era, that provided the impetus for ERP implementation.

In addition, our model suggests that the selection phase will shape ERP implementation at the point where the business case for ERP implementation is being established (see Markus and Tanis 1999). This is due to the fact that retrospection by the primary sensemaker will result in the formation of a basic sensemaking account (Cornelissen and Clarke 2010), and it is these basic sensemaking accounts across the four eras of our case study (which are once again contingent on the existing knowledge and experience of the primary sensemaker) that essentially form the rationale for ERP implementation in each era. For instance, it was the experience of efficiency gains from Era 2, along with the insight of the top management that there were more strategic outcomes to be derived, that precipitated the decision to integrate their systems in Era 3.

Following the selection phase, our model suggests that the enactment phase will influence ERP implementation when the design of the system is taking shape, which “involves understanding the ERP and organizational processes and fashioning a mutual fit” (Pan et al. 2007, p. 406). This is because the basic sensemaking account of the primary sensemaker and the secondary sensemaking accounts of the other relevant stakeholders are being brought into competition with one another in this phase (Maitlis and Sonensheim 2010). And depending on the sensemaking configuration, which defines the roles of the stakeholders involved, as well as the nature of the sensemaking exchanges between them, our model suggests that the competition between sensemaking accounts will have a defining influence on the requirements and design of the ERP system to be implemented. To cite an example from our case study, in Era 1, it was the business monarchy sensemaking configuration, coupled with sensemaking exchanges that were characterized by unidirectional sensegiving from the top management, that resulted in the dominance of the top management’s sensemaking account, which led to a system design that did not adequately reflect the requirements of STC’s business units.

Finally, our model suggests that the retention phase will influence ERP implementation when the actual development of the system is taking place (see Parr and Shanks 2000). The reason for this is that the multiple sensemaking accounts that have been brought into competition with one another in the selection phase are being pared down into a shared sensemaking account in retention phase (Maitlis 2005), and from our findings, it is the unitariness and richness of the resultant shared account that would determine the approach to system implementation at this juncture. For example, in Era 4 of STC’s ERP implementation journey, the approach to system implementation was integrative and reflective of the requirements of all the involved stakeholders because of the unitary and rich nature of the shared sensemaking account. Our findings are consistent with the existing sensemaking literature, which views these attributes of the shared account as the fundamental determinants of organizational action (see Balogun and Johnson 2005; Maitlis 2005).

## Theoretical Contributions to ERP Implementation Research

At the outset of the paper, we noted how there was a lack of research on ERP implementation from a sensemaking perspective even though it is a crucial and inextricable part of the process (Boersma and Kingma 2005). In being one of the earliest targeted at examining the nature and implications of organizational sensemaking directly, this study has made a number of theoretical contributions. First, our study illustrates how organizational sensemaking unfolds in four phases (i.e., initiation, enactment, selection, and retention), and reveals the implications of each phase for ERP implementation. Specifically, initiation leads to the detection of ERP implementation triggers, enactment establishes the rationale for ERP implementation, selection shapes the development of system requirements and design, and retention affects the approach to system implementation. Moreover, our study reveals how each of these phases are affected by the constituent elements of an underlying sensemaking structure, and how this structure is in turn shaped by contextual conditions. In doing so, our study sheds light on both the nature and implications of organizational sensemaking in the context of ERP implementation, and presents a nomological network with a certain extent of explanatory and predictive potential.

Specifically, the process model developed in this paper can be used to explain what went wrong in ERP implementation projects that failed or did not meet their intended objectives. For example, if an ERP system is implemented but it does not meet the needs of its stakeholders (e.g., Barker and Frolick 2003), our model suggests that the technical structure may be inadequate (i.e., the stakeholders that should have input in the design of the system are lacking in ERP knowledge and perceived influence), which can translate into an ineffective social structure (i.e., a sensemaking configuration with inappropriate stakeholders driving organizational sensemaking and dominating sensemaking exchanges). This may result in a sub-optimal system design and/or implementation approach that precipitate the failure of the project. In addition, the process model developed in this paper can be used to predict the approach to, and by corollary the outcomes of, ERP implementation. For instance, from our case study, we observed how the coercive and mimetic environmental pressures imposed upon STC and an organizational culture that is characterized by a deference to hierarchy (Lockett 1988) precipitated the formation of a poorly considered sensemaking structure that arguably doomed the implementation efforts of Era 1 to failure. Our model suggests that the reverse may also be true, in that with an organizational culture that is consultative and encouraging of experimentation (Swanson and Ramiller 2004), as well as a supportive environment where the resources and expertise required for ERP implementation are readily available (Sarker et al. 2012), the formation of an appropriate sensemaking structure, and hence an integrative implementation approach, is more likely. An important caveat to note is that there is no universally optimal approach to ERP implementation (Ward et al. 2005), but depending on the specific implementation objectives (e.g., an organization may be only aiming at automating certain business processes with the deployment of various ERP modules), our model can be used to predict if the intended outcomes are likely to be achieved based on the prevailing contextual conditions and the nature of the sensemaking structure in place.

Second, our study complements the five dominant perspectives of ERP implementation in the existing literature (refer to Appendix A) and reveals the overarching influence of sensemaking over the discrete prescriptions of each perspective. Specifically, the notion of a sensemaking configuration introduced in this paper can be seen as a device for managing the conflict (Basu and Lederer 2011; Haines and Goodhue 2003) between users and developers of an ERP system (i.e., the agency perspective), as well as enhancing the collaboration (Sasidharan et al. 2012) between them (i.e., the coordination perspective). This is because a sensemaking configuration specifies the nature of the relationships between the three stakeholder groups that are key to ERP implementation (i.e., top management, the system developers and the business users). This, in turn, influences the sensemaking exchanges through which the accounts of diverse stakeholders are iteratively refined (Berente et al. 2011), brought into conflict, and pared down to a singular account to enable coordination (Maitlis and Sonensheim 2010).

The sensemaking configuration can also be viewed as a device for controlling (Berente et al. 2010; Soh et al. 2010) ERP implementation (i.e., the control perspective). The reason for this is that a sensemaking configuration defines the authority and decision making role of each stakeholder group in the implementation process, which would subsequently influence the efficacy and applicability of the various forms of formal and informal controls (Kirsch 2004). In addition, the sensemaking configuration has implications for risk management (Aloini et al. 2012) (i.e., the risk management perspective). This due to the way it shapes how the involved stakeholders participate in planning for ERP implementation in the design phase, and rationalizing the stream of events, decisions and activities that occur in the execution phase. The former would influence the identification and ownership of risks, while the latter would determine the risk responses enacted (Sumner 2000).

Moreover, our study presents a solution to the knowledge integration problem (Ko et al. 2005) in ERP implementation (i.e., the knowledge management perspective). Prior studies on knowledge integration in the context of ERP implementation have revealed the need for integrating the different knowledge owned by the various groups of stakeholders (Pan et al. 2007). Some have further suggested the need for the institutionalization of integrative practices (Patnayakuni et al. 2007) and appropriate project governance (Tiwana 2009) for the attainment of this end. The process model of our study is thus an important contribution as it reveals some practices (i.e., the different sensemaking exchanges) and governance approaches (i.e., the various sensemaking configurations) that can facilitate differing extents of knowledge integration in the context of ERP implementation.

## Theoretical Contributions to Organizational Sensemaking Research

This study also makes a number of contributions to the literature on organizational sensemaking. First, our study has introduced the concept of a sensemaking structure, which we conceptualized, drawing on the work of Kamoche and his colleagues on the related topic of organizational improvisation (see Kamoche and Cunha 2001; Kamoche et al. 2003), to consist of a technical structure and a social structure. While the relationship between the structure and process of sensemaking has been acknowledged to be significant in a number of previous works (e.g., Patriotta and Spedale 2009; Weick 1993), there has been little research on the nature of this structure and how it interacts with the process of sensemaking to date. Moreover, although the process of organizational sensemaking has been the subject of considerable attention in the existing literature, there has been very little research on how particular forms of sensemaking come to be (e.g., Balogun and Johnson 2005; Cornelissen and Clarke 2010).

The ideas introduced in our study are thus an important contribution, as it provides a structural perspective of sensemaking to complement the existing process-centric view. In doing so, our study sheds light on the structural antecedents of organizational sensemaking and hints at a number of potential drivers and inhibitors that can shape its form. In particular, it has identified and defined the elements of the underlying technical and social structures and used the possible variation in those elements, influenced by changing contextual conditions, to explain how the process of organizational sensemaking can unfold in a variety of different ways.

Second, by examining organizational sensemaking in the specific context of ERP implementation, our study has revealed the need for a more nuanced view of the phenomenon. In particular, our study demonstrates that the association between the forms of organizational sensemaking and the nature of the resultant sensemaking account is not as straightforward as previously thought (see Maitlis 2005). For instance, data from the BPCS implementation era of our case study reveal that restricted sensemaking does not necessarily translate to a sensemaking account that is unitary to enable consistent action. In fact, if restricted sensemaking is coupled with a lack of the requisite knowledge (Pan et al. 2007) and unidirectional sensegiving, a nominal account based on limited understanding could still result. Overall, our study suggests that in order to understand the implications of sensemaking for organizational actions, a more holistic view of the involved participants (i.e., beyond the two stakeholder groups considered in Maitlis 2005) and their precise roles in organizational sensemaking is called for. In particular, there is also a need to consider the roles that involved stakeholders take on within different sensemaking configurations, as well as the types and directions of sensemaking exchanges (see Figures 1–4). In revealing the influence of these aspects of sensemaking on the resultant sensemaking account, the presented process model not only introduces greater sophistication to the conceptualization of the social processes of organizational sensemaking, but extends previous studies of the implications of recipient sensemaking (e.g., Rouleau and Balogun 2011; Vlaar et al. 2008) as well. The increased sophistication advances our understanding of organizational sensemaking, and enriches the developing “language with which a variety of everyday sensemaking processes can be described, compared, and contrasted” (Maitlis 2005, p. 44).

## Practical Implications

Beyond its theoretical contributions, we contend that our study also has important practical implications. Specifically, our study provides a number of indications on how ERP practitioners can influence the process of organizational sensemaking, so that the potential misalignments in the embedded structures and processes of both the package and the organization (Sia and Soh 2007) can be resolved to increase the likelihood of implementation success.

First, in specifying the elements of the underlying technical structure and revealing their influence on the initial phases of organizational sensemaking, our study can help ERP practitioners to ensure that an appropriate primary sensemaker is installed for an implementation project, so that the sources of package-organization misalignment (see Soh et al. 2003) can be accurately identified and accounted for. In particular, according to our process model, the question of who should be taking on the role is influenced by the ERP knowledge and perceived influence of the relevant stakeholders. As such, ERP practitioners can choose to either (1) install a stakeholder who already possesses an adequate level of both of these attributes in the role of the primary sensemaker, or (2) take steps to enhance both of these attributes if such a stakeholder is not readily present when a more integrative and encompassing implementation approach is required. However, our process model also suggests that choosing between these options will require a careful consideration of the contextual conditions. For instance, if the organization has no prior experience of ERP systems, our study suggests that at the very least, the IT department should be co-opted to lead module development so that there is a certain extent of technical expertise that is factored into organizational sensemaking. This will ensure that the primary sensemaker driving organizational sensemaking has (or can attain) an adequate understanding of the embedded structures and processes of the package (Strong and Volkoff 2010), so that the areas of misalignment with the existing structures and processes of the organization can be targeted subsequently.

Second, if the areas of package-organization misalignment have been identified, our study also provides some prescriptions for managing the misalignment. Specifically, our study suggests that if the misalignment is particularly severe, it can be ameliorated incrementally in a specific sequence. Enable ment misfit (see Berente and Yoo 2012) could first be addressed by having ERP implementation directed by a socia structure dominated by the IT function (i.e., the IT monarchy sensemaking configuration) with the modest aim of simply supporting the existing structures and processes of the organization. This will allow the organization to adapt more gradually to the large-scale organizational changes necessitated by the implementation of an ERP system (Farh et al. 2007), and enable business stakeholders to accrue crucial knowledge on the workings, potential and implications of the system along the way (Pan et al. 2007). Subsequently, our model suggests that coverage misfit (see Strong and Volkoff 2010) could be addressed by increasing the extent of involvement of business stakeholders within the social structure progressively, first by soliciting their inputs when integrating the various modules of an ERP system(i.e., the IT–business duopoly sensemaking configuration) before engaging them in jointly shaping systems development (i.e., the federal sensemaking configuration). In other words, through the installation of a sensemaking configuration that is appropriate to the objectives of ERP implementation (e.g., automation for effi ciency gains or integration for operational effectiveness), ERP practitioners may be able effectively reduce the extent of package-organization alignment, manage the risks of ERP implementation (see Aloini et al. 2012), and avoid overreaching for implementation objectives that they are simply not ready for. Overall, by providing insights into the nature and implications of organizational sensemaking in the context of ERP implementation, it is hoped that our study can help ERP practitioners to make the most of the efforts and resources invested in the implementation of ERP systems, and exploit their fullest potential.

## Limitations and Future Research

This study is not without its limitations. First, although we uncovered four ways in which the process of organizational sensemaking can unfold at STC, we must acknowledge that other variations are possible. Indeed, our model suggests that a sensemaking structure is context dependent and can be shaped by the sensemaking outcome of a previous iteration. As both contextual conditions and sensemaking outcomes can take on a near infinite array of possible forms, varying them could affect the sensemaking structure in a myriad ways, resulting in a kaleidoscopic array of technical and social structures (Maitlis 2005; Weick et al. 2005). In addition, although the progression between the four sensemaking structures appears to be linear in our study, we must emphasize that the transformation of sensemaking structures may neither be evolutionary nor sequential. An organizational culture characterized by uncertainty avoidance (see Martinsons et al. 2009), for instance, could be a boundary condition that influenced the gradual and evolutionary nature of the transformation of sensemaking at STC and explain why it took almost 11 years since the failed implementation of BPCS before STC decided that it was ready for a packaged ERP system again. While it is impossible to capture all possible configurations and transformational trajectories of organizational sensemaking within a single study, those that have not been uncovered in our study would certainly be a fruitful avenue for future research.

Second, although the single case research method adopted in this study is a “typical and legitimate endeavor” (Lee and Baskerville 2003, p. 231) in qualitative research and over half of all case research papers are based on single cases (Sarker et al. 2013), a common criticism of the method is the problem of generalizability or external validity (Walsham 2006). An issue that limits the generalizability of our study, in particular, may lie in its singular context (e.g., the influence of Chinese culture). However, this study invokes the principles of analytic generalization (see Silva and Hirschheim 2007) or what some researchers refer to as “generalizing from description to theory” (Lee and Baskerville 2003, p. 235). In any case, future research can be directed at statistically validating the propositions of our process model, so that the boundary conditions (e.g., organizational culture, ERP knowledge of the various sensemaking entities) of our study can be better defined.

A third potential limitation of this study concerns the retrospective nature of the personal interviews that form our primary source of data. As retrospective responses are susceptible to errors of recall and post hoc rationalizations (Glick et al. 1990), we have tried to circumscribe the problem by adopting a number of well-established measures. First, we only interviewed informants who were personally involved in the process of ERP implementation during the relevant period of interest (Porra et al. 2014). Second, informants were only asked about the events, decisions and actions of significance.

This improves the likelihood and accuracy of recall since important events tend to be remembered better (Leidner et al. 2009). Third, we supplemented our interviews with an historical analysis of secondary documents to ensure that our data is consistent with the accounts or interpretations of others over time (Mason et al. 1997b; Porra et al. 2014). Fourth, we made sure that all of our arguments are triangulated by at least two sources of data (Pan and Tan 2011). Fifth, we verified our findings with our informants to establish the validity of our interpretation (Klein and Myers 1999). Through these means, we hope our readers will at least be able to evaluate the plausibility of our theoretical arguments.

## Appendix A

Existing Theoretical Perspectives of ERP Implementation

<table><tr><td>Theoretical Perspective</td><td>Key Arguments and Prescriptions</td></tr><tr><td>Agency TheoryEmphasizes the minimization of conflict between users and developers of ERP systems</td><td>Studies adopting the agency perspective have examined the influence of factors such as pre-qualification efforts, monitoring, incentive alignment, control over developer behavior, adverse selection, level of domain knowledge and skills, and moral hazard (e.g., Basu and Lederer 2011; Haines and Goodhue 2003) on ERP implementation. From a process-oriented perspective, mechanisms such as reinvention and improvised learning have also been proposed as potential solutions to the human agency problem in ERP implementation (e.g., Boudreau and Robey 2005).</td></tr><tr><td>Risk ManagementEmphasizes the proactive management of project risks</td><td>Studies aligned with the risk management perspective have identified a number of risk factors that could adversely impact ERP implementation. These factors include organizational fit, user skill mix, management structure and strategy, systems design, user involvement and training, technology planning/ integration, extent of business process redesign, vendor stability, and leadership effectiveness (e.g., Aloini et al. 2012; Scott and Vessey 2002; Sumner 2000). A variety of risk assessment frameworks (e.g., Dey et al. 2013) and techniques (e.g., fault tree analysis; see Zeng and Skibniewski 2013) have also been prescribed for the identification and management of implementation risks.</td></tr><tr><td>Control TheoryEmphasizes the judicious application of various forms of control</td><td>Studies adopting the control theory perspective have suggested the importance of enacting different control mechanisms (see Kirsch 2004) in different stages of the implementation process and for different types of stakeholders (Soh et al. 2010). In-depth process studies further suggest that complete control over a process as complex as ERP implementation may be unattainable, and some forms of control may not necessarily be productive but simply enacted for a disciplinary or legitimizing purpose (Berente et al. 2010). Moreover, there is a need to maintain loose coupling between the practices of the involved stakeholders and those embodied by the ERP package (Berente and Yoo 2012).</td></tr><tr><td>Coordination TheoryEmphasizes collaboration between the various entities involved in ERP implementation</td><td>Studies aligned with the coordination perspective have explored the influence of factors such as consultant-client relationships, industry competence, client-specific knowledge, product brand, network reach and technical know-how (e.g., Sarker et al. 2012). In addition, process-oriented studies have suggested that effective coordination can be achieved through the development of certain organizational capabilities. These capabilities include the ability to achieve and maintain an appropriate social network structure (Sasidharan et al. 2012), and establish effective coalitions between the different groups of involved entities with the appropriate implementation roles and decision-making authority (Lyytinen and Newman 2015).</td></tr><tr><td>Knowledge ManagementEmphasizes the need to manage knowledge associated with the process of implementation</td><td>Studies adopting the knowledge management perspective have examined the influence of factors such as absorptive capacity, shared understanding, developer-user relationship quality, intrinsic and extrinsic motivation, and communications capability on knowledge transfer during the process of implementation (e.g., Ko et al. 2005) under the assumption that effective knowledge transfer increases the likelihood of ERP implementation success. Process-oriented studies have also revealed that different types of knowledge are important in different stages of the implementation process (Lee and Lee 2000). Moreover, the management of the different knowledge types requires social capital that bonds and bridges different stakeholder groups to promote knowledge transfer and integration (Pan et al. 2007).</td></tr></table>

## Thematic Interview Guide for Interview with Former CIO of STC

## History of IT Department

• Can you provide a history of the IT department at STC?

• How has the IT department grown over the years?

• How has its status and role within the organization evolved?

## History of ERP Implementation<sup>2</sup>

• Can you describe the ERP implementation journey of STC?

• When was ERP systems first introduced at STC?

• What were the major phases of ERP implementation?

• How have the ERP systems evolved over the years?

## How Key Decisions on ERP implementation were made:

In each phase of ERP implementation...

• How were the key decisions surrounding ERP decisions made?<sup>3</sup>

• Who were the key decision makers<sup>?</sup> <sup>4</sup>

• What information was taken into account in the making of those decisions?<sup>5</sup>

• What were some of the considerations in the making of those decisions?<sup>5</sup>

• How were the decisions communicated to the rest of the organization?<sup>6</sup>

• What was the response of the other organizational members?<sup>6</sup>

## Culture at STC<sup>7</sup>

• How would you describe the culture of STC?

• How did the organizational culture of STC affect ERP implementation?

• How would you describe the culture of the IT department?

• How did the department culture of STC affect ERP implementation?

• How is the organizational culture influenced by the national culture?

• Were there cultural differences between different business units and the IT department?

## IT Governance at STC<sup>4</sup>

Over the years…

• How are decisions related to IT made at STC?

• Were the decisions technology-driven or business-driven?

• What was the top management’s role in the decision making process?

• What was the IT department’s role in the decision making process?

• What was the role of the business units in the decision making process?
