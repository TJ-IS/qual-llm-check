## Abstract

Ecosystems in highly regulated sectors such as banking are orchestrated through institutional policies and technical standards that ensure the interoperability of cloud service infrastructures. Despite such interoperability, actors must still coordinate distributed data control to guarantee that data are treated under equal conditions. Drawing on a case study of the Italian banking sector between 2009 and 2020, we investigate the coordination efforts of ecosystem actors in distributing data control across organizational boundaries and jurisdictions. We show that, despite the availability of interoperability standards, the distribution of data control creates tensions that, on the one hand, hinder integration efforts and, on the other hand, allow disproportionate value capture, together contributing to ecosystem failures. We introduce a process model that details how contractual and procedural coordination mechanisms can mitigate these tensions and facilitate value co-creation. We conclude with a discussion of the contributions and implications of our findings for further research on data control coordination.

Keywords: Ecosystem Coordination, Ecosystem Failure, Interoperability, Open Banking, Cloud Sourcing, Data Governance, Data Management

## 1 Introduction

An ecosystem is an organizational form in which interdependent actors establish multilateral relationships to co-create value (Adner, 2017; Jacobides et al., 2018; Kretschmer et al., 2022). Unlike ecosystems for mobile applications (e.g., Apple iOS), advertising (e.g., Google Ads), social media (e.g., Facebook), and video gaming (e.g., Sony PlayStation), which have attracted much attention in the literature, ecosystems in highly regulated sectors such as banking are not orchestrated by single hub firms that provide digital platforms and define rules for governing ecosystem interactions. Instead, ecosystems in highly regulated sectors are orchestrated via institutional policies and technical standards introduced by supranational bodies such as the European Union (Dinckol et al., 2023; Ozalp et al., 2022). For instance, in banking, the European Union’s revised Payment Services Directive (PSD2) enforces the use of open standards to ensure that data access, processing, and storage in payment services occur under equal conditions beyond organizational and national boundaries (European Commission, 2015, p. 51; see also Gomber et al., 2018; Gozman et al., 2018; Kazan et al., 2018).

A necessary condition for the formation of ecosystems in highly regulated sectors such as banking is the interoperability of their digital technologies. Interoperability is the ability of two or more systems to exchange data and understand the data that is being exchanged (Hodapp & Hanelt, 2022). Ecosystem actors such as banks, cloud service providers (CSPs), start-ups, and stakeholders from the private and public sectors cocreate value by collaboratively processing structured and unstructured data only if technical interfaces ensure the interoperability of hardware and software used for the provision of services (Hodapp & Hanelt, 2022). For example, the recent European Union’s Digital Markets Act (DMA) requires that the provision of any digital services offered on cloud-based ecosystems “ensure[s] access under equal conditions to, and interoperability with, the same operating system, hardware or software features” (European Commission, 2020a, pp. 26-27). Therefore, interoperability is a key requirement to drive and enable seamless, meaningful, and secure interactions among ecosystem actors in highly regulated sectors. The technical mechanisms for realizing interoperability are open standards, application programming interfaces (APIs), and reference architectures. When such mechanisms are successfully implemented, cloud service infrastructures (Venters & Whitley, 2012) permit the formation of an ecosystem through the distribution of data control among ecosystem actors (Abraham et al., 2019).

However, in such distributed settings, no single actor has full control over data; hence, actors need to negotiate, agree on, and implement a set of mechanisms to coordinate the distribution of data control. Interoperability ensures the technical mechanisms with which cloud services interact but does not define how different actors coordinate their data control and under what conditions this leads to the formation of an ecosystem. Moreover, in highly regulated sectors, who controls data becomes a key problem since multiple actors may obtain the right to access both aggregated and nonaggregated data that can be used not just for business purposes (Ozalp et al., 2022) but also to undertake deliberately harmful actions (Ransbotham et al., 2016), as recent investigations by competition authorities have shown (ACCC, 2022; CMA, 2022; European Commission, 2020b). Although regulations such as the European Commission’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) were introduced to address the problem of properly distributing data control rights, recent empirical evidence shows that control over data access, processing, and storage are, in fact, increasingly concentrated in large CSPs (Peukert et al., 2022).

Establishing coordination mechanisms for distributing data control, therefore, is a problem to be addressed when cloud services are deployed to form an ecosystem in highly regulated sectors. Without such data control coordination mechanisms, value co-creation among ecosystem actors is challenged. This leads to our research question: How does data control coordination lead to the formation of an ecosystem in highly regulated sectors?

To address this question, we conducted a qualitative case study on the Italian banking sector from 2009 to 2020, utilizing both primary interview data and secondary archival data. During this period, Italian banks, CSPs, regulators, and fintech companies coordinated data control for the deployment of cloud services. Our findings reveal that tensions emerged over time as banks, CSPs, and fintech companies distributed data control within the sector. Specifically, we identified tensions that impede the seamless integration of complementary data-related operations among ecosystem actors and tensions that arise when updates in regulatory frameworks allow some actors to capture disproportionate value. Both tensions can impact participation and value-creating contributions in the ecosystem, leading to ecosystem failures (Jacobides et al., 2024; Ozalp et al., 2018; Ozcan & Santos, 2015). We discuss how these tensions can be mitigated through contractual and procedural data control coordination mechanisms to co-create value within the ecosystem. The process of achieving data control coordination is nonlinear and recursive, as new tensions arise with the evolution of the regulatory and industry landscapes and with data aggregation problems stemming from joint service consumption.

This paper offers three key contributions. First, we develop a process model that underscores the critical role of data control coordination in building and sustaining effective ecosystems in the absence of a hub firm. Contrary to claims that data control has lost its significance due to the role of standards (Chua et al., 2022), our model shows that distributed data control requires active coordination among diverse ecosystem actors. Second, we theorize the distinct coordination mechanisms that address data control tensions and ecosystem failures in highly regulated sectors. While the dynamics of such failures have been theorized in recent work (Jacobides et al., 2024), we provide empirical evidence of how those can be contained and overcome to enable the formation of new ecosystems. Third, our study elucidates the roles of various actors in the regulatory process of cloud-based infrastructures. As regulatory frameworks evolve, they must address the emerging needs of actors and technological developments to balance data governance and innovation (Biedebach & Hanelt, 2020; Iansiti & Levien, 2004). In particular, we document the crucial role played by intermediaries and local champs to support incumbents and CSPs in navigating the complexities introduced by new interoperability standards and regulatory changes. We conclude with implications for further research.

## 2 Theoretical Background

## 2.1 Data Control in Cloud Service Infrastructures

Cloud service infrastructures point to a computing paradigm in which a network of loosely coupled services provided by large pools of automated scalable hardware and software resources are distributed among interoperable data centers (Cusumano, 2010; Venters & Whitley, 2012; Willcocks et al., 2013). In cloud infrastructures, services communicate by exchanging data between autonomous software applications in the form of attributes for service requests and brokerage. In addition to encrypted personal data and transaction records, these data can be any form of user-generated content or system logs capturing the time, type, sending and receiving ends of a network interaction, or technical details about the operation (Lukyanenko et al., 2019). Though initially designed for the optimal allocation of resources, detection, and recovery from malfunctions and the identification of potentially harmful activities, cloud data can also be used for other purposes by organizations operating in the network (Constantiou & Kallinikos, 2015). For instance, in a mobile network, telecommunication operators can further process cloud data to innovate their products and services (Aaltonen & Tempini, 2014). Therefore, in a cloud service infrastructure, organizational actors can leverage a heterogeneous spectrum of structured and unstructured data for strategic purposes (Chua et al., 2022; Constantiou & Kallinikos, 2015).

A key requirement for the deployment of a cloud service infrastructure is the interoperability of the underlying data (Henfridsson et al., 2018; Hodapp & Hanelt, 2022). Interoperability is based on technical specifications to standardize data semantics, technologies, and interfaces for distributed data control. Though interoperability has shown its potential as an enabler of new forms of data computing interactions in privately owned networks of cloud resources, the distribution of data control in open and fully decentralized cloud service infrastructures can create tensions among organizational actors. In fact, in the absence of a mandate by a leading actor (Hein et al., 2020), data can be used for purposes that may not align with the interests of diverse actors, thus generating tensions among them (Arce, 2022). For instance, in the banking sector, the lack of transparency and due diligence on data-related operations in cloud service infrastructures can lead to tensions in managing cloud sourcing relationships (Gozman & Willcocks, 2019; Gozman et al., 2020; Kaniadakis & Constantinides, 2014). The structural and dynamic complexity of cloud service infrastructures (Ali et al., 2021) and the crosscountry differences in sharing and exchanging sensitive data (Otjacques et al., 2007) have been recognized as major factors limiting the deployment of cloud services.

We refer to these issues as data control tensions, where data control is control of the right to access, process, and store data produced and exchanged within a cloud service infrastructure. Data control may introduce tensions when data are distributed across multiple actors in a highly regulated ecosystem supported by a cloud service infrastructure. The distribution of data control can create tensions when the interests, preferences, and expectations of actors within an ecosystem are different or even in conflict (Vial, 2019).

## 2.2 Coordination in Highly Regulated Cloud-Based Ecosystems

Previous research emphasizes the need for a negotiation process before collaboration, where actors agree on coordination mechanisms to manage dependencies and align their joint processes (Ali et al., 2021; Crowston et al., 2015; Gozman & Willcocks, 2019). In cloud-based ecosystems, effectively addressing data control tensions is critical, as dependencies emerge from decomposing actors goals into assigning the rights to access, process, and store data among actors. Without effective management, these dependencies can lead to functional failures—that is, given the capabilities and resources available among the actors, less value is created and consumed than expected (Jacobides et al., 2024; Ozalp et al., 2018; Ozcan & Santos, 2015). In cloud-based ecosystems such functional failures can occur because of data control tensions, ultimately hindering cloud-based collaboration. Effectively, the ecosystem becomes dysfunctional.

Although data control is typically centralized with the owner of a cloud platform (Hein et al., 2020), such centralization is often impractical in highly regulated sectors. In these settings, multiple actors are mandated to execute data-related operations, creating complex dependencies exacerbated by overlapping regulatory demands (Kira et al., 2021; Paparova et al., 2023). Consequently, distributed data control necessitates interorganizational coordination between heterogeneous actors, particularly when strategic mandates of data control may impede ecosystem formation (Ozalp et al., 2022). This data control problem can lead to distributional failures, where there is a disproportionate distribution of benefits or responsibilities among actors, undermining trust and coordination (Jacobides et al., 2024).

Coordination is defined as the division of labor across actors and the subsequent integration of dispersed operations, crucial for value generation (Castañer & Oliveira, 2020). According to Sobrero and Schrader (1998), a distinction can be made between contractual and procedural coordination mechanisms. Contractual coordination mechanisms define the mutual exchange of rights through legal contracts, whereas procedural coordination mechanisms involve “the mutual exchange of information among the different actors involved to realize the expected goals” (Sobrero & Schrader, 1998, p. 586). Both types of mechanisms are relevant for managing cloud services where data control is partitioned and regulated through contracts as well as procedures such as algorithms and protocols. When successfully managed, these dependencies foster efficient and effective joint value creation (Weinhardt et al., 2009).

Ecosystem coordination relies on a multilateral alignment structure, described as “the extent to which there is mutual agreement among the members” (Adner, 2017, p. 42). This mutual agreement is necessary to manage the diverse and sometimes contradictory expectations or interests among actors (Adner, 2017). Effective management of these dynamics is critical, as unresolved tensions can lead to both functional and distributional failures (Jacobides et al., 2024; Ozalp et al., 2018; Ozcan & Santos, 2015). In practice, data control rights in cloud-based ecosystems are typically integrated and (re)configured to enable efficient collaboration and joint service delivery and consumption (Weinhardt et al., 2009; Jacobides et al., 2024). However, effective collaboration hinges on agreeing on coordination mechanisms, especially in the absence of a central hub firm, a common scenario in highly regulated sectors (Bartelheimer et al., 2022; Beverungen et al., 2022).

Regulation plays a crucial role in fostering coordination and mitigating the risk of failures due to opportunistic behaviors among actors (Kira et al., 2021). For example, the Revised European Payment Service Directive (PSD2) has facilitated the integration of licensed fintech companies into banking ecosystems, supporting transactions and contributing to consumer welfare and broader community development (Dinckol et al., 2023; Kira et al., 2021; Markus & Nan, 2020). These efforts highlight the importance of effective data control coordination mechanisms to ensure the successful distribution of data control and prevent ecosystem failures.

In summary, based on our review of the literature, we identified data control coordination as a major problem to be addressed among ecosystem actors prior to them engaging in value co-creation. We bring together insights from representative literatures to show that, in the absence of a central actor such as a hub firm, ecosystems in highly regulated sectors need to negotiate and agree on a set of coordination mechanisms to manage data control. ## 3 Research Method

We conducted an interpretive case study (Walsham, 1995) on the Italian banking sector. We collected data from 2018 to 2020 to identify key events related to data control tensions in the emerging ecosystem and analyze the coordination efforts of various actors. The setting allowed us to study the process of data control coordination in an ecosystem without a hub firm.

## 3.1 Data Collection and Analysis

We collected primary and secondary data to gain insights into the technical and organizational facets involved in distributing data control in the ecosystem. Table 2 presents an overview of primary data collection. For primary data, we mainly relied on interviews, informal conversations, questionnaires, and observations to gain an understanding of the actors involved in the Italian banking sector. We interviewed key actors from major Italian banks, CSPs, regulators, and third parties to understand the links and interactions among them. In total, we conducted 30 semistructured interviews, which ranged from 45 to 90 minutes. For secondary data, we collected and used various sources of evidence for each actor, such as archival data from websites and press releases, national and international reports, and surveys on new fintech technologies covering the 2009-2020 period (see Appendix A). We also collected protocols and recommendations published by regulators since the Italian banks had to comply with regulatory frameworks at both the national and European levels during that period. Conducting a longitudinal study allowed us to observe different data control tensions, coordination mechanisms, and value co-creation over time due to changes in the industry architecture, regulations, market, and technology.

Table 3 provides a summary of each actor interviewed for this study. Following Biedebach and Hanelt’s (2020) typology, which draws on Iansiti and Levien (2004), we classified actors into general ecosystem roles of leaders, followers, intermediaries, and local champs, and defined two new roles—regulators and digital backbone—to replace the dominators who control data and strategic directions in less-regulated sectors.

We used NVivo 11 to organize qualitative data and facilitate the analysis of interview transcripts. We reconstructed the timeline of main events in the adoption of cloud services and related them to changes in the composition and functions of key actors and regulations. Figure 1 presents the key regulatory events in the project timeframe (see Appendix B for a summary of data-related regulations in Europe and Italy). We used an established methodology in qualitative research (Gioia et al., 2013) to identify first-order codes, second-order codes, and aggregate theoretical categories, as summarized in Figures 2a, 2b, and 2c. This process was iterative and involved a case narrative, inductive coding, and consultation with the literature, thus engaging in deductive coding from existing theory as well (Alvesson & Kärreman, 2007; Gioia et al., 2013). A key element in this process was not so much to identify new aggregated themes from our empirical data, but rather to understand how existing theoretical constructs identified in our literature review interrelated with first- and second-order codes in our empirical data. By considering the dynamic relationships among these inductively derived codes and deductive theoretical constructs, we were able to gain new theoretical insights into the phenomenon of interest.

We first engaged in breaking down the data to define order codes on the underlying process of coordinating data control in the ecosystem. To contextualize the interview data, we used notes from our archival analysis. Each first-order code was labeled consistently with the informants’ terminology. For example, the first order code “Enforcing standards and certifications” (see Figure 2b) corresponds to an interview excerpt with a COO from Bank I: “We ask third parties to show us their own certifications and security standards.” If coding labels conflicted, we discussed and cross-checked emerging codes to ensure that the data were linked to a given code (Gioia et al., 2013). Second, we identified similarities and differences in the first-order codes and aggregated corresponding categories into second-order codes, giving them higher-order theoretical labels (e.g., “Bilateral and multilateral agreements on distributed data control”). The coding process was iterative. After several rounds of coding and interpretation, we solidified our understanding of the data control tension and coordination mechanism applied.

Because the study was aimed at elaborating a process model of data control coordination in an ecosystem, we followed recent literature to interpret our findings. In doing so, we aggregated second-order codes into higherorder theoretical categories (Gioia et al., 2013). This third and final stage produced a data structure with three aggregate theoretical categories, namely, data control tensions, data control coordination, and value cocreation. These aggregate theoretical categories constitute three analysis elements in coordinating data control. In the next section, we present our findings based on this data analysis. We report our findings by presenting three episodes of coordination efforts among ecosystem actors. We analyze the data control tensions, data control coordination mechanisms, and value co-creation within each episode. In defining the time frame for each episode, we triangulated interview data with other sources of evidence such as annual reports on banks’ IT infrastructures published from 2009 to 2020, as described in Table 2. The archival data was produced in close collaboration with regulators, banks, and other relevant institutions, entailing issues and recommendations for IT infrastructures, internal processes, and use cases for emerging technologies, including cloud computing. These data allowed us to understand when and how each data control tension manifested itself, whether and how it was resolved by different coordination mechanisms, and how value co-creation between ecosystem actors emerged. For instance, in Episode 1, although a data control tension was identified, banks and CSPs were not able to distribute data-related operations, despite the interoperability of cloud services. This contrasts with Episodes 2 and 3 where coordination mechanisms led to the distribution and polycentric governance of data control, respectively. The identified episodes illustrate the recursive emergence of data control tensions (Cloutier & Langley, 2020) over time and the value co-creation resulting from the coordination mechanisms made by ecosystem actors to address these problems. ## 4 Findings

Our analysis outlines three episodes of data control coordination from 2009 to 2020. We discuss the major data control tensions and the coordination effort to resolve those while leveraging value co-creation among heterogeneous actors.

## 4.1 Episode 1 (2009-2012): Failure to Form an Ecosystem

The first episode coincided with the financial crisis in 2008, in which international regulatory bodies required banks to be more transparent and responsible in their operations. At the same time, banks wanted to revisit their technology strategies to overcome the rigidity of legacy systems and reduce costs. Although cloud technologies were already common in other sectors, they had not yet been deployed in the Italian banking sector. IT governance experts considered cloud services to be a new paradigm and recommended flexible architectures and efficiency as the main drivers for initiatives regarding cloud technology adoption (AR-2011). <sup>1</sup> Indeed, many IT experts operating in the banking sector engaged in the exploration of cloud technologies, with the latter becoming a topic of conversation and debate in conferences and reports among banks and regulators.

In this episode, our analysis reveals that cloud technologies were associated with the distribution of data control between banks and CSPs. We noticed that banks experienced two main data control tensions related to the transparency and quality requirements for distributed data, as described in detail later in this section. Despite some efforts at the actor level, data control remained uncoordinated among banks, regulators, and CSPs during this episode. Hence, banks opted mainly for other strategies rather than building a cloud-based infrastructure (see Table 4a).

## 4.1.1 Data Control Tensions

The analysis of IT infrastructures in Italian banks conducted in 2009 identified three primary drivers for the adoption of cloud-based solutions: “the reusability of services developed in different contexts, interoperability and flexibility” (AR-2009). While banks, as primary actors in the financial system, focused on the development of financial services such as providing bank accounts, lending to customers (B2C and B2B), and offering transaction services and risk management packages, they realized that their traditional IT infrastructures could not support future growth opportunities. Banks recognized that they needed to direct their attention to reducing their operational costs and increasing the flexibility of IT resources and services to cope with changes in the business environment. At that time, CSPs had been offering a diverse set of cloud-based solutions, which attracted actors in many industrial sectors. Banks rapidly became aware of cloud technology and its benefits. For example, a manager of Bank S noted the benefits of cloud-based solutions as “the velocity of delivery, time to market, standardization and beyond that reproduction of standard tasks and automation.”

This was reflected in the survey of the Italian banking association: “With regard to the benefits (of cloud services), we actually found that the speed of setting up test environments ranks first in importance, followed by scalability and flexibility of use” (ABI-b).

In rethinking their strategies to reduce costs and improve their IT infrastructure, banks realized that cloud technologies could be used to distribute some data control rights to external actors. IT managers envisaged the opportunity to assign data storage and data processing resources to CSPs in IaaS (infrastructure as a service), while some went further in considering PaaS (platform as a service) for middleware, operating systems, database services, and SaaS (software as a service) for business applications. The quote below gives further examples of transferring data control to CSPs, as found in one of the annual reports: “It is possible to contemplate transferring to the cloud provider some responsibilities related to updating hardware and software systems, facility management and technical management of data storage” (AR-2015).

Cloud-based infrastructures had the potential to create significant problems for data control distributed between banks and cloud service providers. To better understand how cloud services would have affected the operational processes of banks, IT managers carefully analyzed the implications of cloud adoption. They concluded that in each cloud service model (i.e., IaaS, PaaS, SaaS, etc.), CSP became the owner of specific infrastructural components (e.g., device, software application) with rights and responsibilities for performing data control.

Such dependencies on CSPs created a transparency tension. On the one hand, banks were under pressure from regulators to be transparent in their data-related operations. On the other hand, distributing data control across organizational boundaries made it difficult to oversee such operations. Banks perceived CSPs as not being transparent enough. The COO of Bank I provided an example:

The typical approach of a bank is that what we are doing is easy to control; so, if I am storing my dataset in my own data storage, I am sure that I am taking control over who is going in, who is going out and what is going on. … I cannot say the same if I am delegating a third party to store my data on his own machines. Then if I consider the complexity of the cloud, I don’t know precisely where the data storage is.

Banks also noticed fundamental differences in goals and practices with CSPs. In that period, most banks showed a risk-averse attitude towards the implementation of new financial services based on cloud technology solutions. In contrast, CSPs implemented a variety of business models around datarelated operations. As the sales manager of a CSP stated: “I [service provider] am not mining the data … I am not (like other service providers), I’m making money by selling the product … not profiting from data on my platform.”

Moreover, while for banks, the protection and handling of sensitive customer data was one of the main concerns, CSPs used different approaches to meet the needs of banks for data control. As stated by a manager of Bank P:

I divide cloud providers into two classes: (1) the one that comes from the enterprise market (2) and the one that comes from the consumer[user] market. They have different approaches … acquiring cloud services from the enterprise market is easier … we know the problems with the users’ market very well.

However, other CSPs’ clients, such as advertising agencies, were more relaxed about how their customers’ data were stored, processed, and used in the cloud, which was generally considered to be in conflict with the banks’ priorities. The fact that CSPs often performed such operations according to their own quality standards made it difficult for banks to ensure the quality of data. As reported in a survey: “The external management of IT systems could also induce a reduction in the ability of intermediaries [banks] to exercise effective control over the quality of services rendered” (Reg- e).

Thus, heterogeneous goals and practices sparked a problem related to ensuring quality control of distributed data control across ecosystem actors.

## 4.1.2 Coordination Mechanisms

For Italian banks, cloud technology was initially “a forbidden word,” as the COO of Bank I told us. It was not until 2010 that banks started to explore the possibility of integrating cloud services into existing IT infrastructures. Within banks, IT managers were responsible for identifying the opportunities offered by cloud services. As a manager from Bank P explained: “Cloud services have reduced the maintenance cost and efforts. Moreover, cloud leads to operational and upgrade benefits.”

In late 2011, after many failed efforts, the National Institute of Standards and Technologies (NIST) finally built a consensus around the definition of cloud technologies.<sup>2</sup> This definition created momentum for the growing interest in technical and managerial benefits and further investigation of various aspects related to the management of relations with service providers, security requirements, operational continuity, and compliance. IT experts systematically assessed risks and clustered cloud risks into strategic (e.g., lock-in), compliance (e.g., privacy, supervision norms), and operational (e.g., business continuity, security). They sought a balance between the opportunities and risks of cloud services, as expressed by the following quote: “Cloud, as a new paradigm, also brings new challenges, related to security, privacy, and governance. These issues must be addressed promptly and with innovative models to establish a relationship of trust and increase the awareness of customers, managers, and suppliers for policy alignment” (AR-2010).

Considering the importance of strategic, compliance, and operative risks in the financial sector, banks began to address the data control tensions. Banks approached CSPs with certain requests to ensure that data processing carried out through cloud services was aligned with banks’ expectations. For instance, banks expected to know the geographical location of data centers and also requested permission to audit the IT infrastructure of CSPs to ensure data security. A manager of Bank P provided another example of a request to better distribute data control: “We prefer to have the encryption key in-house, where we maintain total control of our data centers and data treatment. Thus, there are very different needs and conditions regarding the contract with the CSP in a users’ market.”

However, CSPs mainly overlooked the banks’ requests and were more interested in selling the same cloud services in multiple markets. A manager of Bank I underlined the CSPs’ lack of experience in the financial service sector as a reason for declining banks’ requests:

When I was inquiring about some requirements to a [CSP] I was addressing audit rights’ clauses in the contract … they were looking at me as if I was kidding, not serious. Because I was apparently asking them something that they don’t know how to do … in any case, they were astonished by our requests … The typical answer from them was: “Why do you need to know where your data is stored?”

Therefore, despite banks’ efforts toward managing data control tensions through unilateral requests, CSPs were unwilling to fulfill them. Consequently, no agreement was achieved between banks and CSPs regarding data control coordination.

## 4.1.3 Value Co-Creation

While most banks were planning to implement at least one cloud initiative in 2011-2012, the tensions between banks and CSPs regarding the distribution of data control negatively influenced the integration of cloud services in the banking sector. The problems were associated with concerns about transparency and ensuring an acceptable level of quality and security in distributed data. Not surprisingly, we observed that Italian banks were reluctant to integrate cloud services into their infrastructure, which delayed cloud service deployment. As explained by a manager of Bank P: “Banks hold a great deal of highly sensitive information … and the banking system is reluctant to adopt cloud services for critical applications and data.”

Our informants also mentioned that the rigidity and complexity of norms and regulations made it difficult for CSPs to conform to established regulations. As the COO of Bank I told us: “In reality, the level of complexity and regulation creates many problems … Companies [operating in the financial sector] must be compliant with regulations, creating a barrier for the entry of new companies in the financial sector.”

As the quote above shows, the highly regulated sector created an entry barrier for service providers. At the same time, hesitation about cloud adoption prevented banks from deploying cloud-based solutions. The abovementioned data control tensions could not be solved by solely imposing unilateral requests. As a consequence, these tensions constrained actors’ ability to engage in value co-creation. Although CSPs were offering interoperable services, no ecosystem was formed.

## 4.2 Episode 2 (2012-2016): The Emergence of a Cloud Service Infrastructure

During the second episode, IT resources became more important for banking operations, and many banks continued to modernize their IT infrastructures. While banks began the deployment of their own private cloud, CSPs continued to increasingly offer cloud-based services in the public cloud. Over time, cloud technologies became a trigger for change in banks, as stated in the annual report by IT experts: “Cloud computing cannot be relegated to a purely IT status or linked exclusively to cost reduction needs. Instead, it is an element that can introduce real changes in the culture, processes, and organization of the corporate information system” (AR-2015).

While efforts and attention were directed to identifying new opportunities and threats in the public cloud, our findings show that banks were concerned about the responsibilities of CSPs and the reliability of cloud services infrastructure. In this episode, we show that contractual coordination mechanisms were established to manage distributed data control across an emergent ecosystem. This coordination also enabled some value co-creation between ecosystem actors (see Table 4b).

## 4.2.1 Data Control Tensions

It was not until late 2012 that a few banks moved to adopt a private cloud where they could perform their data control internally. As a Bank P manager expressed: “In 2012, the company adopted private cloud for a cluster of servers. It was an architecture composed of Linux servers based in-house; we finally implemented this infrastructure as a service.”

Despite the successful adoption of private clouds, we noted that banks experienced considerable tension in distributing data control. First, banks were concerned about internal and external responsibilities. Internally, banks pointed to conflicting goals between departments. For instance, the business and IT departments of banks pushed for innovation and cloud adoption, while the compliance department followed different priorities. The COO of Bank I noted: “There were people in the company that tried to kill these initiatives in respect to compliance, legal, security, etc. They are paid to find the good reason to not [adopt cloud].”

Having conflicting priorities, each department expected different data-related procedures and requirements causing tensions in decision-making rights and responsibilities in data control. As a sales manager of a service provider stated:

Security professionals are more concerned about the technical aspects. Then we have legal experts that they are not happy of just having a technical discussion. They have different requirements. … Technical guys want to know mainly whether the service will do what we expect it to do. Security guys are concerned about security. And legal guys are working on potential problems [such as] if something happens who will pay? Who is responsible? What if we want to change the service provider?

Externally, banks faced responsibility tensions with CSPs. Banks had to manage the contractual arrangements by specifying roles and responsibilities. The distributed responsibilities for data storage, processing, and access could have impacted the confidentiality and availability of services and data with direct implications on business operations. Thus, the first step for banks was imposing some data control responsibilities on CSPs (through unilateral requests); however, CSPs often showed low (if not zero) flexibility in responding to such requirements. One of the managers of Bank P provided an example: “There [are] few cloud providers that are willing to negotiate. Not all cloud providers accept the clause of ‘Data Treatment’ which addresses the data control and handling requirements.”

Moreover, banks had to manage contracts in compliance with regulatory requirements. The regulatory bodies, in particular, considered banks to be the ultimate authority for data control. Despite the efforts of regulators at the national or sector level, banks were left with many unanswered questions. Bank managers expressed that the existing regulatory frameworks did not cover the emerging issues in managing contracts and left some ambiguity over data control. Banks expressed the need for more specific guidance from regulators in dealing with security and reliability requirements in distributed environments. Especially for new offers by CSPs such as public clouds, further support was needed to define clearly where the CSPs are responsible and for which data operations. The quote below provides some examples of security and reliability requirements, as found in one of the annual reports:

In the transfer of data management activities to CSPs, [banks] must pay attention to the security and availability requirements of information, to the definition of the rules of ownership and management of data, to the sharing of an adequate service-level agreement system, to the setting of adequate disaster recovery models and the precise design of the service delivery architecture. (AR-2015)

Second, banks expressed uncertainty about the reliability of the overall cloud service infrastructure. CSPs became an important player in ensuring the functioning of distributed data control and mitigating operational risks on the IT infrastructure. As explained by a manager of Bank P: “What is different [in the cloud] is that some of the control is inevitably in the hands of cloud providers.” However, banks struggled in evaluating whether and how data operations performed by CSPs were reliable enough to address failures and ensure the continuity of banking operations. Banks were also concerned about the nested nature of cloud service infrastructures. While CSPs were increasingly reliant on microservices provided by other vendors, banks recognized the need for visibility of third parties’ IT resources to assess their cyber vulnerabilities and to avoid the effects of local security incidents propagating in the cloud infrastructure. A manager of a CSP who engaged with banks reflected the concerns of banks:

[banks] want to know how the cloud services are built. We have software, IT services, and data centers, and we build certain microservices for the cloud. Not all of (those services) were developed by us, but they are part of a solution we must provide. In that case, the customer (bank) wants to know the intricacies of cloud services; namely transparency in how the services are built, which microservices we use, and which parts are provided by subcontractors.

The distribution of data control in the cloud across jurisdictions sparked this tension because banks and CSPs may be subject to different jurisdictions:

When information can be stored anywhere in the cloud, the physical location of the information can become an issue. Physical location dictates jurisdiction and legal obligation. Country laws governing personally identifiable information (PII) vary greatly. What is allowed in one country can be a violation in another. (Reg-g)

In sum, in this episode, banks faced tensions regarding responsibilities in performing data operations and the reliability of the cloud infrastructure. Data control roles and responsibilities of actors were not fully defined through agreement or regulations. Banks initially faced resistance from internal actors demanding heterogeneous data control requirements, thus challenging alignment between cloud decisions and the overall corporate objectives. Similarly, ambiguity over data control responsibilities between CSPs and banks demanded further reflection, especially regarding the demand for banks to exercise their rights and further regulation guiding the management of such rights and responsibilities. These tensions were aggravated when data control rights were recursively distributed across jurisdictions—for instance, when subcontractors or data centers were located outside Italy and the EU. Actors thus needed to coordinate to manage these two data control tensions.

Coordination mechanisms: Later in 2012, regulators at the national and European levels began to set new guidelines to address the issues of overseeing cloud services in the banking sector. The first set of regulations was published in December 2013<sup>3</sup> (establishing a “single rulebook”). The new regulations to some extent facilitated the adoption of cloud technologies in response to the demand for digital service innovation. The rulebook explicitly defined the scope of cloud services, the responsibility of banks regarding distributed data control, and data security risks in the cloud. As pointed out by a supervision advisor of the Bank of Italy, the new regulatory framework was a revolution in the banking sector since it emphasized the strategic role of cloud service infrastructures at the corporate level:

The legislation on IT security took effect as a guideline in 2013 and represented a real revolution compared to the previous situation. It literally “ripped” IT out of the basements of banks and moved those functions into alignment with corporate best practices in other sectors of the economy.

Multiple actors participated in the development of the new regulation. For instance, the ABI Lab shared its knowledge and insights with regulators. Regulators also cooperated with banks to grasp the specificities of distributed data control in financial services. The close cooperation between banks and regulators not only allowed regulators to better understand the main issues experienced by banks but also helped to legitimate and implement regulatory requirements through the exchange of technical knowledge. The collaborative approach in the definition of guidelines and norms led banks and regulators to develop a shared understanding of the scope, responsibilities, risks, and control points related to the deployment of cloud services in banking. The cooperation with various actors allowed regulators to fine-tune the regulatory framework with the expectations and needs of banks. As a manager of Bank U explained: “Regulatory bodies provide guidelines for contracting and supervising (cloud) services. This provides us the opportunity to participate in recommendations and modifications that may be necessary.”

After publishing the new regulations, the adoption of cloud-based solutions gained momentum. An important step in ensuring the alignment of cloud decisions with corporate objectives was the internal coordination efforts made by banks. Despite the differences in strategies among banks, our findings show that banks devoted significant resources to reach internal coordination among the business, legal, IT, and security departments.

The alignment of internal decisions and responsibilities significantly facilitated interactions between banks and CSPs, as banks could more clearly communicate their expectations about data control, including rights for data access, storage, and processing. Moreover, CSPs became more flexible in customizing their standard service-level agreements for banks. As a sales manager of a service provider stated:

We have technical discussions about our services, especially in the highly regulated market like finance. We spend a lot of time and energy dealing with legal compliance and [IT] security. Most of the time, particularly with large customers, we have to write unique contracts for specific customers covering all the salient legal points.

Such dialogues between banks and CSPs paved the way for negotiating the terms and conditions in contracts to distribute the rights of performing data operations. Customizing service-level agreements was particularly important, as specific clauses were enforced by regulators. As a manager of Bank U noted: “If they [CSPs] don’t include that specific clause, the regulator will not approve the contract and therefore the service provider cannot operate.”

Banks also realized the need to negotiate specific procedures for disaster recovery and business continuity with CSPs. As a manager of Bank P told us: “It is exceedingly important to negotiate disaster recovery very carefully. Business continuity is a major concern in any SLA. CSPs in particular should include those terms in the SLA.”

Some banks started to implement a formal procedure that certified CSPs conforming with the minimum security level. This procedure allowed banks’ IT and legal departments to engage with selected CSPs. As a manager of Bank S noted: “At the beginning of the journey, it was the security team that certified cloud providers to provide a minimum level of security before the contract was negotiated. Once that step was complete, we could analyze the application in detail.”

In parallel, through a self-certification process, some CSPs devoted resources to demonstrate that they possessed a sufficient level of accountability and capability concerning the security of data and IT services in the cloud. As the COO of Bank I pointed out: “[One way is to] physically check the data center, hard disks, the storage connections, and whether the firewall is working. Another method is to ask third parties to apply their own certifications and security standards.”

Over time, in addition to banks, other new actors emerged and engaged in certification procedures to guarantee the security of their cloud services. These certifications required CSPs to pursue some prerequisite data control and, as a result, constrained the integration of cloud-based services with banks’ IT systems when a minimum security level was not fully guaranteed by the CSP.

## 4.2.2 Value Co-Creation

The internal coordination efforts illustrated in this episode led banks to develop cloud strategies for managing data control tensions resulting from the distribution of data control across organizational boundaries. Cloud strategies included descriptions of decision makers and internal rules related to the management of cloud services. Although banks pursued different strategies (some saw the cloud as a pillar for future banking, while others envisaged using the cloud only for specific areas and keeping the core component on-site), cloud strategies provided banks’ managers with metrics and processes to define data control requirements, service levels, contract terms, and conditions and selection criteria for CSPs. As a manager of Bank S explained:

[Our] main activity was to define cloud strategies [through internal documents]. Next, we tried to go beyond the technical aspects and to understand the cloud’s role in a regulated market like the banking system. Finally, we engaged internally with all parties that focus on legal issues. It was a long procedure to create a strategy which put together the technical aspects from our side with legal aspects like compliance and regulation.

Banks understood that they needed to address the technical and legal issues simultaneously since cloud deployment was more than a mere sourcing decision; it was also related to a profound change in banking operations. Thus, banks developed new techno-legal tools. Exit strategies are an example of a techno-legal tool enabling banks to establish when and how cloud contracts could be terminated. The aim was to prevent lock-in situations by creating “a contractual framework for cloud adoption,” as noted by a manager of Bank S. As the level of control and responsibilities of actors were different based on service delivery models, banks had to create different techno-legal tools for specific service delivery models. Other examples of techno-legal tools included access control for SaaS, encryption keys for PaaS, and firewalls for IaaS.

Actors involved in distributed data control obtained some benefits from this collaboration. In particular, regulators developed new expertise to monitor distributed data control, and CSPs gained a better understanding of the procedural and legal requirements for data governance in the banking sector, leading to the availability of compliant cloud services. Dissemination activities such as forums and seminars to examine infrastructural, governance, and innovation issues of cloud services organized in 2016 by the Professional Institution allowed banks to exchange good practices and support the development of new capabilities for managing the complexity of cloud-service infrastructures.

Banks, CSPs, and regulators effectively distributed data control based on bilateral and multilateral agreements. Cloud strategies, internal tech-legal tools, and the availability of compliance practices provided clear directions to banks for the recombination of cloud services. One example was a collaboration solution based on the cloud which allowed “a safe and compliant with industry regulations collaboration between bank’s employees and customers, through the Mobile Banking Application and [a] cloud collaboration platform— leveraging messaging and document sharing tools” (press release of a CSP).

The quote below underlines the importance of such recombination, as found in one of the annual reports:

The cloud is increasingly seen as an opportunity for companies of all sizes to enable agility, that is, providing the business with the ability to quickly adapt to the continuous and changing needs of the market … The flexibility of the cloud allows you to launch new digital products and services more quickly … The cloud allows you to quickly experiment with new services and technologies, and [address] customers' changing needs more quickly and offer new digital products. (AR-2020)

However, new data control tensions also emerged once cloud services were successfully deployed. For instance, moving certain personal data (e.g., customer data) to the cloud was still not allowed to avoid data privacy and security risks. As the COO of Bank I stated: “The customer data (today) should not be in a public cloud. This is non-negotiable.” In another example, strategic concerns arose around the physical location of data centers. Since EU privacy regulations explicitly restricted the use of cloud services from CSPs whose data centers were located outside Europe, CSPs increasingly agreed on including specific clauses and adopted their resources and capabilities, leading to new forms of joint service delivery. For example, interviews with bank managers showed that some large CSPs had to relocate (to Europe) the space used to operate and maintain the physical components of their cloud infrastructures. The multiple problems emerging from the distribution of data control demanded both internal and external coordination efforts. Banks needed to locate control points in their IT infrastructure and implement new security controls.

## 4.3 Episode 3 (2016-2020): Responsible Consumption in Cloud-Based Ecosystems

In this episode, new actors such as big tech companies introduced alternative financial services. For instance, Facebook (now Meta) launched its first cryptocurrency “Libra” in 2019 to enable users’ transactions across the globe. Regulators and banks perceived it as a real threat to the stability of financial markets. At the same time, the advancement of digital technologies enabled fintech companies to develop new cloud-based services and collaborate with banks. Regulators were increasingly engaged in updating and enacting new norms and regulations at the national, regional, and sector levels. For instance, the NIS Directive (in 2016), the GDPR (in 2018), and the Cybersecurity Act (in 2019) came into force, resulting in new data control requirements.

Our findings show that the distribution of data control among multiple (new) actors with heterogeneous interests and expectations led to the emergence of new data control tensions. To tackle such tensions, actors needed new coordination mechanisms (see Table 4c).

## 4.3.1 Data Control Tensions

The combination of cloud services with other digital technologies such as artificial intelligence (AI), big data analytics, and software robots has created a wide range of opportunities for innovating financial services. The PSD2 was launched by regulators to foster such innovation and, at the same time, ensure data privacy and security. PSD2 allows third parties (e.g., fintech companies) open access to leverage (with consumers’ consent) customer data that were traditionally managed and controlled by banks. PSD2 required banks to develop APIs as “interfaces with third parties allowing fast and secure interactions” (Reg-e) which allow the sharing of customers’ financial data for the development of new and personalized services. Thus, the Italian banking sector was witnessing the emergence of new actors such as fintech companies offering technology-enabled financial innovation. Some banks began to acquire or collaborate with fintech companies to develop new banking services for payments and finance. APIs—as a catalyst for facilitating and controlling the interaction of banks with fintech companies—created new data control tensions as rights to access and process data were distributed among banks and fintech companies.

While coordinating the distribution of rights to access and process data was possible through PSD2 (open standards), banks and regulators realized the difficulties in managing data control disputes considering the evolution of new technologies. As the regulator reported: “Projects based on collaboration with thirdparty companies or developed in outsourcing, could increase legal risks in the event of disputes not sufficiently governed by contracts between the various operators involved in the provision of a FinTech service” (Reg-e).

This was amplified by the fact that the fintech companies followed different strategies for the development of new financial services by accessing and processing data from different sources. As noted by the Bank of Italy head of IT: “[fintech] are small, innovative, agile and risk-seeking and [their strategy is around] unbundling finance/insurance offers: only one service but personalized, digital, fast and customercentric.”

This concerned banks and regulators, calling into question the viability and the sustainability of their business models. A manager of the Bank of Italy provided an additional insight about regulation:

Banking and financial regulations, at this stage mainly issued at international level, do not always keep up with the rapid changes brought about by fintech. The supervisory authorities have a complicated job: on the one hand, they must continue to preserve the security and soundness of the financial system; on the other, they have to be suitably flexible in “maintaining” the secondary regulations and in applying them alongside the primary legislation, formulated in a predigital age. (Reg-d)

Informants also mentioned different views of engaged actors on regulations. For instance, when the NIS Directive and the CyberSecurity Act came into force in 2016 and 2019, respectively, fintech companies mainly perceived them as a barrier to innovation. The CEO of FinTech Co. explained: “We needed to be compliant in a heavily regulated environment. In many cases [regulators] requested information far beyond product details: this included questions about our corporate structure and composition of our board of directors … It is a very long process.”

Another source of internal tension was related to managing IT operational risks in a timely manner. The adoption of open and interconnected systems implied the exposure to new risks, as noted in an annual report: “At the moment cloud services as a service per se are preferred, but at the same time represent a channel of exposure to various risks that are difficult to predict” (AR-2020). Survey respondents mentioned IT operational risks associated with the illegitimate use of data and processes such as errors, abuses, fraud, and unauthorized information disclosure. This created a higher degree of cybersecurity dependencies on third parties. The focus in risk management shifted from organizational boundaries to new actors such as cloudbased fintech companies and service providers. To manage IT operational risk, banks needed to detect incidents, assess the business impact of cyber threats, and then decide on how to prevent or respond to those. Security controls are heavily based on data-related operations that were difficult for a single bank to perform in such distributed settings. As the COO of Bank I noted: “In some cases the risk profile is not well defined. We have to show we have these kinds of operations, benefits, and risks. Risk can be mitigated by these kinds of security controls before we can approve it.”

Furthermore, banks experienced problems in exercising data control as part of their auditing obligations. Following the promulgation of European regulations, banks had to perform audits on cloud services; however, it was challenging for a single bank to audit large CSPs with data centers that were located across the globe. The COO of Bank I noted: “I know I have an obligation to audit, but how can I audit the [Microsoft] Azure data center?!”

As the quote above shows, some banks began discussing alternative ways for on-site audits to be compliant with the regulations—or, in a more complex scenario, how they might exercise their audit rights when cloud services were provided by third parties. Lack of uniform compliance made banks reluctant to adopt cloud services.

## 4.3.2 Coordination Mechanisms

Several initiatives were launched to address the data control tensions. First, in addressing the difficulties in the alignment of data control across organizations and countries, regulators established a framework to balance innovation and risks in the changing financial sector:

Some of the more important aspects emanate from the perspective of the supervisory authority, which increasingly includes the need to ensure effective risk management. At the same time, it is important not to discourage innovation and as far as possible, not to undermine the underlying stability of the rules and hence their durability over time. (Reg-d)

In one of its initiatives, the Bank of Italy launched the “Fintech Channel,” a website where fintech companies and banks could submit and present their innovations enabled by the relevant technologies to receive feedback. In another example, an innovation center was set up to facilitate the exchange of knowledge among banks, fintech companies, and regulators. Such initiatives were followed by several workshops on big data, artificial intelligence, and blockchain technologies, which were open to all actors. These workshops provided banks, regulators, fintech companies, and academia with a space to discuss, develop, and share best practices in risk management. The CEO of FinTech Co. explained:

[During such workshops], one of the key points was showing how innovation and technology are expanding their boundaries every day, thanks to a collaborative and interconnected environment. Universities, companies, and regulators are now working very closely, including people with different backgrounds and expertise.

Second, considering concerns about cybersecurity, banks realized that they now more than ever needed to collaboratively mitigate the risks associated with new technologies. Actors initiated coordination through the constant sharing of data and knowledge. In one example, data sharing was related to the notification of security incidents. Supported by the Bank of Italy, the CERTFin was created in 2017 as a public-private partnership initiative aimed at enhancing cyber resilience in the Italian financial system and the ability of banking and financial operators to manage cyber risks. Participation in CERTFin is open to every operator of the national banking and financial sector, including payment service providers, banks, insurance companies, and telecommunication service providers. After three years CERTFin had reached 50 members and continues to offer a growing number of services to its constituency. Examples of services include information sharing about cyber threats and fraud phenomena, threat intelligence, and cyber exercises. In addition to the establishment of a new actor, informal knowledge sharing became diffused in the ecosystem. The COO of Bank I mentioned: “There is an informal network among peers focused on how to deal with specific issues such as the cloud.” As a further example, banks noted the increased role of the ABI Lab and the Professional Institution in knowledge creation and sharing. As a professional association director described: “The main point was information sharing [by the association] to give advice to bank members who wanted to implement countermeasures which defended their data and information.”

In response to regulators pushing banks to exercise their audit rights, our findings revealed the efforts of banks to exercise these rights by forming micro-alliances. This amplified the bargaining power of banks. At the same time, banks looked for partners with specific competencies and resources to conduct audits jointly. By leveraging micro-alliances, banks were able to guarantee regulatory compliance to some extent. For example, some banks coordinated their resources and technical knowledge in a way that could audit the data center of a large service provider.

## 4.3.3 Value Co-Creation

In response to the changing and tightening regulatory environment, fintech companies (as new entrants)

found that the knowledge exchange with banks and regulators was especially helpful in the early phase of new service development. The early awareness of inherent risks in new services, as well as the capability to measure those risks, enabled multiple actors (such as regulators, supervisors, and academia) to mitigate many technological risks. Moreover, fintech companies were supported in generating new collaborative services through coordinated data control (i.e. mutually shared control requirements). As a result, fintech companies became more willing to design and develop applications and services that complied with the regulations. At the same time, our informants highlighted that knowledge exchange and interactions with fintech companies enabled regulators to develop and use supervisory technology, such as automation, analytics, and real-time risk alerts, enabling them to become more efficient.

Banks’ initiatives to share data and the diffusion of common operations among actors improved their security response capabilities and, consequently, their resilience to fraud and cyberattacks. As a professional association director noted, information sharing helped actors mitigate risks: “[knowing] the CSP with whom you have shared critical information. It is especially important in case of an attack on one of the providers, and immediately identify which [other] banks might be affected by that occurrence.”

By sharing information about incidents, threats, and vulnerabilities through CERTFin, banks were able to anticipate security threats, deploy preventive measures (e.g., determining and negotiating security requirements for new services), and detect and respond to fraud attempts using data produced by the cloudbased infrastructure. This held true regardless of the size of the bank. Although persuading CSPs was challenging and took some time, building networks and converging on a set of security requirements, offered incentives to service providers to adjust their services to the control requirements of several banks rather than a single bank. This reduced the complexity and cost of auditing for banks and also supported the efforts of CSPs in meeting data control requirements in terms of both time and cost.

Through procedural coordination, banks, CSPs, regulators, fintech companies, and other actors (i.e. telecommunication operators, law enforcement agencies, etc.) were able to collaboratively manage risks in open banking systems. Such collaborative risk management practices included but were not limited to identifying, assessing, and mitigating risks through prevention, response, and communication activities based on distributed data control. This eventually led to responsible joint service consumption as a form of value co-creation. As explained by a supervision advisor of R1, the goal was “embracing digitalization in order to create an ecosystem in which FinTech can develop and spread, benefiting from the Single Market without compromising financial stability or consumer and investor protection.” Some examples of joint service consumption are “a more accurate credit assessment, … innovative e-commerce, mobile payment solutions, and personalized finance management services” (AR-2020).

## 5 Discussion

## 5.1 A Process Model of Data Control Coordination in the Formation of Ecosystems

Our qualitative study on the deployment of cloud services in the Italian banking sector from 2009 to 2020 reveals the complex dynamics of data control coordination among banks, CSPs, regulators, and fintech companies. Table 5 summarizes our findings within three distinct episodes, highlighting key tensions, the deployed coordination mechanisms, and the varied outcomes, ranging from functional failures to successful joint service delivery and consumption. This analysis not only clarifies the formation of cloud-based ecosystems but also critically examines the adequacy of existing coordination frameworks in highly regulated sectors. By examining these interactions, our study provides new insights into the challenges of distributed data control, underscoring the need for innovative approaches to ecosystem governance that can better support responsible service delivery and consumption.

We identified two broad types of tensions that can lead to functional and distributional failures, respectively (Jacobides et al., 2024; Ozalp et al., 2018; Ozcan & Santos, 2015). The first type of tension relates to functional integration, where disagreements on the transparency and quality requirements for data-related operations hinder seamless integration (Episode 1). The second type of tension corresponds to distributional challenges that emerge when actors fail to capture value proportionate to their contributions and responsibilities, often triggered by regulatory updates and data aggregation issues (Episodes 2 and 3).

For instance, during Episode 1, banks unilaterally demanded that CSPs customize data access and processing procedures through, for example, encryption key management. These requests conflicted with the CSPs’ preference for standardized services, illustrating a fundamental disconnect in operational goals. Although these efforts aimed to enhance data control, they inadvertently promoted a vertically integrated control that stifled ecosystem-wide value creation (Khanagha et al., 2022; Rinta-Kahila et al., 2023). Under regulatory guidance, once consensus on service transparency and quality was achieved, it seemed feasible to distribute data control. However, tensions emerged as CSPs positioned themselves to manage significant amounts of data potentially collected through the deployment of cloud services (Aaltonen & Tempini, 2014; Constantiou & Kallinikos, 2015). These tensions were driven by misalignments in data control requirements, where overlapping functionalities or incompatible operational approaches among actors impeded the seamless integration of complementary data-related operations. This misalignment raised concerns about how data control could disproportionally influence ecosystem dynamics, potentially leading to inefficiencies and functional failures across the ecosystem (cf. Jacobides et al., 2024). These issues were particularly evident when CSPs directed interactions towards beneficial business areas, sometimes at the expense of the overarching ecosystem integrity (Krämer et al., 2019).

In Episode 2, the introduction of new norms aimed at fostering competition and innovation generated tensions, as actors needed to agree on how to distribute roles, responsibilities, and reliability requirements. Only when robust contractual coordination mechanisms were established could bilateral or multilateral service delivery begin. However, new tensions emerged as actors gained access to data generated by cloud service deployment. This led to the involvement of new actors (e.g. certification authorities) by altering the industry architecture, which, in turn, implied updates in the regulatory requirements. This created a positive feedback loop that contributed to the formation of the ecosystem yet simultaneously necessitated further contractual coordination. As a result of this iterative process, joint service delivery was achieved through the recombination of cloud services, which was essential for establishing value co-dependencies among ecosystem actors (Wareham et al., 2014; Henfridsson et al., 2018; Majchrzak et al., 2016).

Episode 3 further demonstrated how procedural coordination through polycentric data control, exemplified by the actions of a new intermediary actor like CERTFin, could resolve emerging data control issues. As new actors such as fintech companies joined the ecosystem, they introduced new tensions related to the operational risks posed by their innovative services. These tensions necessitated the development of robust procedural coordination mechanisms, realized through polycentric data control and advanced knowledgesharing systems. CERTFin, serving as a virtual organization, played a pivotal role in monitoring and managing data exchanges within the ecosystem. This polycentric approach not only enhanced security and fostered innovation but also made the ecosystem more inclusive and dynamic by accommodating new actors with complementary capabilities. Ultimately, this procedural coordination led to responsible joint service consumption, which is the value captured by actors participating in the ecosystem.

The process model, illustrated in Figure 3, offers a detailed depiction of these dynamics and the role of data control coordination in the formation of ecosystems in highly regulated sectors. It highlights both the formation and the failure loops and how contractual and procedural coordination mechanisms can mitigate tensions arising from regulatory changes and data aggregation issues, ultimately fostering joint service delivery and consumption.

## 5.2 Contribution to Research on Ecosystem Coordination

First, we provide an answer to our key research question of how data control coordination leads to the formation of an ecosystem in highly regulated sectors through the development of a process model. Our model extends previous research emphasizing the role of platform ecosystems in enhancing consumer welfare (Kira et al., 2021; Markus & Nan, 2020) and co-creating value (Ceccagnoli et al., 2012) by providing explanations for how ecosystem actors resolve data control tensions. Our model shows that distributed data control requires mutual agreements and coordination among diverse ecosystem actors. We identify the coordination mechanisms that resolve data control tensions through distinct modes of value co-creation and value capture. Specifically, we show how contractual coordination leads to joint value delivery through the recombination of cloud services and procedural coordination ensures joint value consumption by mitigating the risks emerging from the distribution of data control. The model provides insights into how data control coordination can help manage interorganizational relationships when cloud service infrastructures are deployed in highly regulated sectors (Grisot et al., 2014; Henfridsson et al., 2018).

Second, we theorize how data control coordination mechanisms mitigate tensions that can lead to ecosystem failures. Our study underscores the effectiveness of bilateral and multilateral agreements on distributed data control, such as contracts and servicelevel agreements, which are pivotal in resolving tensions related to distributed responsibilities, benefits, and risks. We provide support for past research that has theorized how these agreements are instrumental in establishing clear roles, responsibilities, and reliability standards, effectively addressing potential ambiguities and ensuring that all parties have aligned expectations and practices (Aaltonen & Tempini, 2014; Henfridsson et al., 2018; Spahiu et al., 2024). However, we also extend this research by highlighting how polycentric data control and knowledge sharing effectively resolve tensions triggered by changes in the industry architecture, which often challenge equitable value distribution. These mechanisms mitigate IT operational risks, thereby facilitating integration and ensuring equitable value distribution, which are essential for the formation and resilience of ecosystems.

Third, our study provides a novel perspective on ecosystem roles (Biedebach & Hanelt, 2020; Iansiti & Levien, 2004), shifting the focus from traditional views that primarily consider the influence and value derived from the network to a more functional view that emphasizes the roles necessary for successful data control distribution in highly regulated sectors. Unlike previous frameworks which often center on the dominance of a single keystone actor, our findings highlight how incumbents and digital backbones compete to fulfill keystone functions within a regulated competitive framework. This approach ensures compliance across different sectors and jurisdictions. In this dynamic setting, incumbents and digital backbones orchestrate the ecosystem, facilitating the integration and coordination of data control. Niche players, such as fintech companies in our study, act as followers, contributing to joint value creation only after the successful integration of data control orchestrated by keystone actors (Ozcan & Santos, 2015). This integration is often supported by intermediaries and local champs, who are instrumental in resolving tensions arising from new interoperability standards, regulatory changes, and shifts in industry architecture. This reframing of ecosystem roles underlines the critical need for diverse actor participation and collaboration to ensure efficient data governance and regulatory compliance, thereby enhancing ecosystem resilience and innovation (Kazemargi et al., 2023).

In conclusion, our empirical investigation of the Italian banking sector highlights the critical role of data control coordination in addressing tensions and ecosystem failures, while fostering value co-creation and value capture within ecosystems. Our findings provide a robust theoretical framework for understanding the dynamics of ecosystem governance and offer practical insights for regulatory approaches in highly regulated sectors.

## 6 Conclusion & Implications

In this paper, we conducted a qualitative case study on the Italian banking sector to investigate how data control can be coordinated in the formation of an ecosystem between banks, CSPs, and other third parties. We showed that, despite the availability of interoperability standards, the distribution of data control created tensions in the formation of an ecosystem. Ecosystem actors had to agree on mechanisms to coordinate data control before any value co-creation could take place. Based on our findings, we developed a process model that explains how data control coordination can help resolve tensions and create value for the ecosystem.

These findings offer research implications for ecosystem governance (Constantinides et al., 2018; De Reuver et al., 2018) especially in the highly regulated fintech sector (Gomber et al., 2018; Gozman et al., 2018; Kazan et al., 2018). First, current research on ecosystem governance emphasizes a lead actor or orchestrator that sets the rules and norms of behavior (Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013; Huber et al., 2017; Parker et al., 2017; Rietveld & Schilling, 2021; Tiwana, 2015). A lead actor’s governance over the ecosystem can attract complements from third parties (Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013) or motivate their exit from the platform (Tiwana, 2015). In contrast, our study focuses on a more distributed effort to govern ecosystem interactions, where there is no lead actor. The absence of a lead actor generates a coordination problem that is often found in open markets (Parker et al., 2017). However, unlike open markets, ecosystem actors in a distributed cloud service infrastructure in a regulated sector have an incentive to coordinate and co-create value; otherwise, they would fail to comply with regulations and face increased competition from new entrants. Future studies should complement successful cases of data control distribution by focusing on failures to better understand digitally enabled interorganizational coordination.

Second, our study shows that unless data control tensions are resolved, value is captured by those in control, leaving many ecosystem actors with no choice but to resort to hierarchical models of value creation. As we show in Episode 1 of our analysis, unresolved tensions can lead to ecosystem failure. Such ecosystem failure can, for example, impact the scalability of services (Ondrus et al., 2015). Whereas the extant literature focuses on key technology resources as bottlenecks to the formation of an ecosystem (Draschbacher & Rachinger, 2021), our study identifies data control tensions, which, as we show, can make or break the ecosystem. Further research could explore how data control tensions can generate varied ecosystem outcomes, including failures (Jacobides et al., 2024) in more mature stages of the ecosystem. The dynamics of joint service consumption, for example, might strengthen some actors’ positions over time, leading to shifts in industry architecture and triggering new tensions around distributed responsibilities, benefits, and risks. Moreover, examining the interplay between data control tensions and their impact on distributional and functional failures will provide valuable insights into the complex interdependencies that affect ecosystem health. These investigations will expand our understanding of how ecosystems evolve and adapt in response to internal and external pressures, offering critical insights for managing ecosystems in highly regulated sectors.

Third, we propose an alternative view on the relation between CSPs and incumbent firms. CSPs are often seen as potentially harmful actors in highly regulated sectors (Ozalp et al., 2022; Zuboff, 2015, 2019). In particular, while recent debates on antitrust and competition in platform ecosystems have focused on the anticompetitive practices of lead firms (or gatekeepers) on third parties (Krämer, 2020; Parker et al., 2021), our study and process model shows that we need to extend our consideration of anticompetitive practices exercised by a set of actors who may control data, as in the case of CSPs in our case study. Emphasis should be placed on how data control can generate new ecosystem outcomes, whether as new innovations leading to value creation possibilities or failures, as opposed to simply paying attention to the power and size thresholds of ecosystem actors (Cennamo et al., 2023). Further research should examine the distributed efforts of ecosystem actors to mobilize regulators and other stakeholders to counter such anticompetitive practices and establish collective data control coordination. For example, many healthcare organizations currently blindly hand data control rights over to large CSPs, with implications for the security and privacy of patient data. Recent scandals—for example, that of Google DeepMind and the Royal Free London NHS Foundation Trust, which led to the transfer of identifiable patient records across the entire trust without explicit consent—point to the need for collective data control coordination above and beyond regulations such as the GDPR and HIPAA (Constantinides, 2023). Data control coordination needs to be established and enforced between ecosystem actors in alignment with existing regulations.

Fourth, our theory of data control coordination has significant implications for the regulation of interoperability in digital markets (Bourreau & Kraemer, 2022). As underscored by our study, interoperability can play a pivotal role in mitigating monopolistic tendencies, particularly by enabling small actors to effectively engage with larger platforms. However, enhancing compatibility across digital services might also inadvertently solidify the market position of established actors, potentially stifling innovation. Consequently, policymakers face a crucial trade-off between fostering competition through service compatibility and promoting innovation. Our study delineates the conditions necessary to successfully distribute data control across different layers of digital services in cloud-based ecosystems, thereby enhancing innovation and enabling new entrants to add value (Butler et al., 2023; Jacobides & Lianos, 2021; Kira et al., 2021). Policymakers should therefore consider complementing interoperability mandates with norms that foster contractual and procedural coordination. Extending these insights to other highly regulated sectors, such as healthcare, telecommunications, and maritime, could similarly foster the formation of digital service ecosystems by ensuring that interoperability standards are tailored to specific industry needs, thereby enhancing overall sector innovation and competitiveness (Michalec et al., 2022).

Finally, the integration of AI technologies into cloud service infrastructures introduces complexities that necessitate advanced managerial approaches in data governance and ICT risk management (Gupta & George, 2016; Henfridsson et al., 2018). Under the European Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA), management teams are tasked with not only adhering to stringent regulations but also proactively enhancing the resilience of their digital operations (Deloitte, 2020). This involves implementing robust data governance frameworks that ensure that data integrity, privacy, and compliance are maintained, which has become particularly crucial because AI systems frequently process sensitive and critical information. Our theory of data control coordination highlights the importance of establishing clear roles and responsibilities for data handling and decision-making processes. This is essential for preventing data silos and ensuring that data flows seamlessly across various departments and external partners, which is aligned with DORA’s focus on managing third-party risks and enhancing operational connectivity. Managers must also prioritize the development of adaptable ICT risk management strategies that can quickly respond to emerging threats and technological changes, ensuring continuous operational capability. Furthermore, implementing procedural and contractual coordination mechanisms, as suggested by our study, can help managers ensure that all participants in the ecosystem understand their roles and comply with both internal policies and external regulatory requirements (Beverungen et al., 2022). This level of coordination is crucial for maintaining system integrity and fostering an environment where innovation through AI can be pursued safely and effectively without compromising the security or functionality of the broader system.

## About the Authors

Paolo Spagnoletti is a professor of organization studies and holds the Fastweb + Vodafone Chair on Cybersecurity and Digital Transformation in the Department of Business and Management at Luiss University in Rome. He is also an adjunct professor in the Department of Information Systems at the University of Agder, Norway, and serves as president of Cyber 4.0, the Italian national competence center on cybersecurity. His research focuses on digital infrastructures, data governance, and security, and has been published in leading journals including the Journal of Information Technology, Journal of Strategic Information Systems, Information & Management, and Information Systems Frontiers. Paolo is a distinguished member of the AIS and serves as executive editor of the Springer Lecture Notes in Information Systems and Organisation series.

Niloofar Kazemargi is an assistant professor at the University G. d’Annunzio and an adjunct professor at Luiss University. She received her PhD in business management from the University of Rome “Tor Vergata,” Italy. During her doctoral studies, she was a visiting scholar at Cranfield University in the UK. She is the principal investigator of a research project on data governance for innovation (Data4Inn), where she leads a team of scholars from Luiss University and the University of Modena and Reggio Emilia. Niloofar’s research interests include data ecosystems, organizational agility, and emerging organizational models within digital ecosystems. On these topics she has published papers in international journals, such as IEEE Technology Engineering Management, conference proceedings, such as AoM, R&D, EURAM, and book chapters edited by Springer and Edward Elgar.

Panos Constantinides is a professor of digital innovation at Alliance Manchester Business School. He is one of the co-founders of the European Digital Platforms Research Network (EU-DPRN). His more recent research is divided into two broad topics: human-AI collaboration; the design and strategy of digital platform ecosystems. His research has been published in FT50 journals such as Information Systems Research, MIS Quarterly, Organisation Studies, the Journal of Business Venturing and Organization Studies, among others.

Andrea Prencipe is a professor of organisation and innovation and former rector at Luiss University in Rome. His research focuses on innovation-related issues in organisations and higher education institutions and on project-based organising. Andrea has served as an associate editor of the Journal of Management Studies. Andrea’s research has been published in journals such as Administrative Science Quarterly, Organization Science, California Management Review, Research Policy, Industrial and Corporate Change and by international publishers such as Oxford University Press, Palgrave MacMillan and Edward Elgar.
