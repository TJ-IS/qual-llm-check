## a b s t r a c t

With the pervasive use of the Internet, customer information privacy violation is on the rise and companies could suffer by losing their customers' trust. While previous literature has identi<sup>fi</sup>ed factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence trust, less attention has been paid to how trust may be rebuilt after it is violated by negative events regarding customer information privacy. The study synthesized two salient theories (the attribution theory and the organizational justice theory) to investigate the process by which trust is violated by privacy breaches and the extent of its repair by company responses. We examine the moderating effects of two types of privacy violation—hacking and unauthorized sharing—on the trust violation and repair process. We investigate the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of three response types—apology, denial and no response. Data were gathered using a controlled, scenario-based lab experiment. Our results showed the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant moderating impact of violation type on the process of trust violation and repair. Apology emerged as a universally effective response, although its reparative power was far less in unauthorized sharing than in hacking. Denial emerged as a complex response. Furthermore, the results showed that trustworthiness beliefs (ability, integrity, and benevolence) are differently impacted in the violation–repair process. Details of theoretical and managerial implications are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

The frequency and extent of privacy violation in cyber space has increased in scope and intensity and exposure of customer private data on a large scale has become a common occurrence. For example, in May 2013, private data of 50 million customers of LivingSocial, the second largest daily-deal company behind Groupon were hacked [5]. A report by Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3) shows that individual victims have lost an average of \$4600 in 2012 [23]. In a survey of 583 companies, 90% reported that they have been hacked at least once in 2011 and 50% had little con<sup>fi</sup>dence that they could prevent being hacked again [76]. Although there are strong laws for privacy protection, the ease of collection and transfer of customer private data and an increasingly large appetite to combine and integrate customer data for market analysis has made the unauthorized use of customers' private data tempting for companies in their pursuit of larger market share and higher pro<sup>fi</sup>ts [46]. While hacking is an external and unpredicted event, unauthorized use of customers' private data is an internal decision by companies. However, both being breached by hackers and intentional unauthorized use of customers' private data could have damaging consequences for companies, as was demonstrated by the reaction of Facebook users to its change of privacy policy and its more obtrusive search method exposing customer information on Facebook [63].

Breach of customers' private information could violate their trust in the company. It is widely acknowledged that trust is necessary in order for any business to thrive, and it is even more necessary in online environments where the trustor may feel more vulnerable when dealing with a faceless and remote trustee. Loss of trust leads to lost sales and other irreparable and “devastating damages” [72: p. 85]. The consequence of the violation is that it erodes subsequent user trust which may reduce the extent to which the trustor (user) will cooperate with the trustee [41]. Trustor in this research is the user who interacts with the website and develops trust in the business practices of the trustee. Trustee is the business entity or the company that owns the website. Following the extant literature on trust, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne trust as a psychological state of willingness to accept vulnerability based upon positive expectations of the intentions or behavior of the trustee in matters important to the trustor [37,45,57]. It has been reported that negative events and transgressions reduce trust [73]. Bies and Tripp [8] de<sup>fi</sup>ne trust violation as “unmet expectations concerning another's behavior or when [the trustee] does not act consistent with one's values” (p. 248). We, de<sup>fi</sup>ne violated trust as the level of trust after a negative salient event or transgression that could be ascribed to the trustee [72]. Repaired trust is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the level of trust after the trustee has taken positive actions to repair the trust following a violation, which restores trustor's willingness to be vulnerable to the trustee's future actions [24,72].

In other words, in the sequence of pre-violation (prior) trust, postviolation trust, and post repair trust, two events take place: negative information about the event is ascribed to the trustee and a trustee's social-account response (e.g., a public apology) intended to mitigate the negative consequences of the event and restore trust. Following Sitkin and Bies [64] and Tomlinson and Mayer [72], we de<sup>fi</sup>ne a social account as a public explanation of a violation event. While previous literature has investigated the factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence trust, less attention has been paid to how trust may be restored after it is violated. Several researchers [36] have recently called for a deeper analysis of trust repair processes. The study of trust repair has recently started to gain momentum in Management [22,36,59,72], Marketing [77] and MIS [29,41]. However, there is inadequate research in trust repair, particularly after breach of customers' information privacy. There is a need to examine trust violation and repair in online environments from an information perspective, since information serves as the key resource for any online business in general and e-commerce in particular. Moreover, any trust rebuilding examination should be preceded by an examination of violated trust. Echoing similar sentiments Schoorman et al. [59] stated that “it is critical to <sup>fi</sup>rst understand how it [trust] was damaged in the <sup>fi</sup>rst place, since different means of damaging trust are likely to require different repairing responses” (p. 349).

Hence the research questions in this study are: (1) what is the process of trust violation and repair when an information privacy violation occurs? (2) What are the reparative impacts of social accounts? (3) What is the moderating role of violation type in the trust violation and repair process?

To answer these research questions, we synthesize the attribution theory [78] and the organizational justice theory [15] to develop a conceptual model for the process of trust violation and repair when customers' private information has been violated. Based on the taxonomy of the attribution theory, we consider two types of privacy violation: hacking and unauthorized sharing of customers' private information. Moreover, we investigate how violated trust could be repaired by responding via a social account—apology, denial and no response. More importantly we examine the moderating in<sup>fl</sup>uence of violation type on the trust violation and repair process. The research methodology in this work is a scenario-based controlled experiment. The results provide signi<sup>fi</sup>cant theoretical and managerial implications.

The paper is organized as follows. The next two sections present theoretical justi<sup>fi</sup>cations and a salient review of the literature. The research model and the hypotheses are reviewed next, followed by research methodology, analysis and results. Contributions and implications are then discussed. The last section presents conclusions, limitations and future research opportunities.

## 2. Review of salient theories

There are two theories that are salient in trust violation and repair: attribution theory [78,79] and organizational justice theory, which has emerged from social exchange theory and justice literature [15]. Both theories have their genesis in social psychology and human motivation. They have migrated to the management literature with focus on outcomes, performances and their causes, and have been applied in studying trust violation and repair [36,38,61,72]. However, the two theories diverge in the speci<sup>fi</sup>city of their causal antecedents and the scope of their goals.

## 2.1. Attribution theory

The goal of the attribution theory is to identify the causes that can be attributed to performance outcome [30]. However, it was Weiner's seminal work (1985) that proposed the attribution theory and a general taxonomy for causal attributions. The attribution chain starts when a person encounters “a subjectively [emphasis added] important act” [78: p. 564] which sets “the boy [person] overtly or covertly wondering”

(p. 564). Individuals who encounter negative outcomes feel emotional displeasure that leads them to seek causes [72,78]. The causal attribution has three primary, independent, and continuous dimensions: locus of control (e.g., internal to the trustee vs. external to the trustee), controllability (e.g., the extent of “volitional control” by the trustee or another actor), and stability (“the degree to which the cause is perceived to either <sup>fl</sup>uctuate or remain constant”) [72: p. 88].

Although the use of these dimensions is based on the context and individual interpretations, the generality of the dimension remains constant [78]. For example, a student may attribute failing a test to his/her laziness (internal, controllable, and stable), illness (external, uncontrollable, and chance/unstable), or the teacher's habit of giving dif<sup>fi</sup>cult tests (external, uncontrollable, stable). In a later discussion of the attribution theory, Weiner [79] raises the distinction between pre- and post-event perceptions, which applies well to the concepts of pre- and post-trust when the event is a violation that could impact trust.

The generalized taxonomy in the attribution theory allows for theorizing and predicting various behaviors, reactions, outcomes, and performances depending on the category of attributable cause [78,79]. For example, two negative events with different attributable causes with different loci, controllability and stability—say one internal/ controllable/stable and the other external/controllable/stable—could produce two distinctly different cognitive, emotional and behavioral consequences in the actor, one accepting responsibility, regret, and taking corrective action, and the other casting blame, fueling anger, and demanding punishment and redress. This is particularly helpful in our study since we deal with two distinct types of events related to privacy violation: hacking and unauthorized sharing of customers' private information. Drawing on the taxonomy of the attribution theory, we will argue that differences in the attribution dimensions of hacking and unauthorized sharing produce different impacts on the victims.

Moreover, the attribution theory posits that outcomes of causal attribution are not permanent and could be invalidated or modi<sup>fi</sup>ed by reparative efforts such as social accounts [71,72,78], thus mitigating the negative outcomes, such as repairing trust after a violation event [71]. This is another salient aspect of the attribution theory to our study since understanding the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of mitigation actions after a privacy violation is an important topic that has not been addressed in the privacy literature thus far.

## 2.2. Organizational justice theory

The focus of this theory is employees' perceived justice of managers' performance in terms of decision-making and employee-evaluation processes. The organizational justice theory has four categories of justice as antecedents of performance: procedural justice, distributive justice, informational justice, and interpersonal justice [11]. Procedural justice “re<sup>fl</sup>ects the perceived fairness of decision-making processes,” and distributive justice is “the perceived fairness of the decision outcomes.” Interpersonal justice is the fairness and respect in communication of outcomes, and information justice is “truthfulness and adequacy of explanations” [15: p. 200]. The violation of justice in these four dimensions leads to cognitive, emotional and behavioral consequences for the victims, such as their trust, trust beliefs, commitments to the organization, and job performance [12,13]. In a meta-analysis of 493 independent samples, Colquitt et al. [15] have produced an extensive account of the research in the organizational justice theory and have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the differential impacts of justice dimensions on employees' trust, commitment, perceived organizational support, leader–member exchange, affect and behaviors.

The organizational justice theory is also salient to our study since informational justice is the speci<sup>fi</sup>c antecedent relevant to protecting customers' private information. The arguments related to the impact of justice violation on trust in this theory could add rigor to our investigation of privacy violation. Thus, we synthesize the attribution theory and the organizational justice theory in conceptualizing the process of trust violation and trust repair when customers' information privacy has been violated.

## 3. Salient literature

Both the attribution theory and the organizational justice theory have been applied in the investigation of trust, trust violation and trust repair. Although there is an extant body of literature on causal antecedents and trust and a good number of studies in trust violation, the review shows that the researchers have “only begun to give the matter of trust repair much attention” [36: p. 401]. A review of selected salient literature using the two theories is reported in Table 1.

Table 1 shows that (1) the attribution theory and the organizational justice theory are the suitable theoretical lenses for the study of trust violation and repair; (2) the method of choice in most trust violation studies is controlled experiment; (3) most studies in trust violation are carried out in the management literature; (4) the literature shows a very limited number of studies that investigate both trust violation and its repair; (5) in the MIS area there has been limited examination of trust violation, and, with a couple of exceptions, trust repair has remained unstudied; and (6) there is a major gap in the study of trust violation as a result of online privacy breaches and the reparative process. Our study is an attempt to <sup>fi</sup>ll this gap by studying the negative impact of information privacy breaches on trust and the ways to repair the violated trust through social accounts. Following this literature, we use controlled experiment in investigating the process of trust violation and repair.

## 4.1. Synthesis of attribution theory and organizational justice theory

The organizational justice theory asserts that positive or negative perception of justice antecedents have a number of impacts, including employees' trust in and commitment to the organization and employees' performance and behaviors within the organization.

Viewed from the taxonomy of the attribution theory, the four justice antecedents in the organizational justice theory are causal attributions that are speci<sup>fi</sup>c to employee performance evaluation. Their speci<sup>fi</sup>city allows for the investigation of their speci<sup>fi</sup>c positive and negative consequences. Salient to our study is that in organizational justice theory, perceived informational injustice reduces trust [15]. However, informational injustice, viewed from the attribution theory, is internal to the organization since it is the decision made by managers within the organization. It is stable in that it does not happen by chance; and it is controllable and intentional since managers have control over the way they communicate information.

While unauthorized sharing of customers' private information is similar to informational injustice in that it has the same perceived dimension (internal, stable, controllable/intentional), we argue that hacking events have the opposite perceived dimensions because they are viewed external to the organization. Hacking violations are unstable and random since no one can predict when such attacks will succeed They are external and uncontrollable since organizations have no control over hacking attacks. The distinctions among perceived dimensions are a matter of degree. For example, the uncontrollability of hacking is not absolute since the organization could be blamed for not having a stronger protection against hacker attacks.<sup>2</sup> However, organizations do not intentionally choose to be victimized by hacking. Based on the distinct nature of the two privacy violation events, we posit that the type of violation has signi<sup>fi</sup>cant moderating effects on the trust violation and repair process, as discussed below. For the sake of brevity, we will refer to these two events as “sharing” and “hacking.”

In what follows, we discuss trust violation and repair in two parts: trust violation and trust repair.

## 4.2. Prior trust and its violation

In the <sup>fi</sup>rst part, before the violation incident, individuals start with a certain level of trust, which is impacted by the violation event. The perceived seriousness of violation and the starting level of trust (or prior trust) in<sup>fl</sup>uence the level of trust after violation, as discussed below.

Organizations by law have privacy policies intended to protect. This is a psychological contract between the organization and its customers [54]. Based on the organizational justice theory and its embedded social exchange theory, the violation of this contract leads to violation of customer trust [15]. Therefore, sharing and hacking should reduce customers' trust. We call this reduced trust after a privacy violation “violated trust.”

People have different perceptions about the seriousness of a privacy violation. The attribution theory allows for a subjective evaluation of predicaments—those who perceive the violation predicament to be “subjectively” more “important” [78: p. 564] will experience more severe attributions than those who do not. This argument is supported by the research <sup>fi</sup>nding of Jones and George [34] that the magnitude of the perceived violation is positively associated with the shift in trust. Hence, the higher perceived seriousness of violation, the lower violated trust should be. The argument in the psychological contract violation also supports the assertion that perceived seriousness of violation leads to a reduction in trust [29,54].

However, we argue that this negative association is moderated by the type of privacy violation. Based on the attribution theory, trust declines more severely if the trustor perceives that the trustee had the ability to control the event but chose not to, when the attribution is internal as opposed to external to the trustee, and when the trustor perceives that the event has a high likelihood of recurrence [37]. With the unauthorized sharing of user information, the trustor believes that the event was controllable, the trustee could have avoided sharing the information in an unauthorized way, the trustee was responsible, and, if the trustee is unethically involved in information sharing, it might do it again in the future. The organizational justice theory also supports this assertion in that the trustee has violated the fairness of privacyprotection procedure by sharing. This creates the perception of procedural injustice, which impacts trust negatively [15].

In contrast, hacking is done by external unknown hackers, and the trustee has little control over hackers. Therefore, hacking violation is less attributable to the trustee and its procedures [72,78]. Although hacking might be prevented in the future if the trustee updates its software, hardware and policies, sharing should have a stronger causal attribution, leading to a more pronounced reduction in trust because it is internal, controllable and stable. Thus, violation type moderates the impact of perceived seriousness of violation on violated trust.

H1. Violated trust has a negative association with the perceived seriousness of violation such that this negative relationship is stronger when the violation is unauthorized sharing.

Trust does not remain constant and is updated dynamically as a result of events and new information. We de<sup>fi</sup>ne prior trust as the level of trust prior to the violation event. Trust studies have reported that in the dynamics of trust update, prior trust is positively associated with trust revision [45,81]. Since violated trust is a type of trust revision based on the violation event, we expect that prior trust to have a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on violated trust. Trust declines only when one has “developed some level of trust and then perceives distrusting evidence due to the causal attributions made for the negative outcome” [72: p. 89]. This is also supported by the information integration theory that with new salient information, new affective states and beliefs are integrated with the old ones to form the revised affective states and beliefs [1,2,81]. We argue that when the violation has a strong negative impact on trust, its negative impact carries a larger share in the integration process and the share of prior trust in the integration process diminishes. ## 4.3. Violated trust and its repair

Once violated, the question is how the violated trust could be repaired. Trust repair depends on the organization's response, the extent of individuals' privacy concern and the extent of damage to trust. Trust repair de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the attempts by the trustee to restore partly or completely the willingness of the trustor to be vulnerable again in the future [39]. Based on the attribution theory, attributions are not <sup>fi</sup>xed and social accounts could modify them [72,78]. This is also supported by the literature on fairness of response in that a response that is perceived as fair could recover service failure and counter perceived injustice in con<sup>fl</sup>icts and interactions [29,64,69].

The literature on trust repair has focused on two types of social account: apology and denial [37,38,72]. Based on Kim et al. [38], we de<sup>fi</sup>ne “apology” as a public statement that acknowledges both responsibility and regret for the privacy violation. An apology conveys trustee's admission of responsibility and regret and its associated harm to the victim, and may also convey a stated desire to reconcile and continue the relationship [40]. With apology, the company provides explanatory information and accepts accountability for the consequences of the violation. When the company apologizes, it “admits culpability,” but on the positive side it sends a strong signal about its sincerity, reducing the perceived likelihood of future reoccurrences [24: p. 894].

In contrast, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne “denial” as a public statement by the company that denies any wrongdoing regarding the privacy violation event. Denial accepts no responsibility and conveys no regret [38]. Denial conveys information in that it ascribes the violation to another actor, but accepts no accountability for the violation. Denial shifts the blame to an external actor and sends a signal that the event was not controllable. On the downside, it does little to assuage the concerns about reoccurrences of such events in the future [24].

No response is a reticent response. Following Ferrin et al.'s [24] definition of reticence, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne “no response” as the company's refusal to con<sup>fi</sup>rm or deny responsibility for the privacy violation event. No response neither provides any explanation about the actor nor accepts accountability. No response which shows a lack of sincerity and openness on the part of the company, reaf<sup>fi</sup>rms in the mind of the users that the cause of the violation was internal to the website, and heightens the perception of a likely reoccurrence.

Therefore, the extent of response runs on a continuum with apology at the high end, denial next and no response at the low end in terms of providing explanatory information and accepting accountability for the violation event. This argument is supported by Ferrin et al. [24], who reported that reticence was inferior in approach to denial and apology in trust building; and Utz et al. [74], who observed that apology is superior to denial. Hence, we argue that the extent of response positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences repaired trust. However, this in<sup>fl</sup>uence is moderated by the type of violation [69]. Compared to hacking, sharing is more internal, controllable and stable. Therefore, victims should be more willing to blame the company and to hold it volitionally responsible for the violation. Thus, any reparative response should be less effective in the case of sharing than hacking because the company has to overcome a stronger attribution of guilt.

H3. Repaired trust has a positive association with the extent of company's response such that this positive association is weaker when the violation is unauthorized sharing.

Victims' privacy concern plays a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in restoring trust after a privacy violation. There are several perspectives as well as de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of information privacy. Information privacy has been viewed in turn as a right, a commodity, a state and an extent of control [66]. However, information privacy has also been viewed as fairness [44]. Relying on the cognitive and fairness aspects of privacy concern, it could be argued that privacy concern re<sup>fl</sup>ects individuals' desire for a greater degree of control and fairness [44] regarding their information usage. People with high privacy concern are shown to be less willing to trust in various settings with varying degrees of signi<sup>fi</sup>cance [4,21,35,44,75]. With a privacy violation, individuals with a higher privacy concern should be less willing to be vulnerable to the company's action in the future. However, this negative association is moderated by the violation type. When the company is in control of the violation, then individuals with a higher level of privacy concern should be less willing to trust the company again. Hence,

H4. Repaired trust has a negative association with individuals' privacy concern such that this negative relationship is stronger when the violation is unauthorized sharing.

Repaired trust is a revision of violated trust based on the company's social account of the violation. As the attribution is modi<sup>fi</sup>ed by social accounts, so is the resulting outcome [79]. In the context of this study, the outcome of the modi<sup>fi</sup>ed attribution is repaired trust. In other words, violated trust and modi<sup>fi</sup>ed attribution (the violation news modi<sup>fi</sup>ed by response through social accounts) get integrated to produce repaired trust. This argument is also supported by the information integration theory in that any revision of trust is an integration of its prior level with the new information [1,2,81]. Furthermore, Tomlinson [70] has shown post-violation trust to be positively associated with subsequent trusting behaviors [70]. As observed by Tomlinson [71: p. 172], trust is built on the history of relationship, and the trustor assesses the trustee's actions within this historical context. When a trust violation takes place and “the violator has a good relationship historically with the victim, any deviation is more likely to be seen as the exception rather than the rule.” [71: p. 172]. Hence, we argue that the extent of violated trust would be positively associated with repaired trust.

This association, however, is moderated by the type of privacy violation. The violated trust is the level of trust after the violation has become known, representing the strength of trusting relationship after the violation. We argue that a higher level of trust after violation is more impactful in the case of a more serious violation because such a violation requires a stronger level of trust relationship for the company to weath er the damage and repair its customers' trust.

Unauthorized sharing, per H1, is a more severe type of violation because it shows willful and intentional violation by the website's company. We argue that the role of violated trust is stronger in the case of unauthorized sharing. Using a negotiation scenario Tomlinson et al. [71] has found that in the case of more severe violations, past relationship plays a much stronger role in one's willingness to reconcile. Therefore, we argue that the role of violated trust should be stronger in repairing trust in the case of unauthorized sharing. Hence,

H5. Repaired trust has a positive association with violated trust such that this positive association is stronger when the violation is unauthorized sharing.

## 4.4. Control variables

There are individual characteristics that might be salient as control variables in this study. Individuals' familiarity has been reported to play a role in trust [22,29,54,72]. Individuals could form trust even in the absence of any prior familiarity [47]. However, this trust is quite fragile because of its tentative nature [36]. Thus we control for familiarity in the study. Another website-related control is prior experiences with the website, which goes further than familiarity, and is shown to play a role in the formation of initial trust [81]. Propensity to trust is another characteristic that describes the general outlook one possesses about trusting others [26,32] and is shown to be signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in the formation of prior trust [81]. Gender is shown to have a role in trust [17,80]. We also controlled for two website-speci<sup>fi</sup>c variables. Design appeal of the website is shown to in<sup>fl</sup>uence trust [3,16–18], so does the website's reputation [16,81]. All control variables except for prior experience with the website were used as the antecedents of prior trust. Based on trust violation studies, prior experience with the website was used on the control variable for violated trust [29,54].

## 5. Research methodology

The research methodology for this study was a scenario-based controlled experiment. The scenario-based experiment was appropriate as it controlled for extraneous factors, and allows the researchers to isolate the impact of speci<sup>fi</sup>c variables that are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to operationalize in other settings such as surveys and <sup>fi</sup>eld studies. The responses to scenarios are known to be fairly accurate and reliable re<sup>fl</sup>ections of the actual user decisions and reactions [22,68]. It is believed that time is a signi<sup>fi</sup>- cant variable impacting the reconciliation process following a trust violation [51]. We controlled for the effect of time by collecting data sequentially in one sitting. The design was full factorial (2 × 3), leading to a total of six scenarios: two violation types time three social accounts as possible responses.

Data were collected from students in a Midwestern University. It had three phases (Appendix A). In the <sup>fi</sup>rst phase, participants were shown a website and their prior trust was measured. In the second phase, participants were randomly shown one of the two scenarios in Table 2: hacking news or unauthorized information sharing news. Both news scenarios involved the same of number of affected user accounts. Violated trust was measured in this phase.

In the third phase, the participants were shown one of three possible response vignettes: apology, denial and no response (Table 3). Then, repaired trust in the website was measured.

In order to ensure that the participants had given the website some serious consideration, they were later asked to identify the correct name of the website shown to them from a list of <sup>fi</sup>ve options. They were also asked to correctly identify its specialization. Those who failed these two questions were excluded from the analysis. A total of 378 participants completed the study. After removing the students who failed the above test for serious participation, 364 were included in the analysis. Table 4 reports sample statistics.

## 6. Analysis

The construct de<sup>fi</sup>nitions and item development sources are reported in Appendix B. The instrument is reported in Appendix C. To ensure construct validity we used items from existing scales wherever possible. We converted the items to semantic differential (0–10) so as to minimize common method bias (CMB) [10,55,67], and data was collected in three sequences (before the violation scenario, after the violation scenario and after the social response), which reduced the threat of CMB [55]. To check for CMB after data collection, four tests were carried out. We computed Harman single-factor test [56]. The acceptable threshold for variance explained by a single factor is reported as 0.20 to 0.50 in the literature [48,67]. The variance explained by a single factor was 0.23, which was at the lower end of this range. The second test was to run the measurement model with and without single factor that had all items and was constrained to be independent from other factors, following the procedure in [33]. The <sup>fi</sup>t indices of the two models were similar. Furthermore, the test of chi-square difference between the two models showed that the two model <sup>fi</sup>ts were not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different [33,58]. The third test was to examine the correlation structure among constructs to see if any exceeded the 0.90 threshold [53]. None did. The fourth test was to use a marker item to further check for the presence of CMB. We partialled out the potential CMB by adding a marker item to the estimations [53]. The marker item was not statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. Based on the four tests, we concluded that CMB was not a serious concern in this study.

## 7. Post hoc analysis

Recent studies indicate the differential impact of negative events on trustworthiness beliefs—ability, integrity and benevolence [14,24,37,38,

72,80]. To investigate such differences, we carried out a post hoc analysis by exploring the impact of the violation type on three trustworthiness beliefs: ability, integrity and benevolence using the conceptual model. The intention of this analysis was to examine the extent to which each trust dimension is damaged by the violation event and the extent to which each dimension could be repaired in the reparative process (Fig. 1). In the post hoc analysis, we studied the differential impact of the trust violation event on each belief separately in order to preserve the model parsimony needed for analyzing the moderation effect using group analysis. Fig. 4 (a–c) presents the post hoc analysis for the trustworthiness beliefs. In all three trustworthiness beliefs, the path coef-<sup>fi</sup>cient perceived seriousness of violation → violated belief was higher for sharing. However, of the three trustworthiness beliefs, the integrity belief was most affected by perceived seriousness of violation in both hacking and sharing events. Benevolence belief was next most affected by perceived seriousness of violation. Ability was least impacted and the two events had almost equal path coef<sup>fi</sup>- cients. The path coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of prior integrity was the least in the three beliefs. Together, these results indicated that integrity was most affected by violation, and prior integrity belief had the lowest in<sup>fl</sup>uence in counterbalancing the negative impact of violation on violated integrity. In contrast, while benevolence belief was impacted by perceived seriousness of violation, prior benevolence belief had the largest in<sup>fl</sup>uence among the three beliefs to counterbalance the impact of violation.

Apology had the highest positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence in repairing benevolence belief in both types of violation. Apology had similar reparative in<sup>fl</sup>uences on ability and integrity beliefs for both violation types. Denial had similar reparative in<sup>fl</sup>uences across the three beliefs. An unexpected <sup>fi</sup>nding was that in the case of repaired ability, denial had a higher reparative in<sup>fl</sup>uence in unauthorized sharing.

## 8 . Discussions

This study set out to answer the questions regarding the process of trust violation and repair, the differential in<sup>fl</sup>uence of social accounts and the moderating role of violation type. Our conceptual model postulated a two-stage process: trust violation and trust repair. We argued that at the trust violation stage, perceived seriousness of violation and prior trust in<sup>fl</sup>uence the formation of violated trust. At the trust repair stage, the type of social account (no response, denial and apology), the level of privacy concern, and violated trust together lead to repaired trust. The relationships in both stages are moderated by the type of violation (hacking or unauthorized sharing).

## 8.1. Moderation

The estimation results underlined the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant moderating in<sup>fl</sup>uence of violation type on the trust violation and repair process. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings showed that perceived seriousness of violation has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly higher impact on violated trust in unauthorized sharing than in hacking. It shows that when the violation is unauthorized sharing (internal, controllable and stable), the company is considered more culpable and hence trust is impacted more than when the violation could be attributed to external, uncontrollable and unstable events, such as hacking. This is a unique <sup>fi</sup>nding since to our knowledge privacy violations have not been categorized based on their attribution dimensions and their differential impacts on trust have not been previously identi<sup>fi</sup>ed.

In the case of unauthorized sharing, prior trust could not act as a balancing force to counteract the impact of violation, whereas in the case of hacking, prior trust successfully counteracts the force of the violation and plays a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on violated trust. This is another unique <sup>fi</sup>nding regarding the role of prior trust in carrying companies through privacy violations.

## 8.2. Social accounts

We also investigated three social accounts that could modify the attributions of privacy violations: apology, denial, and no response. Based on the literature on trust repair, we argued that these three responses represent the extent of the company's response and are ordinal in that apology is the most reparative of the three responses, followed by denial and then no response. Our results supported this interpretation and showed that the higher the extent of the company's response in terms of providing information and accepting accountability is, the higher repaired trust will be. These results were supported by the literature [19,37]. Apology provides a perception of fairness, sincerity and respect, which give the recipient a feeling of being valued [19]. Kim et al. [37] found that apology was more effective when the trustee acknowledges accountability but the attribution is external, which supports our <sup>fi</sup>nding. Since hacking is attributed to external actors, apology is more effective in hacking than unauthorized sharing, indicating that when the company is perceived as culpable, attempts for modifying attributions through social accounts will be less effective in repairing trust.

## 8.3. Reparative effects of response types

We examined the reparative effect of apology and denial. Results indicated that apology has a far superior reparative impact, and this impact is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly higher in hacking. This <sup>fi</sup>nding is supported by

Utz et al. [74], who reported apology as a superior response that evokes similar sentiments as “voluntary hosting” [50]. In contrast, denial had statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant reparative impact on repaired trust only in hacking. This showed that when the company is perceived as culpable, denial will not work. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings highlight the importance of understanding the attribution dimension of violation events prior to adopting reparative social accounts.

The unexpected <sup>fi</sup>nding was that privacy concern had no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in repaired trust in the case of hacking, whereas it had a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant negative impact as hypothesized in the case of sharing. The positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence of trust violation on repaired trust and its more important role in the case of unauthorized sharing highlighted the integrative in<sup>fl</sup>uence of violated trust in repaired trust, particularly in the case of unauthorized sharing.

The post hoc analysis of trustworthiness beliefs (ability, integrity, and benevolence) supported the moderating in<sup>fl</sup>uence of violation type in most cases, and provided further insights into the differential impacts of violations on these beliefs. We found that integrity is most impacted by privacy violation events. This impact is pervasive and could not be counter-balanced by either prior belief or prior positive experiences with the website. In all three beliefs, apology was the most reparative social account. Furthermore, in both hacking and sharing, the reparative in<sup>fl</sup>uence of apology was the highest for the benevolence belief. The in<sup>fl</sup>uence of apology was similar in ability and integrity. As a social account, denial had a higher path coef<sup>fi</sup>cient for sharing for the ability belief, although its difference from the case of hacking was not statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. Together, these results indicated that although the conceptualized model is a suitable vehicle to investigate the process of prior → violated → repaired beliefs, the results vary among the three beliefs, with integrity most affected by violation and benevolence more repaired by apology.

## 8.6. Visualization

In order to gain a deeper insight about the nature of trust evolution through the violation and repair process, we investigated the mean of trust and trustworthiness beliefs through visualization (Fig. 5).

Fig. 5 shows averages of trust and trustworthiness beliefs (ability, integrity and benevolence) in three stages of trust violation and repair: prior trust, violated trust and repaired trust. The <sup>fi</sup>gure clearly shows that unauthorized sharing violates trust and trustworthiness beliefs far more than hacking. Reparative social accounts do not restore trust or trustworthiness beliefs to their prior levels. This situation is worse in the case of unauthorized sharing.

Apology was reparative in all cases in that it causes trust to rise. However the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of denial is more complex and required a closer look. We divided the sample data set into two groups: high privacy concern and low privacy concern. To categorize participants into low and high privacy concern, we computed the average score of all privacy concern items and then standardized the scores by computing the z-score ((x-mean)/std dev). Those with z-scores below zero were categorized as having low privacy concern and those at or above zero were categorized as having high privacy concern. We then compared violated trust and repaired trust for the three response types (apology, denial, and no response) for hacking and unauthorized sharing.

Fig. 6 shows that in hacking, denial and no response have no reparative bene<sup>fi</sup>t and cause trust to decline even further both in high and low privacy concern groups.

However, it is more complex in the case of unauthorized sharing. For low privacy cases, denial was as effective as apology in restoring trust, whereas in high privacy cases, denial was as damaging as no response, and caused trust to decline even further the same way as no response did. The same pattern was present in all three trustworthiness beliefs (graphs not reported for the sake of brevity)—denial was as effective as apology for participants with low privacy concern in unauthorized sharing, and was as damaging as no response for participants with high privacy concern. This is an important <sup>fi</sup>nding, indicating the universal effectiveness of apology and the complex violation-type speci<sup>fi</sup>c and victim-speci<sup>fi</sup>c impacts of denial.

These <sup>fi</sup>ndings also explain the differential impact of privacy concern. In the case of hacking, apology repaired trust regardless of privacy concern, whereas in the case of unauthorized sharing, apology was less effective, and privacy concern played a pivotal role in denial's direction and magnitude of impact. This <sup>fi</sup>nding explains why privacy concern was signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in the case of unauthorized sharing, but not in the case of hacking.

Furthermore, a comparison of trust and trustworthiness beliefs at prior, violated and repaired stages in Figs. 5 and 6 shows that none of the social accounts was able to completely restore the level of trust or trustworthiness beliefs to their prior levels. This is an important result that con<sup>fi</sup>rms Schweitzer et al.'s [60] warning that trust damaged by some untrustworthy event can never be fully restored.

## 9. Implications and contributions

Our work has several important and novel theoretical and managerial implications. Trust violation has been studied in two spheres of theoretical work: the attribution theory and organizational justice theory. Our synthesis of the attribution theory with the organizational justice theory contributes to the creation of a stronger and uniform framework for the examination of the trust violation and repair process in cybercrime from multiple perspectives: company actors to whom the violations are attributed, the customers who are victimized, and the general public.

Moreover, this paper provides a theoretical framework for the investigation of privacy violation events through the taxonomy of the attribution theory. It opens the possibility of a theory-based analysis of violation events and the prediction of how the public and victims may perceive and react to such events. The conceptual model presented in this work extends our knowledge of the dynamics of trust violation and repair, thus addressing the concern raised by Kim et al. [36] that the literature has “almost entirely” ignored contextual in<sup>fl</sup>uences in trust repair (p. 418).

Our work is also the <sup>fi</sup>rst to study the moderating in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the violation types in trust privacy-based violation and repair. This is particularly important in the online context with its vast potential for privacy and security violations and criminal activities. A theory-based approach to investigating the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of such activities and quantifying their moderating roles on trust is a novel and important contribution to our understanding of the damaging impacts of such activities and the reparative strategies needed to counterbalance them.

Speci<sup>fi</sup>c <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study also have important theoretical implications. The uniform superiority of apology as a social account and reparative action in privacy violation events extends similar <sup>fi</sup>ndings in the organizational literature to information privacy violations. The complex role of denial and its dependency on individuals' privacy concern is another novel <sup>fi</sup>nding.

Another signi<sup>fi</sup>cant theoretical implication is that privacy violation events and reparative actions do not have uniform impacts on trustworthiness beliefs. Our work showed that integrity suffered the most in privacy violation events and benevolence is repaired the most by apology. Investigations of privacy violation events need to be cognizant of the interplay of events' attribution dimensions with the nature of trustworthiness beliefs.

This work has a number of managerial implications as well. There are three factors that make this paper of critical importance to managers—almost all companies have presence on the web, none is immune from privacy violation events, and trust is a precious asset for companies, particularly for those operating on the web. Managers can bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the insights provided by classifying violation events and their differentiating impacts on customer trust. They will be able to gauge the extent of damage by the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of violations and the perceptions of the public and customers. Furthermore, our work makes it clear that they must take action in the light of such events. An apology will be the most reparative social account. Denial will be preferred to no response. However, managers need to be aware of the complex impact of denial, since it could have more damaging impact than no response for people who are sensitive about their private information. Therefore, it is important for managers to formulate wellthought strategies for potential privacy violation events and investigate how their customers may respond to actions beyond the social accounts discussed in this study.

Another important managerial implication is that once violated, trust cannot easily be restored to its pre-event level. Therefore, it pays for managers to invest in control mechanisms that prevent any unauthorized sharing of customer private information since the loss in terms of customer trust could be extensive and permanent.

Our work provided a theoretical framework for classifying such events, investigating their impacts, and measuring the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of reparative actions.

Like all studies of this nature, this work has limitations since the data was collected through a controlled experiment with student participants. Although, the college student population is among the most active participants in the cyber space and constitute a salient population for our study, this work needs to be replicated for different demographics and culture. This work could be extended to other types of illegal events and activities that could be attributed to companies or their websites, such as website phishing, tax evasion, or discrimination.

Our experimental protocol focused on the process of trust violation and repair of different privacy violation types for potential customers/general public. We used a scenario-based experiment, and controlled for time since the passage of time could modify the impact of violation. Future research is needed to examine the effect of time in trust violation and repair through longitudinal data collection. Future research should also examine timing of the breach, and timeliness of company response, which is known to play role in trust violation and trust restoration process [43,71]. It should also be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to conduct research using experimental designs that require subjects to take action as opposed to just reporting the intent [22]. The next step in this investigation is to study the process for customers whose private information has actually been breached and contrast their responses with those of the general public. More work is also needed to study the effect of frequency of transgressions. It will be worthwhile to examine the impacts of content and tone of social accounts and the channels by which they are delivered. Future research could also examine the effect of willingness to reconcile, degree of forgiveness, and use of reparations to compensate the victims. Another area of future work is to examine <sup>fi</sup>eld studies of actual privacy violations events and their reconciliation process.
