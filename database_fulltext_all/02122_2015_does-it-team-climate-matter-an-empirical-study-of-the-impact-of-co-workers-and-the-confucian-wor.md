## Abstract

This study seeks to determine the impact of IT co-workers’ influence on individual deviance behavior in organizations. With data collected from 322 IT employees and their supervisors in Chinese software companies, we examine the impact of Confucian work ethic on deviant behavior. The results suggest that both co-workers’ production deviance and Confucian work ethic have an impact on production deviance. The influence of IT co-workers’ production deviance was greater in high team climates and low team climates than in neutral team climates. Confucian work ethic has no significant influence on production deviance in low team climate.

Keywords: Confucian work ethic, IT co-worker production deviance, IT team climate

## INTRODUCTION

The prevalent use of teamwork in organizations, especially in the Information Technology (IT) industry, has increased considerably in the past decades due to fierce competition, swift technological changes that result in an increased degree of division of labor, and the need for specialized expertise (Cohen and Bailey, 1997; Singh and Muncherji, 2007; Thamhain, 2004). The benefit of teams stems mainly from collective synergy that would be impossible to achieve through individual efforts (Katzenbach and Smith, 1993; Rousseau, Aube, and Savoie, 2006; Salas et al. 2005; Tarricone and Luca, 2002). While a team is characterized by interdependency, interaction, complementary skills, and mutual accountability among team members (Lussier, 2002), the success of software project teams depends on the interaction of knowledge and skills among team members, inherently a process that requires intense social interactions (Hoegel and Parboteeah, 2007). The success of IT project can be influenced by cultural, individual, socio-economic, and situational factors (Camprieu at al. 2007), but only certain factors can be controlled or at least influenced by IT project managers (Belassi and Tukel, 1996; Hyvari, 2006). IT team climate is one of the most crucial factors (Singh and Muncherji, 2007; McDonough, 2000). Issues associated with the team members as reflected in team climate can increase the opportunity of IT project deviance behavior outcome, such as team member turnover, lack of motivation (Wallace, Keil, and Rai, 2004), absenteeism, and intention to leave (Cohen and Bailey, 1997). As such, IT team climate is vital to the ultimate success of IT projects and is the objective of this study which endeavors to extend the line of workplace deviance behavior research in the IS community.

Workplace deviance behaviors are universal in organizations, with nearly 95 percent of all

#

companies reporting various forms of deviance-related behaviors. With the inception of the Internet, organizational employees can be unproductive at work in terms of using organizational IT resources for personal computing. This pressing predicament is amplified given the mushrooming growth of IT implementations in global organizations. In recent years, workplace deviance research has piqued increasing attention from both researchers and practitioners due to its pervasiveness and the financial costs associated with it (Yang, 2008). For example, organizations lost up to \$178 billion annually for employees‟ non-work-related computing (Vitak et al., 2011). In the United States, a loss of \$200 million per year was reported for deviant workplace behavior (Harris and Ogbonna, 2006). Unauthorized web surfing during working hours also cost the United Kingdom £300 million in lost productivity per year (Taylor, 2007). Bullying in the workplace costs Australian employers between 6 and 13 billion Australian dollars annually (Chappell and Martino, 2006).

In essence, the objective of this study is to investigate workplace production deviance in IT settings. Workplace production deviance is generally defined as "the purposeful failure to perform job tasks effectively the way they are supposed to be performed" (Spector et al. 2006). More specifically, this definition refers to “behavior that violates formally prescribed organizational norms with respect to minimal quality and quantity of work to be accomplished as part of one‟s job” (Pulich and Tourigny, 2004). This comprehensive concept sheds light on a dark side of organizational IS settings in which deviant employees have a negative impact on the overall productivity of the organization by means of leaving earlier or taking excessive or unauthorized breaks (Blau, 1998), increased absenteeism and low job involvement (Hanisch, 1998), wasting resources, procrastinating, and intentionally working slower. Compared to deviances with the nature of active sabotage and theft, these trivial and passive production deviances may be less motivated by retaliation but can lead to destructive reactions and an unfavorable work environment. Thus, it is of paramount importance to explore new perspectives and interpret these behaviors in IS research.

Prior studies have primarily focused on the influence of organization and supervisor, such as organizational justice, supervisor support, abusive supervision, and human resources system characteristics (Penney, 2005; Mitchell and Ambrose, 2007; Eder and Eisenberger, 2008; Skarlicki, Barclay and Pugh, 2008; Akremi, Vandenberghe and Camerman, 2010; Arthur, 2011). In addition, studies have also found that organizational justice is a key construct in explaining workplace deviance (Ambrose, Seabright and Schminke, 2002; Berry, Ones and Sackett, 2007) where employees tend to engage in less workplace deviance when they feel they have been treated fairly. In this vein, a plethora of justice-driven factors including distributive justice, procedural justice, interpersonal justice, and informational justice have different impacts on various types of workplace deviance, such as supervisor-directed deviance and organization-directed deviance (Akremi et al., 2010). The pertaining literature has suggested that situational variables, such as organizational support perceived by the employees as a favorable or unfavorable work environment, also affect workplace deviance behavior (Rhoades and Eisenberger, 2002; Eder et al., 2008; Ferris, Brown and Heller, 2009). Furthermore, abusive supervision, which denotes employees perceptions of what they believe are purposeful and unfair supervisor mistreatment, promotes negative reactions in the workplace (Tepper et al., 2008; Tepper et al., 2009). While most existing studies have investigated the role of organizational variables, progress has been made at the individual level in understanding the impact of personal traits (Colbert et al., 2004), employee perception of the work situation (Lee and Allen, 2002), and individual ethical judgments (Liu and Ding, 2012) on workplace deviance. Recent efforts have been made to explore co-workers‟ influence in interpreting workplace deviance behaviors. For example, Glomb and Liao (2003) found that aggressive behavior exhibited by members of an individual work group is a significant predictor of the individual's interpersonal aggressive behavior. Eder and Eisenberger (2008) found that an individual is more likely to withdraw from his or her own work when the employee‟s co-workers exhibit higher levels of withdrawal. Huang et al. (2009) indicated that a revenge motive mediates the relationship deviance behavior in a service group can indirectly affect one‟s behavior towards service customers. However, there still is a paucity of scientific theory-driven investigation on whether IT co-workers‟ influences differ in various situations in IS domain. This article is one of the early attempts to examine the contextual role of IT team climate in co-workers‟ influence and to advance our understanding of IT co-workers‟ role versus workplace norms in deviance behaviors. This study significantly contributes to the IS community by further extending the body of research on IT team climate vis-à-vis work deviance behavior.

Workplace deviance behavior is one of the consequences of the employee co-worker exchange relationship. According to Social Exchange Theory, the interactions between parties in an organization are a state of reciprocal interdependence. Employees expect to gain economic or socioemotional resources in exchange for their engagement in their job-related roles (Saks, 2006). The effects of organizational variables depend on a positive norm of reciprocity. When an employee has a positive perception of the work situation, he is less likely to be engaged in workplace deviance (Colber et al., 2004). Abusive supervision is more strongly related to supervisor-directed deviance when individuals hold negative reciprocity beliefs (Mitchell and Ambrose, 2007). Apparently, negative reciprocity norms explain why employees of mistreatment may be motivated to retaliate. As Social Exchange Theory explains, when employees perceive inequity and feel a lack of support from their co-workers, they become more likely to reciprocate the source of inequity by threatening their co-workers (Liao, 2004) or engaging in some retaliation behavior to restore positive feelings (Felps, 2006). Individuals are guided by some combination of negative reciprocity beliefs in workplace and other beliefs or motives. For example, Ferris et al. (2009) argued that low levels of organizational support frustrated individual organization-based self-esteem and increased organizational deviance. Furthermore, work ethic, the value and importance of hard work, is another important determinant of work-related behavior. Yet little attention has been given recently to the role of work ethic on IT employee workplace deviance in IS research. As such, we believe that it is necessary to further explore the role of work ethics on work deviance behaviors in IT organizations. It is worth noting that, despite the increasing volume of research in this stream, there has been relatively little focus on interpreting employees‟ deviance behavior, especially in IS research. This study will fill the voids and break new ground for IT deviance behavior research.

Departing from prior research, this work contributes to the academic literature on IS workplace deviance behavior in several ways. First, we explore the impacts of IT co-workers production deviance and of IT team climate on employee production deviance. This perspective expands our understanding of IT co-worker‟s influence, especially in the context of Chinese employees. Second, we argue whether the Confucian work ethic influences employee production deviance. This lens supplies a new mechanism to interpret employees deviance behavior. Finally, we explore the influence of IT team climate on Confucian work ethic, an analysis that highlights the complexity of Confucian work ethics influence. The study will present implications and recommendations to help decrease production deviance in organizations.

## CO-WORKER AND INDIVIDUAL PRODUCTION DEVIANCE

Workplace deviance has also been termed counterproductive workplace behavior, antisocial organizational behavior, organizational misbehavior, organizational deviance, workplace aggression (Baron and Neuman, 1996), organizational delinquency (Hogan and Hogan, 1989), and dysfunctional behavior by researchers who also include varying sets of behaviors in their studies (Everton, Jolton and Mastrangelo, 2007). Among the earlier studies, Hollinger (1986) divided deviant work behavior into property and production deviance and recognized distinctions between attacks on organizational processes and attacks on material resources of the organization, but ignored deviant behavior towards persons. In contrast, Robinson and Bennett (1995) distinguished deviant behavior such as production deviance, property deviance, political deviance, and personal aggression; they specified interpersonal forms of deviance as a new dimension. Researchers suggested that there are five categories of counterproductive workplace behavior (CWB): abuse against others, production deviance, sabotage, withdrawal and theft (Spector et al., 2006).

There exists a debate on whether exploring radical antecedents helps us understand workplace deviant behaviors. For example, Ambrose and Seabright (2002) found that injustice was a more frequent cause of sabotage than powerlessness, frustration, facilitation of work, or boredom in organizations. While Spector (2006) found that abuse and sabotage were most strongly related to anger and stress, theft was unrelated to emotion, and withdrawal was associated with boredom and being upset; Tepper et al. (2009) indicated that abusive supervision was more strongly associated with supervisor-directed deviance than with organization-directed deviance when intention to quit is higher. In essence, individual production deviance refers to an organizational employee‟s behaviors that violate the formally prescribed norms and delineate the minimal quality and quantity of work to be accomplished (Hollinger, 1986). Along this line, co-worker production deviance refers to the individual employee‟s co-workers‟ behaviors that violate the formally prescribed organizational norms with respect to minimal quality and quantity of work to be

#

accomplished as part of their jobs (Hollinger, 1986). In general, these behaviors result in reduced efficiency of work output, such as withholding effort for tasks, taking excessive recesses, and intentionally working slow, etc. As Heise (1977) noted, co-workers‟ behavior is an important reference to employee‟s behavior in workplace. He posited that a person who observes another accepting, supporting, or tolerating an object is likely to do the same, and when observing another reject, interfere with, or criticize an object, a person is likely to act similarly. In their study of employee citizenship, Bommer et al. (2003) found that the mean level of organizational citizenship behavior by other members of one‟s workgroup explained significant variance in individual levels of organizational citizenship behavior. Similarly, researchers have also found the influence of aggregate co-worker behavior on individual deviant work behavior. For example, Tepper et al. (2009) stated that employees‟ deviant workplace behaviors are related to the frequency with which their co-workers engaged in these behaviors. Eder and Eisenberger (2008) found that co-workers‟ withdrawal behaviors are positively related to an employee‟s own level of withdrawal behavior. Furthermore, when co-workers hold production deviance behaviors, it is unreasonable to deny the influence of co-workers as “important others” on individual production deviance. Recurring incidents of co-workers‟ deviance behavior constitute a social environment in which an employee is socialized and learns to behave similarly. When an individual communicates with others and also observes their behaviors, the co-workers‟ production deviance will directly impact individual production deviance. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis: H1: Co-workers’ production deviance will be positively related to individual production deviance.

## CONFUCIAN WORK ETHICS

Defined by Chinese Cultural Connection (1987) and rooted in Confucian values, Confucian work dynamism comprises qualities such as persistence, personal steadiness, ordering relationships, a sense of shame, and protecting face. It also emphasizes the values of thrift and hard work, harmony and cooperation, respect for educational achievements, and reverence for authority (Lim, 2003). This study focuses on the individual level of the Confucian work ethics (CWE), which stems from Confucian values and is a motivational construct reflected in behavior and related to attitudes and beliefs in work-related activity in general, not specific to any particular job. CWE emphasize persistence, patience, endurance, Confucian work dynamism blends belief toward work and individuals in workplace, while Tsang (2011) distinguished separate attitudes towards work and towards people. Our work sheds light on attitude and beliefs at individual level toward general work in IT industry, and we define CWE as individual views of persistence, patience, hard work, endurance, and work tolerance.

Prior studies have explored the influence of CWE on employees‟ attitudes and behaviors at the individual level in disciplines such as Organizational Behavior and Management. For example, Lim (2003) found that CWE were positively associated with individuals‟ inclination to budget their money. Yeh and Xu (2010) focused on the “Hierarchy and Harmony” dimension of CWE and showed that innovative environments with CWE positively affected learning both general and professional knowledge. Nevertheless, the influence of CWE on workplace deviance behavior has not been empirically examined and analyzed in IS research.

#

Individuals who adhere to high CWE usually have good work habits in any given workplace. As such, an individual tends to work hard and patiently, even if the IT-related work is loathsome or boring. He or she will complete a task even when facing a change in the workplace. In sum, individual IT workplace employees who hold high CWE will not engage in passive behavior in the workplace. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis: H2: Confucian work ethics will be negatively related to individual production deviance

## TEAM CLIMATE

Teams usually have norms that reflect a set of behaviors aligned with their employers expectations. These norms are either formulated by company policies or by employee practices in the workplace. When frequently interacting with other group members, an employee is likely to align his personal norm with the team norm (West, 1990). Previous research (Salancik and Pfeffer, 1978) suggested that the informal norms adopted by individuals usually are derived from others in their direct social contexts. It is conceivable that co-workers exposed to the same organizational policies, leaders, and contextual characteristics are the important “others.” In IS literature, it has been reported that the work team is a particularly important source of social influence that can affect knowledge workers‟ perception and behavior (Liang et al., 2010). According to Mischel (1977), strong situations will enforce more influence than weak situations in individual behavior. Mischel (1977) indicated that desired behaviors are delivered by the underpinning of correct responses. He also posited that normative expectations in strong situations support learning how to perform desired behaviors, whereas weak situations lack these attributes. Team climate, as one type of situation, emphasizes that norms are an informal means of social control without the force of law (Cialdini and Trost, 1998). This study sheds light on the climate related to employees production deviance. Few employees have production deviance and the normal production behavior is excellent in high team climate, whereas low team climate denotes that more employees have production deviance. In essence, both high and low team climates convey cues and information to individuals and enforce individual learning from others. However, neutral team climate does not provide clear incentive, support, or normative expectations of behavior. In this situation, individual behavior is mostly guided by idiosyncratic judgment on the appropriateness of the behavior.

In addition, psychological stress plays a vital role in the influence of team climate. In both high and low team climates, individuals endure psychological stress when performing anti-norm behaviors because they perceive the stronger team climate and know that others share the common perception. Other studies also found that anti-norm behavior in an organization attracted more negative evaluations than did pro-norm behavior (Abrams et al. 2002; Bown and Abrams, 2003). In neutral team climate, however, there is little psychological stress stemming from the team climate regardless of individual production deviance. Compared with those in neutral team climate, individuals in high and low team climates know the team's normative expectations, desired behaviors, and negative outcome when performing anti-norm behavior. As such, psychological stress restrains personal traits role and strengthens the traits‟ consistency with co-workers‟ behavior in high and low team climates. Given little or no psychological stress perceived in neutral team climate, individuals will perform behaviors according to their own ideas, making it difficult to form normal or deviance behaviors. Thus, we propose the following hypotheses:

H3: As the value of the team climate increases, the positive relationship between co-workers' production deviance and individual production deviance also increases.

The Confucian value system will influence individual behavior, and CWE will guide employees‟ workplace behavior in Chinese organizations. However, the work ethic influence is not always stable and could be disturbed by other factors in the multidimensional Confucian value system. In high team climates, most co-workers have few, if any, production deviances enhancing the individual‟s personal pursuit of Confucian values. Thus, the influence of Confucian work ethics on production deviance will be strengthened. Low team climates, however, where most co-workers have significant production deviances, restrict the individual‟s personal pursuit of Confucian values. Thus, in order to keep harmony with co-workers, individuals may restrict their personal pursuit in order to survive in the workplace. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

H4: As the value of the team climate increases, the negative relationship between Confucian work ethics and individual production deviance also increases.

## RESEARCH METHOD AND MEASUREMENTS

## Participants and Procedure

The participants in this study consisted of IT employees and their immediate supervisors in the product development section of small IT enterprises in a metropolitan city in China. 37 small IT enterprises were randomly selected from a list supplied by the local government-sponsored authority to which the authors submitted an initial inquiry about small local IT enterprises. The authoritative list includes all IT-related companies that are operating in a designated software park in the Chinese metropolitan city. The list also provides contact information with which the authors initialized the communications. The particular list was retrieved at http://www.tianfusoftwarepark.com/en/our-park/our-tenants.html. Amid the 37 randomly selected companies from the list, the product development sections‟ team size of these sampled IT enterprises ranged from 6 to 11 employees. There were altogether 410 employees and 37 immediate supervisors in these enterprises' product development sections. All of the employees and their immediate supervisors have a background related to IT-based projects.

Data collection was conducted by using a questionnaire survey developed for the study. The questionnaire we provided to employees measured co-workers‟ production deviance, employee production deviance, Confucian work ethics, and demographics. Questionnaire II, provided to employees‟ immediate supervisors, contained measurements of team climate. Since some original items were developed in English, they were translated into Chinese by using a standard translation-back-translation procedure in order to ensure language equivalence (Brislin, 1980). Five-point Likert-type scales were used for all items (1 for Almost Never and 5 for Extremely Often or Always). After pretesting and preliminary item analysis, the final version of the questionnaire was developed and utilized (See Appendix).

With the approval of the enterprise's supervisors, the authors entered the product development sections and distributed the survey packet, each including an envelope and questionnaire I, to every employee of the section. All of the employees were assured of confidentiality and were informed that all responses would be used for research purposes only. Then, they were asked to fill out the questionnaire voluntarily. At the same time, a survey packet, including an envelope and questionnaire II, was distributed to their immediate supervisors in their own separate offices. The immediate supervisors were asked to fill out questionnaire II voluntarily. All surveys were returned directly to the authors in sealed

envelopes.

After discarding surveys with missing data and incomplete questionnaires in employees responses, 322 out of 410 responses were obtained for subsequent data analysis. This yields a 78.5% response rate. Table 1 provides the descriptive statistics of the respondents, including gender, age, education, and work experience.

## Individual Production Deviance and Co-worker Production Deviance

The studies by Bennett and Robinson (2000) and Stewart et al. (2009) adapted five and seven items, respectively, to measure production deviance. In our study, we omitted the item “Came in late to work without permission” because most Chinese enterprises regulate this behavior by punishment or sanctions. Because it is difficult to ascertain whether IT co-workers work steadily or spend time daydreaming, and in order to maintain consistency between individual production deviance and co-worker production deviance, these two items were also not included in our scales. In addition, because the Cronbach's alpha values for items “I left my work for someone else to finish” and “My co-workers left their work for someone else to finish” were less than 0.6 in the pretest, the two items were also excluded from the scale. In the end, three items were used for production deviance measurement in our study (See Appendix).

## Confucian Work Ethics

Rooted in Confucian values, Confucian work dynamism usually includes respect for one‟s superior, thrift, persistence, sense of shame, reciprocal courtesy, steadiness, prestige and the respect for tradition (Wu and Chiang, 2007). Confucian work dynamism also blends employees‟ beliefs about their general work with those about relationships in the workplace. In order to separate out the influence of employees‟ beliefs about general work, we followed Tsang (2011), who ascertained attitude towards work in Chinese employees. Because the dimension reflects individual values related to general work, this formulation is well-suited

#

for the current study.

## Team Climate

Because we hypothesize that team climate is related to employees‟ production deviance in a group, we adopted immediate supervisors‟ assessment of the whole team‟s production deviance as the indicator of team climate by using a 5-point Likert scale. By averaging scores on four items, we classified the respondents into three groups: Climate scores from 1 to 2.33 as high team; climate scores from 2.34 to 3.66 as neutral teams; and scores from 3.67 to 5 as low teams. Of the 322 respondents, 169 work within high team climates from 17 product development sections, whereas 96 are in neutral team climates from 11 product development sections, and 57 are in low team climates from 9 product development sections.

## Common Method Bias

In order to control common method bias in data gathering, we used counterbalancing question order, protected respondent anonymity, and reduced evaluation apprehension (Podsakoff et al., 2003). In addition, a one-factor Harman test was conducted to assess the possible existence of common method bias. The principal component factor analysis on the questionnaire items showed three factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, accounting for the 58% of the variance. The first factor only explained 31.2% of the variance, suggesting common method bias is not a problem in this study.

## Statistical Method

To test the proposed research model, we used structural equations analysis and multi-group analysis. Specifically, we used Partial Least Squares (PLS) through Smart-PLS tools for hypothesis testing based on several considerations. A covariance-based structural equation modeling (CB-SEM) would require a large sample size and normality assumptions on indicators (Chin, 2010). With the number of parameters included in this study, a covariance-based structural equation modeling would require 200 cases using Bentler and Chou‟s heuristic of 5 cases per parameter estimated. Our sample size in this study is small in the Neutral (96) and Low team climate (57) groups, and the Shapiro-Wilk normality test for our data was significantly different from normal distribution. In addition, PLS is preferred in causal-predictive analysis and theory development (Reinartz, Haenlein, and Henseler, 2009). The framework proposed in this study has never been tested by IS scholars, and the nature of this study is more exploratory than confirmatory. Thus, we deemed it a suitable analysis technique for this research.

To test the differential impacts of the factors in high, low, and neutral team climates, we adopted the procedure suggested by Hsieh et al. (2008). Therefore, a t-test assuming equal variance was applied to assess the statistical differences in path coefficients for each pair of paths between high team climate and neutral team climate. To assess the stability of the results, a Smith-Satterthwaite test with the assumption of unequal variance was also calculated. T-values greater than 1.96 or less than -1.96 indicate significant differences of path coefficients at the 0.05 level.

## DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS

Production deviance is a prominent phenomenon in IT-related enterprises because it has a profound impact on enterprises‟ efficiency. Prior research primarily focused on the influence of reciprocity mechanisms between employees and enterprises, leaving the mechanisms of influences of co-workers and work ethics largely unexplored. To extend this line of research, this study breaks new ground by shedding light on co-workers' influence and individual work ethics' influence in IT settings.

#

Results of the empirical study revealed that co-worker production deviance had a significantly positive influence on employees' individual production deviance regardless of the team climate (i.e., high, neutral or low team climate). We also found that co-worker influence is not always constant. The influences of co-workers‟ production deviance in high and low team climates are greater than in neutral team climate, which suggests that situational and psychological stress can influence employees‟ production deviance. Specifically, our study found the Confucian work ethic plays a complex role in employee production deviance. There is no difference between the influence of CWE in high team climate and neutral team climates, whereas the CWE are minimized in low team climate.

## Theoretical contributions

While retaliation and negative reciprocity norm are the apparent motives in some workplace deviance (Akremi et al. 2010; Mitchell and Ambrose, 2007; Restubog et al. 2010), the effects of co-worker production deviance shown in this research suggest that co-workers are critical in affecting individual production deviance. At the same time, the Confucian work ethic is a new mechanism with a complex role in production deviance and supplies a new lens for our understanding of Chinese employees‟ production deviance behaviors. This study endeavors to usher CWE to IS research with regards to workplace deviance behavior in various IT settings. Beyond this individual cultural aspect, this study also carries several significant contributions to academic research.

The first set of theoretical implications focuses on the varying effects of IT co-worker influence in different IT team climates. The results support the hypothesis that IT co-worker production deviance has a significant positive influence on employee individual production deviance in high, neutral or low team climates. Chinese employees tend to maintain solidarity with others in groups, and demonstrating behaviors consistent with others in the group is generally viewed as positive and effective by Chinese employees. Thus, our results are not surprising. Such adaptation is not only a safe psychological choice, but it also signals the intention to keep harmony with others. Although we observed a general lack of support for the effect of integration rooted in Confucian values, we believe these findings serve to highlight the core nature of Confucian work ethics, such as sense of righteousness and virtue and tolerance of others in the workplace.

The influences of IT co-worker‟s production deviance in high and low team climates are greater than in neutral team climates, which suggests that explicit and consistent team norms indirectly facilitate or restrict employee production deviance. This result is similar to some studies on organizational level effects (Blau, 1995; Eder and Eisenberger, 2008; Mathieu and Kohler, 1990). For example, pertaining to the relationship between the aggregate behaviors of the work group and individual employee behaviors, Mathieu and Kohler (1990) showed that the frequency of absenteeism among work group members is related to individual employee absenteeism. Blau (1995) found that the aggregated tardiness of employee work groups is related to the tardiness of individual employees. Similarly, Eder and Eisenberger (2008) found that the withdrawal behavior by members of work groups was positively related to employees‟ own levels of withdrawal behavior. These researchers, however, used a group behavior variable different from what we used. They either aggregated all employees behaviors or averaged all employees‟ behaviors, excluding the individual employee‟s own behaviors. In contrast, through supervisor assessment, we define IT team climate as an objective variable toward employees. In addition, prior work emphasizes the direct influence of aggregated behaviors while this research explores the indirect effect of team climate. From prior research, individual perception of group level deviance directly impacts individual workplace deviant behavior. In our research, IT team climates characterized by employees production deviance have an obvious impact on the relationship between co-workers production deviance and individual production deviance. Thus, this study enhances our understanding of the impact of group level workplace deviance.

The second set of theoretical implications focuses on the effect of the CWE, contributing to the rather limited number of studies focused on the effect of one‟s work ethic on workplace deviant behavior. The important conclusion in the research is that CWE play a complex role in employee production deviance. Because of the differences between the Confucian and Protestant work ethics, it is valuable to explore the mechanism of CWE in the context of Chinese employees.

CWE are similar to Protestant work ethic inasmuch that hard work is a core value. In exploring the influence of Protestant work ethic on temporary service employees, Saks et al. (2003) also thought that individuals who endorsed the Protestant work ethic were more likely to persist at a task and to spend more time engaged in work-related activities. However, individuals who endorsed the Protestant work ethic pursued personal success simply through hard work and persistence. Chinese employees also expect to get their supervisor‟s acknowledgement (Li and Madsen, 2009). The results of our study partially support the role of Confucian work ethics in individual production deviance.

We find that the role of CWE is rather limited in certain team climates. There is no difference between the influence of a Confucian work ethic in high team climate and neutral team climates, which suggests high team climate does not enhance the impact of Confucian work ethics as we had anticipated. Individual production deviance does decrease in high team climates, but this effect results from co-worker influence rather than the expression of Confucian work ethics. One possible reason is that high team climate only means an ordinary situation which forms less pressure for individuals to engage in production deviance.

In addition, the Confucian work ethic is minimized in low team climates, which suggests that Chinese employees will choose behavior over belief when facing conflicting situations. According to Fletcher and Fang‟s (2006) viewpoint, Asian cultures are characterized by a situation-accepting orientation where people in them react in a flexible manner. The conclusion in the research reflected that keeping consistent with circumstances was the nature of Chinese social philosophy.

Meanwhile, the result suggests that relativism may be a dominating characteristic in Chinese IT employees. This analysis is consistent with the findings of Peterson (2003), which indicated that individual organizational commitment would decrease when individuals who believe in universal moral rules engage in unethical work behavior. This perceived ethical pressure does not affect organizational commitment of employees who believe ethics are relative.

## Implications for Practice

This study provides instrumental practical implications for software project team management in organizations. We bring to light the importance of team climate and co-worker influence in affecting individual group member deviance behaviors. Software development is a task that is highly complex and requires a high degree of interdependence among team members (Faraj and Yan, 2009). Co-worker deviance behavior can be spread among individual members in group settings (Glomb and Liao, 2003; Robinson and O‟Leary-Kelly, 1998), travel to outside stakeholders (Pearson and Porath, 2005), affect the morale of the immediate community (Krishnan and Singh, 2010), and may lead to low productivity of the whole team. To retain the much needed talent with the required skillsets and experience to attain sustainable competitive advantage, organizations and team leaders should be sensitive to deviance behaviors in IT teams. According to Felps and colleagues (2006), seeing others act against the legitimate interest of an organization makes those behaviors more mentally accessible and lowers an employee‟s inhibition about behaving in a similar fashion. Adequate precaution and prevention measures should be taken prior to any increase in employee deviance behavior (Arthur, 2011) to reduce the negative effects of deviance behaviors since IT team climate in groups has proven to be quite hard to modify once established (MacNeil and Sherif, 1976).

Awareness should also be raised for cultural influence in IT teams. A study by Liao et al. (2004) has shown that when individuals are dissimilar to their work groups in terms of ethnicity, they are less likely to engage in deviance behaviors. With the increased use of offshore, onsite, virtual, globally distributed and self-managed teams in software industry (Sudhakar, Farooq, and Patnaik, 2012), team leaders, who can anticipate the differences in group member national culture or work ethics norms, will be more likely to identify, predict or explain workplace deviance behaviors so that appropriate mechanisms can be installed to reduce the negative consequences of deviance behaviors within the teams.

## LIMITATIONS & FUTURE RESEARCH

There are some inevitable limitations in this study. First, the research only considered production deviance, not all types of workplace deviance behaviors. Therefore, one must use caution before applying our conclusions more generally in other business segments. In addition, the results are based only on Chinese IT employees, which limit the generalization of the findings to samples from other countries or cultures. Furthermore, we only considered one dimension of multi-dimensional Confucian values in this research. Whether other factors (e.g. integration, human-heartedness, work-family conflict, and moral discipline) and some IT-specific factors (e.g. trust, self-efficacy, motivation, and innovativeness) have an impact on production deviance or not needs to be further investigated. It is hoped that future research may further shed light on other IT areas to scientifically examine the role of workplace deviance behaviors versus ethics and IT team climate. Researchers are also recommended to conduct cross-cultural analyses on this phenomenon between Confucian and other competing cultures in IS research.

## Appendix: Survey Instrument

A five-point Likert-type scale 1 (Almost Never) to 5 (Extremely Often or Always) was used for all items.

## Individual Production Deviance

IPD1 I intentionally worked slower than I could have worked.

IPD2 I took an additional or a longer break than was acceptable at my workplace.

IPD3 I worked on a personal matter instead of work for [company name].

## Co-worker Production Deviance

CPD1 My co-workers intentionally worked slower than they could have worked.

CPD2 My co-workers took an additional or a longer break than was acceptable at their workplace.

CPD3 My co-workers worked on a personal matter instead of work for [company name].

## Confucian Work Ethic

CWE1Work carefully and seriously.

CWE2Work persistently and patiently.

CWE3Work hard without complaints.
