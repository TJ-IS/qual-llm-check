## Abstract

This paper is a companion to the paper on generalization in IS research by Williams and Tsang published in this edition of the . Its purpose is to discuss the <sup>Journal of Information Technology</sup>implications of the robust exchange of views about the meaning of the term ‘generalization’ in four papers, two by Lee and Baskerville, and two by Tsang and Williams. The objectives of this paper are, <sup>fi</sup>rst, to help the reader understand the issues by summarizing the arguments in the four papers, and second, to assess the implications of the debate for future IS research. Our conclusion is that when the papers are interpreted from the perspectives of the respective pairs of authors, most of what they say is sound. However, because their perspectives are so different, their differences of opinion are also very real. As a way of showing that neither pair of authors’ conception of generalization is the ‘last word’ on this topic, the paper also compares key concepts from both pairs of authors with those from Seddon and Scheepers. It is argued that although the Seddon and Scheepers’ framework is also not the ‘last word’, it may prove more useful than either of the two preceding frameworks.

## Introduction

his paper is a companion to the paper by Williams and Tsang (2015) published in this edition of the Journal of Information Technology (JIT). Its purpose is to discuss the implications of the robust exchange of views about Tthe meaning of the term ‘generalization’ in four papers: Lee and Baskerville (2003) (henceforth L&B03), Tsang and Williams (2012) (henceforth T&W12), Lee and Baskerville (2012) (henceforth L&B12), and Williams and Tsang (2015) (henceforth W&T15, published in this issue of the JIT).

In brief, the four papers proceeded as follows. First, L&B03 proposed the new, ‘more general’ view of generalization depicted in Figure 1 (and explained in more detail below). Their intention was that in addition to the conventional ‘statistical sampling-based’ inductive conceptualization of generalization used by positivist or natural-science researchers (p. 229), this framework could be used by both interpretive and case-study researchers to justify claims of generalizability in their work.<sup>1</sup> Second, 9 years later, T&W12 criticized L&B03’s framework saying that many of the concepts described were not generalizations at all, and proposing a number of ‘repairs’ (T&W12: 734). Third, L&B12 responded (a) saying that T&W12 had ‘misinterpret[ed] L&B in light of L&B’s own aims’ (p. 759), (b) suggesting that despite apparent differences there were really ‘no consequential differences between their 2003 framework and T&W12’s suggested framework (p. 758) (thereby implying that the ‘repairs’ were unnecessary), and (c) suggesting that T&W were guilty of attempted ‘paradigmatic domination’ (of positivists over interpretivists) (p. 760). Fourth, in the paper in the current issue of JIT, W&T15 have responded saying that there are, in fact, massive and important differences between L&B03’s and their own (T&W12) frameworks, and denying any intention of paradigmatic domination.

The core of the disagreement is simply that Tsang and Williams (T&W) believe that a number of concepts that L&B03 have labelled ‘generalization’ are not generalization. For example they say: ‘Lee and Baskerville’s de<sup>fi</sup>nition of generalization is not the sense used by researchers in natural or social sciences’ (T&W12: 733). Yet in their 2012 reply to T&W12, Lee and Baskerville (L&B) continue to argue that their framework makes sense to them.

The question that the reader of this paper is probably asking is ‘Does this difference of opinions matter to me?’ Our answer is that it does. It does because the goal of almost all IS research – including, most probably, the reader’s – is to make knowledge claims that are relevant to people or organizations other than those in the sample(s) studied. Yet as we showed in the opening pages of both Seddon and Scheepers (2006) and (2012), the treatment of generalization in the IS literature is often unsatisfactory. We believe this is due to lack of under standing of generalization in the IS research community. It is therefore hoped that greater understanding of the clash of views presented in the papers by L&B and T&W will help the reader understand better what generalizing means, and so produce higher-quality research in future. With this overarching goal in mind, the objectives of this paper are, <sup>fi</sup>rst, to help the reader understand the issues raised by L&B and T&W by summarizing the arguments in their various papers, and second, to assess the implications of the debate for researchers making generalizations in future IS research.

## Summary of the arguments in the four papers

To understand more clearly the nature of the differences between L&B03 and T&W12, it is necessary to consider L&B03’s generalization framework in Figure 1 in more depth. Using the abbreviations ‘E’ for ‘empirical’, and ‘T’ for ‘theory’, L&B03’s four types of generalization depicted in Figure 1 are EE, ET, TE, and TT. Further, since three of these have subtypes, L&B03 actually de<sup>fi</sup>ne seven types of generalization that we have labelled EE1, EE2, ET1, ET2, TE, TT1, and TT2 as follows.<sup>2</sup>

EE1: ‘The generalizability of data to a measurement, observation, or other description (such as a descriptive statistic or a thick description)’ (L&B03: 233).

EE2: ‘The generalizability of the resulting measurement, observation, or other description beyond the sample or domain from which the researcher has actually collected data (such as generalizing to the unsampled portion of the population or to the people in the corporation who were not interviewed). In either case, we regard the product of the generalizing to be a description’ (L&B03: 233).

ET1: ‘The generalizability of measurements, observations, or other descriptions to theory’ (L&B03: 236).

ET2: ‘The generalizability of the resulting theory beyond the sample or domain that the researcher observes (such as the unsampled portion of the population or the parts of the organization where the <sup>fi</sup>eld worker has neither conducted interviews nor collected data in other ways) (L&B03: 236).

TE: ‘Generalizing from theoretical statements (in particular, a theory that has already been developed, tested, and con<sup>fi</sup>rmed, such as one reported in a published journal article) to empirical statements (here, descriptions of what the practitioner can expect to observe in his speci<sup>fi</sup>c organization if he were to apply the theory) (L&B03: 237).

TT1: ‘A researcher generalizes from theoretical propositions in the form of concepts (such as a variable, an a priori construct, or another concept) to the theoretical propositions that make up a theory (speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, a set of logically consistent propositions that, pending the results of empirical testing, could qualify as a theory) (L&B03: 238).

TT2: ‘The formulation of a theory based on the synthesis of ideas from a literature review’ (L&B03: 238).

Note that each de<sup>fi</sup>nition above is an exact quotation from L&B03. Of these, T&W12 say: ‘Lee and Baskerville go on to mischaracterize each of the so-called four types of generalization, even within their own terms. In particular, only what they call ‘Type ET generalizability’ is really generalization (p. 735).<sup>3</sup> In other words, from T&W12’s perspective, ET1 and ET2 are generalizations, but EE1, EE2, TE, TT1, and TT2 are not. This, in turn, led them to propose the various ‘repairs’ presented in T&W12. Those repairs are very nicely summarized in T&W12’s Table 2, pp. 738–739.

For their part, in the third paper in the series, L&B12 politely disagree (see summary above, including the claim about ‘paradigmatic domination’). They also emphasize that their goal in proposing their framework was to ‘liberate’ casestudy and interpretive researchers from the con<sup>fi</sup>nes of the widely accepted positivist view of generalization. This liberation idea is expressed in L&B03 as follows:

Type EE generalizability nonetheless provides a useful perspective for exposing the very limited realm where statistical, sampling-based generalizability is relevant. Not only is statistical, sampling-based generalizability a subset of Type EE generalizability, but also, Type EE generalizability itself is, in turn, a subset of the overall generalizability framework (as presented in Figure 5), where it is one of four types of generalizability. The framework is therefore useful for, among other things, summarily demonstrating that statistical, sampling-based generalizability is not a general form of generalizability. It is a special case of generalizability. For many IS researchers, such as those identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in Table 1 above, this demonstration has the bene<sup>fi</sup>t of liberating them to take advantage of additional forms of generalizability.

(L&B03: 235)

L&B’s comments in L&B12, together with the above quotation, suggest that the reason that L&B and T&W have different de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of ‘generalization’ is that L&B tried to integrate both positivist and interpretivist views of generalization into their framework, whereas L&B believe that T&W have clung to the more narrow ‘positivist’ perspective that L&B associate with the term ‘statistical sampling-based generalization’.

With respect to trying to integrate many different views of generalization, we believe that L&B are correct. Irrespective of the merits of their framework, L&B03 were trying to create an integrated framework suitable for use by researchers of all epistemological persuasions. However, in their response to L&B12, W&T15 say that their criticisms are not driven by a positivist mindset, but rather, are made because many of L&B03’s types of generalization are so far from any conventional meaning of the term as to be unhelpful:

We objected to their account of generalization, arguing that their de<sup>fi</sup>nition of it is not the sense used by researchers in natural or social sciences, is too narrow and is contradicted by their own characterization of it.

(W&T15: 19).

## Our interpretation of L&B03’s generalization framework

To help the reader decide whether L&B’s various different conceptualizations of generalization are helpful, we now present our own interpretation of L&B03’s framework (which is not the same as T&W’s interpretation). Our discussion is organized around the concepts in Figure 2. In the central half of this <sup>fi</sup>gure, we show what we have termed L&B’s seven types of generalization, EE1, EE2, etc., with de<sup>fi</sup>nitions as presented in the quotations above. In addition, we have annotated the <sup>fi</sup>gure with six comments about L&B’s framework. These comments have been placed in the six square-cornered rectangles to the left and right of the central framework, and numbered 1–6. We now discuss each comment in turn.

Our <sup>fi</sup>rst comment is that EE1 generalization is a concept that appears to be consistent with L&B’s overarching de<sup>fi</sup>nition of generalization from the Oxford English Dictionary (OED), which is ‘to form general notions by abstraction from particular instances’ (L&B03: 221). For example, the mean of a set of measurements is an abstraction from those measurements. Equally, a thick description of a case study is an abstraction from the case. For example (building on the Geertz example in L&B03: 230–231), Geertz’s (1956) so-called ‘thick’ description of insights about religious practices in a town with pseudonym Modjokuto, in Java, Indonesia, is an abstraction from many conversations and observations of practices in the town over 2.5 years. So our conclusion is that EE1 generalization (abstracting from particular instances) is a legitimate form of generalization that L&B have every right to try to include in their integrated multi-de<sup>fi</sup>nition framework. EE1 generalization is not what is typically meant by a research generalization in the conventional or natural-science tradition, but that does not make it wrong. Furthermore, it may be useful for some case-study researchers to be able to describe their own thick descriptions as generalizations.<sup>4</sup> We have indicated that this EE1 concept of generalization is acceptable in what is deliberately a multi-meaning generalization framework by placing a tick (check mark) beside comment 1 in Figure 2.

Six comments (in the square-cornered rectangles) on Lee and Baskerville’s (2003) generalization framework (their Figure 5).

Our second comment (see top right of Figure 2) is that, as L&B03 explain on pp. 235–237, ET1 and ET2 generalization correspond to what Yin (2003: 31) calls ‘generalizing from case study to theory’. We argue later that it is probably more helpful to call ET1 ‘theory building’, not generalization, but L&B are correct in pointing out that many researchers use Yin’s terminology. Therefore, despite our reservations about the value of the term ‘generalizing to theory’, this second conception of generalization, ET generalization (which includes both ET1 and ET2 generalization), also has a legitimate place in L&B’s attempt to integrate many different views of generalization in their framework. We have indicated that this meaning is acceptable in a multi-meaning generalization framework by placing a tick (check mark) beside comment 2 in Figure 2.

Third, L&B03’s EE2 and ET2 generalization, which both involve generalizing knowledge claims ‘beyond the sample or domain from which the researcher has actually collected data’ (L&B03: 233), correspond to the conventional inductive-infer ence conceptualization of generalization that T&W12 and many readers of this paper endorse (and which L&B03 describe in the <sup>fi</sup>rst three sentences of their paper and in the quotation from Kolakowski in the preceding endnote). Therefore, this third conception of generalization also has a legitimate place in L&B’s attempt to integrate many different views of generalization in their framework. We have indicated that this meaning is acceptable in a multi-meaning generalization framework by placing a tick (check mark) beside comment 3 in Figure 2.

Fourth, L&B03 classify ‘statistical, sampling based generalization’ (which they also often call simply ‘statistical generalization’) as a type of EE generalization.<sup>5</sup> In our classi<sup>fi</sup>cation in Figure 2, it falls under the heading EE2 generalization. In pp. 224–228, L&B03 provide detailed argument to the effect that:

The conclusion to our critique is that we af<sup>fi</sup>rm the legitimacy of statistical generalizability, where we emphasize that it refers to the generalizability of one random sample to other random samples that would result from applying the same sampling procedure to the same population or the generalizability of sample points to a sample estimate.

(L&B03: 228)

The view expressed in this sentence (that statistical generalization involves ‘generalizability of one random sample to other random samples’ (emphasis added)) differs from the view of many statisticians and researchers, which is that statistical generalization involves the use of inferential statistics to make inferences about the population from which the sample was drawn (where the P-value is the probability that a sample with the properties observed could have been drawn from a population where the null hypothesis was true). However, both L&B03’s meaning (just quoted) and the conventional view of inferential statistics seem to us to imply induction, because both involve making claims about members of the population that one has not observed. Further, since statistical generalization is a widely practiced logic for generalization (and L&B03 equate EE2 and statistical generalization) their EE2 generalization certainly belongs in any generalization framework. We have indicated that this EE2 meaning is acceptable in a multi-meaning generalization framework by placing a tick (check mark) beside comment 4 in Figure 2.

Fifth, as T&W12 point out, L&B03’s TE generalization is inconsistent with L&B03’s own de<sup>fi</sup>nition of generalization. L&B03 acknowledge this themselves when they say that TE generalization: ‘is actually deduction, not induction. Indeed, such reasoning is the opposite of the OED de<sup>fi</sup>nition of generalize’ (p. 241). L&B03 justify inclusion of TE generalization in their framework on the grounds that prior researchers have described TE generalization as generalization. However, the prior researchers they cite on p. 237 are actually Lee himself (Lee, 1989) and a paper that Lee (1989) cites, namely, Campbell (1975). Further, by our reading of Lee (1989), the concept discussed on 134 of that paper is NOT the same as TE generalizing.<sup>6</sup> Based on both its incongruence with the OED de<sup>fi</sup>nition and the lack of ANY evidence of prior claims that this concept is a type of generalization, the grounds for inclusion of TE generalization in L&B03’s framework are very weak. We have indicated that this meaning is to our minds NOT acceptable even in a multi-meaning generalization framework by placing a cross beside comment 5 in Figure 2.

Sixth and <sup>fi</sup>nally, L&B03’s TT1 and TT2 generalization, which both involve generalizing knowledge claims from prior researchers’ knowledge claims, seem to us to be valid. Many literature reviews (and meta analyses) seek to synthesize insights from prior research. The synthesizing process is similar to the sense-making process undertaken by case-study researchers as they attempt to ‘generalize to theory’. As noted in comment 1, we would prefer to call this sense-making process ‘theory building’, not generalizing, but given both the wide-spread use of Yin’s (1994, 2003) term ‘generalizing to theory’ (e.g., Gillham, 2005; Ariño and Ring, 2010) and the fact that a synthesis is an abstraction from all the detail in the papers reviewed, these <sup>fi</sup>nal two conceptions of generalization (TT1 and TT2) also have a legitimate place in L&B’s attempt to integrate many different researchers’ views of generalization in their framework. We have indicated that this meaning is acceptable in a multi-meaning generalization framework by placing a tick (check mark) beside comment 6 in Figure 2.

Summarizing, our conclusion is that L&B03’s framework enables them to depict many different ways that prior published work has used or might have used the term ‘generalization’. Further, we believe that the <sup>fi</sup>ve meanings with ticks (check marks) in Figure 2 are acceptable (i.e., have a legitimate place) in a framework where the authors goal is to highlight different meanings of generalization. Whether these <sup>fi</sup>ve concepts are likely to be helpful (or ‘liberating’) to IS researchers, either positivist or interpretivist, is another matter. We explain our position on this question later, in the section headed ‘Looking forward’.

Finally, it is important to add that the above six comments re<sup>fl</sup>ect our interpretation of L&B’s writing. L&B03 is an exceedingly complex paper to read, so despite very careful reading and re-reading, we may have misinterpreted it. For example, one possible misinterpretation is that although we concluded that TE generalization is not generalization, L&B12 devoted a full <sup>fi</sup>ve pages (pp.750–754) under the heading ‘Generalizing a Theory to a New Setting’ to a strong defence of TE generalization. So despite our negative conclusion, they clearly believe the concept is a valuable inclusion in their framework.

## How valid are T&W12’s criticisms of L&B03?

As explained in the Introduction, T&W (T&W12 and W&T15) have arrived at very different conclusions to ours, above, about the validity of L&B03’s framework. In this section we ask if T&W12’s criticisms are valid.

T&W’s two papers (as well as Tsang’s (2014) paper on generalizing from case study research, recently accepted by the Journal of Strategic Information Systems) provide some very clear, logical de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of what they understand the terms ‘generalization’ and ‘induction’ to mean. Those de<sup>fi</sup>nitions, along with some additional comments from both them and us on other concepts in their generalization framework, are shown in Table 1. Note that in Table 1, definitions quoted from T&W12 have been labelled D1 and D2, other comments quoted from T&W12 have been labelled TWC1 and TWC2, and our comments have been labelled OC1, OC2, …, OC8. Further note that the material in Table 1 could have been presented as a series of six (quite complex) paragraphs. We hope that the tabular presentation makes the various points, and who said them, clearer.

Using the de<sup>fi</sup>nitions and concepts in Table 1, T&W conclude, correctly, that many concepts in L&B03’s framework (see Figure 1) are not compatible with their own de<sup>fi</sup>nition of generalization. Their criticisms of L&B03 are nicely summarized in W&T15 as follows:

We objected to their account of generalization, arguing that their de<sup>fi</sup>nition of it is not the sense used by researchers in natural or social sciences, is too narrow and is contradicted by their own characterization of it. We showed that their de<sup>fi</sup>nition of ‘empirical statement’ is circular and that their de<sup>fi</sup>nition of ‘theoretical statement’ is too narrow. We argued that EE generalization in fact refers to deduction and validating measurement instruments; ET generalization misuses the term ‘theory’; their de<sup>fi</sup>nition of TE generalization contradicts their own de<sup>fi</sup>nition of generalization and TE generalization refers to empirical testing; their discussion of TT generalization is not coherent, is internally inconsistent and is irrelevant to generalization as most usually understood. We offered repairs to all of the above problems.

(W&T15: 19)

T&W12’s Table 2, pp. 738–739, provides some excellent suggestions for ‘repairing’ L&B’s arguments should L&B wish to adopt T&W’s de<sup>fi</sup>nition of generalization. However, as we know from both L&B03 and L&B12, this is contrary to L&B’s purpose.

From L&B12’s perspective, T&W12 ‘misinterpret[ed] L&B in light of L&B’s own aims’ (p. 759). This comment is consistent with our own analysis, above. As we understand L&B03, L&B were trying to construct a framework that (a) incorporated multiple different de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of generalization that have been used (or might have been used) in the literature, especially Yin’s (2003) concept of generalizing to theory, and (b) was not built on a foundation of induction (due to their desire to avoid what they, following Campbell and Stanley (1963), call ‘Hume’s truism’).<sup>7</sup> T&W12 seem to have decided that these goals were unhelpful.

## How should L&B12’s reply to T&W12 be interpreted?

In their reply to T&W12, L&B12 essentially said: ‘Our framework makes sense to us. Despite a very careful reading of T&W12, we see no reason to change anything’. (These are our words, not theirs.) In the course of saying this they made two claims that surprised us, both concerning what they describe as the ‘remarkable [level of] agreement between T&W and L&B’ (p. 754). The <sup>fi</sup>rst is that ‘First and foremost, there is agreement between L&B and T&W that induction is necessary and useful’ (p. 754). The second is that ‘Pragmatically speaking, there are no consequential differences between the different types of generalizing that L&B and T&W end up de<sup>fi</sup>ning’ (p. 758). In this section, we focus on those two surprises.

L&B12’s <sup>fi</sup>rst claim, that ‘induction is necessary and useful’ (L&B12: 754), surprised us because L&B03 devoted 20% of their 21-page paper (pp. 224–228) to discussing ‘Hume’s truism’. In that section, they argued that induction or generalization is never fully justified logically (p. 224). They then made 10 further references to Hume’s truism throughout the remainder of their paper.<sup>9</sup> This heavy emphasis on Hume’s truism led us to believe that L&B03 had tried to exclude any inductive-based concept of generalization from their framework. So our <sup>fi</sup>rst surprise was that L&B actually believed that induction was necessary and useful.<sup>10</sup>

Our second surprise was when we read in L&B12 that ‘Pragmatically speaking, there are no consequential differences between the different types of generalizing that L&B and T&W end up de<sup>fi</sup>ning’ (p. 758). After reading and re-reading both L&B03 and T&W12, our conclusion was (as reported above)

that L&B03 had attempted to create an inclusive framework that deliberately included a range of different meanings for ‘generalization’, whereas T&W12 had adopted a much narrower de<sup>fi</sup>nition of ‘generalization’ (see their de<sup>fi</sup>nition in our Table 1). If this is true, how could there be ‘no consequential differences’ between the two frameworks?

Closer comparison of the two frameworks reveals that there are a number of super<sup>fi</sup>cial similarities, but deeper down most of these similarities dissolve. The apparent similarities are that (a) T&W12’s analytic generalization (see our Figure 3) is very similar to L&B03’s ET generalization (and Yin’s (1994, 2003) ‘generalizing to theory’), (b) T&W12’s four types of empirical generalization share some similarities to L&B03’s EE2 generalization, and (c) T&W12’s statistical syllogism (see their Figure 1 and de<sup>fi</sup>nition in their Table 3, p. 741) shares some resemblance to L&B03’s TE generalization. However, at a more fundamental level, the similarity between T&W12’s and L&B03’s frameworks dissolves. First, T&W12’s empirical generalization is not the same as L&B03’s EE2 generalization because T&W12 accept inductive generalization to populations, yet L&B03 do not. Second, T&W12 do not accept that TE generalization is generalization (because the process for arriving at such ‘generalizations from theory’ is deductive, not inductive). Third, as noted above, T&W12 have no concept comparable to L&B03’s TT generalization.

In short, our conclusion is that there are consequential (i.e., major, important, and fundamental) differences between L&B03 and T&W12. Furthermore, W&T15 agree (see next section). In fact, W&T15 devote 30% of the body of their paper (in the section headed ‘Why Lee and Baskerville’s account of induction and of generalization is incompatible with ours’) to explaining why they believe that the differences are consequential.

## Williams and Tsang (2015)

In their short paper in this edition of JIT, W&T15: (a) provide some relevant de<sup>fi</sup>nitions, (b) explain why their views of generalization are NOT compatible with L&B03, (c) explain why they believe that L&B’s use of the word ‘generalization’ ‘is seriously <sup>fl</sup>awed’ (p. 16), (d) argue that they are not guilty of attempted paradigmatic domination, and (e) provide an explanation of how L&B03 have misunderstood Hume’s arguments (Appendix A). The general gist of what they say is consistent with our comments above.

Perhaps the only place where we believe W&T15 are inaccurate is on p. 1 where they seem to equate ‘inductive generalization’ and ‘statistical generalization’: ‘One form of induction, among others, is what is known in logic as “inductive generalization” or in the literature on information systems as “statistical generalization” ’ (W&T15: 18). Our view is that not all inductive generalizations are statistical. We think this because interpretive researchers also make inductive generalizations. For example, when anthropologist Geertz, mentioned earlier, wrote the book ‘The Religion of Java (1960) he appears to have made claims about the whole of Java based on conversations and observations of practices in one town, Modjokuto, in Java, Indonesia, over 2.5 years in the early 1950s. If so, these claims were inductive generalizations, yet they were not statistical.

## Wrapping up

The four papers that we have just reviewed have raised many questions about how the term ‘generalization’ should be understood and used in future IS research. Based on the above analysis, it seems clear that L&B03 were proposing a new generalization framework that included many different meanings. L&B03’s goal seems to have been to help case-study and interpretive researchers defend themselves from the charge that ‘you cannot generalize from a single case’. Their approach is similar to Gregor’s (2006) in her paper on the meaning of the term ‘theory’. In that paper, she documented and organized many different, yet widely accepted, de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of the concept ‘theory’, but endorsed none. Also, in formulating their framework, L&B03 built a strong case that inductive generalizations are ‘never fully justi<sup>fi</sup>ed logically’ (L&B03: 225).<sup>11</sup> Our conclusions are that (a) we agree with L&B03 that inductive generalizations are ‘never fully justi<sup>fi</sup>ed logically’, (b) L&B03 did, indeed, include a number of different de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of ‘generalization’ in their framework, and (c) we do not believe that TE generalization is generalization (see comment 5 in our Figure 2). However, despite L&B03’s claims that their framework (our Figure 1) ‘liberates’ case-study and interpretive researchers, it is not actually clear to us how their framework (our Figure 1) offers much support for such researchers over and above its endorsement of Yin’s (1994, 2003) concept of ‘generalizing to theory’.<sup>12</sup> Finally, from our perspective, L&B did IS researchers a service by initiating a much-needed discussion of generalization practices in IS research.

T&W did not see much merit in L&B03’s goal of integrating many different views of generalization. For them, the only relevant meaning for the term ‘generalization’ in a research context is that generalization means ‘For any two statements P and Q, Q is more general than P when and only when the class of entities that P is about is a subset of the class of entities that Q is about’ (T&W12: 734, also quoted in Table 1). From this perspective, they pointed out many of the con<sup>fl</sup>icts and inconsistencies embodied in the seven different views that L&B chose to include in their framework. From our perspective, T&W did IS researchers a service by showing that many of L&B03’s concepts do not accord with the conventional inductive meaning of generalization (captured in T&W12’s de<sup>fi</sup>nition quoted in this paragraph). This may not have been clear to a casual reader of L&B03.

The subsequent papers, L&B12 and W&T15, have merely served to reiterate the respective authors’ positions. JIT has done IS researchers a service by allowing W&T15 to explain to the world that T&W strongly disagree with L&B12’s comment that ‘there are no consequential differences between the different types of generalizing that L&B and T&W end up de<sup>fi</sup>ning’ (L&B12: 758).

## Looking forward

To this point in this paper we have focused on the ideas of L&B and T&W. This is consistent with our brief from the Editors in Chief of JIT, which was to help readers understand the exchange of views between L&B and T&W. However, we do not want to leave readers with the view that they simply have to choose between L&B and T&W; there are many other valuable ways of conceptualizing generalization. Since our second goal for this paper is to provide readers with a set of concepts that they can use in their own discussions and justi<sup>fi</sup>cations of research generalizations, either as authors or reviewers, we now extend the scope of the paper by considering a number of important aspects of generalization not addressed by either L&B or T&W. We have drawn most of these ideas from our most recent paper on generalization, namely Seddon and Scheepers (2012) (henceforth S&S12). A number of similar arguments are presented in Compeau et al. (2012) (who cite frequently Seddon and Scheepers (2006)).

Since the focus of this paper is on the arguments of L&B and T&W, and readers have ready access to S&S12, we have not attempted to summarize S&S12’s arguments here. However, we believe that the following six points are helpful for understanding many of the issues that L&B and T&W have grappled with, and that the generalization framework offered by S&S12 may prove more useful, going forward, than either of the two frameworks reviewed above. Consistent with the goals of L&B03, all six points are relevant to both quantitative and qualitative researchers.

## The type of generalization of interest to most IS researchers is generalization to other settings

In our own thinking about the role of generalization in IS research, we have come to the conclusion that although the term ‘generalization’ has many meanings, and has been used by researchers in a number of those ways, the type of generalization that matters most to IS researchers is of empirical research <sup>fi</sup>ndings to other settings. This is consistent with T&W12’s position. As evidence to support our view, (a) all 12 of the examples of the ‘Misapplication of the Concept of Statistical Generalizability in IS Research’ that L&B03: 223, used to motivate their paper are concerned with generalization to other settings, and (b) three of L&B’s seven types of generalization in our Figure 2 (EE2, ET2, and TE) are about the transferability of <sup>fi</sup>ndings from one or more research studies to other settings. Generalization to other settings is important because the goal of most IS research is to make knowledge claims that are relevant to people or organizations other than those in the samples studied.

For example, when Lacity and Willcocks (1998) analysed interviews with 145 ‘business executives, chief information of<sup>fi</sup>cers, outsourcing consultants, and vendor account managers’ (p. 364) who had been involved in ‘61 IT sourcing decisions made in 40 US and UK organizations during the period 1991 to 1995’ (p. 363) and identi<sup>fi</sup>ed <sup>fi</sup>ve ‘best practices such as selective sourcing, joint business-and-IT sourcing decision making, etc. (p. 363), the implication of their paper was that (all?) other <sup>fi</sup>rms in the US, UK, and probably elsewhere, would also bene<sup>fi</sup>t by using practices that appeared to have led to success within the 61 decisions studied. In other words, they generalized from their sample of 61 decisions in 40 <sup>fi</sup>rms to something like ‘all large developed-economy <sup>fi</sup>rms’.

L&B would probably describe Lacity and Willcocks’ (1998) claims as EE2 or ET2 generalizations. T&W would certainly describe them as generalizations.<sup>13</sup> We believe that othersettings generalizations such as these are the ones that matter to IS researchers. We have no problem with L&B proposing a framework such as that in Figure 1 that includes many other <sup>fl</sup>avours of generalization. However, we believe that the type of generalization that most IS researchers are interested in is whether the research <sup>fi</sup>ndings in a given empirical study are likely to apply elsewhere (and if so, where). Thus in S&S12 we de<sup>fi</sup>ned the generalizations of interest to us as follows:

A research generalization is the researcher’s act of arguing, by induction, that there is a reasonable expectation that a knowledge claim already believed to be true in one or more settings is also true in other clearly de<sup>fi</sup>ned settings.<sup>14</sup>

(S&S12: 7)

In addition to being consistent with T&W’s de<sup>fi</sup>nition, this de<sup>fi</sup>nition also introduces some additional concepts – (a) the need for argument, (b) induction, (c) reasonable expectation, (d) knowledge claim, and (e) ‘believed to be true in one or more settings’ – that we think are important in a well-rounded understanding of research generalizations in IS research.

Knowledge building, including generalization, is a cumulative, social process. The research community’s con<sup>fi</sup>dence in knowledge claims grows as more and more evidence supporting knowledge claims is produced by different studies

In their concept of TT2 generalization, ‘the formulation of a theory based on the synthesis of ideas from a literature review (L&B03: 238), L&B03 remind readers of the process by which the world’s stock of knowledge is sifted, sorted, synthesized, and pruned by the research community. But the process of global knowledge building is broader and more fundamental than just the ‘formulation of a theory’ that L&B03 describe. What actually happens is that as successive studies present additional empirical evidence supporting their knowledge claims, the world’s degree of con<sup>fi</sup>dence in those claims increases or decreases depending on the strength of evidence offered in each study.

For example, when Galileo (1610) discovered the moons of Jupiter in January 1610, he had observed that three ‘stars’ seemed to move day-by-day with Jupiter relative to the other stars, yet to change their relative positions on successive nights. He explained this phenomenon abductively (Peirce, 1903) by hypothesizing that the three ‘stars’ were actually moons orbiting Jupiter. At the time, his con<sup>fi</sup>dence in the truth of this claim was probably not strong. However, the cumulative evidence in the 400 years since then means that today the US National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) is highly con<sup>fi</sup>dent when it says that ‘Astronomers have found at least 146 moons orbiting planets in our solar system. Another 27 moons are awaiting of<sup>fi</sup>cial con<sup>fi</sup>rmation of their discovery’ (NASA, 2014). The current debate about the extent to which humans are or are not responsible for global warming is another example of this sifting-and-sortingof-evidence process in action.

From this perspective, (a) all human knowledge building, including generalization, is a cumulative, social process, and (b) all human knowledge claims, including generalizations, are subject to revision. These concepts are missing from both L&B’s and T&W’s frameworks, but we think they are fundamental to sound understanding of generalization.

A corollary to the previous point is that what L&B03 called ‘Hume’s truism’ is a red herring. L&B03’s discussion of Hume’s truism emphasizes that ‘induction or generalization is never fully justi<sup>fi</sup>ed logically’ (L&B03: 225). This is undoubtedly true. T&W12’s ‘solution’ to the problem of Hume’s truism was to argue ‘that there is some solution to Hume’s problem, even if we do not know what the solution is’ (p. 732). On this point, we disagree. Like Popper (2002a, b), we do not believe there can ever be a solution to this problem. But despite both L&B’s and T&W’s concerns, we believe that Hume’s truism poses no problems for empirical researchers. It is not a problem because the black-and-white (True–False) truth standards of formal logic set an inappropriate benchmark for assessing the truth of any human knowledge. If one accepts that all human knowledge is bounded, that nothing that humans claim to know is known for certain, and that all knowledge claims are subject to revision, no human knowledge meets the truth standard of formal logic. This is why ‘Hume’s truism’ is a red herring.

## Justi<sup>fi</sup>cations of generalizations to other settings are simpli<sup>fi</sup>ed if researchers talk in terms of their degree of con<sup>fi</sup>dence in bounded knowledge claims

Adopting the preceding position (i.e., that all human knowledge is bounded, that nothing that humans claim to know is known for certain, and that all knowledge claims are subject to revision), we suggested in S&S12 that all research-based knowledge claims should be quali<sup>fi</sup>ed by two attributes: (a) the degree of con<sup>fi</sup>dence currently associated with the claim, and (b) a clear statement of the set of things to which the claim applies.

(a) With respect to the degree of con<sup>fi</sup>dence, as new evidence emerges that supports a knowledge claim, our degree of con<sup>fi</sup>dence in that knowledge claim rises (and vice versa). The nature of this con<sup>fi</sup>dence-revision process has been studied intensively by Bayesian statisticians. Today,

Bayesian logic (Berger, 2003; Goodman, 2005) provides a clear, powerful language for discussing one’s changing degrees of con<sup>fi</sup>dence in a given knowledge claim as new evidence is acquired. Further, although Bayesian logic is usually used in a statistical context, the underlying concepts are equally applicable to analytic generalization.

(b) In addition to thinking of all knowledge claims as having a ‘Degree of Con<sup>fi</sup>dence’ attribute associated with them, it is also helpful to think of all knowledge claims as having a clearly de<sup>fi</sup>ned scope, or boundary conditions. This is not a new idea. Gregor (2006, Table 3, p. 620), for instance, included Scope as one of four components common to all theories.<sup>15</sup> To illustrate, Lacity and Willcocks’ (1998) paper, mentioned earlier, would have been stronger if it had clearly speci<sup>fi</sup>ed the set of <sup>fi</sup>rms to which the authors believed their best practices were likely to apply. Lacity and Willcocks often wrote as if their knowledge claims were general, but never said so explicitly.<sup>16</sup> (The same could be said of many other authors, including the 12 highlighted by L&B03: 223.) Thus we argue that it would be helpful if all knowledge claims were to have an attribute called Scope that speci<sup>fi</sup>es boundary conditions for the likely truth of the claim.

The Degree of Con<sup>fi</sup>dence and Scope attributes are particularly helpful for researchers who wish to distinguish between knowledge claims about a sample (which we call local knowl edge claims) and knowledge claims about the corresponding population, or beyond. Survey researchers reporting path coef<sup>fi</sup>cients, for instance, are no doubt 100% con<sup>fi</sup>dent that their path coef<sup>fi</sup>cients are true for the sample of data they collected. However, their con<sup>fi</sup>dence that the same coef<sup>fi</sup>cients describe the population from which the sample was drawn (which is where inferential statistics such as t-statistics and P-values enter the picture) is much, much lower. (Often the knowledge claim is simply that the path coef<sup>fi</sup>cient is ‘statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly’ different from zero.) This example shows that expanding the scope of a knowledge claim from the sample to the population reduces everyone’s con<sup>fi</sup>dence in those claims. The same is true when generalizing from case studies.

Although we have found that framing all discussions of generalizability in terms of knowledge claims with these two attributes (Degree of Con<sup>fi</sup>dence and Scope) leads to a huge simpli<sup>fi</sup>cation of the discussion, these concepts are largely missing from L&B and T&W. ‘Degree of Con<sup>fi</sup>dence’ is missing from both L&B03’s and T&W12’s frameworks. Scope is captured in T&W12’s framework for empirical generalization (which recognizes temporal as well as population scope), but it is missing from their concept of theoretical generalization. This is a <sup>fi</sup>rst reason why we argued earlier that there may be other, more useful, generalization frameworks than either L&B03’s or T&W12’s.

Both L&B03’s and T&W12’s generalization frameworks rely heavily on Yin’s (1994, 2003) complex concept of ‘generalizing to theory’ Building on the work of Yin (1994, 2003), a fundamental concept in both L&B03’s and T&W12’s generalization frameworks is ‘generalizing to theory’. This concept is particularly relevant to theory-building research, for example, that described by Eisenhardt (1989). However, we <sup>fi</sup>nd it helpful not to think of theories as the products of generalization.

Rather, as argued in S&S12, we think it is clearer to treat Yin’s ‘generalizing to theory’ as a combination of two steps, <sup>fi</sup>rst, local theory building, and second, generalizing.

By ‘local theory’ we mean theory that is intended to describe and explain only the phenomena actually observed, that is, where the scope of the knowledge claim is restricted just to the sample. For example, when Galileo (1610) formulated the knowledge claim that ‘Jupiter has moons’ (see example above), he was undertaking local theory building. This local theory explained the movements of the ‘stars’ (the points of light in the night sky) that he had observed over the three previous days. As explained earlier, the logic underpinning local-theory formulation is abductive, not inductive. It is not generalization as we de<sup>fi</sup>ned it earlier, nor as T&W12 de<sup>fi</sup>ned it (Table 1).

Once a local theory has been formulated and justi<sup>fi</sup>ed to a researcher’s satisfaction, the question of that theory’s generalizability to other settings may be addressed. For example, if Galileo combined his local theory about Jupiter with his knowledge of the moon around planet Earth, he might have induced a more general knowledge claim such as ‘all planets may have moons’. This knowledge claim is a generalization from his sample of two planets (Jupiter and Earth), because its scope is the entire solar system. Note that T&W12’s de<sup>fi</sup>nition of generalization, ‘For any two statements P and Q, Q is more general than P when and only when the class of entities that P is about is a subset of the class of entities that Q is about’ (T&W12: 734), provides an excellent description of the relationship between the local knowledge claims (P), about Jupiter and Earth, and the more general claim (Q), about moons in the solar system.

The key points we are trying make in this section are that (a) only the second half of Yin’s ‘generalizing to theory’ is actually generalization as de<sup>fi</sup>ned both in T&W12 and in this paper, and (b) adopting this two-step interpretation of ‘generalizing to theory’ is incompatible with both L&B03’s and T&W12’s generalization frameworks. We now explain point (b). First, L&B03’s entire 2×2 framework (see our Figure 1) is built around the concepts of EE, ET, TE, and TT generalization. The fundamental concept underpinning this framework is that it is meaningful to generalize both to, and from, theory (T). In this framework, what we have called ET1 generalizing is ‘generalizing to theory’. If the <sup>fi</sup>rst of the two steps in generalizing to theory (the local theory-building step) is removed from ET1 generalizing (because it is not generalization), the remaining concept reduces to either T&W12’s generalization or our concept of generalizing to other settings. Since ET1 generalization is fundamental to L&B03’s EE, ET, TE, and TT framework, their whole framework collapses when this pillar is removed. Therefore, L&B03’s framework is incompatible with the view that ‘generalizing to theory consists of two steps: local theory building then generalization. Second, T&W12’s framework (see our Figure 3) makes a fundamental distinction between theoretical and empirical generalizations. It even de<sup>fi</sup>nes theories as generalizations: ‘a statement is theoretical when and only when it is a generalization that purports to predict and explain the phenomena to which it refers’ (p. 735).<sup>17</sup> If the <sup>fi</sup>rst of the two steps in generalizing to theory (the local theory-building step) is removed from T&W12’s theoretical generalizing (because it is not generalization), the primary distinction in their framework, between theoretical and empirical-level generalization (see Figure 3) evaporates; it simply ceases to exist. Therefore,

T&W12’s framework (our Figure 3) is also incompatible with the view that ‘generalizing to theory’ consists of two steps: local theory building then generalization.

Summarizing, the concept ‘generalizing to theory’ is fundamental to both L&B03’s and T&W12’s generalization frameworks, yet we <sup>fi</sup>nd the term confusing because it combines two logical processes, <sup>fi</sup>rst abduction, then induction, as if they are one. In the interests of clarity, we prefer to restrict the term ‘generalization’ only to the second step, the inductive one (which is precisely consistent with T&W12’s de<sup>fi</sup>nition of generalization, in Table 1). The fact that both L&B03’s and T&W12’s generalization frameworks rely so heavily on what, for us, is an unnecessary complex, compound concept (i.e., generalizing to theory), is a second reason why we argued earlier that there may be other, more useful, generalization frameworks than either L&B03’s or T&W12’s.

## Representativeness of the sample is a key consideration when

generalizing knowledge claims from samples to target populations Neither L&B nor T&W discuss how generalizations are made or justi<sup>fi</sup>ed, yet knowing how to justify a generalization claim is arguably as important as having a clear de<sup>fi</sup>nition (or de<sup>fi</sup>nitions) of what generalization means. The problem in making generalizations is that context is often critical in understanding a phenomenon of interest – particularly in organizational settings, for example, see Pettigrew (1985a, b, 1990) – yet in generalizing, one is deliberately choosing to ignore many aspects of context.

The concept that we have found most helpful for justifying generalizations is representativeness. However, representativeness is a particularly dif<sup>fi</sup>cult concept to de<sup>fi</sup>ne. In S&S12, we ended up saying (weakly) that:

The intuition behind what is meant by a ‘representative sample... is that in a quantitative study: ‘For the variables relevant to the study, statistics calculated from a representative sample are similar to those of the population from which the sample was selected’. In a case-study context, this becomes: ‘Cause-and-effect relationships in representative case studies are similar to those in the population from which the case studies were selected or for which general ization claims are made or implied’.

(S&S12: 12)

Four key points to consider when trying to build general ization-justi<sup>fi</sup>cation arguments on the grounds of representativeness are that (a) random samples are not necessarily representative samples (which is why some researchers use strati<sup>fi</sup>ed-random samples), (b) a given sample may be representative for some generalizations, yet not others, (c) representativeness needs to be demonstrated for the key concepts in the knowledge claim being generalized, and (d) generalization is always a judgement call – backed by analytic argument – because there is always uncertainty about the extent to which one’s sample is representative of the population of interest.

A much-discussed threat to representativeness is nonresponse bias. As Pinsonneault and Kraemer (1993), King and He (2005), and Sivo et al. (2006) have pointed out, few, if any, surveys in IS research achieve a 100% response rate. Such non-response threatens the soundness of generalization claims from surveys because those who choose not to respond to a survey may have views that differ from those of the survey respondents. This means that the sample collected may not be representative of the target population.

S&S12 contains a detailed discussion of the meaning and importance of representativeness, and a series of examples such as generalizing from a study conducted in one country to other national settings, generalizing from old data to the present, etc. Compeau et al. (2012) discuss generalizations from student subjects. Although T&W12 show generalizations of these types in the lower half of their generalization framework (see our Figure 3 and their pp. 740–742), the absence of detailed discussion of representativeness in either L&B03 or T&W12 is a third reason why we argued earlier that there may be other, more useful, generalization frameworks than either L&B03’s or T&W12’s.

## Critical realism’s ‘mechanisms’ are a powerful lens for justifying analytic generalizations

Just as Bayesian logic (Berger, 2003; Goodman, 2005) provides a clear and powerful language for discussing why consistent <sup>fi</sup>ndings from multiple studies lead to increased con<sup>fi</sup>dence in research-based knowledge claims, so Bhaskhar’s Critical Realism (Collier, 1994; Archer et al., 1998; Mingers, 2006), particularly its concepts of abduction (Peirce, 1903) and generative mechanisms, provides a very useful language for discussing both theory building and analytic generalization.

This point is not made in S&S12, but should have been. Tsang (2014), however, shows how Critical Realism provides a very useful lens for explaining why generalizations from case studies should be accepted. In particular, Critical Realism’s mechanisms explain why the ‘cause-and-effect relationships in representative case studies are similar to those in the population from which the case studies were selected’ (see the above quotation from S&S12: 12, on the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of representativeness). The reason being that if the underlying generative mechanisms are the same in both the sample and the population, similar cause-and-effect relationships should be evident in both.

The absence of detailed discussion of Critical Realism’s abduction (Peirce, 1903) and generative mechanisms (Collier, 1994; Archer et al., 1998, Mingers, 2006) in L&B03 and T&W12 is a fourth reason why we argued earlier that there may be other, more useful, generalization frameworks than either L&B03’s or T&W12 (or S&S12’s, for that matter).

## Conclusion

The purpose of this paper has been to provide an independent review of the con<sup>fl</sup>icting positions on the meaning of generalization in a series of four papers published by Lee and Baskerville (L&B03, L&B12) and Tsang and Williams (T&W12 and W&T15). In a nutshell, we have tried to make three points.

First, our understanding is that L&B03’s generalization framework, reproduced in Figure 1, is intended to include many different meanings for the term ‘generalization’. L&B03’s goal appears to have been to help case-study and interpretive researchers defend themselves from the charge that ‘you cannot generalize from a single case’ by presenting a broader range of meanings for the term ‘generalization’ than the conventional induction-based view (which they labelled variously ‘positivist’ and ‘statistical sampling based’). Our views on the merits of their framework are that (a) we agree with L&B03 that inductive generalizations are ‘never fully justi<sup>fi</sup>ed logically’, (b) we do not believe that TE generalization is generalization (see comment 5 in our Figure 2), (c) it is not clear to us how L&B03’s framework offers much support for case-study and interpretive researchers over and above its endorsement of Yin’s (1994, 2003) concept of ‘generalizing to theory’, and (d) we, personally, <sup>fi</sup>nd Yin’s (1994, 2003) concept of ‘generalizing to theory’ (described earlier as L&B’s ET1 generalization) unhelpful, because it confounds generalization with theory building. Finally, as noted above, L&B should be thanked for encouraging the IS research community to take a fresh look at its generalization practices.

Second, T&W did not see merit in L&B03’s goal of integrating many different views of generalization. For them, the only relevant meaning for the term ‘generalization’ in a research context is that ‘For any two statements P and Q, Q is more general than P when and only when the class of entities that P is about is a subset of the class of entities that Q is about’ (T&W12: 734). From this perspective, they pointed out many con<sup>fl</sup>icts and inconsistencies embodied in the seven different views that L&B chose to include in their framework. They also presented an alternative framework for discussing generalization, reproduced in Figure 3. For us, a limitation of this framework is that, like Figure 1, it also contains the idea that it is meaningful to generalize to theory. However, as noted above, we think that T&W did IS researchers a service by showing that many of L&B03’s concepts do not accord with the conventional induction-based meaning of generalization that we have termed ‘other-settings’ generalization. This may not have been clear from a quick reading of L&B03.

Third, having listened to the debate, readers may <sup>fi</sup>nd themselves asking: ‘Do I <sup>fi</sup>nd L&B’s conceptualization of generalization more convincing than T&W’s or vice versa?’ Our advice to such readers is that it is not necessary to accept either! To show that this is the case, in the penultimate section of this paper we presented a six-point argument intended to show that many inter-related concepts are required for an indepth understanding of generalization (e.g., theory, sample, scope, truth, con<sup>fi</sup>dence, local theory, ‘generalizing to theory’, representativeness, and generative mechanism), and that the particular bundles of concepts that L&B and T&W chose to combine in their frameworks are not the only, nor necessarily the most useful, ones for discussing and understanding generalization. Readers seeking further clarity of the meaning of generalization in IS research are encouraged to read the papers by Seddon and Scheepers (2012), Compeau et al. (2012), and Tsang (2014).
