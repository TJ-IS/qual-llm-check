# The moderating effect of the business strategic orientation on eCommerce adoption: Evidence from UK family run SMEs

Yong Wang <sup>a,\*</sup>, Pervaiz K. Ahmed <sup>b,1</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of International Business, Wolverhampton Business School, University of Wolverhampton, Telford, Shropshire TF2 9NT, UK <sup>b</sup> School of Business, Monash University, Jalan Lagoon Selatan, 46150 Bandar Sunway, Selangor Darul Ehsan, Malaysia

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 18 August 2006 Received in revised form 21 October 2008 Accepted 24 November 2008 Available online 7 January 2009

Keywords: eCommerce adoption Family business Strategic orientation Moderating effect

## a b s t r a c t

This study investigates eCommerce adoption in family run SMEs (small and medium sized enterprises). Specifically, the objectives of the study are twofold: (a) to examine environmental and organisational determinants of eCommerce adoption in the family business context; (b) to explore the moderating effect of business strategic orientation on the relationships between adoption determinants and adoption decision. A quantitative questionnaire survey was executed. The sampling frame was outlined based on the OneSource database and 88 companies were involved. Results of logistic regression analyses proffer support that ‘external pressure’ and ‘perceived benefits’ are predictors of eCommerce adoption. Moreover, the findings indicate that the strategic orientation of family businesses will function as a moderator in the adoption process.

## 1. Introduction

Family businesses are often reckoned as one of the economic engines in the post-industrial growth era, as they are credited for nurturing entrepreneurial talent across generations, a sense of loyalty to business success, long-term strategic commitment, and corporate independence (Poutziouris, 2001). In both developed and developing economies, they have been playing important roles and make considerable contributions to societies. For instance in the US, family businesses are estimated to represent 90–98% of all businesses, offer jobs to over 50% of the work force, and create 40–60% of the GDP (Shanker and Astrachan, 1996: Beckhard and Dver, 1983a). In Holland, family businesses represent 75 percent of all companies, In India, 16 family controlled corporations make up 65 percent of all private sector assets (Kenyon-Rouvinez and Ward, 2005). Overall, Donckels and Frohlick (1991) estimate that in the West the family business sector represents over two thirds of all enterprises and accounts for about half of the GDP-economic activities and private employment.

Notwithstanding family business's vital standing in economies, there is a paucity of studies about family firms (Chua et al., 2003). Shanker and Astrachan (1996) offer two reasons explaining this scarcity: (1) only until recently did academics and governmental agencies commence to regard family businesses as characteristically distinct entities; (2) the difficulty in defining family businesses makes research hard to execute. Hitherto only limited understanding has been gained about how a family business acquires new technology, behaves in the market, and utilises resources. Continued efforts need to be directed towards this sector to comprehend how these firms survive and succeed in the increasingly competitive market.

In the current market with the emergence of knowledge economy and the influx of information, eCommerce has become another new fashion. Recently, the British government portal ‘UK Online’ was established, aiming to introduce and demonstrate benefits of Internet trading to businesses (Martin and Matlay, 2001). Nevertheless, despite the wide acknowledgement of the value that eCommerce may add to the business community, the extent of eCommerce usage in the family business realm varies. Some family firms deem eCommerce as a new panacea on the grounds that it enables businesses to purchase and sell products/services through online channels, facilitates firms to approach new customers and suppliers, and reduces transaction costs. Others claim that eCommerce is not suitable to their transaction or product/service promotion; that firms are not ready for this new online technique; and that firms are constrained by resources, and therefore are unable to incorporate this technique. In fact, the extant research on eCommerce adoption in smaller sized businesses often focuses on what environmental, technological and organisational factors facilitate or inhibit firms to migrate from traditional physical transacting channels to the Internet trajectory (Iacovou et al., 1995; Hong and Zhu, 2006; Teo et al., 2003), whereas the impact of the owner family, often substantial in these firms, is rarely addressed.

Family businesses often demonstrate idiosyncratic features due to their intertwined family and business subsystems (Beckhard and Dyer, 1983b; Sharma et al., 1996). For healthy growth or business prosperity, family businesses often undertake technological venturing by launching new products and opening new markets. The stable and patient capital available in the family businesses proffers slack and flexibility, enabling the firms to adopt advanced technologies and sketch innovative strategies (Sirmon and Hitt, 2003; Teece, 1982). On the other hand, family firms are conventionally inward looking, conservative (Aronoff and Ward, 1997; Sharma et al., 1997), and resistant to change (Hall et al., 2001). The excessive concern over the business long-term survival under the family’s governance may inhibit the family firm’s technological venturing, because risky endeavours may lead to wealth loss and thus shake the firm’s foundation (Sharma et al., 1997; Naldi et al., 2007). Given that different perspectives on family business venturing are held and that almost no study has been executed to explore eCommerce adoption in family businesses, the current paper attempts to address two issues: (1) what are the major determinants that determine eCommerce adoption in family run SMEs; and (2) does family business strategic orientation exert any impact in the adoption process?

The remainder of this article includes four sections. In the theoretical background section, the literature that relates to eCommerce adoption and family business strategic orientation is reviewed to form the foundation for the study. Grounded in this review, the conceptual framework is formulated and hypotheses are posited. Following this, the research methodology section focuses on the description of sampling, data collection, and measures. Research results arisen from factor analysis, reliability analysis, and logistic regression analysis are then presented. This is followed by a discussion on practical implications and directions for further research.

## 2. Theoretical background

Most of the eCommerce studies have used the innovation diffusion theory (Rogers, 1983). The diffusion of a new technology often relies on a number of antecedents. Tornatsky and Fleischer (1990) claim that technological, organisational and environmental contexts (TOE) of a firm can influence the diffusion process. In this theoretical framework, the technology context concerns the business’s technological nature, reflected by factors such as technological intensity and competence. The organisational context is in relation to the company’s attributes, such as size, age, structure, business sector, process and resource, whilst the environmental context is about the macro surroundings in which the business engages, including the characteristics of industry, competitors and society. The TOE framework encapsulates principal determinants of innovation diffusion and proffers a scaffold to understand the diffusion process. With reference to the TOE framework, a literature review on the antecedents of eCommerce adoption was conducted in the current study. Determinants influencing eCommerce adoption are summarised in Table 1, which captures the homogenous nature of most studies in this domain.

## 2.1. External pressure

The traditional institution theory argues that organisations are embedded in the environment and there are shared institutionalised views in this environment about what organisations should perform to maintain their legitimacy (Dowling and Pfeffer, 1975). These shared views in the environment can drive organisations to incorporate widely accepted business practices and reflect them in the business process (Baum and Oliver, 1991; Haveman, 1993; Oliver, 1992).

Iacovou et al. (1995) define external pressure as ‘influences from the organisational environment’ (p. 470). They further argue that external pressure is often originated from two sources, respectively, industrial competitors (i.e. ‘competitive pressure’) and trading partners (i.e. ‘imposition pressure’). ‘Competitive pressure’ means when competitors in the industry take the lead and enjoy advantages brought by the new technology, a firm has to consider whether or not to follow its competitors. Not doing so, the firm will take the risk of being technically incompetent and consequently losing its market position. Pressure from trading partners, i.e. suppliers and customers, can also coerce the firm to make an adoption decision. In particular, when the firm’s powerful trading partners have incorporated the new technology, they could use both push and pull strategies, such as ‘threats’, ‘recommendations’, and ‘promises’ (Iacovou et al., 1995), to galvanise the firm. Additionally, if the majority of trading partners become eCommerce-capable, the firm will be pressurised to adjust its operational processes to achieve an environmental fit. Not doing so, the firm will face the challenge of being isolated. The worst scenario is that the firm is eventually excluded from the market. In the eCommerce adoption literature, Chwelos et al. (2001) and Kuan and Chau (2001) find that pressure from trading partners is a major determinant of eCommerce adoption. Kuan and Chau (2001), whilst highlighting the inter-organisational pressure from trading partners, recognise that external pressure can come from the government. Based on the abovementioned, the following hypothesis is posited:

## 2.2. Organisational readiness

The notion of organisational readiness in Iacovou et al. (1995) has twofold connotations, financial and technological readiness. Financial readiness refers to whether an organisation has sufficient financial resources to invest on new technologies, whilst technological readiness is concerned with the level of technology sophistication. In the small business realm, one major impediment to new technology adoption is the finance inadequacy. Due to capital market imperfections, small businesses often experience difficulties in gaining access to external debt and equity finances (Poutziouris and Wang, 2004; Javis, 2006). The finance gap is particularly conspicuous where small firms lack financial records, asset security and equity bases to command external finance support. They have to primarily rely on internal capital, such as personal equity (Peterson and Schulman, 1987), family loan and trade credit (Berger and Udell, 1998; Romano et al., 2000), whilst the provision of finance through these channels is often insufficient. Under this regime, the firms’ eCommerce aspiration may eventually be put off.

Thong (1999) suggests that business’s technology intensity will influence information technology adoption exercises. Firms at lower computerisation level often have difficulties in approaching hardware and software, IT expertise and special ists, which makes the installation and operation of sophisticated information systems (ISs) problematic (Iacovou et al., 1995). They may feel intimidated by complicated ISs and give up any ambitious aspiration. On the contrary, firms at higher computerisation level tend to be more confident about information technology adoption and have wider accesses to technolog ical resources. They are likely to be better prepared for highly integrated and computerised processes.

Apart from the resource related determinants, Grandon and Pearson (2004) report that compatibility between eCommerce and a firm’s culture, values, and processes is an important factor. Premkumar and Roberts (1999), in their investigation of eCommerce adoption in small businesses located in rural US communities, highlight top management support as a discriminator between adopters and non-adopters. Similarly, Mirchandani and Motwani (2001) emphasise enthusiasm of the top management and compatibility of eCommerce with existing business processes. On the grounds of the above evidence, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H2: Organisational readiness is positively associated with eCommerce adoption. That is, family controlled SMEs with higher organisational readiness for eCommerce will be more likely to adopt eCommerce

## 2.3. Perceived benefits

Benefits associated with new information technology can be classified into two types: direct and indirect benefits (Iacovou et al., 1995). Direct benefits refer to operational savings and internal efficiency enhancement in relation to the introduction of new information technology. These are reflected through transaction cost reduction and cash flow improvement, etc. Indirect benefits, on the other hand, signify the impact of new information technology on business processes and market position. For instance businesses, via eCommerce, may improve relationships with trading partners, consolidate and expand customer base, and enhance their market position (Iacovou et al., 1995; Pfeiffer, 1992). Lal (2005) indicates that eCommerce enables small businesses to re-engineer processes across boundaries that normally separate suppliers from customers. Some business activities such as ordering, paying and after sales service can be redesigned and combined to a single process, leading to cost reduction in operations. In addition, with the assistance of eCommerce, small businesses may operate more easily, accurately, and timely (Lal, 2005). They may even play an active role in internationalisation, as the entry barrier to the global market is likely to be lowered by the new Internet technology.

The notion of perceived benefits in Iacovou et al. (1995) is claimed to be associated with ‘the level of recognition of the relative advantage’ (p. 468) of new technology. In a small firm, if senior management reaches a reasonable level of understanding of eCommerce benefits, the likelihood of managerial, financial and technological resources to be allocated to the technology will increase. This may ultimately create an internal environment conducive to the technology. Furthermore, if an organisation-wide positive perception on eCommerce benefits is developed, the shared view may arouse learning activities (Senge, 1990). Learning outcomes will then be reserved as expertise and transformed into new competitive advantage in the end. On the grounds of these debates, the following hypothesis is suggested:

H3: Perceived benefits of eCommerce are positively associated with eCommerce adoption. That is, family controlled SMEs with a higher level of recognition of eCommerce benefits will be more likely to adopt eCommerce.

## 2.4. Family business’s strategic orientations

Research in the family business domain recognises that family firms possess two intertwining subsystems: family and business (Aronoff and Ward, 1991; Vilaseca, 2002). Due to this idiosyncratic characteristic, a family business usually demonstrates unique strategic orientations, different from the firm-value maximisation orientation possessed by professionally managed businesses (Sharma et al., 1996). Singer and Donohu (1992) believe that family businesses can be clustered by their organisational value, either as family-centered businesses or as business-centered families. Since the business’s operating strategies are generally consistent with its value, this classification offers an answer as to why some businesses successfully operate in one way while others achieve success by operating quite differently. Later, studies by File et al. (1994) and Dunn (1995) confirm that family businesses have complex, multiple objectives and varying priorities. Dunn (1995) states that family businesses are willing to employ family members to maintain good family relationships, though sometimes it may lead to sub-optimal performance. These imply that family and business concerns or interests can be competing (Sharma et al., 1996). Degadt (2003) further indicates that family and business’s values and interests can be mutually complementary, which uncovers the complexity of these firms.

Birley et al. (1999) claim that family businesses can be classified into three distinct clusters: (1) the Family Rules group (i.e. family-oriented firms) where decisions in relation to the family, like keeping ownership within the family, generating income to dependent families (Handler, 1990), and maintaining family reputation (Gersick et al., 1990) are the major concerns; (2) the Family Out group (i.e. business-oriented firms) has opposed views to the Family Rules group where the focus is on the business side. Strategic issues such as adoption of new technology, growth strategy of the business (Drozdow and Carroll, 1997), and involvement of new investors may often appear on the board’s agenda; and (3) the Family–Business Jugglers (i.e. balance-oriented firms) do not express clear preference on either side, but are ‘concerned to arrive at an appropriate balance between family and business issues’ (p. 603).

In the process of making the adoption decision. different strategic orientations may directly or indirectly interact with the adoption variables, i.e. ‘external pressure’, ‘organisational readiness’ and ‘perceive benefits’. Family controlled SMEs, unlike conventional small businesses, usually invest a high proportion of the owner families’ wealth in the businesses (Naldi et al., 2007). Whether the firms are successful or not will have a direct impact on the fortunes of the owner families. Consequently, family controlled companies cognitively are sensitive to external pressure. In a family-oriented firm, the business and the owner-manager are often synonymous because of the continued family-centred interests. The organisational networks are usually narrowly defined and are owner-centred (Wang et al., 2004a). Information collected through these ties/networks is therefore restricted, not sound enough to mirror the external pressure comprehensively, Furthermore in such firms there is hardly any formalised routine available for commercial information analysis. The decision-making authority is often centralised. The decisions made on this basis, therefore, are likely to be judgemental (Busenitz and Barney, 1997; Casson, 2005). Grounded in this environment, businesses will encounter difficulties to adopt the new technology even though they are under the pressure. On the contrary, in a business-oriented firm, business’s healthy growth and profit maximisation are prioritised (Drozdow and Carroll, 1997) Guided by this operation philosophy, the networks configured are often formalised and multi-directional. Embedded in such networks are elements of functional specialisation and delegated authority. Market information filtered through these networks, as a result, tends to be more accurate and have a wider coverage, enabling companies to make logical decisions to respond to the external pressure

Family business strategic orientation may also interact with ‘organisational readiness’ and exert impact on the business eCommerce adoption practice. In a business-oriented firm, staff tend to possess sounder technical and managerial knowledge, skills and expertise, because the recruitment often emphasises on the candidate’s capabilities and suitability for the job instead of nepotism. Grable and Lytton (1998) indicate that the education level of key staff is a significant discriminant differentiating risk-taking intensity in businesses. Those who have rigorous education background are inclined to show competitive knowledge exploitation, acquisition, assimilation and transformation capability, which will facilitate the generation of entrepreneurial initiatives and the advancement of the technology base. Furthermore, knowledge-intensive staff may assist in formalising procedures to operate businesses. Thus, the business may embark onto the technology advancement platform more easily. In addition, the top management in such a firm tends to be more receptive to modern technology. They may promote entrepreneurial venturing by funding new technologies, launching new products, and opening new markets. A family-oriented firm, to the opposite, is often unwilling to take any risk to move forward its technology frontline. In these firms, due to the concern on the family’s interests, family members are often recruited because of the nepotism rather than their competences. As a result, the businesses often have to address the latent staff incompetence problem first, when gal vanised to acquire, assimilate and develop cutting edge technologies, Moreover. familv-oriented firms are unique because of their penchant for conservatism (Chua et al., 2003). An overwhelming portion of these firms gears towards ‘lifestyle’, wherein owner-managers are not motivated by traditional financial outcomes, but by ownership monopoly. They enjoy the ‘small is beautiful’ autonomistic culture (Poutziouris and Wang, 2003) and operate as an extension of the ethos of their ownermanagers (Birley et al., 1999). To safeguard family ownership and financial independence from external professionals, these firms would like to see their technological evolution proceeding at a slow pace.

Family business strategic orientation may further moderate the link between ‘perceived benefits’ and eCommerce adoption. Business-oriented firms tend to prioritise business-related initiatives. When they realise the benefits that could be ren dered by the new technology, including transaction cost reduction (Mehrtens et al., 2001), cash flow improvement, inventory level reduction, and operational efficiency (Pfeiffer, 1992) etc., these firms will more likely to take the new technologica solution on board to enhance their competitive advantages. On the contrary, family-oriented businesses often have a ‘climate’ in which family health and prosperity take precedence. Concerns over latent costs and risks may override the perceived benefits and become obstacles against the new technology uptake. In fact, any rash uptake decision may cause uncertainties and rock the relative stability of small firms. Therefore these firms are likely to postpone the adoption activity until the benefits in relation to the technology become clear. In summary, the new technology adoption is likely to be moderated by business strategic orientations. Using these theoretical perspectives as a foundation, a hypothesis and three subhypotheses are posited as follows (see Fig. 1):

H4: Family business’s strategic orientation positively moderates the relationship between eCommerce adoption antecedents and eCommerce adoption.

H4a: Family business’s strategic orientation positively moderates the relationship between ‘external pressure’ and eCommerce adoption. Specifically, the relationship between ‘external pressure’ and eCommerce adoption will be more positive with business-oriented family businesses than with family-oriented family firms.

H4b: Family business’s strategic orientation positively moderates the relationship between ‘organisational readiness’ and eCommerce adoption. Specifically, the relationship between ‘organisational readiness’ and eCommerce adoption will be more positive with business-oriented family businesses than with family-oriented family firms.

H4c: Family business’s strategic orientation positively moderates the relationship between ‘perceived benefits’ and eCommerce adoption. Specifically, the relationship between ‘perceived benefits’ and eCommerce adoption will be more positive with business-oriented family businesses than with family-oriented family firms

## 3. Research methodology

## 3.1. Sample and data

The principal purpose of this study is to investigate eCommerce adoption in the family business context. The sample businesses were selected from the OneSource database, which offers a valuable resource of integrated business information on companies around the world. Particularly, OneSource contains information on the business executive team, which facilitates a search for those firms that have two or more directors sharing the same surname. These firms were assumed to be potential family businesses (in the questionnaire a definition of ‘family business’ based on Leach et al. (1990) was given. Businesses, by referring to this definition, were invited to clarify whether they are family firms or not). Additionally, small and medium sized (fewer than 250 employees) independent British companies were picked out from the database. With the random sampling method, the sample contains 942 family businesses. A questionnaire with a cover letter was posted to the executives/ managing directors of these businesses. Six weeks after the initial mailing, a second wave was sent to the non-respondents. From the two waves of posts, 88 useful responses were received, leading to a response rate of 9.3 percent. This response rate is relatively low. The reasons for the comparatively low response rate may be twofold: (1) the investigated family business sector is insular and secretive, not particularly willing to disclose business information to the public; (2) eCommerce is an Internet technique that only recently emerged in the market. Small sized family businesses due to resource constraints (Thong, 1999) or distinctive strategic concerns hesitate to incorporate this technology. Hence they may consider this study irrelevant to their practices. In fact, the hesitance of family firms to adopt eCommerce may lead to critical problems to the entire business community as family firms constitute about 70 percent of the businesses in the West (Donckels and Frohlick 1991).

To assess the possible non-response bias, checks for differences in demographic characteristics, such as business size (in terms of the number of employees), business age and type of industry, were conducted. The results indicate no significant differences between respondents (88 usable respondents) and non-respondents (854 non-respondents) at the 0.05 level. This suggests that results from the respondents could be broadly generalisable to those in OneSource.

## 3.2. Variables and constructs

## 3.2.1. Dependent measure

To measure a firm’s eCommerce adoption situation, each firm was requested to specify the percentage of its total sales revenues generated via eCommerce in the past twelve months. eCommerce adopters refer to those in which more than zero percent of total sales revenues were generated through the online channel. If no sales was realised through this technology in the prior twelve months, the firm is counted as a non-adopter. Hence the dependent variable used in this study has a dichotomous nature.

## 3.2.2. Independent measures

The independent variables are encapsulated into three constructs. These constructs, developed based on the work of Iacovou et al. (1995), Grandon and Pearson (2004), Hong and Zhu (2006), Pfeiffer (1992), Riemenschneider et al. (2003), and Molla and Licker (2005), respectively, represent external pressure, organisational readiness, and perceived benefits. Each of the constructs consists of a set of items depicting the construct theme from different angles. For each item, a 5-point Likert scale is used (1- being strongly disagree and 5-being strongly agree) to enable respondents to indicate the extent to which their businesses agree with these items. In particular, the external pressure construct consists of items describing the pressure that a business may encounter from its industrial competitors, trading partners, and government. Organisational readiness is measured by a scale outlining readiness from three different perspectives, financial sufficiency, technological capability, and compatibility. Finally the perceived benefits construct is composed of items portraying the anticipated gain hinged with eCommerce in finance, effi ciency, retention and expansion of customer base, and retention and expansion of suppliers.

## 3.2.3. Moderator variable

To measure the family business strategic orientation, the questionnaire has a brief describing two companies (i.e. Companies A and B) with fundamentally different strategic concerns (see Appendix A). The description in the brief follows Wang (2002). Respondents were asked to distribute 100 points between two descriptions depending on how they perceived the statement in relation to their own business situation. For example, if a firm perceives itself similar to Company A (i.e. a family-oriented business) and only remotely like Company B (i.e. a business-oriented firm), one could allocate 90 points to Company A and 10 points to Company B. Having acquired the two scores from each sampled firm, a technical transformation was further executed so that a single variable portraving the business's strategic orientation could be achieved. In the current study the transformation was conducted by using the score of the business-oriented part (i.e. points allocated to Company B) divided by that of the family-oriented part (i.e. points allocated to Company A). Therefore the higher value of the quotient, the more the business is business-oriented; the smaller the quotient, the more the business is family-oriented. Under this circumstance, an extreme case may occur if a respondent allocates full points to Company B and zero to Company A, where the real quotient of 100 divided by 0 does not exist. For the convenience purpose, the quotient of such situation is set up at 100, which means that the firm is entirely business-oriented.

## 3.2.4. Control variables

In the literature, business size and business sector are claimed to be associated with eCommerce adoption (Sadowski et al., 2002; Chappell and Feindt, 2000; Hong and Zhu, 2006). For example, in larger sized companies, IT usage is often extensive given available internal knowledge and resources (Alpar and Reeves, 1990). Unlike larger sized counterparts, small businesses usually suffer from ‘resource poverty’ (Thong, 1999). In the IT technology absorption and assimilation process, they are often constrained by insufficient internal expertise and financial resources. Meanwhile, managerial myopia may create extra barriers as the management cannot foresee long term benefits associated. In terms of business sector, firms in the technology intensive industries such as manufacturing and service sectors are more likely to battle at the technological frontline because of their industrial milieu. Those in the low technology sectors, such as retailing and wholesaling, however are not overly interested in the new IT technology. In the current study, to control firm and sector-specific effects, firm size and sector are employed as control variables. For the operation purpose, firm size is measured by the log of the total number of employees and the business sector is coded by a categorical variable, representing manufacturing/construction, retail/whole sale, and service sectors, respectively.

## 4. Data analyses and results

## 4.1. Measurement model

A principal component analysis with varimax rotation was executed to examine the factor structure of eCommece adoption antecedent measures. Three factors with the eigenvalue above 1.0 arose and they were generally consistent with the constructs proposed, representing the themes of external pressure, organisational readiness, and perceived benefits. These three factors altogether explained a total of 58.0% of the variance. Items were retained in a factor if they had a loading at or above .4 on that factor, and the differences between this loading and two other cross-loadings are more than .3 (Howell et al., 2005; Kline, 1994). The resulting instrument was further examined for validity. Parasuraman et al. ## 5.1. eCommerce adoption

Table 7 summarises the outcomes of hypotheses testing. Through restricted logistic regression analyses, two drivers of eCommerce adoption are identified: external pressure and perceived benefits, respectively. First, external pressure is recognised to have a positive effect on eCommerce adoption. This finding corroborates the results from Iacovou et al. (1995) and Grandon and Pearson (2004) which, respectively, investigates the adoption of EDI (Electronic Data Interchange) and eCommerce in small organisations. Schumpeter (1934) argues that the diffusion of innovation often forms a dynamic driving force in the trade cycle. Whenever an entrepreneur/firm innovates successfully, the efficiency improvement and cost reduction will impose those risk-averse competitors to imitate. Small family businesses are less likely to be the innovation ‘early adopters’ (Rogers, 1983), which are capable of reducing uncertainty of an innovation by adopting it and then conveying their perceptions to near-peers. Nevertheless, when the pressure wave from the industry comes towards them, they have to make a judgement whether it is beneficial or not to ‘metamorphose’ into a member of the ‘early majority’ (Rogers, 1983).

## 5.2. The moderating effect of family business strategic orientation

Family firms are often believed to have idiosyncratic strategic concerns, not only to maximise business’s financial income, but also to augment the so-called ‘familiness’, a phrase coined by Habbershon and Williams (1999) to describe the unique bundle of resources a family firm has as the result of intertwined family and business subsystems. If the firm holds a solid business-first perspective, technology renovation may then be prioritised because technological updating often proffers a clear cutting edge to the firm. Guided by this philosophy, the firm may view the pressure from external environment more positively and incorporate the innovation. Through active interactions, the firm may timely surf on the technological tide, enhance its competitiveness, and protect its survival space against aggressive industrial competition. To the other end, if it adheres to a family-first philosophy, the firm may be risk-averse and exceedingly cautious about technological updating on the grounds that the whole family may rely on the financial income originated by the firm, whereas the payoff in relation to the technological progress is often uncertain. Guided by this logic, the firm may not decipher the pressure from the environment as a positive drive but an irresistible burden. The company may therefore resort to all means to shirk the technological hit.

Benefits rendered by the new technology are broadly recognised, including efficiency improvement (Pfeiffer, 1992) and trading partner relationship enhancement (Pfeiffer, 1992), etc. The literature highlights that small businesses usually suffer from resource insufficiency (Thong, 1999). They have to primarily rely on internal financial and human capital to maintain business operations and most of them only possess limited strategic rents. The advantages eCommerce possesses often fit well on the desirables list of those business-oriented firms. This may drive these firms to perceive eCommerce from a more positive angle. In the family-oriented businesses, on the other hand, the desire to retain ownership control and the fear of losing authority often compel owner-managers to erect strict constrictions against use of external equity (Hutchinson, 1995; Romano et al., 2000) or technological resources. The downside of this insular perception is that it may blockade the businesses’ long-term growth channel. In addition, concerns over associated costs and embedded risks may override the perceived benefits and become obstacles against the new technology uptake. These firms are likely to postpone the adoption activity until the benefits in relation to the technology are clear.

## 5.3. Contributions of the study

Our study builds upon prior research on eCommerce adoption, but makes original explorations in a few ways. First, a plethora of studies have been executed in the past to comprehend the IS adoption in both small (Thong, 1999; Mehrtens et al., 2001; Kuan and Chau, 2001; Riemenschneider et al., 2003) and large firms (Heinze and Hu, 2006; Beheshti et al., 2006). Yet, rarely has any IS adoption research been undertaken in the family business field. This inspires us to make an original attempt and tap into the nascent domain. Second, the current study has developed constructs of external pressure, organisational readiness, and perceived benefits. The reliability of these instruments has been confirmed by the study. In addition, an approach to measure family business strategic orientation has been designed. It can effectively evaluate the degree of family or business orientation of a family firm. Third, the finding arisen that family business strategic orientation can moderate the relationship between eCommerce adoption and external pressure, and perceived benefits adds to the literature of IS and family business. In the past, hardly has any study shown that interests or intentions of the owner family can function as catalysts in the technology adoption process. This finding flags up the idiosyncrasy of this unique business sector. Furthermore, the evidence demonstrates the value of tailoring the eCommerce adoption framework to achieve a comprehensive understanding of the complex IS adoption.

## 5.4. Limitations of the research

This study is an empirical exploration. The methodological challenges in executing family business research imply that a number of limitations may constrain the generalisability of the conclusions. First, this study confronts the sample size limitation. Traditional postal surveys often suffer from a low response rate, which is believed to be the ‘result more from not using available knowledge’ of the survey (Paxson et al., 1995, p. 314) than any other issues inherent in the approach. Nonetheless, recently a further decline of the response rate has been recognised (Dennis and William, 2003). This is partly because many firms encounter a ‘survey fatigue’ of being ‘bombarded’ by overwhelming questionnaires, and a relatively high percentage of these questionnaires are poorly designed. Dennis and William (2003) claim that higher response rates in small business surveys often are accompanied by special circumstances, e.g. government-sponsored projects, which does not sound very encouraging to academic researchers. It has to be pointed out that a small sample does place barriers against analyses and may lead to biased outcomes. Given a larger sample, the study could be more convincing and the research results more robust. Second, the study is limited to incorporated firms, which are available in the OneSource database. Nonincorporated companies, such as sole traders and partnerships, are excluded simply because they are not accessible in any public databases. Conclusions generated from the current study therefore cannot be generalised to those non-incorporated family firms.

## 6. Practical implications

Notwithstanding the fact that the birth and boom of eCommerce is only a recent phenomenon, the potential and competitiveness in relation to this Internet technique is widely acknowledged. It is therefore not irrational to encourage family run SMEs to embark onto the online platform. Provided the unique features family businesses demonstrate and the findings generated by the current study, the following points are highlighted aiming to facilitate a smooth technological transition.

Assessment of external pressure: before taking any adoption initiatives, family firms need to execute an incisive and comprehensive investigation of the external pressure they are facing. The scope of the investigation may cover the extent and the level of eCommerce usage in the industry, initiatives of the governmental bodies, and actions and strategies taken by competitors and trading partners. Insightful inspection will enable firms to realise managerial and technological implications and subsequently draw up a realistic doable adoption plan.

Execution of a self-appraisal: parallel to the above-mentioned investigation, family firms need to implement a comprehensive self-appraisal. In this process, a number of questions have to be addressed, for instance, have the potential benefits of eCommerce been recognised by the firm? What are the current business operation processes? What is the connection between the current operation processes and eCommerce? What is the view of the top management on eCommerce? Agencies and consultants working for the development of family business need to offer constructive and tailored advices at this stage. In particular, they should encourage those highly family-oriented owner-managers to look beyond the immediate family territory. A clear message to be passed to family firms is that eCommerce is a representative of new technologies and can open up broader competitive horizons for firms. Those businesses embarking onto the internet platform may have an opportunity to secure or even boost their market share, whereas conservative inward-thinking, or resistance to change may only hold up business development and engender problems for businesses in a long run.

## 7. Directions for future research

Research on eCommerce in the small business domain remains at an embryonic stage, although a growing demand arises to explore what driving forces impel small firms and what genuine benefits firms can attain through this Internet technology. Motivated by the belief that eCommerce adoption is rarely examined in the family firm context and that the systematic approach may result in advanced understanding, this paper reviews the literature of eCommerce adoption in the small business milieu and administration of family business. A conceptual framework is elaborated and then statistically tested. One of the key contributions of this study is the finding of the moderating effect of family business strategic orientation on eCommerce adoption. In addition, the antecedents of eCommerce which distinguish adopters from non-adopters have been confirmed.

As the proposed framework has only been quantitatively examined, it needs further validation through qualitative studies. In the future, qualitative empirical research can be carried out to examine validity of the constructs, render valuable interpretations of the relationships identified, and test the comprehensiveness of the model. Research using focus group or semi-structured interviews with owner-managers/technology managers may be undertaken to develop in-depth insights into the model. For instance, the investigating lens may focus on how external pressure influences the top management’s decision on adoption, how the directors respond to the potential benefits associated with the technology, and how family business strategic orientation moderates the relationship between eCommerce adoption and external pressure, and perceived benefits. Parallel to this methodological direction, it may be worth further examining whether the model constructed can be applied to the context of other IS adoptions in family controlled SMEs. In addition, if we move one step further to stretch the research boundary to incorporate non-family controlled SMEs, the moderation of the business’s strategic orientation on the IS adoptions can be revisited. Such empirical attempts may enable researchers to extend the generalisability of the model and develop more incisive perspectives on the relationship between business strategic orientation and IS adoption in different business contexts. Indeed, the whole realm of IS adoption warrants more effort before one can expect to develop domain-specific theories.
