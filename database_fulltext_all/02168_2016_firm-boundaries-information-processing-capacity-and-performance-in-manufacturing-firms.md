# Firm Boundaries, Information Processing Capacity, and Performance in Manufacturing Firms

JAIME GÓMEZ, IDANA SALAZAR, AND PILAR VARGAS

JAIME GÓMEZ (jaime.gomez@unirioja.es; corresponding author) is an associate professor of management at the University of La Rioja, Spain. He has been a visiting professor at the Universities of Cambridge, Nottingham, City of London, and Warwick. He received his Ph.D. in business economics from the University of Zaragoza. His work focuses on marketplace strategy, firm innovation, and the diffusion of new technologies. He has published in Strategic Management Journal, Journal of Economics and Management Strategy, and Research Policy, among others.

IDANA SALAZAR (idana.salazar@unirioja.es) is an assistant professor of management at the University of La Rioja, Spain, where she received her Ph.D. Her research focuses on the study of firm boundary decisions, the adoption of technologies, and the effect of information technology investments on performance. She has published in PLoS ONE, B.E. Journal of Economic Analysis and Policy, Business Research Quarterly, and others.

PILAR VARGAS (pilar.vargas@unirioja.es) is an associate professor of management at the University of La Rioja, Spain. She received her Ph.D. in management from the University of La Rioja. Her research interests include the adoption and diffusion of technologies, the effect of intangible investment on firms’ performance, and technological sourcing decisions. Her papers have been published in Research Policy, PLoS ONE, R&D Management, Business Research Quarterly, and others.

ABSTRACT: We analyze the alignment between information processing needs and capacities. First, we explore the relationship between firms’ vertical and horizontal boundaries and information technology (IT) capabilities. The literature postulates that less internalization leads firms to invest more in information technology. However, we argue that the use of taper integration and diversification increases the demand for IT-based resources such as IT infrastructure and IT human resources. Second, we propose that the fit between information processing needs and capacity has a positive effect on performance. Our hypotheses are tested on a panel of Spanish manufacturing firms, and the results provide general support for our arguments. One advantage of the data is that they include not only large firms but also small and medium-size manufacturers. From a theoretical perspective, the study contributes to the literature by providing novel insights on how decisions on the vertical dimension condition investments in IT capabilities. It also adds new evidence on the diversification–IT capabilities relationship and studies the consequences of alignment of corporate strategies and IT capabilities on firm performance. From a managerial perspective, our study suggests that changes in the vertical and horizontal limits should be followed by changes in IT capabilities to improve performance.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: diversification, information processing capacity, information processing needs, information technology capabilities, IS–organization fit, taper integration.

This study uses the information processing view (IPV) to examine how firm boundaries are related to information technology (IT) capabilities. The alignment of IT capabilities with business is a critical management concern [55].<sup>1</sup> The literature has used the IPV to study the alignment of IT and a firm’s strategic decisions [33, 108]. The IPV suggests that a key issue for managers is to align the information processing needs and capacities of firms [62, 65]. Following this idea, in this study we propose that a firm meets its information processing needs through the creation of IT capabilities, which in turn increase its information processing capacity. We study how vertical integration and diversification determine information processing needs and condition the demand for IT capabilities. This question has been examined in the context of business process outsourcing [5, 65, 66, 76] and diversification [26, 37, 49, 88]; therefore, we join these efforts. We also study the consequences of alignment on firm performance.

In this study, we have three objectives that relate to firm boundaries, IT capabilities, and firm performance. First, we study the vertical boundaries of the firm and IT capabilities. We propose an inverted U-shaped relationship between the extent of vertical integration and IT capabilities motivated by the theory of taper integration. Research examining vertical integration often considers two extreme forms that firms use to organize transactions: the market and the hierarchy. Most of the empirical work in this area shows that the relationship between vertical integration and IT capabilities is negative [27, 49].<sup>3</sup> However, in practice firms use governance structures different from the two extreme forms. This has been recognized in different studies [19, 60, 115]. Among the different possibilities used to structure a firm’s activities, taper integration is the most frequently used to organize the vertical dimension [46]. Taper integration implies the execution of some functional activities inside the firm along with the outsourcing of others (within the same function) [38, 49]. In this study we posit that the integration of the market and the hierarchy increases the complexity of information processing—that is, complexity is lower when the firm solely makes or buys and higher when the firm does both. When using the market, the firm has to search for, negotiate with, and establish contracts with external suppliers [113]. Internal supply, however, requires securing capital, allocating capacity, staffing and coordinating internal production activities. When the firm uses both, complexity increases because the firm has to manage two very different types of suppliers, internal and external [72, 82]. When the firm moves toward the market, the number of information links and information portals with which it interacts increases. When the firm moves away from the market, it has to increase information analysis, information creation, information storage, and information management. Therefore, we argue that the combination of distinct forms of governance provides benefits, but it also increases complexity because it adds new activities that are not present in a singular governance structure [72].<sup>4</sup>

Second, we study the relationship between the horizontal limits of a firm and IT capabilities [26, 28, 49, 88, 104]. Research suggests that diversification increases internal coordination costs and the demand for IT capabilities [26, 49]. However, the findings on the different types of diversification (related and unrelated) are mixed [26, 88]. In this study, we use the resource-based view [4, 84, 112] and argue that diversification increases the variety of tasks a firm is involved in. This, in turn, increases the demand for information processing and the need to invest in IT capabilities to manage internal information flows. Internal coordination requires the processing, sharing, and communication of information within the firm. Controlling internal activities requires information on how they are performed [61]. We argue that the increase in information processing capacity is especially important for firms with higher degrees of related diversification, given that the main benefits of this strategy come from the coordination of close activities that generate synergies.

Third, we analyze the consequences of alignment on performance, a topic of much interest for information systems researchers [15] that has received little attention in the firm boundary and IT capabilities relationship. The IPV proposes that the fit between vertical integration and diversification, on the one hand, and IT capabilities, on the other, should improve firm performance. We expect the best-performing firms to be those that align their information processing needs and their information processing capacity. When firms are not aligned, we expect underfit to be more detrimental to performance than overfit.

This study contributes to the literature in three ways. First, we introduce the theory of taper integration into the examination of the firm boundary and IT capabilities relationship. The idea that taper integration increases complexity and IT needs is new in this context and leads to a refinement of the theory. Our findings of an inverted U-shaped relationship between outsourcing and IT confirm the hypothesized model and inform managers about how much they should invest in IT capabilities. The consideration of taper integration also contributes to the understanding of why firms outsource and how this should be managed. Outsourcing has been one of the top management tools in the past decade [93]. Firms that outsource frequently employ taper integration. The arguments provided by the theory of taper integration fit naturally into the evidence that companies are using outsourcing to do much more than cut costs [45]. Outsourcing allows firms to access specialized skills and assets and provides flexibility in the organizational structure [54, 67]. For example, Apple obtained a flexible and responsive supply chain by outsourcing the manufacture of the iPhone to Foxconn [106]. However, the evidence on the performance of outsourced processes sometimes reveals that coordination problems can arise [106]. The use of taper integration may contribute to explaining the mixed results occasionally reported [24].

Second, we use the IPV to better understand the relationship between firm diversification and IT capabilities. While previous research has found that diversified firms demand more IT [26, 49, 61, 88], there is scarce and conflicting evidence about this relationship if different types of diversification are considered. Previous empirical results have found both positive [26] and nonsignificant effects [61] of unrelated diversification on IT.

Third, we contribute by analyzing the outcomes of the alignment of IT capabilities and firm boundaries (see [15]). Although IT alignment has been a top priority for information system researchers and practitioners, the fit between firm boundaries and IT capabilities has received little attention in the literature, especially for firms that engage in outsourcing and multiple business units. Specifically, we contribute to the IT value literature by considering how this alignment affects performance, an issue of much interest for managers. In addition, in this analysis, we study the consequences of two types of misfit<sup>7</sup>: the first stems from having less IT than required by information processing needs (underfit) and the second by having more (overfit). Finally, we also contribute to IT business value studies by examining a representative sample of firms that includes small and medium-size firms.

## A Framework for Understanding Information Processing Needs and Capacities

The IPV posits that alignment between information processing needs and information processing capacity leads to superior performance. Although much effort has been devoted to studying the effects of alignment on firm performance, the empirical literature has found conflicting results (see [15, 102]).

In addition, this stream of research has focused on business strategies, on business processes, or on informal elements of the organizational infrastructure [22] (see [102] for a review). However, it has not examined the relationship between corporate strategy, IT capabilities and firm performance.

The conceptual model underlying the current research is depicted in Figure 1. It suggests that managers should align corporate decisions on firm boundaries and IT capabilities to improve firm performance. The purpose of this section is to present the basic elements of the model and their role in determining fit.<sup>8</sup>

## Information Processing Needs and Information Processing Capacity

Our principal argument in this study is that the IPV can be employed to understand the investments that firms make in IT capabilities. This framework, according to which organizations are information processing systems [66, 108], has recently been used for similar purposes [51, 66, 86]. The amount of information a firm must process depends on the external and internal sources of uncertainty it confronts. Externally, an organization must cope with the unpredictability of inputs, the use of technology, and the changing tastes of consumers, to name just a few factors. Internally, the firm must be effective at solving the coordination problems arising from the division of labor and the diverse challenges associated with the activities it performs.

The information processing needs of an organization must be matched with its information processing capacities [7, 108]. These capacities are determined by several factors. Perhaps the most important is the organizational structure of the firm, which has the role of collecting environmental information and processing the internal data necessary for carrying out activities. However, a firms’ IT capabilities—our focus in this study—are also an important factor in explaining these capacities [7]. The information processing capacities of a firm increase with the adoption of IT that helps the firm collect and manage the information required to produce and sell its products [33, 66]. For example, to facilitate a constant exchange of information throughout its supply chain, Zara uses the latest IT tools [32]. This study contributes to the literature by using the IPV to explore the link between firm boundaries and IT capabilities and by providing novel evidence on this relationship.

## Firm Boundaries and Information Processing Needs

In our model, information processing needs are related to firms’ horizontal and vertical boundaries [28, 88]. Selecting products and pinpointing target customers for these products is one of the main decisions managers must make. This leads to the delineation of the vertical and horizontal limits of the firm: the vertical scope is determined by the number of activities conducted in-house and the horizontal scope is determined by the firm’s number of product lines.

Both the vertical and horizontal limits of a firm are critical to understanding its information processing needs. Contrary to extant research that proposes a monotonous relationship between vertical integration and information processing needs [26, 28, 49], we first suggest that the simultaneous use of the market and the hierarchy (i.e., taper integration) increases incentives for firms to invest in IT capabilities, given the complexities presented by the need for an integrated management of the two mechanisms [96]. Second, we maintain that diversification increases the internal information processing needs by augmenting the complexity and the variety of tasks performed and the coordination and monitoring of costs [88]. This increase in information processing needs will be higher in the case of related diversification, given that it implies the creation of reciprocal interdependences [53, 88].

## IT Capabilities and Information Processing Capacity

The information processing capacity of firms depends on their IT capabilities and on other complementary organizational elements. Information processing capacity is defined as the ability of an organization to collect, interpret, synthesize, and distribute information to cope with uncertainty [51, 108]. In other words, information processing capacity deals with the ability to process the required information in the right quantity.

IT capabilities are defined as “the ability to mobilize and deploy IT-based resources in combination or copresent with other resources and capabilities” [10, p. 171]. To properly define the concept, one must focus on IT-based resources that are likely to build IT capabilities [10, 95]. For example, Bharadwaj [10] maintains that IT capability is built on IT infrastructure, IT human resources, and IT-enabled intangibles. IT capabilities allow the firm to coordinate activities through the use of IT resources [91] and may be used to facilitate the delivery of needed resources by removing the pshysical, spatial, and temporal limitations to communication [10, 13]. IT resources can improve internal and external coordination [37, 64] through two primary functions: (1) automating, which enables an increase in the amount of information processed per unit of time, and (2) informating, which facilitates proper routing of available information among organizational actors within the firm and between firms [66]. We contribute to the literature by improving our understanding of how IT capabilities and IT capacity are related.

## Fit as the Match Between Firm Boundaries and IT Capabilities

Finally, our model considers differences in firm performance as a consequence of alignment. A basic tenet of the IPV is that the fit between information processing needs and information processing capacities is a determinant of performance [33]. Although intuitive from a theoretical perspective [86], the operationalization of fit is a complex issue. Drazin and Van de Ven [29] identify three approaches to evaluate fit: contingency, selection, and systems approaches. Venkatraman [111] proposed a framework that comprises six different perspectives from which fit can be defined, namely, fit as (a) moderation (b) mediation (c) matching (d) covariation (e) profile deviation, and (f) gestalts (see [46] for discussions of each of these perspectives). Although conceived in the context of strategy research, the idea of fit has been applied to information systems research [66, 86].

In our model, fit is achieved when there is a match between information processing requirements, determined by a firm’s boundaries, and its information capacities, determined by its IT capabilities. Thus, we use fit as a matching perspective in which alignment between the two dimensions improves performance and situations of misfit worsen performance. Therefore, one novel aspect of our model is that it distinguishes between overfit and underfit. Another is that we consider alignment in the context of outsourcing and multiple business units.

## Hypotheses

Taper Integration, Information Processing Needs, and IT Capabilities

The literature has traditionally argued that vertical integration leads firms to invest less in IT [26, 49]. In this section, we qualify this hypothesis in order to construct a more nuanced assessment of the relationship. To do so, we focus on the increasing information processing needs that the use of taper integration imposes on firms and analyze its consequences in terms of IT capabilities.

The literature on information systems maintains that the way in which firms govern transactions may affect information processing needs, determining a firm’s IT capabilities. The forms of governance traditionally examined are the market and the hierarchy. Recent literature on management has focused on the analysis of situations in which firms use a combination of both.<sup>9</sup> This is what the literature has termed taper integration [9, 38, 39, 85], and it takes place when “firms are backward or forward integrated but rely on outsiders for a portion of their supplies or distribution” [38, p. 643]. Taper integration is the most common way to organize transactions in the vertical chain: for example, Heriot and Kulkarni [46] show that taper integration is used by 57.9 percent of all the firms in their sample.

To understand why taper integration may increase the information processing needs of a firm more than the use of the two extremes, we must analyze its advantages. Firms’ preference for taper integration can essentially be explained in terms of complementarities. Some of these arise from the competition that the simultaneous use of the market and the hierarchy may create between external and internal suppliers. First, the use of internal sources produces a credible threat to external suppliers that the firm may integrate backward [38]. Second, when firms produce externally, they collect information that can be used for controlling and monitoring internal activities [40]. For example, it “enables [the] firm to monitor outside R&D and marketing practices” and “to understand suppliers’ (or distributors’) cost structures and profit margins” [38, p. 645]. Similarly, internal information can also be used to discipline the activities of external providers. For example, in the apparel industry, Heide et al. [42, p. 1166] explain that “a buyer with in-house production is in a position to ascertain whether poor garment quality is due to genuine manufacturing problems or to surreptitious supplier cheating.”

Complementarities are also created through the combination of knowledge, resources, and capabilities that the firm and external suppliers possess [78]. This complementarity occurs because of collaboration between suppliers and the firm [83]. Rothaermel et al. [96, p. 1036] mention that “the integration of currently valuable internal technical knowledge and new external technical knowledge can produce spillover effects that enable firms to profitably improve current products as well as to introduce additional related products in order to gain and maintain a competitive advantage.”

However, the combination of the two governance forms does not automatically bring the benefits of these complementarities [72]. All of these advantages are based on an intensive use of internal and external information and on their combination. They “require communication, gathering of information, and active comparisons” [72, p. 34]. The use of two kinds of information creates interdependencies between the internal and the external activities, increasing complexity. When firms use taper integration, a complex management process emerges because the two governing systems must be managed simultaneously [73]. Therefore, taper integration “clearly increases the complexity of managerial tasks such as coordinating and scheduling, and also has non-trivial bureaucratic costs” [96, p. 1036]. This complexity results in higher information processing needs compared to those derived exclusively from the market or the hierarchy. The use of taper integration requires managing both internal and external relationships [46] and continuously adjusting the interdependences of the internal workflows of the firm’s production process. This management is developed internally and is more information-intensive than it is to employ exclusively one form of governance.

Therefore, the process of implementing taper integration’s advantages, which reduce information asymmetries and allow the firm to learn from the combination of internal and external knowledge sources, increases firms’ internal information processing needs beyond those of the two competing forms of governance. We expect that the use of the market and the hierarchy together will generate complexity and increase the need for IT capabilities. Consequently, we propose:

Hypothesis 1: Firms that use taper integration require more IT capabilities than firms using either the market or the hierarchy.

## Diversification, Information Processing Needs, and IT Capabilities

The diversification level of a firm represents the extent to which it simultaneously operates in distinct lines of business. The literature on strategic management has largely been interested in the reasons why firms diversify and on the consequences of different types/levels of diversification on performance [81]. Following the resource-based view of the firm [4, 84, 112], firms diversify in order to exploit resources that are subject to market failure [61]. Brand names, reputation, and technical knowledge are examples of intangible resources that cannot be easily traded and whose exploitation in different lines of business may generate synergies that have a significant impact on firm performance. Similarly, indivisible physical resources are also the source of scope economies that reduce the average cost of production [88]. In other words, through diversification, firms can make the most of production factors that cannot be sold efficiently on the market [26]. The synergies created have a positive effect on firm performance.<sup>10</sup>

In addition to the economic benefits generated by sharing resources between different lines of business, diversification also increases internal coordination and control costs. “Effective coordination involves processing, sharing, and communicating information as needed across various business units” whereas control “involves monitoring and evaluating performance to overcome agency conflicts” [16, p. 227] and to reduce influence costs [52]. Coordination costs arise from the interdependences created by the different business units integrated into one firm. Therefore, coordination costs are necessary for the integration of business units to make sense for a firm in terms of value creation. Coordination costs are important to understanding the benefits of diversification, given that they can offset the synergies promised by diversification decisions [89]. Similarly, control costs arise from the need to assess the performance of the business units in order to reduce agency and influence costs. Agency costs arise when a principal hires an agent to act on its behalf [52]. Agency costs tend to increase with diversification. For example, principal–agent relationships are created when firms diversify and division managers are assigned to business units. Assessing the performance of the divisions reduces the likelihood of agency costs increasing disproportionately with diversification. The other source of costs associated with diversification is related to the ability of individuals to influence decision makers in organizations. Influence costs are “the losses that arise from individuals within an organization seeking to influence” decision makers “for their private benefit” and “from the organization’s responding to control this behavior” [70, p. 58]. In the context of diversified firms, the existence of influence costs can lead to nonoptimal allocation of resources [97].

In summary, internal coordination and control costs increase with diversification. Internal coordination requires the processing, sharing, and communication of information within the firm. Controlling internal activities requires information on how they are performed [61]. To cope with the increase in information processing needs, diversified firms depend on IT capabilities to increase their information processing capacities [26, 37, 49]. However, diversification has little effect on external coordination costs: the firm increases its activities and, unlike the vertical dimension, the product need not be obtained on the market [26, 49]. On the contrary, applying existing resources to new lines of business creates new interdependences within the firm, but there is no need for external search processes and external contracts. Thus, sharing resources between different lines of business will not alter external requirements but will involve additional internal requirements [26, 49]. Therefore, we propose:

Hypothesis 2: Diversified firms require more IT capabilities than firms with no diversification.

The distinction between related and unrelated diversification is also helpful for understanding firms’ information processing needs. However, different predictions have been made about the consequences of unrelated diversification on IT capabilities (see, e.g., [26, 61]). To understand the differences that may arise, we must clarify the nature of the economic benefits that firms obtain from the two types of diversification. When firms engage in related diversification, they seek economic benefits by sharing resources across related lines of business [116]. However, the benefits of unrelated diversification mainly derive from the exploitation of internal capital markets [47, 48, 105]. As a consequence, the types of interdependences created by related and unrelated diversification are different, and the associated costs of coordination vary depending on the type of diversification [53]. On the one hand, related diversification is the most costly strategy in terms of coordination because obtaining its benefits implies the creation of reciprocal interdependences. Business units have to fully consider the investment decisions and the resources of other business units. As a consequence, firms with related diversification have to invest in information acquisition [53]. Furthermore, related diversification also increases control costs as it makes the identification of the performance of individual units more difficult. As a result, firms following related diversification strategies tend to implement strategic controls that increase information processing requirements, such as the balanced scorecard [88].

On the other hand, unrelated diversification creates pooled interdependence, a type of interdependence that allows business units to have more autonomy and, therefore, to lower coordination costs [53]. In terms of the costs of controlling the performance of business units, higher autonomy makes it easier to isolate their contribution to the firm. In fact, firms following an unrelated diversification strategy tend to use financial (instead of strategic) controls in order to monitor the performance of their business units [88]. Although the information processing needs of firms following unrelated diversification strategies also increase, they incur lower coordination and control costs than relatedly diversified firms and, hence, they require lower investments in acquiring information.

Therefore, the information processing needs created by related diversification are much more significant than those created by unrelated diversification, and consequently, the importance of increasing firms’ information capacities also changes.

When diversification is related, firms may use IT capabilities to coordinate different lines of business, obtaining economies of scope as the coordination and control of shared resources across different lines of business improve [20, 104]. However, the benefits of unrelated diversification mainly emerge from sharing financial resources between unrelated business units whose performance may be more easily identified. That is, the sharing of resources in related diversification creates reciprocal interdependences within firms and, therefore, a greater need for coordination than unrelated diversification [26, 48, 49, 99]. This means that the demand for IT capabilities is expected to be greater in firms with a related diversification strategy [26, 49]. We propose:

Hypothesis 3: Firms with related diversification require more IT capabilities than those with unrelated diversification.

## Fit, Misfit, and Firm Performance

In the information systems literature, the idea of fit has been captured through different terms, such as alignment, integration, and linkage [15]. Chan and Reich [15] and Tallon [102] provide a comprehensive review of the literature, including definitions, alignment dimensions, levels of alignment, alignment measures, and alignment models, among other issues. The empirical literature on the effects of aligment on firm performance has yielded conflicting results (see, e.g., [15, 102]). In addition, many of these studies tend to adopt a business level perspective [102].

As argued above, the information processing needs–capacities framework suggests that these two dimensions have to be aligned. Therefore, our concept of fit is akin to a matching model [111], in which the highest performance is obtained when the information processing needs resulting from the corporate strategy are satisfied by the information processing capacities provided by the IT capabilities. Like Henderson and Venkatraman [44, p. 472], we maintain that the “inability to realize value from I/T investments is, in part, due to the lack of alignment between the business and I/T strategies of organizations.”

Our conception implies that fit is defined by the three elements reviewed above: (1) the firm’s vertical limits (2) its horizontal limits, and (3) its IT capabilities. As argued in H1, firms using taper integration need more IT capabilities as a result of the need to manage the interaction between internal and external activities [38, 96]. As proposed by H2 and H3, firms pursuing related diversification strategies are more intense users of information [61, 88]. As Tushman and Nadler [108] suggest, firms that have obtained fit will be the best performers [66, 86]. In terms of the underlying dimensions, two situations are possible: (1) one in which a firm has a high level of IT capabilities because it is using taper integration and it is engaged in related diversification, and (2) one in which these capabilities are low because the firm has no diversification and does not use taper integration.

Regarding misfit, the performance consequences will be different [75, 108] depending on the nature of the misfit. In particular, we propose that underfit (insufficient IT capabilities) is more damaging to performance than overfit (excessive IT capabilities) [75]. The reason is that insufficient IT capabilities imply that the firm has a low capacity to perform the tasks assigned. However, when the information processing capacity is higher than information processing needs, there are enough IT capabilities for the activity to be carried out correctly. In this case, the organization is not being completely efficient; however, there is no distortion in the activity, and it can be performed with the desired outcomes. This reasoning leads us to propose:

Hypothesis 4a: The fit between firm boundaries and IT capabilities, as defined in our first three hypotheses, results in superior performance.

Hypothesis 4b: In situations of misfit, performance will be higher when the lack of alignment is due to an excess in IT capabilities (overfit) than when the misfit is due to insufficient IT capabilities (underfit).

Table 1, based on Tushman and Nadler [108], shows the different situations described. The horizontal dimension in Table 1 captures whether the firm has high or low information processing needs. This dimension depends on a firm’s decisions about its corporate strategy. Similarly, the vertical dimension in Table 1 captures whether the firm has a high or low level of IT capabilities.

From Table 1 we can distinguish four different scenarios. Quadrants II and IV represent situations in which the firm has achieved fit as a consequence of matching information processing needs and capacity; therefore, these are the optimal scenarios in terms of firm performance [51, 66]. Quadrants I and III present situations of misfit. These are scenarios in which information processing needs and capacity are not aligned; therefore, firm performance suffers. However, these quadrants are different in terms of the reasons for the misfit. In Quadrant I, the misfit is due to an excessive investment in IT capabilities. In Quadrant III, however, the misfit is due to the inability of the firm to match the information processing needs required by the use of taper integration and related diversification. Therefore, in this case, the misfit stems from insufficient investment in IT.

## Empirical Analysis

## Methodology

Our model considers two types of relationships: (1) the one between information processing needs and information processing capacity, and (2) that between alignment and performance. Accordingly, we employ two different methodologies for the independent tests of H1, H2, and H3, on the one hand, and H4, on the other. In other words, first, we test the relationship between firm boundaries and IT capabilities. The resulting regression provides the sample relationship between the two elements and serves as a benchmark to assess fit [114]. Second, we test the relationship between misfit and performance.

## First Analysis: Firm Boundaries and IT Capabilities

In the first analysis, our dependent variable, IT capabilities, has a censored distribution (the lower threshold is a value of 0). Therefore, we choose a Tobit regression to estimate the relationship between firm boundaries and investments in IT capabilities [36].

We also take further steps in order to be confident about the relationship between firm boundaries and IT capabilities. First, the panel nature of our data allows the estimation of a Tobit model with random effects [36]. The use of this methodology controls for unobserved characteristics whose omission can result in biased estimates. Second, IT capabilities could also have an impact on a firm’s decisions about its boundaries [49]. Hence, the independent variables measuring a firm’s boundaries may be endogenous. Furthermore, the variables representing IT capabilities and firm boundaries could be wrongly measured. We treat both potential problems by using an instrumental variables approach for Tobit models [77]. In this approach, as in Dewan et al. [26], one-year lagged values of the endogenous variables (vertical integration and diversification) and all the exogenous variables are used as instruments. The estimation of Tobit models with endogeneous regressors involves two steps: (1) running a linear regression of each endogenous regressor on the instrumental variables and all the exogenous regressors, and (2) estimating the Tobit model by including the residual terms from step (1) in the list of covariates.<sup>11</sup> The use of one-year lagged values of the endogenous variables as instruments in the first step allows us to isolate the variation in the explanatory variables that is not correlated with the error term.

## Second Analysis: Fit Between Firm Boundaries and IT Capabilities and Performance

To test H4a and H4b, we use the predictions from the first analysis. The use of residuals to examine fit as matching is frequently used in the literature [29, 111, 114]. Following Zajac et al. [114], we compute misfit as the absolute value of the residuals resulting from the difference between the IT capabilities predicted by the baseline model used in the first step and the ones observed [111]. We calculated the residuals using only the significant variables [57]. To test H4b, we also created a variable that identifies the deviation type [114]: we identify the observations in which IT capabilities are higher than those predicted by the model estimated in the first step as having overfit. The second step estimates the impact of misfit and of the deviation type on performance by using the generalized least squares regression. This method is more appropriate than ordinary least squares because of the longitudinal character of our data [63, 114]. To control for common industry and time shocks, the analysis also includes industry and time dummies.

## Sample Description

The data set used in the empirical analysis is the Survey of Business Strategies (ESEE), which has been conducted by the Spanish Ministry of Industry through the SEPI Foundation since 1990. This data source provides information about the characteristics, strategies, and performance of firms belonging to the Spanish manufacturing sector. It also provides information on IT capabilities, including information processing equipment and IT training. The ESEE is designed to provide a representative sample of the population of Spanish manufacturing firms and includes about 2,000 firms per year. The sample covers the entire population of Spanish manufacturing firms with 200 or more employees. Firms with at least 10 employees but fewer than 200 employees were selected by a random sampling scheme in the initial year. As firms drop out of the survey, new firms are incorporated into the sample, using the same sampling criteria as in the base year to ensure that the panel remains representative.<sup>12</sup> Table A.1 (See Appendix A) shows the distribution of firms by size and sector over the period 2002–7.<sup>13</sup>

Two key issues are important when interpreting and comparing our results with extant evidence on firm boundaries and IT capabilities<sup>14</sup>, which mainly come from U.S. firms. First, it is important to bear in mind that the data belong to a European country. The United States is ahead of Europe in both new hightechnology sectors and in the use of information and communication technologies [18, 110]. Investments in information and communication technologies in Spain amounted to 11.6 percent of total gross fixed capital formation in 2011, just below the the European Union average (fifteen countries) and far below the figures of the United Kingdom (19.9 percent) and the United States (22.1 percent) [79]. Second, most of the samples previously used focus on large firms.<sup>15</sup> One strength of our data set is that the analysis includes large and small and medium-size enterprises (SMEs). As a consequence, our sample is expected to show more variation in terms of firm boundaries and IT capabilities than those used in previous studies. The presence of SMEs is a critical issue as firms with fewer than 250 employees constitute 99 percent of all the firms in Spain and the European Union (EU-28) [30]. Similarly, firms with fewer than 100 employees make up 98.22 percent of all U.S. firms [109].

## Variable Description and Measurements

## Dependent Variables

IT stock. The use of IT investments as a proxy for IT capabilities is well accepted in the information systems literature, in general, and in the literature on IT business value, in particular. For example, Chari et al. [16] use investments in IT to measure “IT-conferred capabilities.” Similarly, Ravichandran et al. [88] focus on IT spending, and other studies, such as Dewan and Ren [28], use IT capital stock as a proxy for IT capabilities.

We measure IT capabilities through a variable that combines investments in information processing equipment with a capitalized value of information technology training expenses. Therefore, our measure not only considers investments in technological equipment but also related investments in the skills that are necessary to use them. As stressed above, this measurement is consistent with previous research in the information systems literature [16, 28, 88].

A variable measuring stock was used because it seems to be a more stable and suitable indicator than annual investment, which is more sensitive to short-term fluctuations in firm performance. The variable was constructed using the perpetual inventory method, taking into account the investments in information processing equipment $( I P E _ { i t } )$ from 1994 to 2007. Following Lichtenberg [59], the annual depreciation rate (δ) was assumed to be 15 percent, and in order to calculate the first year value, a presample growth rate of 5 percent was considered.

Regarding the capitalized value of IT training, we assume that computer and IT training (T) create an asset with a service life of three years [25, 28]. Therefore, we calculated a three-year capitalization of computer and information technology training using a depreciation rate, λ, of 33 percent. The combination of the two dimensions, the stock of investments in information processing equipment and the threeyear capitalized valued of IT training, is obtained through the following specification of IT stock:

$$
I T _ {i t} = (1 - \delta) * I T _ {i t - 1} + I P E _ {i t} + \sum_ {k = 0} ^ {2} (1 - \lambda) ^ {k} * T _ {i t - k}
$$

where k is an indicator variable that takes the values 0, 1, and 2, corresponding to the three years considered to capitalize computer and IT training. We take the natural logarithm of this variable as the final measure of IT stock.

Return on sales. To test H4a and H4b, we need a measure of performance. Firm profitability is measured by dividing a firm’s earnings before depreciation, interest payments, and taxes by the total value of production and other operating income and multiplying the resulting figure by 100. This profitability measure is directly provided by the SEPI Foundation and has been used in other studies [1, 74, 94].

## Independent Variables

Outsourcing. Regarding vertical integration (H1), we focus on the production function of manufacturing firms and measure the extent to which firms simultaneously use the market and the hierarchy to organize their transactions. To measure taper integration, we used the following ratio:

$$
\text { Outsourcing } _ {\mathrm{i}, \mathrm{t}} = \frac {\text { Purchases   of   final   products   and   tailored   components(€) }}{\text { Total   value   of   purchases(€) }} \times 1 0 0
$$

This ratio ranges from 0 (when all production is carried out internally) to 100 (when the firm outsources all its production). Other values of this ratio indicate that the firm concurrently outsources and carries out production function activities.<sup>16</sup> This measure has been used before in the information systems literature [3, 23].

Diversification. We use the entropy measures [80] of total (total diversification), related (related diversification), and unrelated (unrelated diversification) diversification. The variable related diversification captures the extent of related diversification within industry groups in the same three-digit NACE code. Unrelated diversification is a measure of unrelated diversification in a firm’s output in different two-digit NACE codes. Finally, total diversification is a measure of total diversification, which results from the sum of the other two entropy measures. These diversification measures are directly provided by the SEPI Foundation and have been previously used, both in the strategic management literature and in the context of information systems [26, 91]. They are used to test H2 and H3.

Misfit. In order to test H4a, we needed a measure of the extent of alignment between firm boundaries and IT capabilities. We followed a conceptualization of fit as a “theoretically defined match between two related variables” [111, p. 430], namely, information processing needs and capacities [29, 65, 114]. Our measure of misfit is obtained through the difference between the firms’ IT stock and that predicted by our model. To create it, we calculate the absolute value of the residuals resulting from the information processing model. In accordance with H4a, the effect of misfit on firm performance should be negative.

Overfit. In order to test H4b, we need an additional variable capturing the type of misfit, overfit and underfit. Accordingly, we created a dummy variable that takes the value 1 when the deviation is overfit, and 0 otherwise. Therefore, misfit assesses whether a firm correctly matches information processing needs and capacities, whereas overfit identifies the type of misfit.<sup>17</sup> Both variables and their interaction are used to test H4b.

## Control Variables

Market stability. The predictability of the environment is one factor to take into account when analyzing information processing needs. Activities developed in unstable markets generate higher information processing needs [86], and the need to invest in IT capabilities may increase. To control for this effect, we included a dummy variable taking the value 1 when all the markets served by the firm were characterized as stable, and 0 otherwise. Therefore, we expect the sign of this variable to be negative in all the analyses (more stable environments require fewer IT capabilities).

Foreign capital. This variable indicates whether foreign investors own part of the firm’s capital. This variable is included because foreign parent companies often transfer new technology to domestic affiliates [49]. We include a dummy variable taking a value of 1 if the presence of foreign capital in the focal firm is higher than 30 percent [68], and 0 otherwise.

Export intensity. The literature on the diffusion of new technologies suggests the importance of taking a firm’s presence in international markets into account when analyzing the adoption of IT. Export-oriented firms use IT mainly to find information about the latest market trends and for the coordination of activities with foreign partners [58]. They may also invest in IT to tackle the higher levels of competition present in international markets. Therefore, we expected a positive relationship between a firm’s export orientation and IT capabilities. Export intensity is measured by the ratio of total exports to sales [58].

Firm size. Previous studies analyzing the diffusion of innovations relate the size of a firm to the adoption of new technologies. These studies establish a positive relationship between the two variables, offering reasons such as the possibilities of obtaining economies of scale or large firms’ better access to resources [2, 56]. Regarding the adoption of IT, Battisti et al. [6] argue that this variable could represent several firm characteristics that explain the positive relationship between size and the adoption of IT. These characteristics include efficiency, management abilities, past innovations, variations in the costs of acquiring the new technology, and scale economies. Size is measured by the number of employees working for a given firm (in thousands), and based on the previous literature [6, 8, 50], we expected a positive sign for the accompanying coefficient.

Market concentration. Market structure has traditionally been linked to the degree of innovation and to the incentives for firms to invest in a given technology.

However, the direction of the influence of the market structure is ambiguous in most cases [92]. As in previous studies [90], we have calculated a concentration variable that adds up the market share of the four largest firms in each industry $( C R _ { 4 } )$

R&D intensity. This variable is included to recognize the influence of absorptive capacity in increasing the likelihood of adopting new technologies. The inclusion of this variable reflects the fact that investments in new technologies depend not only on the amount of available information but also on a firm’s ability to assimilate and apply it for commercial ends [21]. We measure R&D intensity as the ratio of R&D spending to sales and expect that firms presenting a higher ratio will have a greater capacity to take advantage of information and will be more likely to invest in technology acquisition [34, 35].

Industry variables. Other industry features, besides market concentration, could also affect the amount of IT capabilities. To control for these, we include nineteen dummy variables to capture the differences between the twenty industries identified in the survey. These variables are included in all the models.

Time variables. To control for the higher likelihood of adoption associated with the learning-by-contact process, that is, the epidemic effect [56], we include dummy variables. These variables also take into account any other temporal effect related to IT capabilities. Similarly, time shocks can also explain changes in profitability. Therefore, these time dummies are used in the performance models.

## Discussion and Conclusions

## Implications for Research

The results have implications for the study of firm boundaries and IT capabilities. First, the discovery that firms with medium levels of outsourcing present higher levels of IT capabilities is interesting in terms of studying the different forms through which firms may organize their transactions. This result suggests that firms using taper integration have the highest coordination needs due to interdependences between in-house and outsourced production. Harrigan [38] and Rothaermel et al. [96] highlight the advantages of taper integration, arguing that it allows the firm to simultaneously monitor suppliers, produce efficiently, and improve processes, among other benefits. Prior research supports the use of both sources simultaneously, referring to arguments based on uncertainty and synergies [39], control and innovation processes [12], or firm and supplier expertise and technological uncertainty [41]. However, the benefits of these advantages come at the expense of managing a complex process that combines the market and the hierarchy [96]. As a result, firms must invest in IT capabilities that allow external and internal information to be fused.

Second, the fact that taper integration advantages are important in understanding IT capabilities indicates that the latter are not merely a substitute for other inputs of production [17]. For example, IT capabilities can combine internal and external knowledge in a given area, which has been associated with the adoption of Internet business practices [17]. Furthermore, this idea also appears to be consistent with recent research indicating that investments in IT are related to profitability and that they “may have created further opportunities for value creation and value capture” [71, p. 219]. 23 Importantly, this amplified role of IT seems to fit in our context, in which outsourcing is taken advantage of for a variety of purposes beyond just cutting costs [45].

Third, given the necessary fit between the information processing needs and capacities implied by the model and the relation of this fit to firm performance, our results suggest that any change in vertical or horizontal limits should be accompanied by a change in IT capabilities. Variations in the horizontal limits toward more related diversification modify internal coordination costs and create incentives to invest in IT capabilities. Similarly, regarding vertical integration, the decision to use taper integration creates the need to increase the information processing capacity of the firm through IT capabilities.

The study also contributes to the contradictory evidence found in IT business value studies that use accounting or market measures. For example, using returns on sales and other performance measures, Bharadwaj [10] finds that firms with high IT capability outperform a control sample of firms. This result is consistent with the evidence presented in Santhanam and Hartono [98], who offer a replication of Bharadwaj’s [10] study. However, other studies have not found positive results. Chae et al. [14] do not find a significant effect on IT capabilities and return on sales. Tam [103] analyzes the effect of IT on business performance and market valuation in four economies in Asia: Hong Kong, Singapore, Malaysia, and Taiwan. The results show that the relationship between IT capital and return on sales is negative and significant for Taiwan and not significant for the others economies. Importantly, the sample analyzed in our study is representative of the population of firms. The inclusion of small an medium-size firms is an interesting addition, as previous evidence has predominantely focused on large firms.

On a more general note, the results are on the whole consistent with the information processing needs–capacities model, and they also reveal a promising way to integrate the literature on information systems with that on technology diffusion. On the one hand, the results support the arguments developed by Galbraith [33] and subsequent papers [108]. For instance, our results lend support to the idea that activities requiring tight coordination generate higher information processing needs and higher incentives to invest in IT capabilities, as occurs in the case of taper integration. Likewise, firms that diversify are likely to develop a more varied number of tasks than firms with no diversification, increasing coordination costs. However, the results also support the idea that routine tasks developed in more predictable environments generate fewer information processing needs. This is demonstrated by the fact that market stability is negatively related to IT. On the other hand, our success in integrating the analysis of firm boundaries suggested by the information processing needs–capacities model and the variables from the technology diffusion literature recommend that these two fields of literature be integrated more frequently. As demonstrated herein, the two fields of literature appear to be complementary in their depiction of the types of factors that can explain technology adoption. However, the impression that the variables suggested by the literature of diffusion and those proposed by information processing needs and capacities have evolved independently impedes a complete overview of the factors conditioning technology diffusion.

## Implications for Practice

This study has several implications for practicing managers. On the one hand, the curvilinear relationship between IT capabilities and outsourcing is particularly noteworthy. Our results emphasize the complexity of managing outsourced processes in some contexts, especially when the outsourced activities are important for the firm and when they are subject to strong interdependences with the internal activities. IT capabilities may be used to successfully manage this complexity. Similarly, when diversifying, managers should also pay attention to the differences between related and unrelated diversification. Our results suggest that greater IT capabilities are needed in firms that follow a related diversification strategy because of the higher coordination and control costs associated with this strategy. On the other hand, regarding the implications for performance, our results suggest that managers should invest more resources in IT capabilities if their firms are committed to both related diversification and taper integration. Furthermore, they suggest that there are risks associated with underinvesting in IT. However, these risks do not exist when they overinvest.

## Limitations of the Study

Our study is subject to some limitations and also points to avenues for further research. First, our data set does not provide us with a disaggregation of the stock of information technologies into the specific technologies used. From an information systems perspective, a precise identification of the technologies used for managing information flows could be useful for understanding the mechanisms by which these technologies create value. Therefore, future research could focus on different types of IT and study their roles in taking advantage and reducing the costs of decisions related to the horizontal and vertical boundaries of the firm. Similarly, our measure of outsourcing does not allow an identification of the specific inputs that are acquired by the firm.

Second, the study analyzes the increase in information processing capacity derived solely from IT capabilities, and it does not consider other interorganizational mechanisms that may help a firm increase information capacity. Along with IT capabilities, Bensaou and Venkatraman [7] propose two additional interorganizational mechanisms for this purpose. On the one hand, firms could increase their information processing capacities through the use of interorganizational structural mechanisms (number of information channels, frequency of and extent to which information exchange between firms is used for coordination vs. control purposes). On the other hand, process mechanisms (the use of a cooperative vs. collaborative approach) for solving disputes, commitment and joint efforts between firms, etc.) could also affect the information flow within structural mechanisms and, therefore, a firm’s information processing capacity. In the same vein, Mani et al. [66] argue that IT is only part of the information capabilities of the business process outsourcing relationship. In other words, although it is a difficult challenge, the governance structure and relational processes of business process outsourcing should also be studied. Similarly, firms also use different mechanisms for the coordination of internal activities. Although our methodology has controlled for this through the inclusion of firm-specific effects, not including them explicitly impedes a full understanding of the mechanisms used and of their interrelations with IT. Future research should consider interorganizational structural and process mechanisms in order to fully understand information processing capacity.

Third, although we do not distinguish between different types of activities, it is highly likely that the benefits and the costs associated with taper integration depend on the nature of the tasks involved and on their interrelationships with other activities. For example, tight coordination between internal and external activities could be very important in some cases, whereas it could be of minor or no importance in others. This will have an impact on the expected relationship between the scope of the firm and investments in IT. Mani et al. [66, p. 40] point out that “the execution of more strategic processes that involves direct interactions with customers . . . with suppliers . . . and with employees . . . is characterized by more unpredictable information changes.” Therefore, our analysis focuses on a function that is likely to be important for the firm and more unpredictable than others. The question of whether our results can be extended to other functions or not is left for further research.

Some comments are also necessary about the generalizability of our results. On the one hand, we have used a representative sample of Spanish manufacturing firms, including information about firms operating in different sectors, with differing sizes and with variability in their boundary decisions and IT. Therefore, we are confident that our results are relevant and reliable. On the other hand, some caveats should be taken into account. First, the sample belongs to a country that has traditionally lagged in terms of technology adoption vis-à-vis other developed economies. Similarly, Spanish firms are less diversified than their European counterparts [69, 100]. Given that the specific relationships identified depend on the characteristics of the population, we should take the point estimates with some care. In addition, future research could choose samples with a wider variability in both IT capabilities and firm boundaries. In any case, if Spanish firms underinvest in IT capabilities, these estimates may be taken as a conservative assessment of the true relationship between firm boundaries and IT capabilities. Similarly, if the optimal relationship between firm boundaries and IT capabilities were stronger than we identified, some of the firms characterized by overfit would, in fact, be in a situation of fit. Second, the sample does not include service firms; therefore, it offers no indication of how they might differ in their investment behavior from manufacturing firms. Third, we have studied taper integration in a business function that is both complex and important for manufacturing firms. The extrapolation of these results to other simpler and less relevant functions of the vertical chain should be undertaken with care. Finally, our analysis has not paid specific attention to externally oriented technologies [28]. Consequently, the results on the relationship between taper integration and IT capabilities might not be applicable, and further research that considers the type of technology is needed.

## Conclusions

This research had two objectives: (1) to study the relationship between the vertical and horizontal scope of the firm and IT capabilities, and (2) to analyze the consequences of misfit on performance. First, we found that taper integration has the greatest influence on information processing needs and, therefore, demands the highest IT capabilities. As predicted, outsourcing is curvilinearly related to IT capabilities, with the use of the hierarchy and the market at each extreme. Our results confirm that the internal coordination costs generated by diversification increase information processing needs and create incentives to enhance IT capabilities. Second, regarding the analysis of the consequences of misfit on firm performance, our conclusion is that, on average, firms that do not align their information processing needs and capacities obtain a lower performance than those in a situation of fit. Moreover, the consequences of misfit are positive in firms with overfit.
