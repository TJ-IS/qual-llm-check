# An alternative measure of the ICT-Opportunity Index

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 25 November 2007 Received in revised form 6 November 2009 Accepted 12 February 2010 Available online 29 April 2010

Keywords: ICT-Opportunity Index DEA-Opportunity Index Information society Digital divide Linear programming Data Envelopment Analysis

## A B S T R A C T

We developed an alternative approach for measuring information and communication technology (ICT), applying Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA) using data from the International Telecommunications Union as a sample of 183 economies. We compared the ICT-Opportunity Index (ICT-OI) with our DEA-Opportunity Index (DEA-OI) and found a high correlation between the two. Our findings suggest that both indices are consistent in their measurement of digital opportunity, though differences still exist in different regions. Our new DEA-OI offers much more than the ICT-OI. Using our model, the target and peer groups for each country can be identified.

## 1. Introduction

The measurement of different aspects of the information society has been difficult, if not misleading, and the International Telecommunication Union (ITU) has therefore been developing a single ICT index. During the first World Summit on Information Society (WSIS) in December 2003 [9], the heads of states declared their commitment to the importance of benchmarking and measuring progress toward the information society. Consequently, they re-affirmed their Geneva commitments in their second summit held in Tunis in 2005 [10]. This summit approved the core ICT indicators and two composite indices: ICT-Opportunity Index (ICT-OI) and the Digital Opportunity Index (DOI). These were agreed methodologies aiming to turning the digital divide into a digital opportunity for all world economies, especially for those countries that have been left behind or marginalized.

The DOI is based on three clusters: opportunity, infrastructure, and utilization and uses the arithmetic average score measurement, see [8], whereas, the ICT-OI was measured using a geometric mean and focused more on conventional ICT (such as television) and also measured literacy and school enrolment. Thus, the two indices present different aspects of the digital divide but are found to be highly correlated. However, that was not our prime concern, since this comparison had been already made by the ITU. Though both composite indices were noted as WSIS outcomes, they were by no means the only measures available. There are other indices, such as the ICT Diffusion Index, developed by United Nations Conference on Trade and Development (UNCTAD) and the Network Readiness Index, published by the World Economic Forum (WEF) and European Institute of Business Administration (INSEAD). These composite indices measure different aspects of the information society. It was ITU’s intention to develop a single ICT index for use in the long term.

There are increasing numbers of people and countries around the world that are enjoying access to the benefits from ICT. However, ITU’s reports have indicated that disparities and inequality in access are evolving: the digital divide is taking on new forms in terms of differences in the speed and quality of access to ICT.

Access to and use of new ICT remains an increasing concern among scholars [20,30], policymakers [12,15,27–29], IT stakeholders [3,13,23]. Most empirical studies have dealt with determinants of the digital divide [6,21,22,25] and the ICT impact on economic growth [11,16,24,17]. There are wider consensus in measuring ICT-productivity growth [1,26] or telecommunications efficiency [14] at both the country and firm level of analysis [18,19]. Though there has been an interest among international institutions (e.g. UNCTAD, UNDP, UNESCO) on regional and country level ICT benefits, impacts, and access; there has been no attempt to develop and measure a single ICT index (except that used by ITU) by using non-parametric linear programming methodology to accurately measure the digital divide and thus show the disparities that exists.

Existing literature on the digital divide or ICT impacts has indicated that econometric techniques are widely used (e.g. regression analysis), descriptive statistics is commonly used, and there are some studies using factor analysis. The availability and quality of data are common issues faced by anyone studying a complex topic. Our goal was therefore, to fill this gap by providing an alternative non-parametric measure of cross-country ICT access and use, employing the most recent data that is publicly available from ITU.

Using DEA (Data Envelopment Analysis), we produced a new ICT index that enhances the current measurement tools by providing a single ICT index. We then compared results of this with the ITU’s ICT-Opportunity Index (ICT-OI).

## 2. Measuring the ICT-Opportunity Index

The ICT-OI has been generally accepted as a statistical tool to track the digital divide by measuring the relative difference in ICT-Opportunity levels among economies and regions over time. It was based on the dual concepts of a country’s productive capacity and consumption and therefore depended on the degree of a country’s infodensity (the slice of a country’s overall capital and labour stocks, indicative of productive capacity) and its info-use (the consumption flows of ICT). Technically, the ICT-Opportunity value is derived and expressed as:

$$
\mathrm{ICT} - \mathrm{OI} = \sqrt [ 2 ]{(i n f o - d e n s i t y \times i n f o - u s e)}\tag{1}
$$

It consists of 10 indicators that are grouped into 4 sub-indices: networks and skills as indicators of infodensity and uptake and intensity as measures of info-use. Networks are captured by penetration rates of fixed telephone lines, mobile cellular subscribers, and international Internet bandwidth. Skills are measured by adult literacy rates and gross enrolment rates for primary, secondary, and tertiary education. Uptake is captured through Internet users, computers per 100 inhabitants, and the proportion of households with a TV. Intensity indicators involve Broadband Internet subscribers per 100 inhabitants and international outgoing telephone traffic (min) per capita.

The 2007 ICT-OI split world economies into four major groupings depending on the degree to which the country had access and use of ICT: high levels (249 and above), upper levels (between 150 and 248) medium levels (between 68 and 149), and low levels (between 12 and 68).

## 3. Methodology: Data Envelopment Analysis

Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA) is a linear programming technique that initially was developed by Charnes, Cooper and Rhodes to facilitate measurement of efficiency when faced with production units (e.g. a country) that employs multiple inputs to produce multiple outputs. It focuses on evaluating performance of decision making units (DMUs) based on evaluation of the relative efficiency of comparable units. Using observed data, DEA forms an empirical efficient boundary. If the DMU lies on the boundary, it is termed efficient, otherwise it is inefficient. DEA also provides efficient scores and reference units for inefficient DMUs. Reference units are existence or hypothetical units on the efficient surface; which can be regarded as a target for inefficient units.

DEA can be either input- or output-orientated. In the first case, the DEA method defines the frontier by seeking the maximum possible proportional reduction in input usage, with output levels held constant, for each firm. However, for the output-orientated case, the DEA method seeks the maximum proportional increase in output production, with input levels held fixed.

## 5. Discussion of results and the comparison

As shown in Table 2, the overall average of world economies was 125.8 in ICT-OI and 90.3 in DEA-OI. Sweden had the highest score from both indices of 377.7 (ICT-OI) and 100 (DEA-OI). Newly industrialized countries (NICs) of Asia (e.g. Korea, Hong Kong-China, Singapore, Taiwan-China, and Macao-China) had high ICT and DEA scores comparable to those of developed economies. These results implied that their economies provided high productive capacity through extensive ICT infrastructure and highly skilled labour. At the same time, they showed high scores in their info-use uptake and intensity, suggesting a high level of ICT consumption.

Table 3 further illustrated the existence of a digital divide among regions and helps identified digital opportunities for those who were left behind or marginalized. Africa garnered the lowest average score for both indices (46.2 and 80.5 for ICT-OI and DEA-OI, respectively), suggesting a very low productive capacity and consumption of ICT. The result further suggested that African nations should focus on the development of infrastructure networks and on upgrading education and skills toward a more productive capacity and an increased consumption of ICT. Europe, as expected, had the highest average scores of 227.6 for ICT-OI and 96.99 for DEA-OI. This is not surprising, since many developed economies in Europe exhibited a high level of access and use of ICT. The rest of the regions in Asia, America, and Oceania were all categorized, on average, as medium in their level of ICT access and use. Furthermore, the rankings for both the ICT and DEA were also closer, if not exactly the same in some cases.

To further validate our model, we ran a correlation test and found a very high correlation between the ICT-OI and DEA-OI, with a correlation coefficient of 0.89. This is illustrated in Fig. 6. This suggests that both indices are consistent in their measurement of digital opportunity though some differences exist in regional groups. Some factors that explain such ICT gaps are disparities in wealth and income between countries, differences in the literacy and school enrolment, and the variance in technology uptake and intensity, such as access to and use of the Internet, Broadband communications, computers, and television.

Fig. 7 illustrates the percent distribution of economies as to their scale level between the two indices. The figure shows almost the same results for the two indices.

Under the DEA scale, 47 economies (25.7%) have achieved a high level of ICT access and use, while 46 (25.1%) from ICT-OL scale are at the medium level. In both indices, there were 25 member states of the OECD (except the Czech Republic, Greece, Mexico, the Slovak Republic, and Turkey) and the four Asian NICs, that had a consistently high score. There was no African country included in the high-level category for either index. In the upper level, 45 economies (DEA-OI scale) had achieved an elevated level of ICT access and use. This level often had an imbalanced combination, for example, a high level of network but low uptake or intensity. Such information can guide policymakers in identifying whether and where their countries are relatively weak access to the information society.

## 5.1. Regional groupings

## 5.1.1. Asia

There are a total of 46 Asian countries in our sample. Consistently on both indices, Table 3 shows that Asia is second to Europe when it comes to high-level ICT access and use, but first in the medium level category. Moreover, Asia places 1st and 3rd rank in the upper-level category based on DEA scale and ICTscale, respectively. Surprisingly we found that Asia was second to Africa in the low level category but less than half of the total sample of Asian economies had obtained a lower score in both indices. This new result implied that Asian economies were rapidly catching up and narrowing the gap of the digital divide, perhaps, by enhancing education and skills and investment in technology.

They were deficient in their provisions of infrastructure networks as well as having lower rates of Internet and Broadband penetration for their population. ## 5.1.2. The Americas

The America region consisted of 35 economies. Most American countries were categorized as upper level and some to medium level, except Canada and U.S. that both belonged to the high level. Based on our DEA result, no country in America had a low level of ICT. Haiti belonged to a low level category using the ICT-OI scale. It should be noted however; that its ICT-OI ranking (147) and DEA-OI ranking (129) were close. There are six (6) economies that belong consistently to the medium level category: the Dominican Republic, El Salvador, Guatemala, Honduras, Nicaragua, and Paraguay. Eleven (11) countries were categorized into the upper level: Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Ecuador, Guyana, Panama, Peru, Puerto Rico, St. Vincent, Uruguay, and Venezuela. Consistently, four (4) countries obtained higher scores: Aruba, Barbados, Canada, and the United States.

## 5.1.3. Africa

Most economies from the African region of our sample had a low level of ICT access and use. More than half of the 51 African countries obtained lower scores, suggesting that they are lagging behind other economies. Our two indices, moreover, indicated that there are 33 countries in the low level: Angola, Benin, Burkina Faso, Burundi, Cameroon, the Central African Republic, Chad, Comoros, Congo, Coˆte d’Ivoire, D.R. Congo, Djibouti, Equatorial Guinea, Eritrea, Ethiopia, Gambia, Ghana, Guinea, Guinea-Bissau, Kenya, Madagaspar, Malawi, Mali, Mauritania, Mozambique, Niger, Nigeria, Rwanda, Somalia, Tanzania, Togo, Uganda, and Zambia.

On the other hand, seven countries belonged in the medium level: Algeria, Botswana, Egypt, Gabon, Namibia, Tunisia, and Zimbabwe. Overall, the African region is second to Asia in terms of percentage share of the world at the medium level. Most importantly, both indices are consistent in reporting that no African country belongs to the upper-high level. These results may help policymakers recommend improvement of their weak network infrastructure, their Internet penetration, and even to launch Broadband services to benefit from new ICT.

## 5.1.4. Europe

None of the European countries obtained a low score with either of the indices; there are 23 countries that obtained high scores consistently with both. These countries include high income and small size economies such as Belgium, Denmark, Finland, Iceland, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, Norway, Sweden and Switzerland. Meanwhile, Albania was the only country that was classified at the medium level. The upper-level category consisted of 7 countries that are mostly newly emerging economies: Bosnia, Bulgaria, Moldova, Romania, Serbia and Montenegro, The former Yugoslav Republic (TFYR) of Macedonia, and Turkey. The latter is a member of the OECD but obtained a consistent upper-level score from both indices.

## 5.1.5. Oceania

Oceania had 11 countries in our analysis. The developed countries of Australia and New Zealand were consistent in obtaining high scores with both indices. Fiji and Samoa are also consistent in belonging at the medium level, and none is identified at the upper level. Moreover, Papua New Guinea is the only country that is classified as having low level ICT access and use, according to both indices.

## 5.2. Added value of the DEA-Opportunity Index

Our DEA-Opportunity Index provides an overall objective numerical score, rankings, and potential improvement targets for each of the inefficient units/countries and also identifies peers for each country. The new index can also provide information by which inputs can be reduced and which outputs can be increased in order for a country to reach a more effective level. This information could help the ITU in identifying appropriate indicators for ICT measurement. Policymakers in each country could use the slack information to help them identify which factors need improvement to achieve a high level of ICT access and consumption or help in bridging the digital divide.

## 6. Conclusion

We developed a new approach to measuring ICT by using the DEA model. We found that our index methodology was highly correlated to the ICT-Opportunity Index and suggest that it can be taken as an alternative measurement approach for benchmarking and measuring progress toward the information society. Our index is novel in its measurement of digital opportunity and a supplement to the current ITU’s index methodologies.

There are important policy implications that can be drawn from our empirical results to help government policymakers craft effective ICT policies. Governments can use the results to identify their positions in the global ICT, realize their strengths and weaknesses, and take appropriate measures to boost their productive capacity and ICT use.

To attain the high-level status and enjoy ICT benefits, low level countries must strive for persistent innovation and advancement of their infrastructure networks and continuous upgrading of their education and skills. By doing so, the digital divide that exists between countries and regions may be turned into digital opportunities, and ICT benefits may be evenly distributed across the world.

Our paper has some limitations. Some advantages of DEA may be considered as disadvantage, such as being deterministic in nature and its inability to allow for random errors. Any deviation from the efficient frontier of ICT was assumed to be due to poor access to each country’s ICT. The results of DEA should thus be taken with caution in crafting effective ICT policies. Also the generated DEA-index is based only on the 10 indicators due to a difficulty in accessing more complete data sets. Increasing the number of indicators may change the rating and ranking of countries in the global ICT context. Thus, policymakers should interpret our results with caution.
