# Investor Platform Choice: Herding, Platform Attributes, and Regulations

ABSTRACT: Online peer-to-peer (P2P) lending, one of the most successful technology-enabled initiatives in the fintech revolution, has drastically changed the way individual investors and borrowers meet and transact. While prior research has found herding among investors at the listing level, such social behavior has been underexplored at a macro, platform level. In this study, we attempt to fill this gap by examining whether subsequent investors follow their predecessors’ actions when choosing which platform to invest, and if so, how various platform attributes and regulations moderate herding behavior. We collected a novel data set from leading platforms in a large P2P lending market. Our baseline analysis reveals that herding exists at the platform level. Using a multilevel model, we further identify several interesting moderators: the investor’s herding behavior is accentuated by platforms market share and the cumulative amount funded, but attenuated by their time in operation. Finally, we find that government regulatory events dampen the magnitude of the herding effect, suggesting that more information disclosure and stricter operation standards reduce the value of observational learning. The results from our analysis provide implications for P2P lending investors, platform designers, and policymakers.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: crowdfunding, fintech, herding, multilevel models, peer-to-peer lending, regulations.

Information technology (IT) has been changing the way business is done [26], leading to the revolution in the financial technology industry. One of the most successful initiatives in the fintech revolution is peer-to-peer (P2P) lending, a practice that connects investors with borrowers directly without involving a traditional financial intermediary. P2P lending activity has been expanding in recent years, attracting large transaction volumes. For example, Lending Club and Prosper are the two leading P2P lending platforms in the United States. The former transacted over US\$2.4 billion in 2013 and the latter transacted US\$6 billion in 2014. In the United Kingdom, total P2P lending volume has doubled every six months, reaching US\$1.7 billion [4]. Although P2P lending is increasingly prevalent, investors’ behavior under this novel finance model is still underexplored. Understanding investors’ choices in the P2P lending market and the underlying decision-making mechanisms is crucial to both researchers and platform owners.

The growing peer-to-peer lending market has changed the way investors make investment decisions. In particular, it facilitates investors’ herding behavior, a well-documented social dynamic in which individuals follow the actions of their peers [3, 7, 8] in several ways. First, P2P lending operates without a formal intermediary, such as a commercial bank. Individual investors directly transact with borrowers who are often strangers via a third-party online platform. As a result, they face high information asymmetry [24, 25], and therefore greater transaction risk. Investors may refer to the decisions made by previous investors to gather insight on the trustworthiness of borrowers and the credibility of the platforms. Second, the increasing digital visibility of this online lending environment grants investors greater access to information about their fellow investors [7].

For example, investors can observe the bidding logs of a particular listing, the characteristics and historical transactions of online lending platforms. Thus, it is possible for prospective investors to observe and learn from their fellow investors’ investment strategies. Third, there is a huge amount of structured and unstructured information regarding borrowers, listings and platforms [15]. Individual investors, however, usually lack the expertise and time to exploit this information for better decision making [1, 5, 21]. Following the actions of others seems to be a convenient way for investors to deal with such information overload [12]. Taken together, the P2P lending practice shows that the choices of other investors have the potential to affect the later investor, which is an important practical question that needs to be addressed.

On the other hand, researchers’ understanding of how P2P lending investors behave, especially how they respond to others’ choices mentioned above, is still limited. Existing studies on P2P lending have found that prior investors’ actions have a strong influence on later investors’ decisions (i.e., investors’ herding behaviors) [21, 22, 39]. However, they mainly concentrate on investors’ herding behavior at the listing (i.e., loan application) level, and mostly on a single lending platform. We believe that understanding such a compelling social dynamic at a macro level is of equal significance for the following reasons. First, from a perspective of decision making, online P2P lending investors need to first choose platforms that are trustworthy and reliable before looking into loan applications. This is not a simple task given the huge number of alternative platforms. Investigating herding behavior at the platform level will help us gain more insight into investors’ behavior at the early stage of the decision process.

Second, from an investor’s perspective, choosing a platform to invest is much more complex than choosing a commerce site for purchasing. By their nature, P2P lending platforms vary substantially, either in observable characteristics (e.g., borrower profiles) or in unobservable characteristics (e.g., management style), leading to dissimilar levels of risk perceived by a prospective investor. The heterogeneity among platforms may affect investors’ platform choice and perhaps their social learning behavior. Understanding the moderating role of platform attributes on herding behavior may also provide useful managerial implications, which can lead to actionable strategy for platform owners.

Last, and perhaps most important, the P2P lending sector has attracted the government’s concern because of the risk stemming from information asymmetry that investors encounter. To protect investors, the government issues regulations for the P2P lending industry, for example, the Jumpstart Our Business Startups (JOBS) Act. Thus, it is important to evaluate whether regulations imposed by the government can help mitigate investors’ uncertainty, which may be reflected in a dynamic change in their herding behavior. Taken together, understanding P2P lending investors’ herding behavior at the platform level is crucial to researchers, platform owners and policymakers.

In this research, therefore, we aim to examine online P2P lending investors herding behavior at a macro, platform level. Specifically, we seek to address three research questions: (1) whether investors follow prior peers’ actions when they decide which platforms to invest; (2) whether and how platform attributes moderate the magnitude of herding behavior; and (3) whether government-initiated regulatory events have an impact on investors’ herding behavior in the P2P lending market.

We collected a weekly panel data set from 127 peer-to-peer leading platforms over a period of 44 weeks from a large P2P lending market. We have access to a variety of platform attributes that are available to potential investors, including number of listings, number of investors, number of borrowers, loan duration, loan amount, interest rate, operation time, search index, transaction volume, and registered capital. Our measure of herding is constructed based on the cumulative number of investors of a platform during a given week. This observable summary statistic is informative when studying investors’ herding behavior [7], as neither the investors nor the researchers know the order in which previous investors acted at the market level.

Our results suggest that herding exists at the platform level: subsequent investors follow their predecessors’ actions when choosing their own P2P lending platforms, after controlling for unobserved platform heterogeneity and time effects. Next, we explore the moderating role of different platform attributes. We utilize a multilevel mixed-effects model (also known as a hierarchical model), which allows for variation in herding effects across platforms for better control. Our results indicate that the herding behavior of subsequent investors is rational, as it is moderated by various platform attributes. For example, investors’ herding behavior is accentuated by platforms’ market share, but attenuated by the amount of time that they have been in operation. Furthermore, to examine the impact of government regulations on investors’ herding behavior, we identify five government regulatory events, and introduce them into our baseline model. Our results show that the magnitude of the herding effect declined after the occurrence of those events. We also conduct additional checks that demonstrate the robustness of our results.

Our research contributes to the literature in the following aspects. First, we contribute to P2P lending research by providing empirical evidence of investors’ herding behavior at a macro level—the platform level. For P2P lending investors, choosing a platform is their first step when making an investment. The quality of platforms may affect their potential returns systematically. This perspective is of great significance to both researchers and practitioners, but has not been carefully examined. Existing P2P lending work mainly explores investors’ herding behavior at the listing level [22, 39]. Our work also complements research on P2P platforms by examining an investor-driven mechanism (i.e., herding) across multiple platforms, whereas prior research typically investigates platform-driven mechanisms in one or two sample platforms.

Second, we contribute to the herding literature by offering additional insights: P2P lending investors’ herding behavior seems to be rational at the platform level. As has long been documented in the finance literature, herding can be rational or irrational. Despite some researchers’ findings that herding in P2P lending is rational, this evidence has mostly been at the listing level. For example, investors’ herding can be modified by favorable and unfavorable listing attributes [39]. Our work, which quantifies the moderating role of various platform attributes, demonstrates that investors are rational followers. They adjust their herding behaviors in accordance with the information revealed by different platform attributes. This finding helps mitigate the concern that investors are easily misled in this nascent financial market setting.

Moreover, to the best of our knowledge, our study is among the few that empirically look into the relationship between government regulations and P2P lending investors’ behavior. The existing P2P lending literature has studied the impact of policy changes on the platform—such as pricing changes, elimination of the rewards for group leaders, and participation restrictions—while our research examines government policy, which is perceived to be influential at the market level. We find evidence that the magnitude of herding is reduced after regulatory events occur, verifying the power of regulations in the P2P lending market. In particular, by quantifying the relative importance of various types of regulatory events, we gain insight into the types of events that are more effective in reducing herding. These findings offer useful implications for both platform owners and policymakers from the aspect of investor education and market supervision.

## Related Literature

## Online P2P Lending

In recent years, online P2P lending has attracted an increasing amount of attention from researchers. Our research is about investors’ decisions, the interaction among investors, and platform-related information and mechanisms. As to investors’ decisions, most P2P lending studies investigate what factors may affect investors decisions. They find factors such as the interest rate, borrowers’ characteristics, hard information (e.g., credit score and debt history), and soft information to be influential [2, 13, 18]. This stream of literature focuses on factors at the relatively micro level, such as loans or individuals. Our work, in contrast, investigates how platform-level features may affect investors’ decision.

Studies on how interaction among individuals affects investors’ behavior also relates to our research. These studies focus on how one’s decision affects the others decisions within a certain social network, such as an online friend network, offline friend network [25], individuals from the same region [23], and online virtual communities [39] (e.g., a project that relies on the collective action of multiple individuals is actually a virtual and informal social network). For example, Lin et al. [24] show that the relational aspects of networks can positively predict lending outcomes, such as the likelihood of a loan being funded. The structural aspects, however, have limited effect. Liu et al. [25] examine the role of friendship in P2P lending. They find that a borrower’s friends are more likely than strangers to offer loans, and endorsements from a borrower’s friends have a negative effect on subsequent investors. Lin and Viswanathan [23] document that lenders are more likely to contribute to borrowers that are in the same state in the P2P lending market (i.e., home bias). Zhang and Liu [39] find that well-funded listings tend to attract more funding after controlling for unobserved listing heterogeneity and payoff externalities. Similar studies include Herzenstein et al. [17], Kim and Viswanathan [21], and Lee and Lee [22]. Participation externalities, similar to herding, are shown to have contributed to growth of two electronic trading platforms [29]. These studies, especially the work on herding behavior, are closely related to our research. A main difference between our work and these studies is that they focus on investors’ herding at the loan level, while ours concentrates on herding at a relatively macro level—the platform level. We will discuss this in detail in the following literature review section.

There is also research on P2P platforms. These studies investigate different platformrelated mechanisms (e.g., loan pricing, trust building, and incentive design), and the associated impact on participant benefits. For example, Chen et al. [9] investigate the uniform-price auctions that Prosper used until 2010. Their theoretical analysis shows that borrowers pay a lot when lenders seek to maximize their profit. This finding holds in both complete and incomplete information settings. Wei and Lin [35] investigate the impact of two price mechanisms: auctions and posted prices. They find that the posted-price mechanism, which Prosper adopted after December 2010, brings shortterm benefits to both borrowers and lenders, but results in a net loss in social welfare. Greiner and Wang [16] study trust building in P2P lending. They find that the central (e.g., borrowers’ economic status and credit score) and peripheral issues (e.g., listing quality and borrowers’ social capital) can influence lenders’ trust behavior. Hildebrand et al. [19] examine the impact of an incentive mechanism change regarding group leaders, and the associated loan performance that occurs. By leveraging the change in rules that eliminate the origination fee, they find that, while group leaders’ bids are perceived as a signal of high loan quality, they have high default rates too. The default rate decreases only when a group leader has sufficient skin in the game or when the origination fee is eliminated. Similar to these studies, our article looks beyond the loan level. Yet unlike these studies, which concentrate on one or two representative platforms, we look into how the internal platforms represented in a P2P market are constituted, and how investors respond to them.

## Herding Behavior and Individual Choice

Herding in individual choice has been studied in a wide range of areas, including fads and fashion, online communities, and crowfunding platforms [8, 14, 33]. In this section, we mainly review herding literature that relates to individuals in the context of finance, e-commerce, information technology and systems, and online P2P lending activity.

In the finance literature, herding is described as “everyone is doing what everyone else is doing even when their private information suggests doing something different” [3, p. 798]. Scharfstein and Stein [31] set up a theoretical learning model to study the forces that lead to managers’ herding behavior, and argue that it is rational for managers who are concerned about their reputation to imitate the investment decisions of other managers. Banerjee [3] develops a basic model to study the rationale behind the sequential decision-making process and makes extensions and modifications of the basic model. There are also financial studies on herding in initial public offerings of equities [37], herding among security analysts [36], and the relationship between herding and heavy tails in stock returns [11].

IT and systems researchers are also interested in studying the herding phenomenon in decision making regarding online community activities. Walden and Browne [34] provide support for the operational model of information that cascades in the adoption of e-commerce technology, and show that fad-like behavior is a rational response to making decisions in an uncertain context. Duan et al. [12] use a fixedeffects model to examine informational cascades in online software adoption. They find that users’ software choices are heavily influenced by the download ranking and the popularity of information, suggesting the existence of herding. By examining the group-buying, discount-pricing mechanism, Kauffman and Wang [20] find that the number of existing orders has a positive effect on new orders placed during each three-hour period. In online bookstores, the product ratings and others’ choices have an effect on purchase decisions. Recommendations from other consumers are more influential than those from experts [10]. For eBay DVD auctions, inexperienced bidders often herd into auctions with more existing bids, but neglect other salient factors such as lower starting prices in previous decisions [32].

Recent studies have been interested in the herding phenomenon in the context of P2P lending. Zhang and Liu [39] use a listing’s cumulative amount of funding as the main measure of herding and find that well-funded listings tend to attract more funding after controlling for unobserved listing heterogeneity and payoff externalities. Luo and Lin [27] use a decision tree to illustrate investors’ decisions and model the formation of herding. This study introduces the change in average time intervals between two consecutive bids as an indicator, finding that investors are more likely to herd into listings that gain more bids and friend bids, but their benefit will be reduced. Lee and Lee [22] propose that online P2P lending platforms satisfy the characteristics of herding behavior illustrated by Duan et al. [12]. They employ a multinomial logit market-share model and prove that an auction with a higher participation rate attracts more bids with a diminishing marginal effect. Similar to Simonsohn and Ariely [32], Herzenstein et al. [17] define herding behavior as a greater likelihood of receiving additional bids. Their empirical study provides evidence of strategic herding behavior. Investors are more likely to bid in an auction with more existing bids before the auction is fully funded, and then herding diminishes. They find that strategic herding in a loan auction is positively associated with its subsequent performance.

Other researchers investigate the rationality of investors’ herding behavior. Puro et al. [30] study online auctions at Prosper.com. They identify heterogeneous bidding strategies; people seemed to adjust their bidding depending on their learning experience and what kind of listing they were bidding on. For example, bidders are more cautious about high-risk investors and observe the other bidders’ valuations until they make their own bids. Zhang and Liu [39] provide evidence of rational herding among investors on listings on Prosper.com. They find that investors observe their predecessors’ decisions and use publicly observable defects or favorable characteristics to moderate their judgment. Specifically, defects exacerbate herding while favorable characteristics attenuate herding. Liu et al. [25] provide evidence of relational herding; investors are more likely to herd when the crowd includes friends rather than strangers. Furthermore, herding happens more often among offline friends than among online friends and strangers. Kim and Viswanathan [21] find that later investors make sophisticated use of early investors’ decisions. App developers and experienced investors could influence later investors because they have better knowledge of the product and market performance.

## Data

## Data Source and Variable Description

We collected our data from wdzj.com, an independent third-party website, which provides a comprehensive list of attributes for most P2P lending platforms in China. We include platforms that were in operation for at least 3 months at the time of data collection. Doing so allowed us to restrict our attention to platforms that are operationally more stable. The resulting data set is a panel containing 127 platforms spanning a period of 44 weeks, from January 1, 2014 to November 4, 2014.<sup>1</sup>

Table 1 provides descriptions and descriptive statistics of the main variables. All variables are weekly measures at the platform level. On average, a platform had 257 successful listings totaling US\$216 million in funding per week. For those materialized loans, there were about 894 investors lending money to 99 borrowers with an average investment amount of US\$11,000 and an average interest rate of 18 percent. An average platform had been in operation for 68+ weeks, and was searched around 250 times per week.

We further classified all variables into two categories: salient and nonsalient variables. We refer to salient variables as basic lending statistics that are provided by each platform and typically displayed in an evident place like the front page. Individual investors can easily observe salient variables, such as Listings, Investors, Borrowers, Loan Duration, Loan Amount, and Interest Rate. By nonsalient variables, we mean information that is not typically displayed on each platform but is available upon investors’ own effort. Operation Time, Search Index, Market Share, Registered Capital, Inv. Concentration, Bor. Concentration, Funding Time, and Amount Funded are nonsalient. The motivation of this taxonomy is straightforward. In our analysis, we first attempt to model investors’ platform choices by leveraging information that is evidently available to them. We then take into account other potential influencers that are not directly observable to investors from the platforms. Note that we use log-transformed values for variables that are hyperdispersed.

## Herding Measures

As documented in the herding literature, the observability of summary statistics on the actions of predecessors may be informative and lead to herding [7]. In this section, we define measures of herding based on summary statistics on prior investors’ actions from three aspects.

We first define the herding measure based on the number of investors. Studies show that herding behavior can be inferred through the number of investors [22]. As we previously discussed, investors can easily track the total number of prior investors, as this information is typically displayed in a noticeable place on a platform’s website. We use the cumulative number of individuals who have lent money on a given platform by the end of a given week (Cum. Investors) as our primary measure of herding. This measure reflects prior investors’ collective assessment of a certain platform. We argue that investors as a whole demonstrate herding behavior if the cumulative number of prior investors has a positive impact on the decision of subsequent investors. Although this measure has the advantage of simplicity, it may ignore aspects other than the number of investors that may reveal the herding tendency.

To address this concern, we define two alternative measures based on the investment amount and funding time. A second measure is related to investment amount; this reflects previous investors’ decisions [22, 39]. We adopt the cumulative investment amount per investor (Cum. Amount Funded) to investigate herding from the perspective of funding allocation. All else equal, herding will exist if platforms with a larger cumulative investment amount continue to attract more funding than those with a relatively smaller investment amount. In addition, as suggested by the existing literature, time-based measurements can also be used to capture herding behavior [27], by assessing the rate at which investors converge to uniform investment behavior. We use the average time it takes for a listing to be funded (Funding Time) to quantify herding behavior. We will be able to confirm the existence of herding if we can find that the average time for a listing to be fully funded during a given week is shorter on platforms with low Funding Time during the previous week relative to those with high Funding Time. We will use the first herding measure in the main analysis, and examine the other alternatives in robustness checks.

## Empirical Analyses and Results

In this section, we begin our analysis by examining whether investors as a whole demonstrate herding behavior when choosing the platform through which they will invest. Then we introduce a multilevel mixed-effects model (also known as a hierarchical model) to identify the moderating role of several platform attributes. Finally, we test the impact of government regulations on the magnitude of herding.

## Baseline Analysis of Herding Behavior

We posit that the number of investors on a certain platform during a given week is dependent on various attributes of the same platform observed in the previous week. Specifically, our regression model can be expressed as:

$$
y _ {i t} = \alpha + \beta Y _ {i, t - 1} + X _ {i, t - 1} \gamma + \varepsilon_ {i t}.\tag{1}
$$

The dependent variable $y _ { i t }$ is the logarithm of the number of investors on platform i during week t. The covariate of interest $Y _ { i , t - 1 }$ is the logarithm of the cumulative number of investors who have invested on the same platform prior to week t. In this simple form, a positive and significant estimate of β will confirm the existence of herding, as it indicates that investors at any given week are more likely to choose a platform that has previously attracted more investors. The matrix $X _ { i , t - 1 }$ includes salient, platform-specific variables, such as the number of listings, loan duration, loan amount, interest rate, and number of borrowers, all of which are observed prior to week t. As we discussed in data section, the rationale behind Equation (1) is that all the independent variables $( \mathrm { i . e . , } Y _ { i , t - 1 }$ and $X _ { i , t - 1 } )$ are displayed on each platform’s website in an observable way (typically on the front page). Considering the fact that some unobservable attributes, such as marketing expenditure and risk management capability, may also influence investors’ decisions, we added a platform-specific fixed effect $\mu _ { i }$ into Equation (1) to control time-invariant unobservable heterogeneity among platforms. In a similar spirit, we also added a week-specific fixed effect $\nu _ { t }$ to control for potential time trend and holiday effects during the data collection period. Accordingly, Equation (1) can be rewritten as:

$$
y _ {i t} = \alpha + \beta Y _ {i, t - 1} + X _ {i, t - 1} \gamma + \mu_ {i} + v _ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t}\tag{2}
$$

In other words, the increment in the magnitude of herding effect decreases as the cumulative number of investors increases. This finding is in line with that of Lee and Lee [22], but contrasts with Zhang and Liu [39].

The above nonlinear pattern motivates a further examination of the relationship between the magnitude of the herding effect and the number of investors. To support that, we employ a quantile regression to examine whether such a relationship exhibits a systematic change. From Table 3, we see that the estimated coefficients for our herding measure remain statistically significant across different quantiles (denoted by τ), suggesting that herding does exist across platforms regardless of the 3 size of investors.

More interestingly, the coefficient magnitude decreases as the quantile of the dependent variable (i.e., the number of investors) increases. Such a systematic change suggests that herding is stronger on platforms with relatively few investors. The coefficients for Interest Rate decrease in a systematic manner. On the contrary, the coefficients for Loan Duration increase as the value of τ goes up. These systematic differences together reflect that higher interest rates and shorter loan durations (translate into higher returns on investment) are more effective in attracting investors for platforms with less investors, ceteris paribus.

Taken together, our quantile analysis reveals an interesting finding regarding investors’ decisions. When facing platforms with a smaller number of prior investors, latter investors have the tendency to gather more information. They not only tend to pursue a higher interest rate during a shorter duration, but also resort to the information embedded in predecessors’ actions, hoping to reduce risk and uncertainty associated with a smaller platform. While on platforms that have a larger number of prior investors, latter investors make decisions more independently. They show a weaker herding tendency and a higher level of acceptance of a low interest rate and long duration. This is because investors may already have obtained sufficient information about larger platforms from elsewhere (e.g., media reports), and thus do not rely on others so much [7]. In contrast, platforms with fewer investors have less information available; thus, investors herd to get information from the actions of previous peers.

## Moderating Role of Platform Attributes

So far, we have shown that later investors follow their predecessors’ choices, after controlling for salient variables. However, we believe that there are some other platform-related factors that may also influence investors’ decisions. Ignoring them may bias the herding behavior estimated from our empirical model [32]. Next, we extend our analysis by including nonsalient platform attributes and gauging their moderating effects on the magnitude of herding.

We consider seven additional platform attributes, which are used to measure different aspects of each platform, including platform awareness, public interest, funding status, competitiveness, and participant composition. Along this line of analysis, we first include Operation Time to control for the potential advantages of a long-operated platform, such as a platform’s brand awareness. Next, we use Search Index as a proxy for the public’s interest in a given platform, following a widely accepted approach in the literature (cf. [28]). The inclusion of this measure helps us to better control for some unobserved, time-varying attributes like a platform’s marketing expenditure. Since a prospective investor may perceive a platform that holds a leading position in the market to be more reliable, we also include Market Share and Cum. Amount Funded to control for this effect. These two variables have different economic interpretations. The former captures the relative competitiveness of a platform in the short run, while the latter measures the funding status of the platform in the long run. We also include Registered Capital, the amount of capital that a platform has, to control for a given platform’s initial funding status. Finally, we include Inv. Concentration and Bor. Concentration to capture the participant composition of a platform. As discussed in data section, the former measures the fraction of debt to be collected by the top ten investors (evaluated in funding), while the latter measures the fraction of debt to be paid by the top ten borrowers.

While our data have a panel structure, a challenge arises when it comes to model estimation. To be more specific, we do not observe sufficient variations for some nonsalient variables such as Registered Capital, Investor Concentration, and Borrower Concentration. To address this data issue, we employ a multilevel mixed-effects model. The modeling advantages of a multilevel mixed-effects model are twofold. First, we are able to identify the magnitude of the herding effect for each specific platform. Second, it allows us to estimate the moderating effect of both time-varying and time-invariant variables simultaneously. The full model can be expressed as:

$$
y _ {i t} = \beta_ {0 i} + \beta_ {1 i} Y _ {i, t - 1} + W _ {i, t - 1} \beta_ {2} + Y _ {i, t - 1} \times W _ {i, t - 1} \beta_ {3} + \varepsilon_ {i t},\tag{3.1}
$$

$$
\beta_ {0 i} = \gamma_ {0 0} + Z _ {i, t - 1} \gamma_ {0 1} + \xi_ {0 i},\tag{3.2}
$$

$$
\beta_ {1 i} = \gamma_ {1 0} + Z _ {i, t - 1} \gamma_ {1 1} + \xi_ {1 i}.\tag{3.3}
$$

First note that the dependent variable and the key covariate in the “first-level” Equation (3.1) are still $y _ { i t }$ and $Y _ { i , t - 1 }$ . However, both $\beta _ { 0 i }$ and $\beta _ { 1 i }$ have an $\ " \boldsymbol { l } \ " \boldsymbol { \mathbf { \mathit { l } } }$ index since they are estimated at the platform level rather than the market level, as seen in Equation (2). From a modeling perspective, $\beta _ { 0 i }$ helps us control for a platformspecific, persistent effect, similar to the purpose of $\mu _ { i } ,$ while $\beta _ { 1 i }$ allows for variation in the herding effect across 127 platforms. Matrix $W _ { i , t - 1 }$ includes nine time-variant attributes, including the five salient variables in Equation (2), plus the first four nonsalient variables. We allow $W _ { i , t - 1 }$ to interact with our herding measure $Y _ { i , t - 1 }$ such that the estimate of coefficient vector $\beta _ { 3 }$ will inform us of the moderating role of $W _ { i , t - 1 }$ on herding.

Matrix $Z _ { i , t - 1 }$ includes three time-invariant variables, such as Registered Capital, Inv. Concentration, and Bor. Concentration. The econometric interpretation of the “second-level” equations is as follows. The platform-specific parameters $( \beta _ { 0 i }$ and $\beta _ { 1 i } )$ are estimated as a function of $Z _ { i , t - 1 }$ and some unobserved errors $( \xi _ { 0 i }$ and $\xi _ { 1 i } )$ . If we plug $\beta _ { 0 i }$ into Equation (3.1), we can rewrite it as:

$$
y _ {i t} = \gamma_ {0 0} + Z _ {i, t - 1} \gamma_ {0 1} + \beta_ {1 i} Y _ {i, t - 1} + W _ {i, t - 1} \beta_ {2} + Y _ {i, t - 1} \times W _ {i, t - 1} \beta_ {3} + (\xi_ {0 i} + \varepsilon_ {i t}).\tag{4}
$$

The estimates of $\gamma _ { 0 1 }$ will capture the linear relationship between $y _ { i t }$ and $Z _ { i , t - 1 }$ Similarly, plugging $\beta _ { 1 i }$ into Equation (3.1) yields:

$$
y _ {i t} = \beta_ {0 i} + (\gamma_ {1 0} + Z _ {i, t - 1} \gamma_ {1 1} + \xi_ {1 i}) Y _ {i, t - 1} + W _ {i, t - 1} \beta_ {2} + Y _ {i, t - 1} \times W _ {i, t - 1} \beta_ {3} + \varepsilon_ {i t}.\tag{5}
$$

Clearly, the estimates of $\gamma _ { 1 1 }$ will quantify the moderating effect of $Z _ { i , t - 1 }$ on herding behavior. In sum, the parameters of our particular interest are $\beta _ { 3 }$ and $\gamma _ { 1 1 }$ .

Table 4 reports the estimation results of Equation (3). In what follows, we facilitate our discussion by looking at the moderating effects of platform-related and participant concentration-related attributes.

First of all, we can see that the estimated coefficients for interaction terms (Cum. Investors × Market Share) and (Cum. Investors × Cum. Amount Funded) are both statistically significant and positive, suggesting that Market Share and Cum. Amount Funded accentuate herding in investors’ platform choice. These findings are reasonable in that platforms that account for a large market share are associated with higher profits, operational efficiency, and better risk-management solutions. Investors are more likely to perceive these platforms as reliable, and therefore follow others to invest on these popular platforms without worrying too much about risk. As to platforms where a large amount of investment occurred, subsequent investors may tend to follow others because they infer that they have been duly diligent, since prior investors have demonstrated a larger cumulative investment amount. As a result, their herding behavior becomes stronger. In contrast, when the platform has a smaller cumulative amount of investment, subsequent investors cannot infer the quality of the platform based on such insufficient evidence.

A platform’s duration in operation, in contrast, attenuates herding behavior, as indicated by a significant and negative coefficient for the interaction term (Cum. Investors × Operation Time). This result is somewhat counterintuitive since a typical investor often considers a long-operated platform to be more reliable in terms of management capability. From an information perspective, however, the estimated negative correlation seems reasonable [39]. It is quite natural that there would be more information, such as media news or word of mouth, for platforms that have been in operation for a longer period of time. As a result, an individual investor may attach less value to the decisions made by prior investors on those platforms. In contrast, investors tend to exhibit stronger herding behavior to reduce uncertainty due to a lack of information for relatively new platforms. Finally, registered capital does not have a significant moderating impact on the magnitude of herding. This makes sense because P2P lending platforms typically set their registered capital so as to pass the required threshold. For this reason, individuals may not take this piece of information into consideration when making their investment decision.

We next turn to the moderating role of participant composition on herding. On one hand, the estimated coefficient for the interaction term (Cum. Investors × Inv. Concentration) is negative. This result can be attributed to two reasons. The first possible reason is related to the dominating power of high-profile players. Compared to individual investors, high-profile investors, such as institutional ones, have substantial financial resources and are therefore in a better position to fund listings with desired characteristics (e.g., higher interest rates). The market outcome is that individual investors would have little chance to successfully fund the listings with a high interest rate, which, in turn, reduces their return on investment. Another explanation is associated with the value of information. Information helps decision makers reduce uncertainty. In the context of online P2P lending, information about a platform lowers investors’ tendency to herd [39].

When investor concentration is low, subsequent investors are more likely to interpret the decisions of previous investors as informative. However, when investor concentration is high, individual investors may interpret prior investors’ collective action as a market outcome controlled by institutional investors, rather than as the collective wisdom of the crowd. Taken together, individual investors may hesitate to follow their peers on platforms where very few investors contribute to a large fraction of the total amount funded. On the other hand, Bor. Concentration is also found to weaken herding. A possible explanation is that, on platforms where a large fraction of the total loan amount was requested by a few big borrowers, the level of risk is largely dependent on their debt paying behavior. Once they default, individual investors will suffer a lot. In this case, risk-averse individuals may reduce their tendency to rely on decisions made by their early peers [6].

We further examined the herding effect on “top” platforms, defined as platforms with Market Share higher than its median value. From Column (3) of Table 4, we see that the magnitude of herding is even stronger on platforms with high market share. In addition, the moderating effects of operation time and market share are larger on the top half of platforms. In contrast though, the moderating effects of investor concentration and borrower concentration became insignificant. These differences imply that, on platforms that already have a considerable share of the market, investors pay less value to participant concentration, as the larger market share itself is a signal of good market quality and lower transaction risk.

Since greater access to information regarding platforms helps investors reduce uncertainty, and hence lower their incentive to herd [6, 32], we expect the estimation of $\delta _ { j } ^ { \prime } s$ to be negative. The estimation results are presented in Table 6. Visualization of the changes in herding magnitudes over time is shown in Figure 2.

The estimation results confirm that those government-imposed regulations have a strong impact on investors’ decisions. Four out of the five estimated coefficients for the interaction terms are significant and negative. This indicates that investors responded to those regulatory events in a positive manner: the need for herding lessened as the P2P lending market became more mature and regulated.

To gain more insights, we next categorized the five events into different groups based on their nature. Figure 2 visualizes the dissimilar impact of each event on herding. The findings from this posterior analysis are threefold. First, events that designated competent authorities (E5) and issued government-imposed regulations (E2) had a stronger impact in reducing herding behavior among investors. This makes sense as a tighterregulated market with better information transparency helped investors to reduce their uncertainty and acquire more information about loans, reducing the necessity of herding. Second, in contrast, forum-based events that sought opinions from practitioners and released tentative regulatory proposals were less influential (E1, E4). Last, events specifically related to P2P lending were more impactful relative to events concerning a broader domain such as fintech in general (E3).

## Potential Estimation Issues

We calculate variance inflation factors (VIFs) for all variables to assess whether our model suffers from multicollinearity. The mean of all the VIFs is 2.66, which provides no evidence for severe multicollinearity. Another concern is that the variance of the error terms may vary across platforms. One possible cause of heteroskedasticity may be the platform size. We use registered capital as a proxy for the platform size and divide all variables (including both dependent and independent variables) by the registered capital. We reestimate the model and the results suggest that herding is still significant (see Column (1) of Appendix Table A2). Furthermore, we employ the fixed-effects model with first-order autocorrelation to deal with potential serial correlation among the error terms. The new parameter estimates are consistent with those of Equation (2), suggesting the robustness of our results (see Column (2) of Appendix Table A2). In addition to the fixed-effects model specified in Equation (2), we also estimated a random-effects model (Column (3) of Appendix Table A2) and performed a Hausman specification test [38] to assess their respective performances. The $\chi ^ { 2 }$ value is 258.30, which is significant at the 0.01 level, suggesting that the fixed-effects model is statistically appropriate.

## Expanded Sample Test

In 2014, the P2P lending business in China was not well-regulated. It was reported that a few platforms inflated their loan interest rates so as to attract more funding from individual investors, casting doubt on whether our main finding is valid. To assess the robustness of our results from the time period, we collected an expanded data set that spanned a twoyear period from September 21, 2015, to September 1, 2017. The expanded data include a total of 662 platforms that remained in operation until the end of the data period. The new results remain qualitatively the same (see Table A3), confirming that herding was present even after the market became more mature and better regulated.

## Conclusion and Implications

The literature on P2P lending has focused on herding behavior at a micro-listing level. The platform level of herding analysis however has been underexplored. This article fills this gap by concentrating on platform-level herding behavior. Using a unique data set for 127 peer-to-peer lending platforms across 44 weeks from a large P2P lending market, we investigated the existence of herding behavior, explored the moderating role of several platform attributes, and examined the impact of government regulatory events on herding.

We first investigated herding behavior at the platform level. Our empirical findings suggest that herding exists at the platform level. P2P lending platforms with a larger number of previous investors tend to attract more investors subsequently, after we control for unobserved platform heterogeneity and time effects. Platform-level herding diminishes as the number of previous investors increases though. Moreover, we used quantile regression to test for the herding effect under different quantiles of the dependent variable’s value. The results show that herding is positive and significant across all of the different quantiles.

We next tested whether platform attributes moderate herding behavior. We introduced both salient and nonsalient platform attributes into our analysis to avoid potentially biased inferences. Our platform attributes capture public awareness, capital status, competitiveness, and the composition of the participants for each platform. Using a multilevel mixed-effects model, we obtained evidence that herding behavior is moderated by several platform attributes. Specifically, platform attributes such as operation time and the composition of participants (including both investors and borrowers) can weaken the herding effect, while the accumulated investment amount and market share strengthen the herding effect.

Last, we investigated the relationship between government regulatory events and herding behavior. These events were initiated by the government to regulate the P2P lending market, including regulations on P2P operational standards, principles, and supervisory details. We found that these regulatory events had a significant effect on the number of investors in the P2P lending platforms, and the magnitude of herding decreased after these regulatory events occurred. This indicates that investors positively responded to these regulatory events, and they adjusted their perceptions and their herding behavior accordingly. In addition, we conducted a series of robustness checks regarding the sample period, model specification, and alternative measures of herding. The results confirmed our main findings.

The results of this research have implications for both platform owners and policymakers. First, we demonstrated that platform attributes moderate investors’ herding behavior. Platform owners can find opportunities to improve their operations by leveraging platform attributes better. For example, platform owners should try to build a stronger installed base of cumulative investments and market share to accelerate investor herding. They should also avoid large-scale herding and maintain a relatively stable investor group, especially if they have been operating for a long time. Second, we showed that investors positively responded to regulatory events that are associated with beneficial risk principles and operational standards. Policymakers should continue to release information and offer guidance to the public about their P2P lending activities. Regulatory activities will help to encourage positive impressions on the part of investors on a platform’s efforts to implement risk prevention and risk control, while contributing to the stable and healthy development of the P2P lending market in the long run.
