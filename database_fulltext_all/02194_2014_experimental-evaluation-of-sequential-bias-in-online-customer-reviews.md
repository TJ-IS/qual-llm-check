## A B S T R A C T

Explicit information used in the development of recommender systems includes online customer feedback. Such explicit input from customers suffers from several types of bias, which directly affect the quality of the resulting recommender system. Recent research has identified sequential bias to be present in online customer reviews. However, to our knowledge, no study to date has confirmed its existence in this context. Given the nature of sequential bias, confirming its existence necessitates a controlled experimental study, which is lacking in extant published literature. We attempt to address this gap. Results from our study show evidence for the existence of sequential bias.

## 1. Introduction

Before the existence of the Internet, a prospective customer’s best option for recommendation was a (or, a few) trusted someone who has experience with an item of interest or is somehow a maven (an expert, relatively speaking) on such items. While this mode of operation worked reasonably well in the distant past, the existence of widely available product information over the Internet has completely changed the way in which customers seek out necessary information before they make their product/service purchase decisions.

Online customer product/service reviews are very widely used by customers and their influence cannot be overestimated (e.g., [16]). The reasons for their influence are multi-fold and include (1) their ease of availability, (2) multiple perspectives, all assembled in one location (e.g., Web page), (3) their seemingly impartial appearance, (4) the lack of other credible source(s) of information, among others. These reviews are in turn used by online retailers to generate recommendations through their recommender systems. In addition to recommending (seemingly) appropriate products/ services, these recommender systems also help the customer discover new products/services through serendipity. Regardless, to a certain extent, online product/service reviews and recommender systems are slowly becoming indispensables for customers seeking information on products/services.

In an ideal world, online product reviews as well as recommendations generated by recommender systems would be perfect and free of bias of any kind. However, the reality is that both online reviews as well as the recommender systems that use them to generate recommendations are replete with several kinds of biases [21] and intentional manipulations (e.g., fake reviews that are generated by paid reviewers who may not necessarily have used the product/service before submitting their reviews online). For an excellent overview of several types of bias in general information processing, the interested reader is referred to Solloway [19] and Hu et al. [8,9].

We consider one type of bias that we believe is inherently present in online product reviews and, therefore, in recommendations generated by recommender systems that use such explicit online product reviews as a part of their input. Specifically, we consider sequential bias that is introduced as a direct consequence of the order sequence in which existing online reviews are perused by a new reviewer of a given product/service. Sequential bias, which is unintentional and results from existing reviews that are diametrically opposite to a new reviewer’s opinion/experience of a product or service, precipitates in a reviewer submitting a review and/or review rating that is different from what was originally intended. To our knowledge, based on extant published literature, sequential bias has been studied (a) from an analytical perspective with necessary assumptions (e.g., [14]) and (b) through use of data collected at online retailer sites such as amazon.com (e.g., [17]). In the former, the authors support the possible existence of sequential bias through the existence of related phenomenon (e.g., first impression bias) and derive theoretical results based on this premise. Since the authors in the former do not show evidence of sequential bias in reality, the authors in the latter take it upon themselves to either prove or disprove the existence of sequential bias. They use customer review data collected at online retailer Web sites to operationalize their study. We argue that the use of such data to confirm the presence or absence of sequential bias is faulty simply because there is no control over the sequence in which existing reviews are read.

To determine the existence of sequential bias, the experimenter has to ensure that the perusal of reviews occur in a sequential manner before a new review is added. Data collected from online product review Web sites do not support this possibility. This is due to (a) some product/service review Web sites allowing users to sort reviews (based on date posted, most positive, least positive, etc.), (b) the users reading reviews in random order, and/or (c) inconsistencies in that not all new reviews are written after the perusal of existing reviews in sequential order. Clearly, based on these conditions, the methodology used in Sikora and Chauhan [17] cannot help evaluate the presence of sequential bias. They did not control for the sequence and their study may not necessarily be measuring sequential bias. This motivated our present study. We purport to address this gap in extant published literature by considering the dynamics of sequential bias in an online product review context through controlled experiments.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: We provide a brief discussion of some relevant publications in Section 2. This is followed by discussion of our experimental setup in Section 3. We discuss results from this study in Section 4 and discuss results from the paper in Section 5. We conclude the paper with a brief discussion on managerial and academic significance of this work in Section 6.

## 2. Background and related literature

We first discuss related background on recommender systems and how sequential bias could significantly affect their performance. We then discuss relevant literature that is tangentially as well as directly related to sequential bias.

## 2.1. Background

The knowledge-base in online recommender systems are developed using both explicit and implicit data (Fig. 1). Explicit data includes online customer reviews and implicit data are generally gathered from customers’ online browsing behavior (e.g., time spent visiting an item or a Web page, previous Web page or item visited, next Web page or item visited, items product/service reviews read), items that were placed in their cart but later removed, previous purchase record, among others.

Fig. 1. Recommender system.

A recommender system consists of, at a minimum, a learning module and a knowledge-base. The learning module learns patterns present in input (implicit and/or explicit) data and the knowledge thus generated are stored in the knowledge-base. To prevent staleness, the knowledge-base is continually and incrementally updated with new/updated knowledge as new input data are received. Recommendations are then generated with the help of the knowledge-base and user-specific information. The user information is obtained either through knowledge of this user’s past (purchase, browsing) behavior or information retrieved from the current online session (e.g., the mix of items that were considered, the amount of time spent on each considered item). Most online retailers allow the customer to create an online account, which can be used to track/trace specific customer online behaviors while at their online store. The information thus gathered can be used to fine-tune recommendations that are subsequently generated for this customer.

It is generally accepted that the more the recommender system knows about a customer and his/her interests and past behavior, the better it is in recommending appropriate products and/or services to this customer that would result in a purchase. While there are concerns with respect to privacy and security issues associated with recording online customer behavior when visiting an online retail store, these issues are completely under the retailer’s control and are not as severe as in other related scenarios (e.g., the use of RFID tags to track/trace a customer by third parties even after the customer has left the Brick&Mortar store with a purchase). Nevertheless, the placement of cookies in the customer’s computer has raised concerns in the past, and this continues to remain an issue. However, oftentimes there is an option on whether to accept placement of such cookies in the customer’s computer. Recommender systems have other issues as well, including the cold start problem that arises primarily when a new product/service with no history in the recommender system is introduced, as well as when recommending a product/service to a new customer without knowledge of this customer’s characteristics, interests, among others.

Since explicit input data in the form of online customer reviews comprise an important component of input to recommender systems, we are interested in considering online reviews written by reviewers and the process by which they write such reviews, including the inputs they implicitly and explicitly take into consideration. These inputs that go into the generation of an online review can be conscious as well as subconscious in the sense that the user may intentionally or unintentionally generate parts of the review – specifically, the numerical rating score that is assigned to a product or service of interest. In general, a new review is generated by a reviewer based on several different inputs (Fig. 2).

For example, these inputs could include the reviewer’s firsthand experience with the product or service of interest, existing reviews by other reviewers, the numerical scores provided by these previous reviewers, the risk tolerance of the new reviewer, the significance of the product or service to the reviewer, any bias (e.g., sequential bias) that may be present, among others. The service/product significance indirectly refers to the degree of indifference of the customer toward this service or product. For example, while a customer may be indifferent when purchasing a pack of chewing gum, the same may not be true when this customer purchases a new car. Among these different sets of information the reviewer uses as input for providing a review, there are certainly some that evoke a stronger response from the reviewer while the reviewer may be indifferent to other sets of information. For example, when considering the purchase of a camera to take very high resolution pictures, the resolution specifications of a given camera as well as reviews and one’s own personal experience with this camera that specifically concern resolution-related issues may evoke a stronger response from a potential customer/reviewer than when considering the purchase of a relatively inexpensive camera for everyday use. Moreover, the sequence in which information is obtained by this potential customer/reviewer may also (possibly subconsciously) affect/bias the review score given by this customer for a product/service of interest. We are interested in this sequential bias that is introduced while processing the existing set of information.

## 2.2. Related literature

Although somewhat related to the topic of interest in this paper – sequential bias – researchers have been studying the dynamics associated with biased processing of information for a long time. For example, Festinger [5]’s cognitive dissonance theory states that once an alternative is chosen, people generally prefer information that support their decision than those that are not in favor of their decision. Several variants of this phenomenon have been studied by researchers over the years (e.g., [6,7]). Ditto and Lopez [3] as well as Edwards and Smith [4] show that contradictory information are evaluated more critically than confirming information.

Using experiments where they sequentially present information, Jonas et al. [10] show the existence of confirmation bias whereby the respondents selectively seek out information that confirms their previous decision. They distinguish sequential presentation experiments from those that involve sequential processing of information.

Although economists, psychologists and sociologists have studied sequential bias or its variants (e.g., first impression bias), sequential bias has not received its fair share from an online product/service review perspective (e.g., [1,2,15,20]). Sequence bias in an online review context was first discussed in Piramuthu [12]. This was then extended with more theoretical analysis and results in Kapoor and Piramuthu [11], Piramuthu [13], and Piramuthu et al. [14]. The basic premise in these [11–14] analytical studies is that in the presence of sequential bias, a reviewer with strongly positive experience with the product or service of interest will provide an even more positive rating score when all or a majority of existing reviews are negative with negative rating scores. The rationale is that the reviewer tries to subconsciously compensate for the overall negative existing rating scores by providing an even more positive rating score since he/she has a positive experience with the product/service of interest. On the other hand, when all existing reviews are very positive with highly positive rating scores, a reviewer who intended to provide a mildly negative rating score (based on his/her negative experience with the product/service) will provide a strongly negative rating score to (subconsciously) compensate for the overly positive existing reviews.

While it is a good first step to consider and evaluate the dynamics of sequential bias from an analytical perspective, the fact is that it is a psychological phenomenon and therefore necessitates careful analysis through controlled experiments using real-world data. Analytical studies, by their very nature, necessitate making assumptions that essentially drive what follows, including results obtained and conclusions reached. Assumptions, by themselves, are not necessarily bad since these assumptions can be based on solid evidences. However, when studying psychological phenomena, an incorrect assumption can easily lead to wrong conclusions. There is, therefore, an urgent need to experimentally evaluate results from analytical studies of psychological phenomena in order to confirm or refute these results.

An excellent recent study on biases in online reviews is that of You and Sikora [21], who use a multi-agent system to simulate the interactions among buyers and sellers in an electronic marketplace. They simulate a marketplace with two sellers and 20 buyers. They consider three existing reputation models (traditional, reputation with trust, and adaptive models) and study the effects of three types of biases (simple, underreporting, and sequential bias).

To date, we are aware of only one study that has attempted to show the existence of sequential bias in online reviews using realworld data. In their excellent study, Sikora and Chauhan [17] use the Kalman filtering technique with panel data on 19 different products from amazon.com and show the existence of sequential bias. They also observe that this sequential bias depends on previous reviews as well as reviewer characteristics. Interestingly, their results indicate that the dynamics of sequential bias is different for experience and search products, with the former predominantly explained by reviewer characteristics and the latter through review and reviewer characteristics. They develop a means to identify and separate sequential bias in new online reviews without having to store and then consider all previous reviews. They then find that the unbiased rating estimated by their model compared to the average rating is generally larger, which does not confirm one of the theoretical results derived in Piramuthu et al. [14].

We believe that, so far, the Sikora and Chauhan [17] paper has set the standard for studying sequential bias using existing data from an online retailer. The only major drawback that we can identify in this study is the lack of control for the sequence in which existing reviews may have been perused by a new reviewer since amazon.com allows for sorting existing reviews in several different ways (e.g., most recent first, most useful first). We argue that since most online retailers allow for reordering existing reviews, it is rather difficult to study the sequential bias phenomenon using data collected from such online retailer sites. There is zero control over the sequence in which any given reviewer perused all or a selected few of the existing reviews before writing his/her own review. Moreover, since not all online retailers verify the purchase of a given product before allowing a reviewer to write his/her online review, the possibility for the introduction of fake reviews is significant. Fake reviews essentially introduce unnecessary noise into the system and such noise are hard to remove unless the very first fake review is removed before other reviewers have had the chance to read those reviews. A fake review has a cascading effect since it has the potential to introduce bias in all future reviews of this product/service in unpredictable ways.

Even if an online reviewer allows only verified customers to write reviews and once posted these reviews are not allowed to be sorted, there is no control over whether a new reviewer read any existing reviews (and, if yes, which existing reviews and in which order since the new reviewer could read existing reviews from top to bottom or bottom to top for example) before generating his/her own review. Barring online retailers who do not allow for re-sorting online customer reviews and who have complete control over what (and the order in which) a new reviewer reads, we believe that a controlled experiment would be the best in terms of isolating effects due to sequential bias. We believe that it is a challenge to study the existence and dynamics of sequential bias using online review data collected from online retail stores. Moreover, since sequential bias occurs at a subconscious level, it is essential to control for possible environmental (both exogenous and endogenous) constraints that could wash away its effects. We develop a controlled experimental setup that would allow for the identification of sequential bias, when present.

## 3. Experimental setup

Given the constraints associated with studying sequential bias, there is a need for a specifically designed and developed experiment. The experiment must standardize a setting where user bias can be studied. We develop and use the following to guide our experimental study:

1. The generation and display of reviews must follow a sequence whereby the second reviewer has read the first reviewer’s review, the third reviewer has read the first two reviewers’ reviews in that order sequence (i.e., read the first review and then the second review), and so on. Please note that the first reviewer does not have the opportunity to read any (of the non-) existing reviews. Otherwise the sequential aspect may not necessarily be captured.

2. The number of existing reviews should not be too large, to facilitate each prospective reviewer to read through all existing reviews. It is widely acknowledged that readers do not peruse all existing reviews, due to some variant of bounded rationality [18], when the number of such reviews is large.

3. Each reviewer must have read through all existing reviews, in the order in which they were generated, and have a fairly good knowledge of the product/service that is being reviewed before assigning a review rating score and writing his/her review. Items 2 and 3 are imperative to ensure that reviewers do not lack prior information – achieved by physically limiting the number of reviews to be read – and reviewers overwhelmed with extraneous noisy that may cause unintended bias.

4. There has to be an effective means to capture each reviewers’ review (or, score that is given by this reviewer for the product/ service of interest) both (a) in an absolute sense (i.e., when the reviewer has not seen any other reviews, and therefore does not have the opportunity to be biased due to any of them) that is purely based on his/her thoughts/opinion/experience of the product/service, and (b) in a relative sense, when the reviewer writes his/her review after having experienced the product/ service and read all existing reviews on this product/service in the presented order sequence. Preferably, the absolute and relative scores have to be obtained for the same product and the same reviewer within a reasonable amount of time in-between without introducing any additional bias. This is extremely critical since it is necessary to obtain a reviewer’s (absolute) score for a product/service without the presence of any sequential bias and then obtain the (relative) score for the same product/service with the (possible) introduction of sequential bias. The absolute and relative scores cannot be obtained with a high degree of certainty in any existing online retailer data set since reviews are not controlled for sequential bias and (to our knowledge) none of the online retailers collect the absolute product/service rating as well as the rating after the reviewer has read through all existing reviews in the same order.

5. The product/service studied should not be trivial as to evoke indifference among the participants. The participant must have a certain degree of passion toward the product/service that is being reviewed/rated. Without a strong degree of passion toward the product, the reviewer may not necessarily give much thought into writing his/her review nor would he/she read or give any importance to what the other (existing, if any) reviews state. To understand both the sides, it is appropriate to use products/services that are different from one another to capture the underlying dynamics.

6. Just as in any online product review scenario, the reviewers must not communicate with one another and introduce other (e.g., coercive) types of bias

Based on the above set of guidelines, we created an experimental setup where each participant rates a product/service in a sequential manner after having read all existing reviews of the product/service. These results will provide a sense as to the existence or absence of sequential bias. Our singular goal here is to determine the presence of sequential bias in online product/ service reviews and, if present, its general dynamics. To this end, we consider any evidence, however small it may be, that provides support for the existence of sequential bias.

To operationalize our experimental study, we requested undergraduate students from two different course sections (GROUP1 comprising students from OPIM302 – Information Systems and GROUP 2 comprising students from OPIM 408 – Marketing Analytics) at a major research University to participate in a short inclass exercise. Since product/service ratings can somehow naturally be incorporated into these courses, the students were not told about the exact purpose of the study but rather that we are interested in how they rate these products/services. We used three different products and services to prevent simultaneous indifference among all participants: Gursel Tours, Hangar, and iPhone 4.0. Gursel Tours is the only bus service provider at this University. Most of the students use its services several times a week. Hangar is the popular and the largest restaurant/cafeteria at this University campus. These two service providers were selected because the majority of the students have had prior experiences with them. iPhone, on the other hand, was chosen as it is the most widely used and most well-known cellular phone in this general geographical area at the time of our data collection experiments.

The students in each course section participated in only one of two product/service reviews assigned for that course section: GROUP2 students were randomly selected to participate in reviewing Hangar or Gursel; GROUP1 students were randomly selected to participate in reviewing Gursel or iPhone. Each student participated in reviewing only one product/service. Once the students in the two course sections were assigned to a product/ service, the person conducting the experiment met individually with each of these students selected to review the product/service (e.g., GROUP2 student S1 chosen to review Gursel). We conducted a pre-test to ensure that all participants were familiar with the product/service being considered. During this individual (pre-test) meeting, the person conducting the experiment asked the participant about their knowledge/experience with the product/ service of interest. This was done in a manner to avoid the introduction of any bias. The students were not asked for their opinion – just the fact that they were aware of the product/service with enough familiarity, and that they have used the product/ service enough to be able to form an opinion.

After the pre-test, all students from a course section who are assigned to review a given product/service were assembled in a classroom equipped with a computer and a projector. At the beginning of the study, we asked the participants to write their score and their name on a post-it note, which we then collected later, and instructed them to not share their score with anyone else during that class session. The students were instructed to use a rating score from 1 to 10 based on their experience with this product/service. The absolute rating (i.e., the one that is not biased by the rating given by other participants) is private in that it is not known to the rest of the participants in the room at least until the end of the study. The students were not allowed to talk with one another during the study.

We then randomly chose all students in this classroom to participate in the next step, which is to provide their public review of the product/service. The students were not aware of their order in this sequence before-hand. We asked the participants to provide a brief review of the product/service and then an updated score. The review and the update score for each participant were then input into a spreadsheet. A running average of all previous review scores was also displayed for use by subsequent reviewers. We ensured that every new reviewer read through all previous reviews and scores for this product/service before posting their review and score. The reviews and scores were typed into the spreadsheet in a sequential manner. The order sequence was maintained and it was not re-sorted for any reviewer. Every participant knew all the previous reviews and rating scores.

Each of the participants gave a narrative of their review as well as a rating score. We ensured that all new reviewers read through all existing narratives as well as rating scores before generating their own narrative and rating score. The rating scores essentially reflected the narrative rating in that when the narrative was positive, the rating was positive (i.e., any score above 5) and vice versa. Since the range of the rating scores was between 1 and 10, we informed the participants that a rating score of 1 signifies complete negative opinion while a 10 signifies complete positive opinion. The remainder of the scores (i.e., 2–9) represented increasing degrees of positive opinions of the product/service of interest. We also informed the participants that a neutral score would be 5 or 6 depending on whether they were (even if infinitesimally small, which for all practical purposes can be classified as neutral given the nature of this exercise) leaning toward the negative or positive side of the rating score scale. For all practical purposes, we considered both 5 and 6 as neutral rating scores.

We thereby collected data on the participants’ previous (unbiased) score as well as the updated (possibly sequentially biased) score and their review of the product/service. The updated score need not necessarily be different from the previous score that was written on a post-it note and it was indeed the case in a majority of cases (70.59%). Only in 29.41% of the cases were the rating scores updated (to be different from their original – post-it notes – rating scores) by the participants upon reading reviews by other participants and their corresponding rating scores. This was as expected since not all products/services simultaneously invoke the same level of strong emotions in all participants which was the rationale for our choice of three different products/services in this study. Even though the sample size is relatively small, the results collected in this study showed all possible directions and magnitudes with respect to the movements between absolute and relative participant scores.

To reiterate, we ensured that the guidelines that we imposed on the study as listed in the beginning of (this) Section 3 were strictly followed. We ensured that #1 was strictly followed by repeating all earlier reviews for each new reviewer. Since the class size and hence the number of participants in any given experiment ranged from 10 and 22, #2 was not an issue. As in #1 and the fact that every participant was familiar with the product or service before providing his/her review and rating score, #3 was followed. By using a before-score, which was kept secret from other participants, and related after-score, we ensured that #4 was strictly followed. None of the products or services that were used was trivial for the entire population of participants since these are frequently used and the participants had strong opinions that they shared with the rest of the class. #5 was therefore not an issue in this study. We also ensured that #6 was strictly followed.

## 4. Analysis and results

There were a total of 68 respondents between the two course sections divided among the different products and services (Hangar, Gursel, and iPhone). Table 1 presents a summary of the before (i.e., written on post-it notes at the beginning of the study) and after (i.e., updated scores after viewing existing reviews by other participants) rating scores. Specifically, the last row in Table 1 refers to the average score provided by the reviewers before (i.e., on a post-it note) and after (i.e., provided on the spreadsheet).

As can be seen in the last row in Table 1, the average of the scores that were displayed in class are higher than the average of the scores that were noted on the post-it notes. This confirms theoretical results provided in Kapoor and Piramuthu [11] as well as Piramuthu et al. [14] – these previous analytical studies indicated that the new review scores will always be more in the presence of sequential bias. However, this contradicts one of the results in Sikora and Chauhan [17]. Does this aggregate result from our study provide evidence that sequential bias may exist, at least as far as that indicated by our results? To answer this question, we need to consider each of the individual responses since when average values are used, higher values in one set of cases may have washed away lower values in its complementary set of cases.

We now consider the individual responses where the before – and after – scores were different and look for any pattern that may be present. Among the 68 cases, only 20 cases had different before and after rating scores. A summary of this distribution is given in Table 3. Table 3 only includes those cases where there were updates to the (before to after) rating scores. Since there were eight different types of cases, to facilitate comprehension, we assign codes for these types of cases and list the different types in Table 2.

The first column of Table 2 contains the update code. The second column contains the before-characteristic, i.e., the score that was written on a post-it note. This characteristic has values negative, neutral, and positive corresponding to scores of 1–4, 5–6, and 7–10, respectively. The third column contains the average value of previous (post-it note) reviews of other reviewers relative to this reviewer’s post-it note score. We use higher, same, and lower to represent the relative values. The last column represents direction of the after-score (vs. the before-score), which is the updated score by this reviewer in response to his/her post-it note score and the (post-it note) score given by others.

As can be observed in Table 3, a majority (8/20 = 40%) of the update cases belong to update code $\cdot _ { \mathrm { c } _ { \mathrm { , } } } ,$ which represents the case where a positive review score is decreased in response to a lower average score by other (earlier) respondents. This is an example of herd behavior whereby a reviewer adjusts his/her score in the direction of the average score of existing reviews. The next high frequency type is $\cdot _ { \mathbf { g } ^ { \prime } }$ with 15% (=3/20) of the update cases. These cases too are similar to the $\cdot _ { \mathrm { c } } \cdot$ cases in following the crowd. Three types come next with 10% (=2/20) each: $\mathbf { \dot { a } } , \mathbf { \dot { d } } ,$ and ‘f.’ Although the ‘a’ cases are similar to the ‘c’ and $\cdot _ { \mathbf { g } } ,$ cases, the ‘d’ and ‘f’ cases exhibit sequential bias behavior. The $\cdot _ { \mathrm { d } } \cdot \mathrm { \mathbf { \Omega } }$ cases are the ones that were neutral to begin with (as represented by the written post-it notes score) and decreases in response to a higher average existing score by other reviewers. The $\mathbf { \cdot } _ { \mathbf { f } }$ cases are negative to begin with and decrease in response to a higher average existing score. All other update types have only one case each and represent 5% of the overall number of update cases. Among these, case $\mathbf { \cdot } \mathbf { b } ^ { \mathbf { \cdot } }$ is hard to explain since one would expect a neutral score to remain unchanged in response to the exact same average score (as the earlier score from this reviewer) from the other reviewers. However, the respondent decreased his/her updated score. We do not have an explanation for this update type. The other two update $\mathrm { t y p e s - " e " }$ and $\mathbf { \delta } \cdot \mathbf { h } ^ { \prime } \textrm { - }$ show sequential bias. Update type $\cdot _ { \mathrm { e } } ,$ is initially positive, and increases in response to a lower average from the other reviewers. Update type $\mathbf { \cdot h } ^ { \prime }$ is initially neutral and increases in response to a lower average from other reviewers.

Among the update types observed, $\mathbf { \dot { d } } , \mathbf { \dot { e } } , \mathbf { \dot { f } }$ and ‘h’ are evidences for sequential bias. These cases make up 30% (=6/20) of the update cases. These cases are spread between Hangar (GROUP2) and Gursel (GROUP1) product/service. Within Hangar, there are about a third of the cases $\scriptstyle ( = 2 / 6 )$ that show evidence for sequential bias. With Gursel, about a third of the cases (=4/12) show evidence $f o r$ sequential bias. Within the iPhone case, all two cases show evidence for herd behavior. Gursel and Hangar are two service providers that are popular among students. However, most students do not know all the details about their services. For example, a student typically commutes one or two routes, and may typically order one or two different dishes among all possible selections. During data collection we realized that students found out many details about both Gursel and Hangar that were previously unknown to them. However, with iPhone we observed that this was a well-known and widely-used product, and information sharing aspect of our study did not surface as a result. We felt that with iPhone our exercise was less about information/view sharing as all the participants knew about the product.

So far, we considered all existing reviews as having the same influence on the new reviewer. However, this may not always be true. ## 5. Discussion

Sequential bias has the potential to introduce unintended errors in recommender systems that could significantly reduce the accuracy of recommendations. Even though sequential bias has not received its fair share of attention from researchers in the context of online customer reviews, there is a need to study this phenomenon in detail in order to understand its dynamics as well as its effect on the resulting recommender system performance. Unlike several other facets of research on recommender systems where data from online retailers can be directly used after appropriate preprocessing, no amount of pre-processing of existing data would help in the study of sequential bias since data collection needs to be done within necessary constraints such as the sequence in which a reviewer reads existing reviews, etc. It is also difficult to accomplish this using data from an online retailer when the online retail store Web site allows sorting of reviews before they are viewed. Given the above-restriction, it is necessary to conduct highly controlled experiments that ensure the guidelines mentioned in the beginning of Section 3 are followed in order to minimize capturing effects due to other phenomenon or just sheer noise.

We used three different non-trivial products and services and followed the listed guidelines. Results from our study provide evidence for the existence of sequential bias. We sincerely hope that these results would provide the impetus for researchers to conduct extensive and careful studies of the sequential bias phenomenon in online reviews as well as their implications in recommender systems.

A drawback that we can immediately notice in this experimental study is that, unlike in a real-world online consumer product/ service review scenario, the participants know (or, at least, are aware of) one another. These could possibly have introduced their own bias due to the implicit pressure to ‘follow the crowd’ in such a closed setting. Regardless, we believe that the few data points that still display sequential bias points toward its existence as well as its significance.

A missing element in this study that we intend to incorporate in an extension of this study is a post-experimental interview of some sort that asks the participants the reason for change/update in the previous (i.e., post-it note) score, if any. Even if they did not modify their original score later, it would help to know why they acted that way after reading all existing reviews and knowing all existing rating scores. Studying what, if the participants can vocalize their thoughts, leads to a modification in their rating score could possibly reveal some components of the underlying dynamics of the sequential bias phenomenon.

To our knowledge, this is the first study that has attempted to show evidence for sequential bias in the context of online product/ service reviews through a controlled experimental setting. Such a setup is necessary toavoid consequences related tounknownperusal sequence of existing reviews before a new review is submitted. Since sequential bias is a relatively unknown phenomenon in the general area of recommender systems, we sincerely hope that this paper would raise awareness so its underlying dynamics can be revealed. Since sequential bias is shown to exist in online product reviews, the next step would be to determine means to alleviate its deleterious effects in recommender systems. Understanding any patterns that are present in sequential bias and its manifestations would help in the removal of its effects in recommender systems. This would translate to better quality recommender systems that are trusted by both online retailers as well as customers.

## 6. Academic and managerial significance of this research

According to one study by a consumer research firm,<sup>1</sup> only 15% of the consumers do not base their purchase decisions on online reviews. The same study shows that over 30% of the consumers regularly and over 50% of the consumers occasionally read user reviews before making a purchase decision.

Even online retail businesses alone account for a large chunk of overall retail industry. According to NRF (National Retail Foundation) with 74 Billion USD in revenues in 2013, the first introducer of ‘‘online user reviews’’, Amazon, now ranks at #11 among the Nation’s largest retailers, up from #15 in 2012, and #19 in 2011. Its Chinese counterpart Alibaba group sports an economic valuation of 120 billion USD (estimation by The Economist magazine) and includes Taobao, a Chinese equivalent of eBay. All of these online retailers’ sales also depend heavily on user reviews.

User reviews do not just affect online retailing, but also affects local business/product/service selection. It is crucial to better understand user reviewing process and take necessary measures to reduce bias from business perspective for user satisfaction.
