# How Do EHRs and a Meaningful Use Initiative Affect Breaches of Patient Information?

Seung Hyun Kim,<sup>a</sup> Juhee Kwon<sup>b</sup>

Abstract. Given the cost of electronic health records (EHRs) to society and hospitals and the heightened concern about breaches of patient information, it is imperative to understand the risks that EHRs and the pursuit of a meaningful use (MU) initiative, apart from actual attestation of MU, may pose to the privacy of patient information. In this study, we examine how the adoption of EHRs and an MU initiative affect the occurrence of breaches of patient information. We also study whether and how EHRs and MU initiatives may increase the risk of accidental and malicious breaches of patient information. Using data from multiple sources, such as the American Hospital Association (AHA), the Healthcare Information and Management Systems Society (HIMSS) Analytics Database, and the Privacy Rights Clearinghouse (PRC), our study shows that implementing EHRs leads to a 3.081 times higher risk of a breach of patient information. This heightened risk is mostly driven b the occurrence of more accidental breaches. Although the effect of an MU initiative on the overall number of breaches is not significant, undertaking MU increases the risk of accidental breaches 1.771 times. We also found that these risks increased more among relatively larger hospitals. However, simply having more components of EHRs does not necessarily increase the risk of breaches. Overall, our findings show that breaches of patient information may be rooted in the digitized data and processes enabled by technologies such as EHRs and their usage rather than resident in the technological com ponents themselves. We conclude that, despite recent evidence that usage of EHRS has improved the quality of healthcare, quality must go hand in hand with the protection of patient information.

## 1. Introduction

Effective use of electronic health records (EHRs) is considered an important step toward the goal of improving the quality of U.S. healthcare while reducing its costs (Blumenthal and Tavenner 2010, Furukawa et al. 2010, Buntin et al. 2011, Appari et al. 2012, Bhargava and Mishra 2014). As a part of the American Recovery and Reinvestment Act (ARRA) in 2009, the Health Information Technology for Economic and Clinical Health (HITECH) Act was enacted to encourage U.S. healthcare providers to adopt EHRs and the necessary supporting technologies. The HITECH Act authorized the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) to spend billions of dollars in incentive payments to qualifying physicians and hospitals to encourage “meaningful use” (MU) of adopted EHRs. Healthcare providers can be meaningful users if they adopt and meaningfully use certified EHRs to improve the quality of their care and to submit their information on clinical quality measures to the CMS. As a result of this cash infusion, a rapid transition to EHRs has moved healthcare information toward more digital forms. Consequently, vast quantities of patient information have become accessible and shared among healthcare providers and facilities.

The HITECH Act is expected to motivate a change of heart by hospitals that had hesitated to adopt EHRs because of their high implementation cost. However, although EHRs are expected to improve healthcare qual ity through improved efficiency and fewer errors (Furukawa et al. 2010, Appari et al. 2012, Bhargava and Mishra 2014), the risk of unauthorized access and dissemination of digitized healthcare information and the attendant risks to patient privacy have become one of the greatest concerns associated with EHRs (Lamberg 2001, Hampton 2008, Angst and Agarwal 2009). Specifically, breaches of patient information not only risk revealing patient data, but also result in remediation costs, penalties, or reputational damage. In addition, patients are unlikely to share their private information unless they are confident it will be handled securely (Anderson and Agarwal 2011); such withholding may lead to poor health outcomes. Thus, healthcare security is closely related to the quality of care and deserves special attention.

Given the cost of EHRs to society and hospitals and the heightened concern about breaches of patient information, it is imperative to understand the risks that EHRs and the pursuit of MU may pose to the privacy of patient information. Furthermore, it is crucial that policy makers and managers of healthcare institutions and facilities understand under what conditions EHRs and undertaking MU initiatives may increase the occurrence of different types of breaches. One of the main premises of this paper is that pursuing an MU initiative, apart from actual attestation of MU, may increase the risk of a breach of patient information as hospitals newly deploy EHRs or use them more extensively to comply with the MU requirements without proper protection. That is, although hospitals should improve their physical and network security to attest to MU, we focus on a potential increase in risks over the course of seeking to attest for MU.

In this study, we examined how the adoption of EHRs and an MU initiative<sup>1</sup> affect the occurrence of breaches of patient information. We also studied how they affect different types of breaches, that is, accidental and malicious, differently and under what conditions EHRs may increase the risk of breaches. Based on a data set compiled from multiple sources, such as the American Hospital Association (AHA), the Healthcare Information and Management Systems Society (HIMSS) Analytics Database, and the Privacy Rights Clearinghouse (PRC), our study shows that implementing EHRs leads to a 3.081 times higher risk of a breach of patient information. This higher risk is mostly driven by an increase in accidental breaches instead of breaches caused by a malicious intent. Although the effect of an MU initiative on the overall number of breaches was not significant, undertaking MU increases the risk of an accidental breach by 1.771 times. We also found that the risk of breaches increased mostly among relatively larger hospitals. However, simply having more EHRs components does not necessarily increase the risk of breaches. Overall, our findings show that breaches of patient information may be rooted in the digitized data and processes enabled by technologies such as EHRs and their usage rather than resident in the technologica components themselves.

Our study contributes to the literature in three ways. First, to the best of our knowledge, we are among the first to examine the effects of the EHRs that are extensively used in hospitals and MU initiatives on incidents of breaches of patient information. Prior studies have dealt with how concerns about privacy breaches (Angst and Agarwal 2009) and other factors, such as physicians community identity (Mishra et al. 2012), social net works (Zheng et al. 2010), and regulations (Miller and Tucker 2009), may influence hospitals’ adoption of EHRs. One exception is Kwon and Johnson (2018) that examined the effect of the actual attestation of a stage 1 MU on breach occurrences. Notably, our study highlights the negative impact of health information technology (IT), whereas the health IT literature has focused on the positive impact of IT investments (Devaraj and Kohli 2000, Miller and Tucker 2011b, Mukhopadhyay et al. 2011, Menon and Kohli 2013, Lin et al. 2014). Second, we contribute to the information security and privacy literature by distinguishing between different types of breaches—accidental and malicious— and considering the distinct mechanisms that lead to each type. Lastly, our study reveals how the size of hospitals that invest in IT may result in a differential risk of breaches; little research has been devoted to this.

Our study offers important practical implications for policy makers and practitioners in its evidence of increased risks after deployment of EHRs and MU initiatives. As for governmental implications, we demonstrate that accidental breaches are important consequences of EHRs and MU initiatives that should be reflected in future government policies and in revisions of the requirements for different MU stages. Hospital managers should be also vigilant in preventing breaches of patient information after they adopt EHRs and while pursuing MU initiatives. Our supplementary analysis reveals that larger hospitals should expect to invest a higher percentage of additional resources in protecting patient information than smaller facilities will find necessary.

## 2. Theoretical Backgrounds 2.1. Literature Review

In the information security and privacy literature, information breaches have been studied as impor tant failures of an organization’s information security management systems. The literature has examined various IT and non-IT factors that influence information breaches, especially in healthcare organizations. First, investments in health IT and information security countermeasures may affect breaches of patient information. For example, proactive investments in security countermeasures without having experienced any security incident are more effective in preventing breaches of patient information than reactive investments after a security incident (Kwon and Johnson 2014). However, adoption of encryption of patient information alone may not decrease the risk of such a breach (Miller and Tucker 2011a).

Other organizational characteristics of hospitals may also affect the occurrence of a breach of patient information. The actual attestation for stage 1 MU may affect breach occurrences in various ways (Kwon and Johnson 2018). By classifying patient information breaches into three categories, accidental internal, malicious internal, and external, the actual attestation for stage 1 MU was found to reduce external breaches in the short term but had no effect on them in the long term. In contrast, the actual attestation for stage 1 MU decreased accidental internal breaches in the long term. Malicious internal breaches were not affected by the actual attestation. Furthermore, compliance may affect breach occurrences differently in operationally mature and immature hospitals (Kwon and Johnson 2013). Competitive environments (Gaynor et al. 2012) and institutional factors (Angst et al. 2017) may also influence occurrences of patient information breaches.

In a nonhospital context, board structure may be associated with information breaches (Hsu and Wang 2014). A time trend also may be associated with breaches. For example, breach occurrences because of negligence and malicious intent remained constant between 2006 and 2015, but the number of records affected by malicious types of breaches slowly decreased over time (Edwards et al. 2016).

In summary, prior research studied various factors, such as investments in IT and security countermeasures, organizational characteristics, and environments, that may increase or decrease the risk of breaches of patient information. The deployment of EHRs and pursuit of MU introduce dramatic changes in how hospitals operate because this deployment involves installation of software and hardware, digitization of patient information and processes, integration of systems to enable retrieval of patient information from multiple sources, and revision of policies governing data access and use (Miller and Tucker 2011a). Changes in organizational culture also may be involved with EHRs and MU initiatives (NLC 2013). Thus, the net effect, in terms of breaches of patient information, on information security from deploying EHRs and pursuing MU initiatives is not yet clear.

Among the studies reviewed above, Kwon and Johnson (2018) that examined the effect of attestation for stage 1 MU on patient information breaches is most closely related to ours because one of our focal variables involves MU as well. In contrast to their focus on the ex post effect of successful MU attestation, our interest centers on a potential increase in risks over the course of seeking to attest for MU, no matter how successful or unsuccessful the effort was Our study departs from previous ones in that, based on human factor theory and routine activity theory, we offer more theoretical accounts of the mechanisms that lead to two different types of breaches, accidental and malicious. Notably, prior studies that investigated the effects of different technologies or policies on two (Edwards et al. 2016) or three (Gaynor et al. 2012, Kwon and Johnson 2018) types of breaches relied more on empirical examination than on theoretical explanations.

## 2.2. Patient Information Breaches and EHRs

It should be noted that breaches of patient information occur for various reasons. Similar in spirit to Edwards et al. (2016) that analyzed breaches arising from negligence or malicious activities, we classify breaches of patient information as either accidental or malicious. Although some studies divided breaches into smaller categories [e.g., accidental internal, malicious internal, and external in Kwon and Johnson (2018) or malicious attacks, unauthorized disclosures, and lost or misplaced protected health information in Gaynor et al. (2012)], our two-category classification is consistent with the classification of violations in the Health Insurance Porta bility and Accountability Act (HIPAA) as unintended or willful causes of patient information breaches.<sup>2</sup> In our classification, malicious breaches are those caused by deliberate intent to gain unauthorized access to patient information or to use patient information for identity theft or other illegitimate purposes Breaches caused by hacking into a network are also categorized as malicious. In contrast, accidental breaches are those that occur with no intent to access patient information. The reasons for accidental breaches include lost or misplaced computers, laptops, and portable data storage devices. Hospitals are required to publicly post all breaches in local news outlets and on the Department of Health and Human Services (henceforth HHS) website. They also must notify affected patients without regard to whether the breaches are accidental or malicious. Both types of breache impose direct remediation costs or fines on hospitals Indirect costs are also incurred through secondary misuse, such as identity theft or credit card fraud. It is notable that prior studies focused more on deliberate malicious breaches than on accidental breaches (D’Arcy et al. 2009, Liang and Xue 2009, Ransbotham and Mitra 2009, Wang and Kim 2009, Yue et al. 2019).

Both types of breaches are prevalent today, although in the media and in our data set accidental breaches outnumber malicious breaches. In general, we posit that implementation of EHRs and pursuing MU initiatives make hospitals’ information systems more vulnerable to failure, as measured by breaches of patient information, both accidental and malicious. We adopt human factor theory to understand the effects of EHRs and MU initiatives on accidental breaches of patient information. We also draw on routine activity theory to explicate their effects on the incidence of malicious breaches.

Human factor theory studies the process of accident causation in work environments (Reason 1990, Shappell and Wiegmann 2001). The theory deals with events that directly or indirectly result from human activities and errors. According to a recent view of human factor theory, an error is considered a system failure, and human error is an outcome of latent conditions in the system beyond the psychological or physical factors within individuals (Reason 2000). The theory has been used to better understand accidents in various contexts, including aviation, mining, road transport, nuclear power plants, and construction (Macwan and Mosleh 1994, Lawton and Ward 2005, Shappell and Wiegmann 2009, Patterson and Shappell 2010, Hosseinian and Torghabeh 2012). Human factor theory is suitable for understanding how EHRs may affect the risk of accidental patient information breaches because it helps analyze whether EHRs introduce systematic changes in hospital environments that make these facilities more or less vulnerable to an accidental breach as a system failure exclusive of any malicious intent.

In human factor theory, an accident is a consequence of (1) unsafe acts, (2) preconditions for unsafe acts, (3) unsafe supervision, and (4) organizational influences. The latter three create latent failures, but unsafe acts trigger actual failure (Reason 1990, Shappell and Wiegmann 2001). Unsafe acts include errors caused by poor skills, bad decisions, or perceptions and violations of rules and regulations; the preconditions for unsafe acts encompass substandard conditions and practices by operators; unsafe supervision relates to inadequate supervision and supervisory violations; and organizational influences include components such as resource management, organizational climate, and operational processes.

Compared with the management of paper-based patient records, the changeover to EHRs comes with numerous challenges (Sittig and Singh 2009, Carter 2013, Yaraghi 2016). First, many hospitals lack both the skilled IT labor and management expertise required by EHRs. Second, management often lacks a blueprint to guide the comprehensive integration of the healthcare systems that make EHRs effective and efficient. Third, many EHRs systems have poor usability and disrupt existing workflows. Moreover, because many parts of clinical workflows have been paper-based, there are no standards to guide clinical workflows related to EHR systems. Fourth, with digitized data and processes, management must develop and implement more rules and policies, a step that by its very nature means a potential increase in the number of violations. Despite this increased risk, many hospitals have insufficient and inadequate training of EHRs users. For example, one of the most common types of breaches of patient information is a lost or stolen laptop containing sensitive patient information. Thus, digitized patient information requires new rules and standards about accessing, exchanging, and processing it. These risks and challenges, largely nonexistent with paper-based patient records, increase the vulnerability of hospitals with EHRs to unsafe acts by EHRs users and to more frequent violations of new rules and policies. Both risks may be compounded by inadequate supervision by management. Therefore, it is possible that the deployment of EHRs inherently leads to a higher risk of an accidental breach of patient information.

To understand the effect of EHRs on malicious breaches, we adopt routine activity theory (Cohen and Felson 1979) from criminology, where it has been applied and proven to be useful in explaining victimization and offender patterns in several contexts, including copyright infringement, corporate crimes, unauthorized access, employee theft, and cybersecurity (Tewksbury and Mustaine 2010, Wang et al. 2015, Hui et al. 2017, An and Kim 2018). According to this theory, structural changes in daily activities affect crime rates by chang ing criminal opportunities. Perpetrators are regarded as rational decision makers, and motivated perpetrators commit violations by balancing their own capability and guardianship against a potential victim’s vulnerability and attractiveness (Cohen and Felson 1979). A rational perpetrator assesses the suitability of a target by four criteria: visibility, inertia, value, and access (Cohen and Felson 1979). Visibility refers to the extent that a perpetrator can identify a personal or property target as a victim; inertia is the extent to which a target can resist illegal treatment by offenders because of its weight, size, or locked features; value refers to the monetary or psychological benefit of a target; and access is a perpetrator’s ability to approach and gain a target.

Deployment and use of EHRs introduces a structural change in the daily routine of hospitals. Consequently, EHRs without proper controls in place make patient information more suitable as a target compared with paper-based patient records. Unlike when paper documents were stored in multiple locations, patient information is highly visible when EHRs compile information from multiple sources. It is also easier to copy or move digitized information than a pile of documents. And because more comprehensive information about a single patient can be retrieved with EHRs, the stolen information may be of higher value. Moreover, digitized patient information may be more accessible than paper-based information stored in a physically separated and protected area. With EHRs, more physicians, nurses, and employees are in an environment in which they can access the systems from their own terminals. These features apply to both internal perpetrators and external intruders who seek to gain access to patient information. In addition to target suitability, the “guardians” of EHRs are less visible than those of paper-based records. Thus, we also suppose that the deployment of EHRs may lead to a higher risk of malicious breaches of patient information.

## 2.3. MU Initiatives and Breaches of Patient Information

Meaningful use is the use of certified EHRs to achieve four main objectives (HealthIT 2015): (1) to “improve quality, safety, efficiency, and reduce health disparities,” (2) to “engage patients and family,” (3) to “improve care coordination, and population and public health,” and (4) to “maintain privacy and security of patient health information.” These main requirements include e-prescribing, electronic exchange of health information, and reporting of quality measures (Jha 2010). As a result, MU compliance is expected to lead to “better clinical outcomes, improved population health outcomes, increased transparency and efficiency, empowered individuals, and more robust research data on health systems (HealthIT 2015).” The HITECH Act provides practicing clinicians and hospitals with financial incentives to qualify as “meaningful” users of EHRs. For example, under the Medicare EHR Incentive Program run by the CMS, a qualified practicing clinician can receive a maximum incentive of \$44,000 and a qualified hospital can collect between \$2 million and \$10 million (Jha 2010). The objectives of “meaningful use” required for clinicians and hospitals evolve over three stages. Stage 1 MU focuses on capturing and sharing data and became available for attestation in 2011. Beginning in 2014, participating hospitals had to demonstrate stage 2 MU, which is aimed at advancing clinical processes. Stage 3 MU, which is directed at improved health outcomes, started in 2017. From the viewpoint of information security and privacy protection, stage 1 MU, as one of the core objectives, requires hospitals to protect electronic health information by conducting a security risk analysis, applying necessary security updates, and addressing identified security deficiencies.<sup>3</sup> Stage 2 MU further requires encryption of data. Protection of patient information becomes more important at stage 2 because the stage 2 objectives (and the stage 1 objectives updated in 2014)<sup>4</sup> include allowing patients to access their health information online.

When the goals of EHRs were first declared, around 10% of hospitals and 20% of physicians adopted EHRs, although few of these adopters were using EHRs in a way that satisfied the preliminary definition of MU (Jha 2010). Thus, a significant gap existed between “acquisition” and “assimilation” (Fichman and Kemerer 1999) of EHRs. The information systems (IS) literature has long noted that technology creates value in its postadoption stages through its ingrained and extensive usage in a particular process. For example, a processoriented view of the impact of IT (Barua et al. 1995, Soh and Markus 1995, Barua and Mukhopahdyay 2000) argues that the effect of adopted IT systems on organizational performance is closely related to its use in business processes in its postadoption stages. The value of IT may be created not by IT spending alone but by actual IT usage (Devaraj and Kohli 2003, Zhu and Kraemer 2005). Therefore, the MU initiative is mindful that the quality of care is not improved by mere installation of EHRs but by the full usage of EHRs in hospital processes.

However, the increasing dependency on EHRs linked to critical clinical and administrative processes may augment the possible risk from accidental and malicious threats. A hospital’s pursuit of MU implies progress to a more advanced form of EHRs from paper-based patient records. With an MU initiative, more physicians, nurses, and employees must use EHRs extensively in more clinical and administrative processes and share more health information than before. However, the structural changes that MU entails are likely to magnify the risks of the numerous shortcomings often associated with implementation of EHRs (Yaraghi 2016). As noted earlier, these risks include lack of IT staff, inadequate training and guidance of EHRs users, poor compliance with new rules and policies because of lack of oversight, and disruptions in clinical workflows From the perspective of human factor theory, this situation will result in a higher risk of accidents because of more unsafe acts by EHRs users, more frequent violations of new rules and policies, and inadequate supervision by management.

In this paper, we are interested in changes in the risk of breaches in the course of preparing for and pursuing attestation for MU. Apart from actual attestation for MU, we believe that simply embarking on an MU initiative affects hospitals, even before actual attestation. First, hospitals have to start using certified EHRs before actual attestation because they must report their past usage of EHRs. Second, given the complexity of EHRs, hospitals often phase in implementation or upgrades of EHRs for MU attestation to avoid the high risks involved with big bang implementation (Culp et al. 2005). Thus, in many cases, hospitals start using EHRs to a certain degree to comply with MU requirements far earlier than the reporting period required for attestation. Taken together, a hospital’s pursuit of MU may lead to a higher risk of accidental breaches.

MU also makes patient information more attractive to intruders than before because more records about a single patient that are drawn and integrated from multiple sources become easily available (Yaraghi 2016). Because MU facilitates the exchange of patient information within and between health facilities (Vest and Gamm 2010, Ayvaci et al. 2015), it becomes easier for inside and outside intruders to access this information from different terminals and over a network (Yaraghi 2016). A target could become more visible when a hospital’s MU initiative is actively discussed within a hospital and in the media. Given this, we posit that the pursuit of MU may increase the likelihood of a malicious breach of patient information.

Although more extensive use of EHRs for MU may increase the likelihood of breaches, stage 1 of MU requires as one of its objectives a measure for protecting EHRs and a risk management process. To successfully attest to stage 1 MU, hospitals and professionals must conduct a security risk analysis that involves reviewing certified EHRs and implementing security updates as necessary. Therefore, despite the “theoretical” increased risk by assimilation of EHRs with clinical and administrative processes, a hospital’s management may carefully plan its security infrastructure to mitigate the risk of unsafe acts, violations, and improper supervision. As for malicious breaches, MU may be effective in establishing a trustworthy security system as a guardian to deter crimes and make it more difficult for external intruders to compromise a hospital’s systems. However, such efforts are not guaranteed before successful attestation of stage 1 MU.

## 3. Econometric Methods 3.1. Preliminary Analysis

Figure 1 provides model-free comparisons of the number of breaches per hospital over time between hospitals with and without EHRs as well as hospitals with and without MU initiatives. Figure 1(a) shows that between 2005 and 2012, our study period, hospitals with EHRs had more breaches than those without EHRs.<sup>5</sup> Figure 1(b) also illustrates that hospitals with MU initiatives experienced more breaches than those without MU initiatives.<sup>6</sup> A similar pattern can be found with regard to the number of breaches normalized by hospital size in Figure 1, (c) and (d). Although these graphs may show the possible increased risk after EHRs and MU initiatives, they do not necessarily reflect a causal effect. Thus, we describe our econometric approach to examine the effect of EHRs and MU initiatives in the next subsection.

## 3.2. Econometric Analysis

Our main analysis is expected to take into account the right-censored nature of breaches (Kwon and Johnson 2014). We employed the Cox proportional hazards model to account for time-to-breach. Specifically, we modeled the duration between the beginning of our observation and a breach occurrence. In the Cox proportional hazards model, the hazard h(t) for hospital i is assumed to be

$$
h _ {i} (t) = h _ {0} (t) \cdot \mathrm{Exp} (\beta X),\tag{1}
$$

where $h _ { 0 } ( t )$ denotes the baseline hazard; $\beta$ is a vector of estimated coefficients; X is a vector of independent variables. In the model, an estimated hazard ratio greater that one would indicate an increased risk of breaches because of each covariate. Because we had yearly observations of all the independent variables in our data set, all the independent variables are time varying, except for a few time-invariant variables, such as the types of hospitals. This is necessary because all hospitals implement EHRs at different times, which is a discrete time-varying covariate. Some hospitals might have already implemented EHRs at the beginning of our observation period, but others might only have done so in the middle of an observation period. Because of the possibility of the reoccurrence of breaches, we clustered the standard errors within each hospital and allowed in the model for repeated occurrences of breaches per hospital. We assume that the baseline hazard remains the same after a breach occurs. Thus, robust standard errors are reported for this analysis.

However, implementation of EHRs may be endogenous and self-selected. For example, hospitals’ managers who anticipate a lower risk of breaches of patient information after implementation of EHRs are more likely than others to implement EHRs. The hospitals that choose to implement EHRs may differ inherently from those that do not. If we do not account for this type of endogeneity, our estimate may be biased (Heckman 1979, Van De Ven and Van Praag 1981, Boyeset al. 1989).

One of the most common approaches to addressing the endogeneity issue is to use a two-stage least squares (2SLS) estimator. However, the 2SLS estimator is inconsistent in a nonlinear model, including the proportional hazard model (Terza et al. 2008, Hadley et al. 2010). Alternatively, the two-stage residual inclusion (2SRI) estimator in which the residuals from the firststage estimation are included as an additional control variable in the second-stage estimation is known to be consistent and performs better. Thus, in the first stage, we used a random effects probit model with year fixed effects to estimate the probability that a hospital implements EHRs and undertakes an MU initiative, respectively. Then, the difference between the predicted probability of implementing EHRs and the actual status of adoption of EHRs, labeled as $R e s i d u a l s _ { ( E H R s ) } ,$ is calculated and inserted in the second-stage model. Similarly, we obtained the residual term for the MU initiative, $\dot { R e s i d u a l s } _ { ( M U ) } ,$ , and added it as a control variable

In the health economics literature, the practices in the same geographical area have been frequently used as valid instrumental variables (Stukel et al. 2007, Hadley et al. 2010, Miller and Tucker 2011b). In addition, based on institutional theory (DiMaggio and Powell 1983), coercive pressure, such as state-level regulations, and mimetic pressure, such as adoption, by other organizations may drive organizations to adopt certain technologies, including EHRs (Teo et al. 2003, Angst et al. 2010). However, EHRs and MU in the same geographical area are not necessarily correlated with breach occurrences, a disconnection that typically makes them eligible as exogenous instruments. Nevertheless, one may argue that our instrumental variables are correlated with the error term because of knowledge spillover in the region. For example, employees in one hospital may move to another or learn from others by attending a local conference. However, if there exists any effect of knowledge spillovers in the region, it is likely to decrease the risk of information breach. In our data set, the state level EHRs and MU variables are not negatively correlated with patient information breaches (0.02 and 0.03, respectively) while their correlations are very low. In fact, it has been criticized that it is extremely difficult for a hospital to learn from other hospitals in the same geographic area about how to better protect patient information and what led to prior failures (Kuypers et al. 2016). Therefore, we believe that our state-level instruments are valid. The rationale for using the month in which the fiscal year ends is that fiscal calendars may influence the timing of investments and budgeting, whereas any shock in the occurrence of a breach is supposed to be independent of fiscal calendars.<sup>7</sup>

Because human factor theory and routine activity theory were not previously adopted in the empirical studies in the context of hospitals and information breaches, they are limited in guiding a selection of particular control variables for use in Equation (1) to explain the occurrence of breaches of patient information. Therefore, we referred to related empirical studies in the literature. First, prior studies on breaches of patient information frequently controlled for the basic characteristics of hospitals, such as size and type (Miller and Tucker 2011a, Kwon and Johnson 2014), because the risk of a breach may be associated with various hospital characteristics. Thus, we included the number of beds and dummy variables to indicate academic status, acceptance of Health Maintenance Organization (HMO) insurance, and acceptance of Preferred Provider Organization (PPO). Second, the risk of breaches may be associated with a high degree of digitization; prior studies on breaches of patient information included various IT systems used in hospitals, such as the number of computers and security-related IT (Miller and Tucker 2011a; Kwon and Johnson 2014, 2018). Because a high degree of digitization in hospitals may lead to more breaches, we believe it is appropriate to control for the implementation of large-scale systems, such as enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems, and popular health IT, such as cardiology IS and intensive care unit applications (Kwon and Johnson 2018), which were coded as dummy variables. Statelevel characteristics, such as privacy notification laws, have also appeared in related prior studies, because they may affect hospitals’ incentives to protect patients’ information (Miller and Tucker 2011a, Kwon and Johnson 2014) and were added as a control variable. A hospital’s expense was also controlled for in related studies (Miller and Tucker 2011a, Kwon and Johnson 2014); therefore, we included the expense per fulltime employee (FTE) (in millions of dollars).

## 4. Data and Variables 4.1. Data Sources

Our study covers the period between 2005 and 2012 and uses data collected from multiple sources. First, we collected information on hospitals’ EHRs and MU status from the AHA. The AHA database is based on an annual health IT survey of U.S. hospitals that targets chief information officers (CIOs) and those responsible for IT in each hospital; it tracks the adoption of EHRs and hospitals’ initiatives to attest to MU.

Second, additional information on hospitals’ demographics and their use of health IT was collected from the HIMSS Analytics Database. The HIMSS data set provides comprehensive information about healthcare organizations, such as number of beds, expenses, and hospital types. The hospitals in the HIMSS data set were matched with those in the AHA data set by using Medicare identification numbers in accordance with prior studies (Miller and Tucker 2011a).

Third, the data on breaches of patient information between 2005 and 2012 were collected from the PRC and the HHS websites. The data set from the PRC (www .privacyrights.org), a nonprofit organization based in California, has been widely used in prior studies on information breaches. The HHS website archives breaches affecting 500 or more individuals.<sup>8</sup> The PRC database considers a breach as an event in which “an individual name plus Social Security Number (SSN), driver’s license number, medical record or a financial record/credit/ debit card is potentially put at risk” by unauthorized access, in either an electronic or a paper format. Healthcare organizations should promptly report breaches as required under federal laws and regulations, such as the HITECH Act, Section 13402, and HHS notification rules. These requirements make reports of breaches of healthcare data more reliable than breaches reported in other industries.

Fourth, and finally, the data on state-level privacy notification laws were collected from the Nationa Conference of State Legislatures (NCSL), which lists state and federal legislation. All the data sets used in this study have been used often in prior studies on health IT and information security (Jha et al. 2009, Angst et al. 2010, Hah and Bharadwaj 2012, Kwon and Johnson 2014, Lin et al. 2014).

4.2.1. Dependent Variable. Our main dependent variable is the time elapsed to the occurrence of a breach of patient information. Unlike earlier studies of these breaches, we used, whenever possible, the actual date of a breach instead of the publicized date so as to gain a more precise estimate of the effects of EHRs and MU.<sup>9</sup> Then we calculated the number of days elapsed from the beginning of our observation period to the time each breach occurred. We later distinguished between two types of breaches, accidental and malicious. Our coding methods for these two types are also explained in the online appendix (see Section 1-ii). Table A1 in the online appendix summarizes the number of breaches for both types during the period covered in this study

4.2.2. EHRs and MU Variables. On the basis of the AHA annual survey on health IT, we coded the variable for EHRs as 1 if a hospital had EHRs at the beginning of each year and 0 otherwise.<sup>10</sup> The 2010 AHA annual survey on health IT asked in what year each hospital first deployed electronic medical records (EMRs)/EHRs.<sup>11</sup> Right next to this question, the survey defined EMRs/ EHRs as “Electronically originated and maintained clinical health information derived from multiple sources about an individual’s health status and healthcare. An EHR replaces the paper medical records as the primary source of patient information.” The survey also explicitly asked hospitals not to “include billing/scheduling systems.”

The AHA’s definition differs from other conventional definitions of basic EHRs and offers two distinct advantages.<sup>12</sup> First, it implies that EHRs involve more than implementing a single component of EHRs technologies, and that the information from multiple EHRs components can be derived for use. Second, the AHA definition requires that EHRs be used to become the primary source of patient information by replacing paper-based medical records. Thus, this variable indicates a hospital’s deployment of multiple EHRs components that are used in daily practices, not mere acquisition of an individual component of EHRs. It is also to our advantage that the AHA survey explicitly asked not to include basic EHRs components for billing and scheduling. That is, the AHA definition captures the effect of implementing and using a more advanced form of EHRs than the effect of having a single basic component of electronic records. Noting this difference is important because more than 98% of hospitals implemented at least one rudimentary EHRs component, such as laboratories, radiology, and pharmacies, in the HIMSS database as of 2005. Thus, a too broad definition of basic EHRs will not help in understanding the effect of using advanced EHRs on patient information breaches. To confirm that implementing EHRs in the AHA data set refers to adopting more advanced EHRs components, we further analyzed our data set, which can be found in the online appendix (see Section 1-iii).<sup>13</sup>

## 5.1. Main Results

Table 3 presents the effects of EHRs and MU initiatives on the risk of a breach of patient information. As presented in Table 3, column (1), we first ran a model without using the 2SRI estimator in which the estimated hazard ratios as well as the robust standard errors are reported. The hazard ratios for EHRs and MU initiatives both exceed one and are statistically significant. That is, the risk of breaches tends to increase with EHRs and MU initiatives. However, as discussed earlier, these variables may be endogenous. Thus, in column (2), we report our estimates with a 2SRI approach, and the effect of EHRs alone is positive and significant.<sup>15</sup> The probability of a breach of patient information is 3.081 times higher after implementing EHRs $( h ( t ) = 3 . 0 8 1 ; p < 0 . 0 \dot { 5 } )$ . The effect of an MU initiatives was not significant, although the estimated hazard ratio exceeded one. Given the different results for MU initiatives in columns (1) and (2), the pursuit of MU could possibly be endogenous. Notably, the residual term for MU initiatives was significant as well.

In columns (3) and (4), we estimated the model for two different types of breaches, accidental and malicious, by using the 2SRI approach. Our results indicate that the increased digitization inherent in EHRs and MU initiatives leads to more accidental breaches (column 3) but does not affect malicious breaches (column (4)). Note that the hazard ratios for both EHRs and MU initiatives in accidental breaches (column (3)) have become larger than those in the model with all the breaches (column (2)). Specifically, the probability of an accidental breach is 4.063 times higher with EHRs (h(t) = 4.063; p < 0.05) and 1.771 times greater with an MU initiative (h(t) = 1.771; p < 0.05), respectively. This finding implies that implementation of EHRs and the pursuit of MU bring heightened exposure to risks through the mishandling of patient information by physicians and other members of the healthcare staff. However, according to our results, EHRs and MU did not necessarily make a hospital a more attractive target for breaches of patient information.

Although they are not the main focuses of our paper, the coefficients of other control variables merit mention. Interestingly, mere implementation of security countermeasures, such as intrusion detection systems, encryption, and firewalls, did not significantly affect the risk of breaches, but according to column (2) of Table 3, a higher level of process integration induced by ERP did. This supports the idea that the risk of breaches is heightened by the integration of processes with IT, but investments in security countermeasures may not automatically lower this risk.

## 5.2. Economic Impact

Now we attempt to interpret how the estimated effect of implementing EHRs translates into economic costs to hospitals. To estimate the economic cost, we applied the following simple formula:

Expected cost of breach per hospital

1 probability of patient information breach occurrence × 2 average cost of breach per stolen record × 3 average number of records breached per incident

For term (2), we used the cost estimated by the Ponemon Institute, an independent cyber security research organization, by using an activity-based costing method (Edwards et al. 2016, Ponemon 2015). According to the Ponemon (2015) report,<sup>16</sup> a breach in the healthcare industry costs an average of \$363 per record. For term (3), we used the average number of records breached per case since 2005; as calculated from our data set, the average was $7 3 , 5 7 9 . ^ { 1 7 }$ In our sample, about 6.3% of the hospitals suffered at least one patient breach during our observation period. Because the proportional hazards model does not estimate the baseline probability level of an incident, we assume this as a baseline probability of having a patient information breach incident. Then, a 208% increase in risk because of EHRs can be translated into an extra cost of about \$3.5 million over an 8-year period. ## 5.3. Robustness Checks

We performed two additional robustness checks. First, despite our diligence in collecting all the publicized breach incidents as well as strict state- and federal-level regulations mandating that hospitals report incidents of data breaches, the possibility remains that some hospitals did not disclose breaches. Reporting may be especially suspect for hospitals in states in which a statelevel breach notification law was not enforced before 2009, when the HIPAA imposed federal requirements. Thus, we reran our analysis by excluding the hospitals in the states with a breach notification law as of 2005. Even with this subset of the hospitals more vulnerable to inaccurate reporting, the main finding, the impact of EHRs and MU initiatives on accidental breaches, remained unchanged, as shown in Table 4, column (1)

Second, although our proportional hazards model accounts for industrywide time effects in the form of baseline hazards, they are not explicitly captured. Thus, we inserted a set of year dummies into our Cox hazards model to control for some possible year-leve shocks. Again, the qualitative nature of the results remained the same as shown in Table 4, column (2).

## 5.4. Supplementary Analysis

We sought answers to three questions to gain additional insights into the effects of EHRs and MU initiatives. First, we examined whether these effects are heterogeneous across hospitals of different sizes. Based on human factor theory and routine activity theory, we expected a higher risk of accidental and malicious breaches in larger hospitals because of more users who potentially can perform unsafe acts and the presence of more patient records as an attractive target. Answering this question has important policy implications because hospitals at greater risk of breaches can be advised to invest more resources in prevention. Second, we investigated whether implementation of EHRs and the pursuit of MU are related to the scale of breaches, which goes beyond the mere occurrence of breaches. This analysis can be informative about the nature of breaches that occur because of EHRs and MU initiatives. Third, and finally, we examined whether breaches of patient information resulted from implementing more components of EHRs. This analysis may have the potential to add credibility to one of our main premises, which is that EHRs and MU increase the risk of breaches through usage in the same way that the value of IT is created in actual usage and assimilation (Devaraj and Kohli 2003, Zhu and Kraemer 2005). ## 6. Discussion

In this study, we found that deployment of EHRs and undertaking an MU initiative increased the risk of breaches of patient information. Interestingly, both were associated more with accidental breaches, such as mishandled devices and accidental disclosure, than with malicious breaches. This implies that breaches of patient information after implementation of EHRs are less likely to be the result of malicious and deliberate intent but more likely to be the result of inattentiveness and low awareness among users of EHRs and hospital management of the potential risks. Our study complements Yaraghi (2016) that argued based on its interviews of 22 experts that the risk of privacy breaches could have increased because of a higher availability of medical data and EHRs. In addition, our results can provide further insights compared with Yaraghi and Gopal (2018) that observed the decreased number of breaches among business associates after the implementation of the HIPAA Omnibus Rues in 2013 compared with covered entities including hospitals.<sup>20</sup> However, Yaraghi and Gopal (2018) noted that the number of breaches increased among covered entities steadily despite the HIPAA between 2009 and 2017. Then, there remained a question why the number of breaches increased among covered entities despite the HIPAA that was already in place. We believe that our study can complement Yaraghi and Gopal (2018) because our results suggest a possible reason for the steady increase in the number of breaches among covered entities since 2009, the period after MU initiatives, is possibly because of EHRs and MU initiatives.

Further, our supplementary analyses yielded several important findings. First, EHRs and MU substantially increased the risk of accidental breaches among larger hospitals. Thus, larger hospitals should be more aware of, and attentive to, their increased risk and invest more resources in prevention of accidental breaches. The consistency of this finding with human factor theory adds credibility to our main results. Second, we found that the number of breached patient records because of malicious intent was greater in hospitals that implemented EHRs than in those that did not. Therefore, implementing EHRs may be associated with the scale of breaches when they occur, especially malicious breaches. Third, and finally, implementation of more individual EHRs components did not significantly affect breaches of patient information. Overall, our findings show that breaches of patient information may be rooted in the digitized data and processes enabled by technologies, such as EHRs and their extensive usage, instead of resident in the technological components themselves.

This study contributes to the IS and privacy literature in the following ways. First, to the best of our knowledge, we are among the first to examine the effect on breaches of hospital patient information of integrating into daily routines and practices a more advanced form of EHRs and the similar effect of undertaking MU initiatives. Prior studies focused on various factors that affect the adoption of EHRs, such as concerns about privacy (Angst and Agarwal 2009), physicians’ community identity (Mishra et al. 2012), social networks (Zheng et al. 2010), and regulations (Miller and Tucker 2009). However, few studies addressed whether EHRs actually increase the risk of privacy breaches. The strength of this study lies in its robust analysis of large data sets from multiple sources. In reference to MU, although Kwon and Johnson (2018) showed evidence of reduced breaches in some cases after actual attestation of stage 1 MU, we highlighted the increased risk involved even in the course of preparing for and pursuing attestation for MU. Specifically, our study shows the possible negative impact of investments in health IT, whereas the health IT literature has primarily focused on the positive impact of IT investments (Devaraj and Kohli 2000, Miller and Tucker 2011b, Menon and Kohli 2013, Lin et al. 2014).

Second, we distinguished between different types of breaches and considered the distinct mechanisms behind each type. Meanwhile, we explored how rich theories in other disciplines may be adopted to understand the effect of digitized information and processes on the risk of different breaches. Interestingly, we showed that EHRs and the pursuit of MU are associated more with accidental breaches than with malicious breaches. As a process-oriented view prevalent in the business value of IT literature (Barua et al. 1995, Soh and Markus 1995, Barua and Mukhopahdyay 2000) argues, business performance is enhanced with a higher level of integrated and digitized processes, but these processes amplify the IT risks as well.

Third, and finally, our study reveals that conditions such as size, but not the degree of implementation of EHRs components, heighten the risks associated with IT investments. Previous studies on information breaches have not examined such diverse conditions, except in Kwon and Johnson (2014) that examined regulatory conditions and Angst et al. (2017) that considered institutional factors.

This study carries important implications for policy makers and practitioners. Government policy makers can benefit from our evidence of an increased risk from accidental breaches after deployment of EHRs and MU initiatives. In fact, the requirements for stages 1 and 2 MU attestation tend to focus on reducing external risks (e.g., malicious attacks) by adopting security safeguards (e.g., patch management, antivirus software). However, they barely touch on how to improve security awareness among EHRs users, set up clear security guidelines, strengthen supervision aimed at preventing accidental breaches, and avoid unsafe acts. Thus, we argue that the government’s future revision of the criteria should better reflect the risk of accidental breaches. Furthermore, the government requirements should provide clear guidelines by incorporating latest cybersecurity challenges and specifying the procedures and steps that are necessary to better protect patient health information because the current legal systems including the HIPAA are limited in doing so (Yaraghi 2016). In fact, there were many confusions about the requirements for the stage 2 rules to protect electronic patient health information among hospitals as well. Although the requirements for stage 3 MU attestation attempted to address some of these points, the deadline to meet the stage 3 MU requirements has been extended to 2019. Our study also suggests that policy makers should carefully address the possible increase in the risk of privacy breaches when considering whether to promote industry-wide adoption of digitized data and processes.

Our study can heighten awareness among hospital managers of the necessity for extra precautions to prevent breaches of patient information after they implement EHRs and undertake an MU initiative. Our study also sheds new light on the conditions under which EHRs and a hospital’s undertaking of MU may increase the risk of different types of patient information breaches. For example, a large hospital should invest more resources in prevention of breaches of patient information because of EHRs and MU. Given that such breaches may be caused by digitally integrated processes instead of the mere installation of most EHRs applications, implementing information security management systems, such as ISO/IEC 27001, together with EHRs and MU, may be helpful in systematically improving hospitals’ security infrastructure.

## 7. Conclusion

Although our study presents important findings, our paper is not without limitations. First, despite our diligence in collecting all the publicized breach incidents and robustness checks, there still remains the possibility that some hospitals did not disclose breaches.

Second, we relied on a secondary data set, and thus the EHRs and MU variables as well as some control variables were binary. If possible, it may be better to obtain hospitals’ actual usage or spending on different IT components. Furthermore, an alternative way of coding the EHRs variable may be to measure which particular processes were digitized and integrated with the EHRs. However, we did not have access to such detailed information.

Third, we arrived at our own estimate of the costs of breaches because we were unable to study the exact cost of a breach, which may be more important to hospitals than the breach itself. However, this type of information was not reported by hospitals after a breach. Nevertheless, we believe that our estimate offers important practical and policy implications.

Fourth, although the primary respondents in the AHA’s annual health IT survey are CIOs and those responsible for IT in each hospital, the accuracy of our measurement of the EHRs variable depends on the respondent’s ability to report their status of EHRs truthfully, as required by the survey question. Therefore, our measure may require respondents to have accurate recall over a longer period than other measures. Similarly, our measure of MU initiatives may be limited in that we are unable to specify the degree to which “meaningful use” was actually achieved during the course of pursuing MU attestation. However, our measure has some advantages over other measures of EHRs used in prior studies that were largely based on acquisition and installation of enterprise EHRs software components (Angst et al. 2010; Miller and Tucker 2009, 2011a, b). That is, our measure of EHRs may be more highly correlated with the actual capabilities and usage of EHRs instead of mere installation of EHRs components.

Fifth, and finally, because more accidental and malicious breaches are internal instead of being caused by external attacks, our results may have been affected more by internal hospital factors that lead to internal breaches. However, because internal breaches are as costly and detrimental as external breaches, hospitals lack incentives to favor disclosing or not disclosing one form of breach over another. Thus, our results are unlikely to be biased by any underreporting of external breaches.

Our study can be extended in many ways. For example, it may be possible to examine the effect of the actual attestation of different MU stages and EHRs levels as defined by the HIMSS.<sup>21</sup> For example, the requirements for stage 1 MU attestation cover enhancing both physical and network security for EHRs, and thus the risk of breaches may be reduced after actual attestation (Kwon and Johnson 2018). Because the three stages of actual MU attestation have only recently been opened, we were unable to examine these effects, but this will serve as an interesting research question in the future. It also would be interesting to study the impact of digitally integrated processes on incidents of information breaches in a nonhospital context. This extension would help further generalize our findings. Another interesting extension would be to study how hospitals might learn to manage EHRs and secure their patient information more effectively. For example, it is known that an organization can learn by doing in terms of managing information security countermeasures (Kim and Kim 2014).

Digitally integrated processes have been shown to improve productivity in many different business environments. Medicine may derive unprecedented benefits from EHRs in terms of the quality of patient care and process-level efficiency as well. However, healthcare quality must go hand in hand with the protection of patient information. We hope that this study raises a cautionary flag amidst the rosy picture painted for EHRs and hereafter serves as a guideline to establish the protection of patient information as a major goal of EHRs and MU.
