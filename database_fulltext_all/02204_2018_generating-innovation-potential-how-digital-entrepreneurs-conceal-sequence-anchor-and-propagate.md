## Introduction

Organizations can no longer remain static. They either innovate or become disrupted by those that do. Facing this new reality, organizations across sectors increasingly recognize the need to provide employees the freedom to identify opportunities and pursue them by combining resources in novel ways (Ireland et al., 2009; Phan et al., 2009). Research shows that providing employees the freedom for such entrepreneurial action helps organizations develop product and service innovations and to strategically transform their organizing logic (Corbett et al., 2013; Sharma and Chrisman, 1999). Thanks to the ease by which software applications can be developed and scaled on digital infrastructure and platforms (Bygstad. 2017: Yoo et al., 2010). digital technology promises to make entrepreneurial activities all the more potent and prolific. Yet, how this will afect entrepreneurial processes in organizations and the structures intended to support them remains elusive (Nambisan, 2016). This article aims to explore one aspect of this matter, namely that as digital entrepreneurship proliferates many entrepreneurial processes will begin to unfold more or less synergistically (Boland et al., 2007; Sambamurthy et al., 2003). The link between entrepreneurial processes and innovation outcomes is accordingly blurred. Thanks to the malleability of digital technology, the outcome of an otherwise failed entrepreneurial process may, indeed. provide a potent stepping stone for another in unforeseeable ways (Lyytinen et al., 2016; Nambisan et al., 2017); including removing the obstacles that made the former fail. Innovation outcomes can as such have many origins and constitute both end points and potentia for further entrepreneurial action. In this vein, we ask: how do digital entrepreneurs generate potential for innovation in organizations?

Our response to this question is based on a case study of digital entrepreneurship at a Norwegian hospital. Specifically, we stud the hospital’s efort to develop and scale an application for patient-oriented time planning and resource analytics. It was initiated in a unit that specialized in interdisciplinary assessments of patients and assisted by a Swedish software firm that hoped to leverage its established position in Scandinavian higher education to enter the healthcare market. Since patient assessments involved many healthcare professionals and was resource intense both clinicians and administrators welcomed the application and it soon found managerial support. The hospital, which has served as a testbed for research and innovation in rehabilitative care for many decades, has nevertheless yet to realize the innovation potential generated by the otherwise successful implementation. This ambivalent outcome makes the case ideal for rethinking the link between entrepreneurial processes and innovation outcomes.

We develop the notion of digital entrepreneurship as generating innovation potential as follows. First, we review the literature on corporate entrepreneurship to reveal gaps in our understanding of how digital technology afects the way entrepreneurial processes unfold in organizations. Then, we articulate a lens that captures three tactics that help employees to generate innovation potential as digital technology is implemented and used: concealing, sequencing, and anchoring. Having detailed our methodology, we demonstrate how employees at the hospital used these tactics to develop and scale the time planning application and zoom out to examine other technological applications that may well continue to shape and be shaped by their efort. This leads us to capture a fourth tactic, propagating, which uses the malleability of digital technology to drive many entrepreneurial processes synergistically (propagate means cause to multiply from layers or shoots). Following a discussion of our case and implications for corporate entrepreneurship and IS strategy research, we conclude by noting some implications for managing digital entrepreneurship strategically.

## Corporate entrepreneurship with digital technology

Corporate entrepreneurship is generally defined as the entrepreneurial action by which organization members such as managers and employees identify opportunities and recombine resources to pursue them. That organizations must now foster such activities to continuously develop competitive advantages grounded in innovation is increasingly recognized by strategy research (Guth and Ginsberg, 1990; Phan et al., 2009); not the least due to digital technology transforming competitive environments at a breathtaking pace (Ireland et al., 2009; Sambamurthy et al., 2003). Whereas the focus of corporate entrepreneurship research has evolved over the decades (Kuratko and Morris, 2018), its outcomes, conditions, and processes therefore continues to draw attention.

## Outcomes and conditions for corporate entrepreneurship

Corporate entrepreneurship produces innovation outcomes via corporate venturing and strategic entrepreneurship. Corporate venturing concerns the launch of new ventures. These can be launched in organizations (Kuratko and Morris, 2018) or by externa parties that organizations invest in or acquire (Covin and Miles, 2007; Morris et al., 2011). These may also result from joint ventures with other organizations. New ventures enable organizations to leverage core competencies in new markets (Burgelman and Doz, 2001), probe other business domains (Dess et al., 2003), recombine resources to pursue opportunities (Kanter, 1989), and catapult into more prosperous industries (Donahoe et al., 2001). By contrast, strategic entrepreneurship concerns the transformation of the logic on which organizations are built. The term denotes a range of actions of diferent scope and frequency and is often evaluated in terms of how much the organization is transformed relative to its existing strategy, business model, composition, market position, and product/service oferings, or its competitive environment (Kuratko and Morris, 2018). Common examples include the formation of a new strategy (strategic renewal); the marketing of new products and services (sustained generation); the creation of new markets (domain redefinition); the design of new processes, capabilities, and structures (organizational rejuvenation); and the development of new business models that provide organizations a distinctive edge (business model reconstruction) (Corbett et al., 2013).

A vast body of research links these innovation outcomes to antecedent conditions. Research in this stream mostly examines the structures that organizations implement to foster corporate entrepreneurship and the reasons for doing so, but increasingly also why individual organization members become entrepreneurs (Corbett et al., 2013: Ireland et al., 2009). This literature often emphasizes formal sanctions and resource commitments as necessary conditions for corporate entrepreneurship to succeed (Alterowitz, 1988; Burgelman, 1983, 1984; Sathe, 1989; Schollhammer, 1982; Sykes and Block, 1989) and how these result from internal and externa pressures to innovate. For example, studies find that new ventures are likelier to succeed when organizations have structures in place that foster their development and integration, and that individuals in organizations with few management layers and a high tolerance for failure are more keen to see opportunities in changing environments (Kuratko and Morris, 2018).

## Processes and implications of digital technology

The processes and associated mechanisms by which corporate entrepreneurship unfolds are far less understood, not the least as digital technology makes these more prolific. Most research in this stream take organizations as the unit of analysis, examining how managers select projects, allocate resources, and orchestrate learning (Dess et al., 2003; Zahra et al., 1999), based on the assumption that corporate entrepreneurship must be sanctioned (Covin and Miles, 1999). A few studies, however, challenge this assumption, examining how some individuals act outside formal structures to promote new ideas (Daily and Dalton, 1992; Lumpkin and Dess 1996; Zahra and Filatotchev, 2004), and improvise to recombine resources and mobilize support (Burgelman, 1983). Research into how digital technology transforms organizations supports this view. For example, Orlikowski (1996) showed that organizations may transform when employees improvise in response to local technological breakdowns and opportunities. Jarvenpaa and Ives (1996) went one step further. They showed how emplovees from several different units used the Internet as a platform for developing and showcasing a new organizing logic behind the management’s back. Able to exploit Internet technology more freely, they could string together many, small entrepreneurial actions and thereby scale up innovative services which won the management over.

Overall, these insights show how digital technology implicates corporate entrepreneurship by increasing the need for innovation and ofering employees potent means to pursue opportunities as they see fit. However, research has yet to clarify the implications of the consequent proliferation of corporate entrepreneurship with digital technology. One reason for this is that studies generally examine single processes with clear cut outcomes rather than pay attention to how the conditions for entrepreneurial action evolve as a result of many technological applications. Such attention is warranted as the malleability of digital technology makes innovation outcomes increasingly open ended: software applications are indeed not fixed but hold potential to be used in many ways and may further many entrepreneurial processes (Henfridsson et al., 2018; Nambisan et al., 2017). Clarity accordingly requires attention to the consequent multiplicity in IS use and how corporate entrepreneurs in this regard prime applications for new technological roles (Sambamurthy et al., 2003). Corporate entrepreneurship with digital technology may then be more precisely defined as the entrepreneurial action by which organization members identify opportunities and pursue them by recombining resources in such a wa that the development and scaling of new applications creates potent stepping stones for further action.

## Digital entrepreneurship as generating innovation potential

To understand how digital entrepreneurs generate potential for innovation, it is important to remember that the application of technology creates both inertia and change (Orlikowski. 20o0). Therefore, entrepreneurs must generally decouple new applications from the organizing logic they seek to transform or otherwise depart from to stage innovations in IS use. By organizing logic, we mean the “managerial rationale for [responding organizationally tol environmental and strategic imperatives”" (Sambamurthy and Zmud. 2000: 107), which underscores the existing arrangement of resources that gives incumbent technology meaning. By reducing the socio-technical complexity of the situation, entrepreneurs can more easily scale applications and showcase new organizing logics that are fueled by digital technology and developments in the competitive environment (Hanseth and Lyytinen, 2010; Yoo et al., 2010). To bring their innovations to bear organizationally, entrepreneurs must finally recouple applications with the organizing logic, so as to build momentum for recombining related technological capabilities (Nambisan et al., 2017; Henfridsson and Yoo, 2014).

This simple process model suggests three challenges that digital entrepreneurs must overcome to generate innovation potential. The first challenge stems from the fact that entrepreneurs pursue opportunities that others do not recognize. As a result, they must typically develop applications under the radar until investments can be justified (Grisot et al., 2013; Jarvenpaa and Ives, 1996). We call this concealing. The second challenge stems from the fact that to justify investments applications must be rendered appropriate for use in many diverse organizational settings. These may in practice pose conflicting demands, why entrepreneurs must carefully choose in which order to consider and align diferent motives and intentions when mobilizing support (Chae and Poole, 2005; Hanseth and Lyytinen, 2010). We call this sequencing. The third challenge stems from the fact that no matter which sequence they choose, applications will reach a limit: technology use constitutes structures that cannot be immediately transformed (Henfridsson and Yoo, 2014; Orlikowski, 2000). As a result, entrepreneurs must make sure that applications are implemented in such a way that their use improves the conditions for change (Sambamurthy et al., 2003; Sanner et al., 2014). We call this anchoring. We next detai how we built on this view to examine how digital entrepreneurs generate potential for innovation in organizations.

## Exploring the tactics of digital entrepreneurship

We conducted a longitudinal case study of digital entrepreneurship at a hospital called Sunnaas. The case study, which took place from October 2015 until November 2017, was part of a research project that aimed to help Health South-East, Norway’s largest healthcare region, overcome IT system fragmentation. For historical reasons, the region was hard pressed to generate innovations such as patient-oriented information flows and care services. which require data from many different IT systems to be integrated. The project involved participants from several hospitals, the regional administration, and the regional IT unit, Hospital Partner.

Health South-East operates 11 hospitals. We chose to explore how innovation potential is generated at Sunnaas for three reasons. First, Sunnaas has been renowned as a maverick ever since Rolf and Birgit Sunnaas founded the hospital, in 1954. Extreme cases are ideal for generating theory as they bring phenomena into sharp relief (Flyvbjerg, 2006). Second, Sunnaas is small. It treats 3000 patients annually and employs 800 people compared to 78,000 in Health South-East. It also had structures in place for encouraging entrepreneurial behavior, including an innovation school, mechanisms for sourcing ideas, and dedicated funding programs. These features accentuated the role of digital technology as they made entrepreneurial processes more immediate and connected. Third, Sunnaas was well situated both geographically and politically in the project, allowing us to sample data purposefully (Peppard, 2001).

We established contact with Sunnaas in October 2015. The first of nearly 20 visits took place in January 2016 when we were invited to a regional innovation cluster event. During the event, we were presented with seven innovation projects. These ranged from the use of smartphone sensors in remote rehabilitative care services (mMovement) to the development of an app-based information presentation platform called IPP that could securely deliver such services to patient devices. In the 2-hour round table discussion that concluded the event we were presented with a common barrier: Hospital Partner’s staf had little in mind but to secure operations and maintain the many applications already in use—a central part of what we here term the technical logic. On the boat back to Oslo, we selected one project likely to yield insights into how corporate entrepreneurs generate innovation potential. Sunnaas was about to trial TimeEdit, a cloud-based application for time planning and resource analytics, at the unit for assessment. This enabled us to deploy an open-ended research design. We would launch our inquiry at this unit and follow the hospital’s efort to develop TimeEdit for use at Sunnaas and in the region. Due to its potential role as a platform for TimeEdit and other innovations, we would also follow IPP closely. To contextualize observations, we would attend regional innovation cluster meetings and industry events.

To collect data we primarily used semi-structured interviews, participant observation, and document analysis. Table 1 details the 27 interviews conducted during the 24-month study. These ranged from 30 to 60 minutes in length and were transcribed to facilitate analysis. As table 2 shows, informants were sampled to reflect the site. Some, such as the project champion, the chief information oficer (CIO), and the chief of innovation (CI), were interviewed several times. Observations were made during visits to Sunnaas and included a training session given by the Swedish software firm. In addition, we participated in 2 innovation cluster meetings, 4 case study meetings, 3 industry events, 9 project meetings, and 4 practice-based seminars hosted by the authors. Analyzed documents include board meeting minutes, studies of previous developments, project documentation, technological evaluations, press releases, strategy reports, and Egil Houg’s book Mitt Soria Moria, published in 2004 to commemorate Sunnaas’ 50th anniversary.

Our analysis emerged in tandem with data collection and can be said to have developed via three stages of interpretation (Klein and Myers, 1999). To push beyond a common sense account of how digital technology implicates organization and postpone analytic closure (Sarker et al., 2013), we actively searched for theoretical conundrums concerning change (Alvesson and Kärreman, 2007). An initial conundrum was how the presence of multiple organizing logics afects the implementation and use of digital technology. It led us to wonder how organizations can strategically harness the consequent multiplicity in IS use to promote innovation.

The first interpretation was grounded (Corbin and Strauss, 2008) and served to narrate the observed events. Specifically, we used open coding procedures to develop themes, which incorporation into the narrative informed the generation of code categories. For example, an early theme was the influence of IS use on the organization, which we refined by relating data to three organizing logics: clinical, administrative, and technical. The second interpretation was theoretical and consistent with the principle of abstraction and generalization (Klein and Myers, 1999) served to relate the narrative to change theory in general. We found many candidates to draw on in pursuit of clarity and conundrum. For example, we found explanations for how entrepreneurs decouple, scale, and recouple change in the institutional entrepreneurship (Battilana et al., 2009), organizational transformation (Jarvenpaa and Ives, 1996), and bootstrapping literature (Hanseth and Lyytinen, 2010). However, neither literature clarified how entrepreneurs can use multiplicity in IS use to propagate change in organizations, which seemed to be a vital aspect of digital entrepreneurship. Consequently, the third stage of interpretation was explorative (Klein and Lyytinen, 1985) and served to extend a more general understanding of how change occurs toward a practice-based process theory of how digital entrepreneurs generate potential for innovation

In reality, the analysis was messier. For example, our core construct (generating innovation potential) emerged in a conversation with the editor. It is also impossible to separate our present theorizing from our understanding of how digital technology implicates organizations that we have gained from previous experience. To demonstrate the authenticity of our analysis some final remarks are thus in place. Practice theory sensitized us to the fact that the recoupling of an application is a decoupling at another scale insofar IS use is never complete. This analytic move from process (decouple, scale, recouple) into practice (decoupling/recoupling as a way to generate innovation potential as applications scale) allowed us to revisit our data. Specifically, we reanalyzed data coded using the simple process model with Besson and Rowe’s (2012) recommendation to investigate how organizations navigate socio-technical, political, and economic inertia in mind. By relating each form of inertia to diferent aspects of the challenges that digital entrepreneurs must overcome to generate innovation potential, we theorized three tactics: concealing, sequencing, and anchoring. For example, sequencing involves rallying (political aspect), scaling (socio-technical aspect), and pivoting (economic aspect), while anchoring involves stabilizing (political aspect), recoupling (socio-technical aspect), and priming (economic aspect). On this ground, we then used a similar procedure to conceptualize a fourth tactic: propagating. It focused our attention on how digital entrepreneurs marshal many applications synergistically to harness the multiplicity in IS use that increasingly malleable digital technology affords

## Generating innovation potential at Sunnaas hospital

The Norwegian healthcare system was historically divided in five regions. While hospitals were regionally operated, the cost was carried by the state. Hospitals therefore enioved great autonomy and innovation usually came from core clinical practices. In 2000. Norway elected a prime minister who promised to modernize the healthcare system. Enabled by previous investments in clinical and administrative IS development, such as electronic medical records (EMRs) and patient administration systems (PAS) back in 1987, the state first separated governance from operations. Managers would oversee budgets, allocate resources, and develop the administration. healthcare professionals would focus on patients. It next seized control. 84 hospitals were turned into 47 independent health companies owned by five state controlled regional trusts. Health South-East resulted from the merger of two large trusts in 2007.

## Modernization of Health South-East

The modernization aimed make the “availability, quality, and care for the citizen meet [demands for] rational resource management and comprehensive social and managerial responsibility.” In short, centralized reports and standardized operations would help to clarify roles and responsibilities among the participants in the healthcare system. Relative autonomy in clinical practice would allow hospitals to develop associated innovations in IS use. To enable this vision, Health South in 2003 founded Hospital Partner as a “shared service provider for ICT, HR, and supply chain management.” Today, the regional IT unit employs about 3000 people. Few IT staf work in the hospitals. The centralization of the IT function was motivated by system fragmentation and consequent needs to standardize the digital infrastructure. To further tackle this issue, Health South-East, in 2016, invested 300 million euro to consolidate IT systems, in addition to 30 million in annual investment (Motion 069-2016 ICT infrastructure modernization in Health South-East). One major ambition was to reduce the number of applications that the 11 hospitals used from around 3000 to 700.

The board agenda 086-2015 ICT strategy laid out the foundation for modernizing the trust’s ICT infrastructure as a basis for digitalizing clinical and administrative work flows. A premise for this work is a common ICT-infrastructure in Health South-East. The board means thai it is important to rapidly carry out such a modernization. It will boost quality and patient security in the sector—Mariann Hornnes, Administrative Director (15 September 2016).

The modernization put the hospitals in a tight spot. Escalating commitment to combat system fragmentation via centralization and standardization was sensible. Increasing specialization of clinical practice created an endless need for new applications, which were typically integrated with either PAS or EMRs, but developed in such a way that data was hard to extract and reuse. Health South-East saw this as an impediment to digitizing the trust’s organizing logic. However, resources for developing innovations in IS use were as a result scarce at the hospitals and local circumstances and priorities made collaborations difficult

## Organizing conditions at Sunnaas

Hospital care typically requires standardization and rapid assessment of patient conditions so that patients can be subjected to a predefined care process. High certainty in the care process helps hospitals to plan for and manage patient treatments from admission to discharge at scale. It also mitigates the need for sophisticated integrations between IT systems. However, patients in need of rehabilitative care rarely fit this logic. Every patient is unique, but these sufer from multiple conditions with high degrees of comorbidity why it is dificult to know how to best treat them upfront. This poses diferent information requirements for efective care.

The unit for assessment took care of especially complex cases at Sunnaas. It specialized in control of and functional assessments for patients with long-term pain, neurological disease, and trauma including brain injuries. For patients that have been assessed, the unit also ofered group-based pain treatment, intensive language training, and care of brain injury and cerebral palsy. The unit for assessment had 50 beds and treated around 1250 patients annually, making it substantially larger than other units at Sunnaas.

If you are having a surgery the hospital’s logic and planning rules. You just show up on time and do as you are told. Few disciplines are involved and they rarely involve many others. Here, you have to take into consideration 4–6 professions and many sites [in and out of Sunnaas]. For a knee or hip surgery, or some of the simpler and more elective things that we do, you will be… you are more of a parcel that the hospital moves through a set of predefined steps. [In this case], we need to include the patient in the planning—CI.

Team coordinators were responsible for scheduling patient treatments at the unit. Patients were typically admitted on Monday and discharged before the weekend. By then, a team coordinator must have compiled an interdisciplinary assessment report (the average admission lasted 6–8 days, but it was not unusual for patients to be admitted for a month). While admitted, patients have very dense care processes. In other words, they must meet with an ensemble of healthcare professionals (e.g. physicians, nurses, physiotherapists, occupational therapists, and social workers) and in short time undergo many examinations at diferent places, each with its own equipment and resource needs. This complicated staf planning and efective resource allocation.

## Digitizing of treatment plans at Sunnaas

Since 1989, team coordinators had used a Microsoft Word template to create patient treatment plans. The Word template was introduced by the current acting director and chief of clinic at Sunnaas in an attempt to showcase the use of personal computers to support planning. Treatment plans set up next week’s activities. Every Friday, coordinators printed out the plans and communicated them to the other team members. In practice, this meant delivering print outs to the nurses’ ofice, a common area, patient rooms, and other places in the hospital. Organizing on paper was not ideal. While the plans aimed to make all meetings and examinations timel and efective, they were often subject to change during the assessment. As a result, information had to be exchanged on the fly. In the meantime, nurses had to maintain a continuity in care by using various shadow systems. This created uncertainty. For example, patients felt obliged to check in with nurses before taking walks. That plans were up to date was not the least important because other care facilities, such as the gym and the pool, used these to control patients’ equipment use and care intensity.

With TimeEdit you can create a patient treatment plan that is accessible for employees and patients, which generates affordances for coordination that we currently miss. When I introduced the Word template, in 1989, I thought it would only be a few years until we would be able to use this new, exiting tool: the personal computer. We are still not there—Chief of clinic and acting director.

A digital plan would produce extensive benefits. For example, it would reduce the need for writing sensitive patient data on post-it notes, bedpost lists, and envelopes until plans were updated. It also promised to make operations transparent and accountable Financial controllers in this regard sought to document as many aspects of the care process as possible, such as examination rooms and equipment use, staf assignments, and durations. This would enable Sunnaas to ensure that it made the most out of its resources and that patients were given the best care. Operational eficiency was imperative to maintain autonomy: although Sunnaas was Norway's largest specialist hospital in the field of physical medicine and rehabilitation it was by far the smallest hospital in the trust. To improve efiency was also important because Sunnaas was rebuilding 5600 m<sup>2</sup> (∼20 %) of the hospital to scale up its operations.

It is not merely [about TimeEdit] but also about utilizing the data we have in the EMRs and the PAS. We want to extract statistics for each treatment program and see the exact amount of time spent on each patient, for example, by physiotherapists. This would enable us to research the efect of giving 10 versus 5 hours of patient treatment—Chief of clinic and acting director.

In 2015, Sunnaas entered into an agreement with TimeEdit to trial their time planning and resource analytics application with the same name. Thanks to its structures for encouraging entrepreneurial behaviors and appropriating new technology , TimeEdit was successfully implemented at the unit for assessment and other units were soon on their way. This is not to say that the implementation went as planned. During the process, Sunnaas learned that it was unclear whether the resources it could allocate to developing TimeEdit for use in Health South-East would sufice in light of Hospital Partner’s technical demands. The Swedish software firm as a result came to doubt its entry into healthcare. Change is nevertheless likely to propagate thanks to other developments in the region, not the least IPP. ## Flying in under the radar

Consistent with the idea that organizations should provide emplovees freedom to identify and pursue opportunities, the implementation of TimeEdit did not originate within formal structures. Table 3 shows how the process began when an IT staf member (and champion of IPP) invited the Swedish firm to demonstrate their application. IT staf at Sunnaas were few and had an ambiguous role as Hospital Partner had taken over most traditional duties. Their work was primarily innovation driven (in 2016, the IT unit became part of the innovation unit) and sought to support innovation in lean ways; “what we are doing is not projects and that is the point.” This arrangement made IT staf prone to pick up problems and scan the market for solutions. TimeEdit was no exception. The IT staf member knew that both clinicians and administrators needed time planning and resource analytics support.

We went to the users: Is this good? Do you want to try it? To keep the thresholds low. we never made it a formal project. [We knew] that there was a need. Planning on paper caused problems. It had been raised in several management meetings but of course no one had taken a hold of it. So the CIO and I sat down and said that we had to figure it out. We found TimeEdit [and] reached out to them. [To avoid risking a costly project failure and get quick results:] We just invited them—IT staf

An innovation counselor that attended the demonstration soon after announced that TimeEdit was an ideal candidate for her work smarter program. A financial controller had recently outlined the many benefits of digital planning in a master’s thesis. Work smarter was an administrative initiative for promoting innovation projects that could improve clinical practice while reducing time and cost. Principally, this was to be achieved through improved collaborations (e.g. teleconferencing and screen-based infrastructure for communication), better role division and information sharing (e.g. planning systems that enables seamless and reliable interactions with patients), and enhanced resource use (e.g. patient-oriented analytics and equipping health professionals, patient rooms, and patients with smart devices and sensory technology). TimeEdit showed potential in each regard.

## Sequencing the alignment

The trial of TimeEdit was initially one-sided, consistent with the idea that organizations should decouple new applications to ease their development. The innovation counselor described the hospital as comprised by a clinical core around which the administration formed a belt that occasionally released funds for clinicians to innovate with. The champion’s task was accordingly to verify the potential for using TimeEdit to save time and money and thereby secure the hospital’s board for developing the application further. To suspend the clinical logic made sense because everyone involved knew “hand on heart,” as the CIO said, that the application was needed and would help patients to take charge of their rehabilitation. But to get to that stage, it would have to first comply with Hospital Partner’s technical demands. This was not the least necessary in order to input patient data in the application.

The innovation counselor suggested that we start by looking at the stafing. So we sat down and looked at the data which showed huge room for improvement. That baseline helped us rally others as we could demonstrate that the resource allocation [was unsustainablel. And we found that TimeEdit did in fact work in clinical practice. We just had to make a few changes—Team coordinator/champion

The champion soon reported clear administrative benefits from implementing TimeEdit to the hospital board, but everyone was not convinced by the trial. For example, one unit leader questioned whether the hospital should invest in a new application rather than use existing solutions (e.g. Outlook). Others questioned the decision to suspend the clinical logic. Presented with a vision for how TimeEdit could be used to improve patient care, however, and the administrative logic aligned, the board did not hesitate to approve the implementation. When the leader of the unit for assessment expressed concerns that Sunnaas would thereby rally staf behind an application that “in the end will not work,” due to unresolved patient security issues, she was moved to another unit.

[TimeEdit] manages reservations of many kinds […]. It is easy to see what is booked and what resources can and cannot be reserved […]. TimeEdit has improved planning at the unit for assessment. It speeds up and prevents double bookings why we can spend more time with the patients. [The patients] indicate increased empowerment. They feel more included, are aware of changes to the treatment plan, and can more easily communicate with clinicians—Project evaluation.

Backed by the board, the project team’s focus shifted. Whereas they had so far avoided Hospital Partner to ease development, the now had to precisely understand the technical conditions for rendering TimeEdit appropriate for use in Health South-East.

TimeEdit has promised a virtual server so that we can deploy the application [on Hospital Partner’s secure ICT platform]. But it depends on the cost and we don't know that vet. The cost of Hospital Partner's work and maintenance, what TimeEdit will charge. I don't think it will be that much but Hospital Partner is a joker. It also depends on what we can achieve by that. We might get onto the platform after all. That has to be the point of this so that we can input patient data—CIO.

Although the work smarter framing was efective in freeing up resources, aligning TimeEdit with Hospital Partner’s technical logic proved dificult. Sunnaas used extraordinary funding to prop up its IT budget (∼110,000 euro annually) with a 75,000-euro investment, which was shared by the Swedish firm and the project team (e.g. the champion was funded on 20 %). Yet this was not enough. One innovation counselor blamed miscommunication between TimeEdit’s marketing and development units: “I could be wrong but my impression is that their developers didn’t fully understand that we want TimeEdit to run on [the secure ICT platform].” According to the CIO, the firm had “realized that the market potential is limited." The application would not meet Hospital Partner's demands without further development the cost of which was uncertain. Though a contract was signed no one seemed to know what had been agreed upon. “They want out of the contract [or rather] no longer want to deliver on the commitment.”

## Anchoring the application

Consistent with the idea that organizations at some point meet a limit as new technology is applied, the TimeEdit implementation ultimately focused on creating a stepping stone for further action. The path ahead was blocked for two reasons. First, Sunnaas was unable to extend the administrative logic (e.g. to access various regional innovation funds) by integrating TimeEdit with an enterprise system, such as the ERP module for staf planning. Technically, this was not a problem—“it is rarely the case in these situations,” as the CIO said. Rather, IT system vendors wanted Hospital Partner to develop these integrations who in turn said “the trust already have three applications for planning.” Second, Sunnaas was unable to gain the regional ICT forum’s support. The ICT forum had the mandate to tell Hospital Partner to aid the development. But in order to win its support Sunnaas would have to first demonstrate the clinical use case, which they could not do on their own.

One logic […] devastates the other. It creates a situation where [it is] hard to see a way out. One logic points at the other and says, you! Then you point to you, you to you, and you to you. It would have been fascinating if it was not dead serious. It is so stupid—CI.

To make the most out of the situation, the project team decided to use room numbers to identify patients. Because this prevented financial controllers from combining data from many IT systems, this ran counter to the initial intent. But the work around was “not that complicated and works for the clinicians,” as the innovation counselor said. The clinicians saw “benefits in using TimeEdit, even if we do not get the version we want.” Moreover, using TimeEdit would “ready the entire organization for the day we manage to get Hospital Partner or someone on board” to develop the application further.

We are in the implementation stage now. There has been resistance, which escalated when TimeEdit signaled their reluctance […]. We have discussed with several units whether to implement TimeEdit or not. I spoke to the leaders of the two units who are [next in line]. Both want to proceed. Because the deciding factor is not whether this is TimeEdit or another tool. It is about improving the work flows [so] although there are limitations it is still positive for the organization to […] take it to the next level. [We will] implement TimeEdit in accordance with the hospital board’s decision: it was to buy the application as is. [Integrating it] was really our idea—CIO

Having had to scale back their vision, the project team again decided to lay low. They needed to understand the bigger picture, as the CIO said; “we will present a progress report [this month] where we will probably surface this.” The plan had originally been to restrict the implementation to one wing, but word spread fast at the hospital and a unit from another wing expressed interest. “So we are thinking why not. There is a need and they want it. Let’s just do it.” The team thus decided to let TimeEdit develop “if not on its own, freely enough” for new clinical uses to emerge organically. As a result, TimeEdit became increasingly decoupled from the administrative logic on which it initially rested. This pivot well reflects the “do first ask for forgiveness later” mentality of employees and managers at the hospital. Nevertheless, the lack of immediate success was frustrating.

We lack useful integrations. Outlook […] would have been great. The EMRs. Maybe the ERP. We get none of that [...]. That said, TimeEdi might get us part of the way. Our goal here has been to remove Excel sheets and Word templates. That is what is central. There are also patient security aspects. Today, we are placing sensitive, patient-specific information in common areas—CIO

## Propagating further developments

Consistent with the idea that organizations can strategically harness multiplicity in IS use, the project team constantly looked for ways to link TimeEdit to other technological developments within and without the hospital. Such propagation of change provided entrepreneurs at the hospital with a mechanism for tackling obstacles that were otherwise out of reach. If one development came to a halt, resources could be moved to another in hope to open up a new path forward. As the implementation of TimeEdit also shows. doing so was important not the least due to the barriers for innovation that Hospital Partner imposed.

We depend [on the region’s] support. We have to be loyal to their principles because they are decisive for the strategic ICT development […]. Yet I feel that we are so many who want to do so much, and we do come up with many innovations. But just as we are ready to roll these out [into the region] and get economies of scale we are stopped—Chief of clinic and acting director.

Harnessing multiplicity requires entrepreneurs to marshal many developments in synergistic ways. The IT staf member who set TimeEdit into motion thus noted, alongside others, that one important aspect of Sunnaas ability to generate innovation potential was the structures in place for promoting entrepreneurial behavior (e.g. an innovation school, internal mechanisms for sourcing ideas, and funding programs). These were honed over many decades and solidified following the merger of three clinics at Sunnaas in early 2000, which saw one of the clinic chiefs appointed as CI. He soon hired the first innovation counselor. Together, they observed the need to structure the ways in which employees could submit innovative ideas. To keep up with all of the submissions they received, more innovation counselors were hired and a need to work systematically with innovation emerged. This ultimately led the original innovation counselor to develop an innovation school where project champions could share experiences and cultivate entrepreneuria insights collectively (as of 2017, the innovation unit had 11 staf members, including those at the IT unit).

It is not just about being able to quickly reach a state where an idea can be presented to the board. It is also about how these structures allow us to pick up an idea in one place and readily see if it can be applied somewhere else—Innovation counselor.

Synergies were not only driven by the organizational structure. Digital technology itself was potent in its capacity to drive change. One reason is the ability of one application to become part of another. We saw many cases of this but none more potent than IPP. The champion explained how IPP being “built on industry standards all the way" allowed applications to be built at low cost and required a “minimum of knowledge to extend” (e.g. patients would automatically receive tailored and location dependent interfaces using HTML5 and CSS). Like TimeEdit, IPP had humble origins and took many turns. Sunnaas had received funding from a project called REACH (Responsive Architectures for Innovation in Collaborative Healthcare Services) to improve patient communication. Early on, the champion recognized that “an app that presents hard-coded data is meaningless” and disbanded development But, when the mMovement project started it created the need for a platform that could read and communicate smartphone sensor data securely. “It had no funding so we thought: we have the REACH funds, if every patient is to have an app we might as well try to bundle all services into one.” He soon discovered two other projects using sensor technology at Sunnaas and established contact with a software firm willing to undertake development at low cost in exchange for being able to do sat their own accord.

This was quite fortunate. Otherwise we would probably have lost out on the REACH funding as that project was about to end. Thanks to mMovement we were able to stretch those funds and buy ourselves time to develop IPP further—IT staf.

The development of IPP was significant to TimeEdit because if the champion succeeded to integrate the application with EMRs (e.g. to present patients with the clinical progress report, which summarizes the why’s, what’s, and what’s next in their treatment) or some other enterprise level system. it could readily extend its access to TimeEdit—enabling administrative and clinical innovations needed to digitize planning at Sunnaas by opening a gateway into Hospital Partner’s secure ICT platform. It also demonstrates how entrepreneurial action increasingly depends on conditions that no single organization controls as they evolve as a result of many technological applications. For example, the champion was eyeing the development of an Internet portal that would provide patients in the region access to their health records as one possible way to integrate IPP with EMRs. To secure funding, he also sought joint ventures with various IT system providers. As we left the site, the champion and colleagues at Oslo University Hospital were awaiting a decision from Health South-East whether they would receive funding for a joint venture to simplify application development. As noted by the IPP champion, one important takeaway from our case is accordingly that innovation potential is generated over long time by many actors in diferent constellations that thanks to digital technology naturally span across many organization. Since al hospitals in the region faced similar challenges there were indeed many parallel developments to consider.

That’s where innovation needs to go: a center for the region, maybe Norway, where you can pitch innovative ideas […] and see if there are existing developments. It’s a problem that we don’t know what’s out there. There are many projects, probably also solutions, but we don’t know [...]. It drives costs. We just learned by accident that the Oslo University Hospital has a project similar to IPP [...]. It's typical that two people work on the same idea—IT staf.

## Discussion

Organizations that support corporate entrepreneurship are dynamic—ready to seize opportunities when they arise. Because there are always more opportunities to explore than can be exploited, strategy research has long examined how managers decide which projects to invest in (Dess et al., 2003: Zahra et al.. 1999) and how emplovees vie for their attention in order to promote innovative ideas (Burgelman. 1983: Zahra and Filatotchey. 2004). These aspects of corporate entrepreneurship will only become more relevant to examine as digital technology makes entrepreneurial activities more potent and prolific. However, digital technology not only creates a demand and conditions for innovation. Thanks to its increasing malleability, digital technology also fundamentally changes how entrepreneurial processes unfold in organizations. The ability of software applications to take on new technological roles indeed enables entrepreneurial processes to interact and create unforeseeable cascades of innovation as conditions for action evolve (Boland et al., 2007; Nambisan et al., 2017) driven by multiplicity in IS use (Henfridsson et al., 2018; Sambamurthy et al., 2003). Exploring this aspect of corporate entrepreneurship was crucial to evaluate the implementation of TimeEdit fairly. While corporate entrepreneurship research has generally examined single processes with clear cut outcomes that can be understood as mere successes and failures (cf. Cecez-Kecmanovic et al., 2014), our entrepreneurs understood that even failures may provide potent stepping stones for further action. TimeEdit did not prove the clinical success envisioned, but indeed put Sunnaas in a better spot to digitize planning should additional resources be found or new paths open up as a result of other technological developments. Were the IPP champion to succeed, for example, his platform would remove the technical obstacle that stopped Sunnaas from realizing the potential generated by the otherwise successful implementation. Digitized planning would in turn showcase IPP and improve the conditions for further action. To explore this aspect, we conceptualized four tactics whereby digital entrepreneurs generate potential for innovation in organizations: concealing, seguencing, anchoring, and propagating, In so doing, we showed how managing digital entrepreneurshir strategically requires organizations to harness the multiplicity in IS use that increasingly malleable digital technology afords.

## Tactics for generating innovation potential

The concealing tactic builds on the idea that digital entrepreneurs must decouple new applications to reduce the socio-technical complexity of the situation in which these are to develop. Previous research shows this to be a vital aspect of digital entrepreneurship (Grisot et al., 2013; Jarvenpaa and Ives, 1996). In our case, the project team did not need to hide their activities from the management thanks to the organization’s culture. Flying under the radar did, however, help them get the trial of the ground as it allowed them to move less restrained by Hospital Partner’s scrutiny. Table 4 accordingly notes how concealing helps entrepreneurs maneuver the political landscape and frees them from economic constraints. As the need for concealment stems from how new applications of digital technology are initially judged using the same logic that digital entrepreneurs seek to transform or otherwise depart from, it is important to examine how diferent uses of this tactic play out. For example, organizations increasingly form skunk works projects and create innovation labs to pursue new opportunities outside of formal structures. These measures alter the socio-technical situation in diferent ways and impose distinct economic and political conditions on corporate entrepreneurship with digital technology why their efects on entrepreneurial porcesses and innovation outcomes and processes will vary.

The sequencing tactic extends on the idea that digital entrepreneurs must render new applications appropriate for use in diverse organizational settings for these to scale. Previous research recognizes that entrepreneurial processes therefore often involve detours and false starts (Aanestad and Hanseth, 2002; Besson and Rowe, 2012). It also recognizes the mutual constitution of entrepreneuria processes and innovation outcomes (Nambisan et al., 2017). In our case, the project team ultimately aimed to transform clinica practice, but to develop the application to this end, they first had to align it with the administration and Hospital Partner. Table 4 notes in this regard how sequencing applications enables entrepreneurs to rally political support which they can then use to pivot from one logic to the next. That pivoting is key to rapidly scaling applications is well observed in the literature (Huang et al., 2017) and may as Sunnaas shows require entrepreneurs to abandon an initial alignment as limits that cannot be immediately transformed are reached. The failure to align TimeEdit with Hospital Partner’s technical logic despite strong administrative alignment indeed pushed the entrepreneurs to scale back their initial vision in search for emergent clinical uses on which a new attempt to develop the application could be launched at a more opportune time.

The consequent anchoring tactic is quite poorly understood, Observing several pressures that stabilize applications, Tyre and Orlikowski (1994) suggested that organizations have only a short window in which to act before the opportunity for change closes. In our case, the window did indeed close as the project team ran out of options to develop and scale the application further. But our case also demonstrates that new windows may open at any time thanks to the malleability of digital technology and the associated proliferation of entrepreneurial activities. As Table 4 notes, it is therefore important to closely examine how entrepreneurs stabilize applications to prime them for new technological roles. While sequencing helps to improve the initial conditions for entrepreneuria action that concealing afords, the ways in which applications are anchored in use indeed afects whether and when the consequent innovation potential can be realized or extended upon. The decision to implement TimeEdit in such a way that new clinical uses could emerge organically not only bought the project team time to tackle the obstacles that they faced. It also served to expand the opportunity that digitized planning presented by enabling many uses to root. Future research of anchoring may accordingly build upon and inform digital options theory (Sambamurthy et al., 2003).

Last but not least, our case study suggests that organizations must learn to harness the multiplicity in IS use. The propagating tactic whereby digital entrepreneurs marshal many applications synergistically accentuates this vital aspect of corporate entrepreneurship with digital technology. Our understanding of how the increasing malleability of digital technology afect entrepreneurial processes by making innovation outcomes open ended has greatly expanded in recent vears (Henfridsson et al., 2018: Nambisan et al., 2017). Yet, strategy research has far from concluded how organizations should tackle this challenge. Sunnaas in this regard demonstrated the benefits of developing structures that not only encourage entrepreneurial behaviors but also enable emerging innovations to be locally appropriated. However, organizations will show varying degrees of multiplicity in IS use and accordingly need to develop diferent structures. Because corporate entrepreneurship with digital technology is an important driver of organizational transformation, future research would do well to investigate how such structures evolve alongside the innovative IS uses they bring about and condition (Besson and Rowe, 2012). In particular, we call for studies into how organizations make multiple and diverse developments, many of which are driven from without, converge despite the absence or even impossibility of a clear goal.

## Implications for research and practice

The focus of corporate entrepreneurship research has evolved over the last four decades (Kuratko and Morris, 2018). Research initially aimed to understand the phenomenon and explain how entrepreneurship could at all exist within established organizations (Hanan. 1976: Hill and Hlavacek. 1972: Peterson and Berger. 1972). Studies into how established organizations promote innovation subsequently led corporate entrepreneurship to be conceptualized as organizational behaviors that require formal sanctions and resource commitments (Alterowitz, 1988: Burgelman. 1983: Sathe. 1989: Schollhammer, 1982: Sykes and Block, 1989). This understanding shifted the focus from how organizations innovate to improve their market position to how they develop the organizational structures and individual skills needed for corporate entrepreneurs to thrive (Barringer and Bluedorn. 1999; Birkinshaw. 1997; Borch et al., 1999; Jennings and Young, 1990; Merrifield, 1993; Zahra et al., 1999) and how these measures contribute to innovation outcomes in practice (Guth and Ginsberg, 1990: Sharma and Chrisman, 1999). As the understanding of innovation outcomes have become increasingly nuanced over the years this focus has only deepened (Ahuja and Morris Lampert, 2001; Smith and Di Gregorio, 2002; Schildt et al., 2005). The tactics whereby digital entrepreneurs generate innovation potential in organizations overall suggest a number of implications for research and practice. First, research must rethink the link between entrepreneurial processes and innovation outcomes in light of how digital technology not only creates a demand and conditions for innovation. but also makes entrepreneurial activities more potent and prolific. This calls for research designs that understand innovation outcomes as resulting from many entrepreneuria processes, which distribution in time and space create complex interactions. The framework presented in this article supports such designs by allowing researchers to examine many entrepreneurial processes systematically. This in turn enables fresh theorizing of how conditions for action evolve as organizations digitize their organizing logics and, accordingly, also brings needed order to the non-linear paths through which digital entrepreneurship unfolds in organizations (Besson and Rowe, 2012).

Second, the challenge for strategic organization no longer centers on balancing planned, top-down implementations of digita technology with emergent uses that give organizations new direction bottom-up. Rather, IS strategy research must now explain how organizations transform from within and without—multiplicity in IS use implies not only that a lot goes on inside the organization, but also that organizations will develop connections to many external parties. We are not the first to point out the need for IS strategy research to take a more systematic view of the implementation and use of digital technology (Merali et al., 2012; Nolan, 2012). However, much remains to be said about how organizations harness their environment (Besson and Rowe, 2012; Lyytinen et al., 2016) and unleash innovation in practice (Karpovsky and Galliers, 2015). Our framework supports such an agenda by focusing on the individual organization members that make innovation happen and by accentuating the vital role of multiplicity in IS use.

Third, organizations that seek to support corporate entrepreneurship should ensure that associated structures take the malleability of digital technology seriously. This entails both ensuring that applications can easily spread across the organization and prioritizing developments that aim to remove a common obstacle—such as platforms that can provide integrations to enterprise systems to range of applications or otherwise ease the implementation and use of new technology. Because these obstacles may be visible only to those facing them on the frontline of development (Henfridsson and Yoo, 2014), taking the malleability of digita technology seriously also means implementing structures that allow expertise to be retained and experiences to be shared. Managers have a lot to learn from Sunnaas in this regard, not the least when it comes to making sure that corporate entrepreneurs have a say in the boardroom and are given proper support and education. Whereas our conceptualization was primarily developed with research in mind, managers would also do well to consider how the structures they implement enable and constrain the associated tactics.

## Conclusions

Thanks to the ease by which software applications can be developed and scaled on digital infrastructure and platforms (Bygstad. 2017: Yoo et al., 2010). digital technology makes entrepreneurial activities all the more potent and prolific. This article revealed the need to demark entrepreneurial processes and innovation outcomes to appreciate how this will afect corporate entrepreneurship. Specifically, we showed the need to understand how digital entrepreneurs generate potential for innovation in organizations given that even failures may provide potent stepping stones for further action as a result of the increasing malleability of digital technology (cf. Orlikowski. 1996). To explore this matter, we conceptualized four tactics that digital entrepreneurs may use and reported how these helped employees at a Norwegian hospital develop and scale an application for patient-oriented time planning and resource analytics. Our case study revealed that one important way in which digital entrepreneurs generate innovation potential is to propagate new technology by marshalling many applications synergistically. One takeaway from this study is accordingly that the innovation potential digital entrepreneurs generate lies as much in the connections between diferent applications that they make possible as within any one of them. Organizations must therefore learn to harness multiplicity in IS use to strategically manage corporate entrepreneurship with digital technology. Following in our steps will require a certain tolerance for complexity. Nevertheless, we hope to have inspired others to develop our nascent understanding of how organizations should tackle this challenge
