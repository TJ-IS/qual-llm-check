## a b s t r a c t

Degradation of environment can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affect human existence and development. Therefore, environmental protection deserves signi<sup>fi</sup>cant attention. Low-carbon products have become an attractive option due to global warming. To ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently and effectively achieve the aim of eco-friendly electronic product design, this paper develops a decision-making methodology for low-carbon electronic product design. First, the proposed methodology estimates the released amounts of greenhouse gases for different product designs throughout the major phases of a product's life cycle. This research can subsequently determine the benchmarking low-carbon design parts (as compared with other competitive products) and discern the causes of the poor carbon footprint (CF) performance. This will stimulate innovation to propose the low-carbon design alternatives. This research creates an evaluation model of low-carbon design alternative combinations in order to assess their performance. A multiobjective genetic algorithm is employed to determine an optimal low-carbon design alternative combination satisfying the CF constraint of a new product and minimizing the design time and cost. Finally, this research utilizes an MP3 player as a case study to showcase the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of the proposed methodology.

## 1. Introduction

Greenhouse gases (GHGs) trap heat within the earth's atmosphere that accelerate global warming [9]. Due to rapid industrial development around the world, the emission and accumulation of GHGs are increasing and signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly raising the concentrations of these gases in the atmosphere. There are six GHGs regulated under the Kyoto Protocol, including carbon dioxide (CO ), methane $\mathrm { ( C H _ { 4 } ) }$ , nitrous oxide $( \mathsf { N } _ { 2 } \mathsf { O } )$ , sulfur hexa<sup>fl</sup>uoride (SF ), hexa<sup>fl</sup>uoroethane (C F ) and hydro<sup>fl</sup>uorocarbons (HFCs), which differ in their ability to trap heat. These gases therefore have different global warming effects. Because CO is the common gas created by manufacturing activities, scientists use the radiative forcing of CO as a reference in comparing the warming effects of other GHGs. Hence, this research adopts a widely used global-warming potential (GWP) as a relative measure to convert each gas into a comparable CO equivalent (CO -e). The GWP of CO is assigned a value of one. The CO -e for a speci<sup>fi</sup>c GHG is obtained by multiplying the mass and the GWP of the particular gas. The total carbon footprint (CF) of a product can thus be acquired.

Precautionary measures must be adopted to reduce GHG emissions and slow down global warming. Because many consumers and enterprises now recognize that eco-friendly products have lower environmental impacts, the adoption of green products has become more popular [20]. As for environmental regulations, the Energy-using Products (EuP) directive became law in the EU in 2007 [10] and has required <sup>fi</sup>rms to satisfy eco-design requirements in order to gain access to the European market. The EuP directive is considered an effective measure in meeting GHG reduction requirements. With this in mind, low-carbon products are of vital importance, and reducing carbon emissions in a cost- and time-effective manner is a critical challenge.

This research proposes a decision-making methodology for ecofriendly with a focus on low-carbon electronic product design. At the detailed design stage, the proposed methodology employs backpropagation neural networks (BPNNs) to assist product design engineers in accurately forecasting the amounts of GHG emissions for all parts of an electronic product during the major phases of a product's life cycle in order to obtain the total CF of the new product. If the total CF of a new product cannot satisfy the low-carbon emission constraint, the CF based performance evaluation model for parts of electronic products can search the benchmarking low-carbon design parts to <sup>fi</sup>nd clues for improving the CF performance of the non-benchmarking parts, thus motivating the creation of low-carbon design alternatives. This research also proposes a bi-objective performance evaluation model of lowcarbon alternative combinations in order to analyze their performance. To help companies select the optimal alternative combination to reduce GHG emissions throughout the major phases of the product's life cycle, this research employs a multi-objective genetic algorithm (MOGA) [27] to determine the best low-carbon alternative combination with the minimum design time and cost. Finally, this research utilizes an MP3 design project as a case study to showcase the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of the proposed methodology.

The remainder of this paper is as follows: Section 2 reviews relevant environmental design research. Section 3 describes a decision-making methodology for low-carbon electronic product design. Section 4 clari<sup>fi</sup>es and veri<sup>fi</sup>es the effectiveness and the practical contributions of the proposed methodology by using an MP3 design project as a case study. Finally, the conclusions of the study are presented in Section 5.

## 2. Literature review

This review of existing literature can be divided into: (1) life cycle assessment (LCA), (2) performance analysis of the low-carbon design and (3) environmental design decision-making. This research high lights the primary limitations of the existing approaches or models.

Because environmental awareness and regulations have increased the pressure on <sup>fi</sup>rms to take environmental considerations into account in their product design process, newer methods and tools of LCA have been developed to support environmental design. Arena et al. [3] selected key environmental dimensions and constructed a set of indicators to develop a streamlined LCA framework to measure the environmental impacts at the vehicle design phase. Sharma and Manepatil [33] applied LCA to analyze the environmental impacts of the parts of a bicycle; the results allowed designers to understand the Design for Environment (DfE) level. Lu et al. [25] used a 0–5 rating system to perform an LCA in order to comprehend the environmental impacts of the major parts of a coating machine throughout the product's life cycle. However, these methods only provide rough LCA information, which cannot be employed to analyze the reasons for the strong environmental impacts.

Verghese et al. [39] developed an online streamlined LCA tool for packaging design decision support by utilizing embedded life cycle inventory (LCI) data. Because small and medium-sized enterprises lacked human resources and capital, Chiang et al. [7] developed an ISO 14048- based integrated service platform to provide LCI data for standard electronic parts in order to reduce the cost of performing an LCA. Fet et al. [13] created a database to conduct an LCA for selected products and identify possible improvement. However, these systems only focus on speci<sup>fi</sup>c products with LCA data and cannot provide its detailed information on the non-standard parts. BPNNs are used for forecasting LCA during the product design process. Sousa and Wallace [35] used the high-end product characteristics and the environmental impact values of the products to train the BPNN. Based on the forecasting results of an LCA, product design engineers can estimate the environmental impacts at the detailed design stage, and decide whether the eco-design performance of a product should be enhanced. Park and Seo [28] created a knowledge-based approximate LCA system based on a BPNN to accurately evaluate the environmental impacts of product design alternatives. According to Lau et al. [23], the BPNNs can provide more accurate predictions than the methods of exponential smoothing and multiple regression. The aforementioned research shows that BPNNs are very useful for estimating environmental impacts. Because an environmental impact level is a synthesized performance assessment value, it cannot provide detailed LCI information for further analysis to improve the environmental ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of a product design. Therefore, this study uses the high-end product attributes as inputs and the amounts of GHG emissions as outputs to train the BPNNs for establishing the estimation models of GHG emissions.

To evaluate the performance of the low-carbon design, Kuo [20] constructed a CF disclosure system. The system can store the GHG emission data for the parts of electronic products and then calculate the CFs of electronic products. Hence, product design engineers can compare the performance of the low-carbon designs of different electronic products. Song and Lee [34] developed a DfE system based on a bill of materials to con<sup>fi</sup>gure a low-carbon product by replacing problematic parts. In this case study, the CF of the liquid-crystal display (LCD) TV module was reduced 639 kg ${ \mathsf { C O } } _ { 2 } – { \mathsf { e } } .$ However, these systems only provided the existing parts to replace the high-carbon parts. In addition, the carbon-emission constraint of each part needed to be subjectively set by the product design engineers in order to identify the problematic parts. Moreover, these methods and systems cannot help enterprises understand how to improve CF-based design performance by comparing their existing products with other benchmarking competitive ones. Therefore, companies can only adopt a subjective approach to setting goals for decreasing CFs. As a response to this problem, Chiang et al. [6] devised a performance evaluation model for DfE based on the Technique for Order Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution (TOPSIS) method to obtain the maximum reducing CF of 22.1052 $\tt { g C O } _ { 2 } – \tt { e }$ for the optical mouse in the case study. However, this method cannot simultaneously consider multiple GHG emissions at the different phases of the product's life cycle to reasonably evaluate the reducing GHG amounts from a global perspective. To overcome the above problem, this research employs Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA) to develop the CF based performance evaluation model. This model can assess the low-carbon design performance of a variety of parts of electronic products. For nonbenchmarking low-carbon electronic product designs, this model can estimate the needed reductions in the GHG emissions for each part of electronic products at the main phases of the product's life cycle. In this way product design engineers can learn how to improve the inef<sup>fi</sup>cient low-carbon electronic product designs.

With regard to environmental design decision-making, Kuo et al. [21] applied the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) method to evaluate the performance of different environmentally conscious design alterna tives. The evaluation criteria contained recycling, energy consumption, hazardous substances, costs and materials. Sadiq and Khan [31] used the AHP method and Fuzzy Composite Programming to develop a multi-criteria decision-making approach to rank environmental impact improvement alternatives. The multi-criteria framework includes two aspects, i.e., environmental impacts and cost. The environmental im pacts include ozone depletion, acid rain potential and global warming potential. However, the AHP method must assume that the relationships among evaluation criteria are independent. Hence, Kengpol and Boonkanit [19] used an Analytic Network Process (ANP) model and a distance-to-target model as a decision-making tool in order to select the best environmental design idea. Because the AHP and ANP models are subiective decision-making methods. different experts may give the different opinions, hence leading to different decision-making results. Therefore, Bovea and Wang [5] adopted a quantitative method to express environmental impacts and costs for eco-redesign alterna tions, and constructed environmental and cost comparison matrices to identify environmental improvements and cost variations for selecting eco-redesign alternatives. Dormer et al. [12] used the spreadsheet analysis to estimate the CFs of poly-ethylene terephthalate (PET) trays with different recycled contents. They found that if the tray was manufactured with 100% recycled PET, the CF is reduced by 24% to become 1.344 kg ${ \mathsf { C O } } _ { 2 } – { \mathsf { e } } ,$ compared to the current 85% recycled content scenario. However, the method is not suitable to evaluate the perfor mance of the low-carbon design alternatives of the complex product structures. Su et al. [36] developed a decision support system to evalu ate the carbon emission and manufacturing cost for the design of the as sembly structure and sequence of a computer chair. By using the system, the carbon emission of the computer chair was decreased 6.37 kg ${ \mathrm { C O } } _ { 2 } – { \mathrm { e } } .$ Kuo et al. [22] developed a multi-objective planning model to screen the existing components provided by the suppliers to produce a low-carbon product structure design with the lowes manufacturing cost. In this case, the CF of the router was reduced from 60.515 kg $C 0 _ { 2 } { \mathrm { - e } }$ to 50.493 kg ${ \mathsf { C O } } _ { 2 } – { \mathsf { e } }$ . Xu et al. [42] proposed a multi-objective low-carbon optimization con<sup>fi</sup>guration design model. In this model, a product's function modules are classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into three categories: (1) the core module, (2) the accessory module, and (3) the optional module. By using the con<sup>fi</sup>guration design model, the CF of the touring bicycle was decreased by 28.5 kg $C 0 _ { 2 ^ { - } } { \mathsf { e } }$ to become 140.9 kg CO -e with a minimal production cost. Chiu et al. [8] developed a knowledge-based system to connect the functions of the products with physical components in a design repository. The system can gener ate possible design concepts, and considers the supply chain costs and

CFs of the design concepts to create a low-carbon product. In this case study, the CF of the bicycle was changed from 67 kg ${ \mathsf { C O } } _ { 2 } – { \mathsf { e } }$ into 60 kg $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 ^ { - } }$ e. However, these decision-making methods are just suitable for evaluation and selection of different product structures using the existing components to achieve the low-carbon product design. To <sup>fi</sup>nd an appropriate means to reduce the CF of the electronic image projector, Trappey et al. [38] employed a system dynamics model to simulate the results for low-carbon design scenarios. In this case, the carbon emission of the projector was lowered from 121.51 kg $\mathsf { C O } _ { 2 } – \mathsf { e }$ to 104.189 kg $C 0 _ { 2 } { \mathrm { - e } }$ by using the third scenario. However, the product design engineers can propose a variety of low-carbon part design alternatives. These methods cannot analyze the performance of different low-carbon alternative combinations. In addition, they cannot simultaneously consider the product design time and cost. Table 1 shows the reduced absolute and relative amounts of carbon emissions of low-carbon product design cases given by the abovementioned literature.

In this literature review, we learn that few studies focus on the estimation of GHG emissions in the detailed design phase to evaluate the CF of a new electronic product design. To stimulate low-carbon design innovation, product design engineers need to determine the benchmarking low-carbon design parts and then elicit the important design information to create low-carbon design alternatives. At the same time, little research can help product design managers evaluate and identify the optimal alternative combination with the minimum design time and cost. Therefore, this research proposes a decision-making methodology for low-carbon electronic product design. This proposed methodology will assist product design engineers in estimating the quantity of GHG emissions to discover benchmarking low-carbon design parts so as to create low-carbon design alternatives. Finally, this research employs a MOGA to help product design managers <sup>fi</sup>nd the best low-carbon design alternative combination with the minimum design time and cost.

## 3. A decision-making methodology for low-carbon electronic product design

To assist product design engineers to ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently and effectively develop low-carbon electronic products, the proposed decision-making methodology can reasonably evaluate the GHG emissions throughout the main phases of a part's life cycle. Therefore, product design managers can determine whether the low-carbon constraint of an electronic product is satis<sup>fi</sup>ed at the detailed design stage. If the low-carbon constraint cannot be satis<sup>fi</sup>ed, the CF-based performance evaluation model can identify the benchmarking low-carbon design parts. The design information of these benchmarking low-carbon parts can help product design engineers propose low-carbon design alternatives. The bi-objective performance evaluation model of low-carbon design alternative combinations can be employed to assess the performance of different alternative combinations. Finally, this research utilizes the MOGA algorithm to rapidly <sup>fi</sup>nd the best alternative combination to achieve a low-carbon design objective with the minimum design time and cost.

Fig. 1 shows an analytical procedure for a decision-making methodology for low-carbon electronic product design. The analytical procedure of the proposed methodology can be expressed as a drilldown hierarchical structure, including the stepped, macro and micro levels. The stepped level illustrates the abstracted steps of the analytical procedure. The <sup>fi</sup>rst step is the estimation of GHG emissions. The second step is the CF based performance evaluation and improvement. The third step is the selection of an optimal low-carbon design alternative combination. The macro level describes the main activities of the stepped level. The micro level delineates the detailed procedure. The following paragraphs elaborate on each step of the decision-making methodology for low-carbon electronic product design.

Step 1: Estimation of GHG emissions. At the detailed design stage, product design engineers can input the design speci<sup>fi</sup>cations of the parts of an electronic product to the estimation models of GHG emissions to predict the amounts of GHG emissions for each part produced throughout the main phases of the part's life cycle. The product design engineers use the part's weight of each material as an input in the GHG estimation model for calculating $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 } , \mathrm { N } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O }$ and $S \mathrm { F _ { 6 } }$ emissions for the material extraction phase. They then use each part's weight and dimension for estimating $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 } , \mathrm { N } _ { 2 } 0 , \mathrm { S F } _ { 6 }$ and ${ \mathrm { C } } _ { 2 } { \mathrm { F } } _ { 6 }$ emissions during the manufacturing phase. Similarly, the total operating time, voltage and current of an electronic product are used to find $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 } , \mathrm { N } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O }$ and ${ \mathrm { S F } } _ { 6 }$ emissions during the usage phase. A detail of this estimation process is shown in a <sup>fl</sup>ow diagram in Fig. 1.

Step 2: CF-based performance evaluation and improvement. After obtaining the estimated amounts of GHG emissions, product design engineers transfer the amounts of GHG emissions for the main phases of a part's life cycle into ${ \mathsf { C O } } _ { 2 }$ -e and then calculate the total CF of a new product. By using this information, product design managers can subsequently determine whether the goal of a low-carbon electronic product design is achieved. If it has not been achieved, the product design engineers must reduce the CF. To assist product design engineers in proposing low-carbon design alternatives, this research uses a CF based performance evaluation model for parts of electronic products to <sup>fi</sup>nd the benchmarking low-carbon design parts. The high-end design speci<sup>fi</sup>cations of benchmarking lowcarbon design parts can let product design engineers acquire information about how to enhance the low-carbon designs and then <sup>fi</sup>nd the improvement methods of low-carbon electronic product designs.

Step 3: Selection of an optimal low-carbon design alternative combination. After obtaining the improvement methods of low-carbon electronic product designs, product design engineers can propose low-carbon design alternatives. Product design engineers can apply the estimation models of GHG emissions to evaluate the CF of the new design part, and then calculate the decreasing amount of ${ \mathsf { C O } } _ { 2 } – { \mathsf { e } }$ for each option of a low-carbon design alternative, comparing it with the original design of the part. At the same time, product design engineers need to estimate the times and costs for all options of each alternative. To help product design managers evaluate and <sup>fi</sup>nd the best way to reduce GHG emissions, this research develops a bi-objective evaluation model of low-carbon design alternative combinations, while applying a MOGA algorithm to select the optimal low-carbon design alternative combination, achieving a lowcarbon design objective with the minimum design time and cost. The following subsections clarify each part of the decision-making methodology for low-carbon electronic product design.

## 3.2. CF based performance evaluation and improvement

If a new electronic product design cannot satisfy the constraint of the ${ \mathsf { C O } } _ { 2 } – { \mathsf { e } }$ emission, the product design engineers must improve their electronic product designs. This research uses a BCC-data envelopment analysis (BCC-DEA) [4] to create the CF based performance evaluation model for parts of electronic products, as shown in Eq. (10). The inputs of this model are the quantities of GHG emissions generated during the material extraction, manufacturing and usage phases while the output is a part. A CF based performance evaluation value of 1 for an electronic part indicates that the part is a benchmarking low-carbon design part. Therefore, this model can identify all benchmarking low-carbon design parts by comparing the CFs of other parts of similar products to form ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency frontiers. The benchmarking low-carbon design parts on the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency frontiers show that it is not possible to reduce any CF of a part emitted during the speci<sup>fi</sup>c phase of a part's life cycle without increasing the CFs of other phases of a part's life cycle. The detailed design speci<sup>fi</sup>cations of the benchmarking low-carbon design parts can help product design engineers stimulate innovation to create low-carbon design alternatives.

However, an option can only be selected because it is exclusive among the options of an alternative. At the same time, different alternatives can be combined. In addition, product design engineers must provide the engineering change times (ECTs) and the engineering change costs (ECCs) of all options for each alternative. In the future, when managers deal with decisionmaking problems of the same type, they can provide the case data and then the MOGA based decision support system can quickly <sup>fi</sup>nd the optimal solution. Hence, this research utilizes a MOGA to rapidly and effectively establish the best low-carbon alternative combination with the minimum design time and cost. Fig. 3 displays the analytical procedure of a MOGA. The following paragraph explains each step of the MOGA below:

• Step 1: Chromosome encoding. Each chromosome is considered as an individual. Each individual represents a low-carbon design alternative combination. Fig. 4 shows the chromosome structure for a low-carbon design alternative combination.

• Step 2: Initial population generation. An initial low-carbon design alternative combination is formed by randomly selecting k alternatives and assigning an option for each alternative selected. By using this method, we can obtain N individuals to generate an initial population.

• Step 3: Calculation of the bi-objective evaluation values for lowcarbon design alternative combinations. Eq. (11) is used to calculate the bi-objective function values of each low-carbon design alternative combination.

• Step 4: Sorting individuals. In this study, the low-carbon design alternative combinations are ranked using a non-dominated sorting GA [11]. First, each combination must be compared with other individuals in the population to <sup>fi</sup>nd all individuals of the <sup>fi</sup>rst non-dominated front. Afterwards, the combinations of the <sup>fi</sup>rst non-dominated front are labeled as Level 1 before temporarily removing them from the population. Next, the individuals with non-dominated solutions in the second non-dominated front are labeled as Level 2. By repeating these steps, all low-carbon design alternative combinations are labeled with a non-dominated level. Finally, the individuals are prioritized based on their non-dominated levels.

The revised <sup>fi</sup>tness function value of each lowcarbon design alternative combination is divided by the total <sup>fi</sup>tness function value to obtain the probability for the individual to be reproduced [14]. Therefore, low-carbon design alternative combinations with higher <sup>fi</sup>tness function values have more opportunities for reproduction in the mating pool.

• Step 8: Crossover. By swapping the genetic codes of two individuals, the two-point crossover produces the new low-carbon design alternative combinations.

• Step 9: Mutation. To create the diversity of combinations in a population, individuals are randomly selected to change the option of these low-carbon design alternatives.

• Step 10: Creation of a new population. A new population is formed by combining the former population and new individuals produced by crossover and mutation. ## 5. Concluding remarks

Electronic products quickly penetrate the global market to cause a lot of GHG emissions. Therefore, low-carbon electronic product design has become an urgent matter. Addressing the above challenge, this research creates a decision-making methodology for low-carbon electronic product design. With regard to LCA, earlier research has shown that BPNNs are a very suitable method for creating relationships between high-end product attributes and environmental impacts such as greenhouse effects. However, it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to understand the actual conditions for the macro-level synthesized performance values of the environmental impacts. To accurately determine the effects of various products on GHG emissions, many countries encourage <sup>fi</sup>rms to calculate the CFs of products throughout the product's life cycle. With this detailed information, companies can comprehend the physical state of their GHG emissions and can present proposals to decrease these emissions. To date, there is little research on methods to reasonably forecast GHG emissions in the detailed design stage. As a result, this paper employs BPNNs to develop the estimation models of GHG emissions to predict their amounts in each part produced throughout the major phases of the part's life cycle.

Although a variety of techniques have been proposed to evaluate the environmental performance of products, these product-level evaluation techniques cannot assist product design engineers in reducing the carbon emissions of electronic products. Due to a lack of performance evaluation methods of the part-level CF, product design engineers cannot objectively analyze and understand the environmental performance of the parts of a new electronic product and determine the benchmarking low-carbon design parts through comparison with other similar electronic products. To enhance the green competitiveness of new products in a <sup>fi</sup>erce business environment, the CF based performance evaluation model for parts of electronic products has been developed to analyze the environmental design performance of each part of a new product to <sup>fi</sup>nd the benchmarking low-carbon design parts of other similar electronic products. By comparing the high-end attributes of the benchmarking low-carbon design parts, product design engineers can obtain useful hints on how to improve the carbon emissions of their non-benchmarking parts and proposing low-carbon design alternatives.

To accomplish the goal of low-carbon design, a variety of low-carbon design alternatives can be offered. Major papers have described multiattribute decision-making methods with subjectively pairwise comparisons such as AHP and ANP in order to assess an alternative's performance. Achieving low-carbon design with a minimum design time and cost is a crucial issue for companies. Hence, <sup>fi</sup>rms must have an objective quantitative evaluation model for low-carbon design alternatives. In addition, a single alternative may not accomplish the aim of the low-carbon electronic product design. If a company adopts two or more alternatives, the reduced carbon emissions and the design times and costs of different alternative combinations are different. However, only a few articles have reported the related methods to help product design engineers evaluate the performance of different alternative combinations and determine an optimal alternative combination. This research therefore proposes the bi-objective evaluation model of low-carbon design alternative combinations to evaluate the performance of different alternative combinations. To <sup>fi</sup>nd an optimal approach to achieve the objective of the low-carbon electronic product design, this research utilizes the MOGA to create the best low-carbon alternative combination with the minimum design time and cost and satisfy the CF requirement of a new electronic product. Finally, this paper employs an MP3 player design project as an authentic case study demonstrating the noticeable contribution of the presented methodology.

There are some limitations and shortcomings in this research. First, the presented methodology is only appropriate for the electronics industry.

Other industries (such as heavy industries and service industries) need to develop their own suitable methods to create low-carbon products and services. Second, with regard to the CFs produced during transportation, further research is needed to evaluate whether further improvement through adopting an environmentally friendly transport mode or method can be achieved. Third, the short life cycle of electronic products causes a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant amount of electronic waste. However, this research does not analyze the CFs of recycling and waste disposal. The above topics have enormous potential for future study.

His current research interests include production/operations management, supply chain management, information management, and applications of metaalgorithms.
