# THE ASYMMETRIC BENEFITS OF RELATIONAL FLEXIBILITY: EVIDENCE FROM SOFTWARE DEVELOPMENT OUTSOURCING $^{1}$

Anandasivam Gopal
Robert H. Smith School of Business, University of Maryland,
College Park, MD 20742 U.S.A. {agopal@rhsmith.umn.edu}

Balaji R. Koka

Jesse H. Jones School of Business, Rice University,

Houston, TX 77055 U.S.A. {Balaji.R.Koka@rice.edu}

In this paper, the interacting effect of formal contracts and relational governance on vendor profitability and quality in the software outsourcing industry are examined. We focus on a critical manifestation of relational governance—the presence of relational flexibility in the exchange relationship—and argue that the enacted observation of relational flexibility is driven by perceptions of exchange hazards. In a departure from extant literature, however, we propose that the benefits accruing from it are asymmetric and depend on how the exchange risks are apportioned by the formal contract. Formally, we hypothesize that relational flexibility provides greater benefits to an exchange partner that faces the greater proportion of risk in a project, induced through the contract. In addition, we hypothesize that these benefits manifest on the performance dimensions that are of importance to the risk-exposed partner. We test our hypotheses on 105 software projects completed by a software outsourcing vendor for multiple clients. The results show that relational flexibility positively affects profitability in only fixed price contracts, where the vendor faces greater risk, while positively affecting quality only in time and materials contracts, where the client is at greater risk. We thus provide evidence for the asymmetric benefits from relational governance, thereby arguing for a more contingent and limited view of the value of relational governance, based on risk-exposure, rather than the more expansive view prevalent in the literature contending that relational governance provides benefits for all parties to an exchange. We conclude with a discussion of the research and managerial implications of our findings.

Keywords: Relational governance, relational flexibility, formal contracts, software development, outsourcing, exchange hazards, regression analysis, quality, profitability

## Introduction

In recent years, relational governance of interorganizational relationships has emerged as a dominant perspective in vendor–client relationships. Situating the vendor–client relationship in the center of a series of economic and social interactions between organizations (Dyer and Singh 1998; Macneil 1980), the relational view emphasizes noncontractual elements of the relationships such as trust (Gulati 1995), reciprocity and flexibility (Heide 1994) as safeguards against opportunism. Many researchers (Goo et al. 2009; Poppo and Zenger 2002) have argued that the effectiveness of these relational mechanisms depends on the level of exchange hazards in the relationship. As exchange hazards increase in the relationship, formal contracts have to be more complex to effectively govern them (Mesquita and Brush 2008; Poppo and Zenger 2002; Ring and Van de Ven 1992). However, such complex formal contracts are still limited in their ability to rein in opportunism due to their incompleteness (Williamson 1979). It is in these contexts that relational governance plays a critical role by allowing parties to enact flexibility, forbearance, and reciprocity in the relationship (Gulati 1995; Macneil 1978), thereby leading to the broadening consensus in the literature that relational and formal governance act as complementors (Poppo and Zenger 2002).

Within this view of the complementarity of formal and relational governance, we identify two critical gaps in the literature. Scholars have shown that relational governance, in the presence of formal contracting, has a significant impact on the outcomes of economic activities (Gulati and Nickerson 2008; Hoetker and Mellewigt 2009; Mani et al. 2011; Poppo and Zenger 2002). However, in much of this work, it is implicitly assumed that these enacted relational mechanisms provide symmetric benefits to all parties. In a departure from this assumption, we argue that since relational governance is designed to mitigate risk in the exchange, its benefits are more likely to flow to the partner who faces greater risk in the exchange. Since vendors and clients in an exchange typically face differential risks, they also face differential benefits from the exchange based on relative risk exposure. As the formal contract is primarily a risk-sharing instrument, particularly in the outsourcing domain (Gopal et al. 2003), the benefits from relational governance will be driven in part by the formal contracting regime in place (i.e., contingent on risk exposure induced by the contract). Thus, more formally, we hypothesize that the formal contract moderates the value provided by enacted relational governance to the specific party by varying risk exposure.

The second and related gap we identify is, how is value from relational governance captured for different parties in inter-organizational economic activities, specifically outsourcing projects? If the benefits of relational governance are indeed moderated by risk exposure instituted by the formal contract, it follows that these benefits to the contracting parties will likely manifest themselves along different dimensions of outcomes such as quality and project profitability. We posit that the benefits from relational mechanisms will add value to clients by enhancing quality in formal contractual regimes that expose it to greater risk. Conversely, these mechanisms will benefit the vendor's project profitability in the contractual regime that exposes it to greater risk.

Our paper thus provides a more nuanced understanding of the relationship between formal and relational governance by contending, first, that the benefits from relational governance are contingent on risk exposure from the contract type and, second, that these benefits will manifest, on the margin, on those dimensions that are of importance to the more risk-exposed exchange partner. As prior research has indicated, there are two primary types of contracts used in software outsourcing: fixed price (FP) and time and materials (T&M) (Banerjee and Duflo 2000). FP contracts specify a fixed amount to be paid to the vendor in exchange for completion of a specified project. T&M contracts, on the other hand, are cost plus contracts where the vendor's services are bought at a specified billing rate. FP contracts are riskier for the vendor since cost and schedule overruns are borne by the vendor, while T&M contracts are riskier for the client. These contract types impose differential risk structures that favor the client (FP) and the vendor (T&M), allowing us to test our arguments about risk sharing and relational governance on project outcomes.

In this paper, we focus on a critical manifestation of relational governance: the presence of relational flexibility between the exchange partners in exploring these questions. By definition, relational governance represents ex post, extra-contractual aspects of the relationship in that these are not contractually stipulated but represent what actually transpired during the project. Under the meta-construct of relational governance, researchers have studied the role of specific mechanisms such as relational flexibility (Heide 1994; Wathne and Heide 2004), joint action (Mani et al. 2011), trust (Goo et al. 2009), quasi-integration (Zaheer and Venkatraman 1995), and collaboration (Artz and Brush 2000; Poppo and Zenger 2002). In the specific context we study (i.e., turnkey software outsourcing projects), relational flexibility is a commonly observed manifestation of relational governance in the exchange relationship. We therefore focus our hypotheses development and empirical testing on the extent to which relational flexibility is observed in the economic exchange. We test our hypotheses on a dataset of 105 software development outsourcing projects completed by an Indian software vendor. Software outsourcing is characterized by high uncertainty, ambiguous performance measurement, and high asset specificity, thereby being highly suitable for testing our hypotheses (Goo et al. 2009; Poppo and Zenger 2002). In addition, most contracts used are incomplete due to the high level of risk and uncertainty inherent in software projects, enhancing the salience of risk partitioning through the formal contract. Our results show that, on the margin, the effect of relational flexibility on vendor profitability is higher in FP contracts where the vendor bears the greater level of risk. In contrast, the impact of relational flexibility on quality is higher in

T&M contracts where the client bears the greater portion of risk. In the next section, we develop our research hypotheses in more detail after briefly reviewing the literature relating relational governance and formal contracts.

## Theoretical Background

## Relational Governance and Formal Contracts in Vendor–Client Relationships

The importance of a formal contract in interorganizational activities is well-established; rarely ever are economic exchanges, such as outsourcing, initiated without some type of a formal contract in place. The contract enables the exchange relationship by specifying the expectations and obligations of the contracting parties. In complex economic exchanges, however, it is not possible to devise complete contingent contracts. In such environments, the residual incompleteness manifests itself through differential risk structures that benefit one party more than the other, leading to conditions where one party tends to bear greater risk in the relationship. Typically two primary types of contracts have been used in this context: fixed price (FP) and time and materials (T&M). FP contracts are riskier for the vendor since cost and schedule overruns are borne by the vendor while T&M contracts, on the other hand, are riskier for the client since the client is typically responsible for project cost or schedule overruns (Banerjee and Duflo 2000).

Both of these broad contract types remain incomplete in the software outsourcing context as the underlying complexities of software development outsourcing cannot be captured entirely through the formal contracting process. It is precisely in these complex exchanges that relational governance assumes importance. Specifically, relational governance holds that dyadic relationships are embedded in a broader context of interorganizational relationships that binds firms (and individuals) into industry-wide networks (Granovetter 1985; Macneil 1980; Uzzi 1997). Such networked relationships provide parties with mechanisms to reduce the impact of exchange hazards, manage disputes, and equitably distribute surplus from the exchanges. These mechanisms include trust (Gulati 1995; Nooteboom et al. 1997; Poppo and Zenger 2002; Zaheer and Venkatraman 1995), joint action and reciprocity (Heide 1994; Zaheer and Venkatraman 1995), expectations of continuity and fairness (Artz and Brush 2000; Heide and John 1990), and reputation and repeated interactions (Dyer 1997; Kalnins and Mayer 2004). Thus, while formal contracts set the stage for the relationship and the associated risk partitioning, relational governance provides for flexibility by enabling shared interpretation, renegotiation, and adjustments in project parameters outside of the formal contract, thereby allowing parties to better manage exchange hazards.

Such reasoning has been well supported by considerable empirical evidence establishing the presence of relational governance in relationships that exhibit high levels of exchange hazards, asset-specific investments and potential opportunism (Dyer 1997; Heide 1994; Heide and John 1990; Nooteboom et al. 1997; Zaheer and Venkatraman 1995). In addition, the literature shows that formal and relational governance are complementary, with respect to their effects on performance, starting with the seminal paper by Poppo and Zenger (2002). Recent evidence for this complementarity is found in the auto sector (Gulati and Nickerson 2008), telecommunications (Hoetker and Mellewigt 2009), business process outsourcing (Mani et al. 2011) and R&D activities (Carson et al. 2006). However, much of this work incorporates two assumptions.

First, much of this work assumes that benefits from relational governance accrue to both parties. By definition, relational mechanisms such as joint action and flexibility are bilateral in nature (Ring and Van de Ven 1992) and, therefore, if parties to an exchange jointly invest in these mechanisms, the benefits can be assumed to be symmetric to all parties. For instance, Poppo and Zenger's measurement of performance covers aspects of the exchange's costs, quality, and vendor responsiveness (p. 715). Similarly, Gulati and Nickerson's (2008, p. 695) measure includes aspects of price, support, quality, flexibility, and so on (a total of 10 items). In both of these examples, performance is captured holistically at the exchange level, reinforcing the assumption that all parties should benefit from observed relational mechanisms. However, the formal contract allocates risks in the exchange differentially, with one party typically bearing greater risks. Since the primary role of relational governance is its effect on mitigating exchange risks, it follows that the enacted relational mechanisms should provide greater benefits to the party that bears the higher risk in a given engagement, thereby explicitly arguing for asymmetric benefits from relational governance based on relative risk exposure. Therefore, we propose that the relationship between relational governance and its benefits depends on how the risk is apportioned (i.e., on contract type).

Second, the issue of differential risk exposure makes salient the problem arising from different performance dimensions. Within the contracting literature, scholars have recognized the need to consider multiple dimensions of performance in evaluating agent effort and the interactions between these alternative performance dimensions (Dixit 2002). Consider the common problem of incentivizing cost minimization for agents by setting strict cost controls; clearly, the agent can minimize cost by shirking on quality (Holmstrom and Milgrom 1991). Therefore, for the principal, monitoring both outcome dimensions becomes important. This is reflected in the outsourcing literature as well; Couto et al. (2006) show that clients care about quality, security, and process discipline in addition to cost savings. In a similar vein, vendors care about customer satisfaction and reputation (Banerjee and Duflo 2000) in addition to costs and profitability. $^{2}$ The critical point to note here is vendors have differing marginal costs of effort along these performance dimensions (Baker 1992), which indicates that vendors are more likely to respond to incentives or pressures along their preferred outcome dimension. Therefore, vendors are more sensitive to certain outcome dimensions than others. Similarly, clients are more sensitive about certain outcomes on the project while being indifferent or less sensitive about others. The role of the formal contract is thus critical now; the risk exposure induced by the formal contract emphasizes the extent to which exchange hazards influence the contracting party’s preferred outcome dimension. In contracts where the vendor (client) bears greater risk, on the margin, the focus of the vendor’s (client’s) attention will be greater on his/her preferred outcome dimension. Therefore, the benefits of relational governance will be measurably higher, first, for the contracting party that bears greater risk and, second, along the performance dimensions of critical importance to that party.

## A Model for Use of Relational Flexibility in Software Development Outsourcing

In this paper, we examine these issues in the context of outsourced software development projects, contracted individually on a turnkey basis (Davis et al. 2006). Each project represents a separate and identifiable series of tasks or activities undertaken to achieve a specific information systems objective within certain technical specifications, with relatively well-defined start and end dates (Pressman 2005). These projects are characterized by many of the exchange hazards studied within relational exchange theory (Ring and Van de Ven 1992). First, there is considerable uncertainty in projects, emerging from the very nature of software development (Pressman 2005) in that it is difficult to predict the interaction between client requirements, technical parameters, team/manager experience, and technology platforms in the project ex ante. Second, significant measurement difficulties are associated with outsourced projects (Alchian and Demsetz 1972) due to the intangible nature of software services (Nayyar 1993). This highlights the incentive problems that are exacerbated in incomplete contracting settings (Baker 1992). Third, vendors need to make asset-specific investments in the project (Ethiraj et al. 2005). These asset-specific investments involve technical skills, often through specific training, as well as managerial expertise. While technical knowledge per se may be acquired more easily in the market, the combination of technical knowledge and experience is a rare and valuable capability (Mata et al. 1995). Finally, turn-key outsourced software projects tend to be idiosyncratic in that no two projects within the same domain may be similar. The presence of these features in outsourcing projects thus leads to considerable exchange hazards in the project, which may be manifested in different variables across projects.

The enactment of relational governance in this specific domain is likely to highlight flexibility in the immediate environment of the project rather than longer-term mechanisms such as joint action or reciprocity. Thus, within the higher-order construct of relational governance, the specific mechanism we observe in short-term, idiosyncratic knowledge work (such as software outsourcing) is relational flexibility on the project (Heide 1994; Wathne and Heide 2004). Relational flexibility enables the partners to mutually adjust their obligations, expectations, and interpretation of the contractual terms as unforeseen contingencies arise in the project and represents ex post, extra-contractual aspects of the relationship that are not contractually stipulated but represent what actually transpired during the project. In this context, it is the willingness of the exchange partners to go beyond the existing contract to negotiate adjustments to existing processes and procedures that is critical for completing the task as environmental or organizational factors change (Heide 1994). These processes may not be written down in explicit form but left open-ended such that adjustments can be made as and when required. In the remainder of this paper, we use the term relational flexibility to represent the context-specific instantiation of relational governance and propose a model for relational flexibility based on two broad sets of factors observed in the literature. The first set relates to exchange hazards while the second set relates to the nature of the relationship between the partners. The model is described in detail next.

## Exchange Hazards

In software outsourcing, two significant sources of uncertainty pertain to unstable project requirements and assetspecific investments on the project (Pressman 2005; Nidomolu 1995; Banerjee and Duflo 2000). Changing requirements are an unfortunate consequence of the intangible nature of software outsourcing; in some instances, requirements can only be expressed in an inexact form. Some projects exhibit greater requirements instability due to the domain or technological platform of the project. Requirements instability on the project entails considerable costs for both clients and vendors on the project. High requirements instability necessitates considerable rework, which adds to project costs. More importantly, it implies significant redesign while simultaneously affecting quality and potential delays in completion. It also affects the deployment of personnel as well as project schedules. Thus, in projects with high requirements instability, relational flexibility enables vendors and clients to adapt to the changing requirements that emerge during the life cycle of the project, if any.

In a similar vein, tight labor markets and a premium attached to skilled personnel make human asset specificity a significant hazard (Ethiraj et al. 2005; Lacity and Hirschheim 1993). Software development success depends critically on the extent to which human resources are specifically invested in the project (Poppo and Zenger 2002). These investments are built by either hiring specifically trained personnel for the project or training existing personnel specifically for the project. While this training may be leveraged in other projects, the extent to which this training is specific to the current project will limit alternative options for both clients and vendors. For clients, changing trained programmers for the project will entail a cost of retraining and possible delays on the project. But such costs have to be tempered with the fact that continuously staffing such projects with highly trained resources entails additional costs. For the vendor, the cost of replacing personnel trained or experienced for a project will be higher and possibly more damaging since it is responsible for staffing the project. But the vendor must balance these costs with the potential benefits of deploying such resources on more lucrative/important projects. Such contradictory demands indicate the need for flexibility between the partners in how and when these resources may be deployed. Therefore, highly asset-specific human investments on the project will necessitate and enhance the use of relational flexibility.

A similar argument can be made for the presence of employee turnover from the project as well. Tight labor markets imply that these specialized resources are at a premium, resulting in potential employee turnover on the project (Ethiraj et al. 2005). Here again, the implications of high asset specificity and turnover affect the vendor's costs directly as it involves considerable training up front and significant losses when trained personnel leave. However, even in contexts where there is little asset specificity, employee turnover affects project outcomes since frequent reshuffling of personnel on the project has adverse implications for the quality of the software developed and the delivery schedules (Lacity and Hirschheim 1993). Thus, the greater the level of employee turnover in the project, the greater is the need for relational flexibility. Therefore,

Hypothesis 1a: Greater requirements instability is associated with greater relational flexibility in the vendor-client relationship.

Hypothesis 1b: Greater presence of employee turnover from the project team is associated with greater relational flexibility in the vendor–client relationship.

Hypothesis 1c: Greater vendor human asset specificity in the project team is associated with greater relational flexibility in the vendor-client relationship.

## Relational Factors

Beyond exchange hazards, other factors act as antecedents to relational flexibility by enabling the development of appropriate norms between client and vendor. Referred to as relational norms, they provide the conditions under which parties can institute relational governance in the exchange (Poppo and Zenger 2002). One of the primary antecedents of observed relational flexibility is trust between the client and vendor (Gulati 1995; Zaheer et al. 1998). Trust is important as it allows the contracting parties to go “beyond the contract” in establishing norms and practices built on expectations of reciprocity, fairness, and future business. In outsourcing, the presence of prior interactions between vendor and client has been shown to enhance trust in the exchange (Kalnins and Mayer 2004; Sabherwal 1999). Prior interactions result in greater understanding between the partners as to their motivations, goals, and objectives, thereby leading to a higher occurrence of relational flexibility on the project.

A significant factor that drives the use of relational flexibility in the project is the client's capabilities (Ethiraj et al. 2005). There are several roles that the client plays in these settings. First, the client is vital in terms of knowledge sharing that is essential for most projects. Software development outsourcing is an interactive knowledge-oriented process and the business domain knowledge resident in the client is critical in ensuring project success. Prior literature in knowledge sharing and boundary spanning attests to the critical role that client abilities play in enabling successful projects (Levina and Vaast 2005). Second, the client plays an important coordination and control role within the exchange (Kirsch et al. 2002; Mellewigt et al. 2007). This role pertains to the ability of the client to provide value-added input to the vendor on open issues in the project, act as a controller, and provide feedback on various technical and project-related matters. Third, an experienced client MIS department fosters greater interorganizational trust in the relationship (Dyer 1997), thereby enhancing the use of relational flexibility on the project. Finally, the clients' experience is critical in terms of his/her understanding of the difficulties inherent in the software development process and is thus a factor in predicting his/her willingness to be flexible in the interpretation of the contract terms. Therefore, we propose

Hypothesis 2a: The greater the number of completed projects for the same client, the greater will be the relational flexibility in the client-vendor relationship.

Hypothesis 2b: Greater client MIS department experience with outsourcing is associated with greater relational flexibility in the vendor–client relationship.

## The Moderating Effect of Formal Contract Type on the Value of Relational Flexibility

Consider the FP contract, wherein the vendor's revenues are fixed. Any materialization of unforeseen risks on the project is likely to endanger the vendor's bottom line. Consequently, the vendor's incentives are directed at reducing these effects on profitability. The presence of relational flexibility allows the vendor and client to mutually adjust project parameters ex post, in spite of the presence of ex ante parameters that may not be attainable; indeed, this is one of the primary benefits of relational governance discussed in the literature (Dyer and Singh 1998). Consider, for instance, a situation where the requirements on the project change due to changing business conditions; as the project unfolds, these changing requirements will likely affect vendor profitability under the FP contract. Relational flexibility allows the vendor to work together with the client in ensuring that the vendor's profitability from the project does not suffer. In the absence of such flexibility, contractual parameters are likely to be interpreted strictly, with detrimental effects on profitability. Similarly, consider the warranty conditions specified ex ante. Again, information revealed during development could induce higher costs on the vendors to provide requisite warranty conditions, affecting its profitability. However, relational flexibility will allow the vendor and client to work together, ensuring that the "spirit" rather than the "letter" of the contract is retained, thereby ensuring that vendor profitability does not suffer. The effect of the vendor's strong incentives from FP contracts to protect profitability will direct, on the margin, relational flexibility on the project toward addressing exchange hazards that affect profitability, all else being equal (Baker 1992). Therefore, we argue that in the riskier FP regime, relational flexibility will have a greater positive effect on vendor profitability compared to the T&M regime.

In contrast, within the T&M contract, the vendor's risks are minimized. The literature argues that in T&M contracts, the management of the project is heavily contingent on the client's capabilities and skills, given the client's risk exposure (Lacity and Hirschheim 1993). Higher risk exposure suggests that the clients face greater risk of opportunism by vendors. Opportunism in the T&M contract faced by clients takes two forms. First, vendors tend to place their best resources and people on FP contracts since they face the most risk in those contracts (Arora and Asundi 1999). Even when the client can specify skills that are required to be placed on their projects, these are hard to verify and, on the margin, can be manipulated by the vendor. Second, the vendor has a perverse incentive to extend the duration of the project by adding functionality and rework to the project (Bajari and Tadelis 2001; Banerjee and Duflo 2000).

Both forms of opportunism have significant implications for service quality on the project. Less capable resources deployed on the project leads to quality problems, as shown by research in software development (Krishnan et al. 2000; Ravichandran and Rai 2000). The imperfect observability of the quality of these resources by the client increases the risk of opportunism. In addition, additional rework and changing functionality on the project, which increases the vendor's project revenues, could lead to considerable negative effects on quality (Harter et al. 2000). $^{3}$ Even vendors point out that unnecessarily extending functionality on a project can have negative consequences on service quality (Gopalakrishnan et al. 1996). It thus follows that the benefits of relational flexibility in T&M contracts will be manifested for the client, on the margin, toward reducing the occurrence of these forms of opportunism in the project (Williamson 1979). Rather than sticking to the letter of the contract, relational flexibility in such a context will result in the vendor willingly deploying higher qualified personnel (more than that specified by the contract) to the project, resulting in better software design, lesser rework and higher quality. Alternatively, the vendor will be willing to extend warranty beyond the contract, enhancing reliability in the developed software. Relational flexibility thus adds significant value to the client in T&M contracts by allowing the client to better manage the risk of opportunism and its effects on quality.

On the basis of the above reasoning, we propose the following two hypotheses:

Hypothesis 3: There will be a greater positive association between relational flexibility and vendor profitability in FP contracts than in T&M contracts. In effect, the contract type will moderate the relationship between relational flexibility and vendor project profitability.

Hypothesis 4: There will be a greater positive association between relational flexibility and service quality in T&M contracts than in FP contracts. In effect, the contract type will moderate the relationship between relational flexibility and service quality.

We also note the import of our arguments for the effects of relational flexibility on the alternative performance dimension in the contractual regime which does not induce higher risk exposure (i.e., effect of flexibility on quality in FP and profitability in T&M). We do not postulate that quality will suffer in FP projects or that the vendor will incur losses in T&M projects. Rather, to the extent that the vendor's attention is focused more on profitability than on quality and the client's attention focused more on quality than on vendor profitability, we will observe differentially higher benefits for relational flexibility on preferred dimensions in the appropriate contract regimes. Our objective is to assess the marginal impact of relational flexibility on outcomes, contingent on formal contract used. Figure 1 depicts our conceptual model.

## Research Methodology

The data for this paper was collected on projects completed by a leading software services firm in Bangalore, India. Its primary area of expertise is software development and maintenance of business systems. Focusing on a single vendor enables us to control for geographical and cultural variations between projects, which has been hypothesized to affect the dynamics between vendor and clients in outsourcing (Davis et al. 2006). The use of micro-level firm-specific data is also helpful in controlling for project characteristics that might not be clear in larger, industry-wide samples (Ethiraj et al. 2005). A random sample of 120 projects from the projects completed by the firm over a two-year period was identified for cross-sectional data collection. From the 120 projects identified, we were able to collect data on 105 projects; the remaining project data was not complete due to the unavailability of project managers or business unit managers because of travel and attrition. Of the final sample of 105 projects, 61 were FP contracts and 44 were T&M contracts. The sample size and ratio of FP to T&M in our sample was obtained through a random selection process and is similar to other published work (Banerjee and Duflo 2000; Ethiraj et al. 2005).

Primary data was collected through two questionnaires that were developed based on prior research in software outsourcing and governance and in discussion with business managers at the research site. The questionnaires were pre-tested by the research site's quality assurance group. One questionnaire measured project-level information on technical and project-level variables while the second questionnaire included information on the business environment around the project and the vendor-client relationship. Table 1 provides the summary statistics for the variables used in the study by contract type. Table 2 shows the correlation matrix while the Appendix shows the questionnaire items used in data collection.

We sought two sources of data for each project. The project manager provided information on the project while the business unit manager was the source for data on relational flexibility and quality. A key consideration in the context of empirical field studies is targeting the key informant to elicit information (Pinsonnealt and Kraemer 1993). Prior work in outsourcing has indicated that responsibilities for a project are split between different parties, given the business and technical domains that need to be managed (Gopalakrishnan et al. 1996). Technical matters are handled by project managers while relationship and client management is handled by the business unit manager. The business unit manager is thus in the best position to provide data on relational flexibility, from a key informant viewpoint. S/he is responsible financially for the project, understands the elements and consequences of flexibility in the project, and is also the executive liaison between the vendor firm and the client (Gopalakrishnan et al. 1996). Therefore, we collected project-level data from the project manager while quality and relational flexibility was provided by the business unit manager. Finally, we collected project-level quantitative data from company databases.

A second key variable in our analysis is project profit, measured as the difference of the revenue accruing to the project and the total costs allocated to the project. The cost of the project includes all project costs, including overhead and apportioned costs. This data was collected from company databases for each project and is used by the firm for their accounting purposes.

We measured service quality, using a set of five questionnaire items that have been tested in the software development context (Henderson and Lee 1992; Nidomolu 1995) and are concise enough to capture the relevant aspects of service quality. The items are consistent with prior work in assessing quality through surveys (Ravichandran and Rai 2000; Tiwana 2004). While quality could be collected from the customer side, the literature is clear about the difference between service quality and customer satisfaction; these are distinct though related constructs. In addition, a significant intermediating variable in the link between customer satisfaction and quality is the ease of evaluating ex post quality (Anderson and Sullivan 1993; Taylor and Baker 1994). Given the credence of good quality of software outsourcing (Nayyar 1993), evaluating ex post quality for the client is difficult. Therefore, from a face validity viewpoint, collecting service quality from the customer becomes problematic. We collected service quality data from the business unit manager (Cronbach's alpha = 0.76).

An alternative measurement for quality used in the software development context is defect rates; however, this data is only available for 46 projects in our sample. As a robustness check, we compared the quality measure to defect rate data for these projects. We use two sources of defect rate data: the number of reported defects during acceptance testing (Ethiraj et al. 2005) and the number of modification requests (MRs) (Herbsleb and Mockus 2003). Our quality measures are significantly and negatively correlated (high quality–low defects) with defect rate data, providing support for validity of our quality measure.

The contract type is a binary variable, with 0 for T&M contracts and 1 for FP contracts. Prior projects for the same client was measured by the number of projects completed before the focal. Requirements instability (Cronbach's alpha = 0.90) was measured by four questionnaire items adapted from Nidomolu (1995) and was provided by the project manager. Employee turnover (Cronbach's alpha=0.73) is often difficult to measure accurately since personnel occupy different levels of importance in a team. Therefore, rather than using the number of members leaving the team during the project, we use two questionnaire items that were filled out by the project manager, consistent with prior work (Gopal et al. 2003). Human asset specificity (Cronbach's alpha = 0.77) in the project was measured as the extent to which trained people were deployed in the project and is adapted from Lacity and Hirshheim (1993). Three questionnaire items were used in measurement and the data was provided by the project manager. Finally, client MIS experience (Cronbach's alpha = 0.77) was operationalized using four questionnaire items adapted from Lacity and Hirschheim's discussion of the client's experience, provided by the project manager.

In addition, we included the following control variables. Project size is included as a control variable and is measured as the total effort on the project (Ethiraj et al. 2005; Gopal et al. 2003). Project duration, in number of calendar days between start and finish of the project, is used as control since relational governance is more likely for longer projects, all else being equal (Dyer 1997). Team size is included as a control; larger teams are harder to manage and represent risk in the project (Guzzo and Dickson 1996). Finally, we control for project type, a binary variable denoting whether the project involved new development or reengineering of existing systems. Development projects are coded 0 and reengineering projects are coded 1.

As a first step, we established construct and discriminant validity of our measures. The items pertaining to the perceptual variables were subjected to a factor analysis with varimax rotation, shown in Table 3. The items loaded well on underlying constructs with factor loadings of over 0.60, with cross-loadings less than 0.35, indicating acceptable discriminant validity (Hair et al. 1998). In addition, in exploratory factor analysis performed separately on each construct, the set of items for the construct loaded well on one underlying factor, with the first factor capturing between 55 percent and 75 percent of total variance. We also used the internal consistency reliability indicator (Venkatraman 1989), which captures the ratio of the latent construct variance to the construct plus error variance for our constructs. Venkatraman (1989) has indicated that composite reliability of 0.50 is acceptable (i.e., at least 50 percent of the measured variance is captured by the construct). As shown in the Appendix, all of the constructs display composite reliabilities of over 0.50. The variables were created by averaging the items pertaining to each construct for subsequent use.

## Data Analysis

We used a multi-pronged analytic strategy to test our hypotheses. Recall that our hypotheses pertain to, first, a model to explain observed relational flexibility and, second, the interactive effects of relational flexibility and contract type on project performance (i.e., quality and profitability). We are, therefore, faced with two endogenous variables—relational flexibility and performance (quality and profitability)—in addition to contract type, which has been treated as endogenous in much of the literature (Gopal et al. 2003). Therefore, using standard OLS raises questions of validity and robustness as OLS coefficients may be biased and/or inefficient. In addition, we face the challenge of estimating endogenous interaction terms. To our knowledge, no clear method exists for addressing endogenous interactions in the literature. Therefore, we use a set of diverse analytical tests to triangulate on the relationships of interest. Each analytical technique is associated with a set of assumptions and caveats; we outline them as we develop the analysis in stages. We then summarize the broad set of results in the discussion section. $^{5}$

We represent the three main constructs succinctly in the following system of equations:

$$
\text { Contract   type } C = f (X _ {c}, R F) + e 1\tag{1}
$$

$$
\text { Relational   Flexibility } R F = f \left(X _ {R F}, C\right) + e 2\tag{2}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{c} \text {Performance (Profitability / Quality) P =} \\ f (X _ {P}, R G, C, R G \times C) + e 3 \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

Where $X_{c}$ represents the set of covariates in the contract choice model, $X_{RF}$ represents the covariates in the model for relational flexibility, and $X_{P}$ represents the covariates in the performance equations. The error terms are represented as e1, e2, and e3, respectively. For the purposes of our hypotheses, we focus attention on equations (2) and (3), shown below in linear form.

$RF = f$ (Effort, Duration, Team Size, Project Type, Prior Projects, Requirements Instability, Employee Turnover, Human Asset Specificity, Client MIS Experience, Contract) + e2

(4)

Profit = f (Effort, Duration, Team Size, Project Type,
Prior Projects, Requirements Instability,
Employee Turnover, Human Asset Specificity,
Client MIS Experience, Contract, RF, $RF \times Contract$ ) + e3'

(5)

Quality = f (Effort, Duration, Team Size, Project Type, Prior Projects, Requirements Instability, Employee Turnover, Human Asset Specificity, Client MIS Experience, Contract, RF, RF × Contract) + e3"

(6)

As a start, we estimated equations (4), (5), and (6) using OLS, presented in the first columns of Tables 4, 5, and 6. The hypothesized variables and their coefficients are in bold in the tables. Before estimating these equations, we confirmed the assumptions of the OLS model; the Durbin-Watson statistic for autocorrelation, close to 2 in all three equations, indicated the absence of autocorrelation in the residuals of each individual regression (Greene 1997). The residuals were tested using the Shapiro-Wilks test, indicating no violation of the normality assumption. We tested for heteroskedasticity in each equation using the Breusch-Pagan test, which failed to reject the null of homoskedasticity in each equation. Multicollinearity testing found variance inflation factors under 2 and condition indices under the threshold value of 15 (Belsley et al. 1980).

Since Hypotheses 3 and 4 postulate a moderation model which focuses attention on the interaction term between contract and relational flexibility, we follow Baron and Kenny's (1986) guidelines to test these hypotheses. The results from the moderation model for profit and quality, based on OLS, are shown in the first two columns of Table 5 and 6, respectively. The first column estimates the base model while the second column contains the added interaction term $RF \times Contract$ . In order to reduce collinearity from the interaction variable, all variables in the model for profit and quality were centered.

As noted above, the OLS results are likely biased due to the endogeneity of the contract type, performance variables and relational flexibility. Therefore, we use a system of equations approach to estimate the coefficients in the contract equation (equation (1)), the relational flexibility equation (equation (4)), and performance equation (equations (5) and (6) respectively) simultaneously. Since a contract choice model is not the focus of this paper, we adapt the contract choice model estimated in Gopal et al. (2003), who use a probit specification to model the choice between FP and T&M contracts. Since the contract choice is a nonlinear model, we use nonlinear three-stage least squares (n3SLS) to estimate equations (1), (4), and (5) (or (6)) (Wooldridge 2002). Exclusion restrictions require that the number of excluded exogenous variables from the equation must equal or be greater than the number of included endogenous variables for the linear case (Wooldridge 2002, p. 215); the system of equations shown above meets these conditions. The results from the 3SLS are in the first column of Table 7 for the contract choice model, the second column of Table 4 for relational flexibility, and column 3 in Tables 5 and 6 for profitability and quality.

## Endogenous Interaction Terms: Approaches to Estimation

While 3SLS provides an estimate of the direct effects of contract type and relational flexibility on performance, it cannot be used to estimate the impact of the interaction term RF × Contract. This is because both C and RF are endogenous, which renders their interaction also endogenous. There is no systematic or widely accepted technique for estimating endogenous interactions (Harrison 2008b). In some cases, an interaction of an endogenous and an exogenous variable may be estimated with some precision; however, these econometric properties do not extend to situations where both interacting variables are endogenous, as we have here.

One possible option to estimating the endogenous interaction effect is to treat the interaction term itself as a new endogenous variable; Wooldridge (2002, p. 233) suggests this as an option when the interaction term is a nonlinear function of either one endogenous variable or a function of two endogenous variables, under some strict rank conditions. In our case, this would imply estimating an additional equation of the following sort:

$$
“ R F \times C ” = f (X _ {R F x C}) + e\tag{7}
$$

Where $X_{RFxC}$ pertains to covariates of the “interaction” variable. This interaction variable could then be introduced into the performance equation to estimate its effect. However, this is theoretically problematic for two reasons. First, there is little validity to such an artificial construct to justify a separate equation. Finding a set of covariates to predict such an equation would require theoretical justification. Second, identification would become an issue since it would be necessary to find new variables to meet the exclusion conditions required for the system of equations. We have not been able to locate any empirical papers that have used this approach to modeling interactions between two endogenous variables.

In light of these issues, we use a hybrid approach to estimate the impact of the interaction effect of contract and relational flexibility on performance. We first assume exogeneity of contract type and treat relational flexibility as endogenous; this allows us to use a standard 2SLS analysis as well as the hat approach from above (since relational flexibility is postulated as a linear model). Subsequently, we endogenize contract type, exogenize relational flexibility.and use a treatment effects and switching regression model to estimate the interaction. These are described in detail next.

## Endogenizing Relational Flexibility

We start with assuming that relational flexibility is endogenous and contract type is exogenous. This allows us to temporarily disregard the contract choice function and estimate just the flexibility and performance equations using 2SLS. In this analysis, relational flexibility is estimated in the first stage and the respective performance equations are estimated in the second stage. Identification requires the inclusion of instruments in the equation for flexibility that are absent in the second-stage models (Greene 1997). Therefore, we use three instruments in addition to the existing variables in the flexibility model: availability of functional requirements for the project (binary), client size (number of employees), and the extent of financial risk in the project relative to the vendor's existing projects (measured on a 5-point Likert scale). Even in the presence of some correlation between the instruments and the second-stage variables (quality and profit), 2SLS provides efficient and unbiased coefficient estimates (Greene 1997). The 2SLS results for quality and profitability are shown in column 4 of Tables 5 and 6, respectively. They provide a test for the stability of the direct effect coefficients for profitability and quality, but do not allow estimation of the interaction. Following Harrison (2008b), we then use the hat or non-interacted 2SLS approach, which provides more unbiased results in small samples to estimate the interaction term of flexibility and contract (i.e., linear endogenous term interacted with a categorical moderator, as described by Harrison (2008b)). We use the model of relational flexibility (equation (4)) to create the predicted values $RF_{hat}$ and estimate the following using a two-step procedure:

Profit = f (Effort, Duration, Team Size, Project Type, Prior Projects, Requirements Instability, Employee Turnover, Human Asset Specificity, Client MIS Experience, Contract, Rel Flex, $RF_{hat} \times Contract$ ) (8)

Quality = f (Effort, Duration, Team Size, Project Type, Prior Projects, Requirements Instability, Employee Turnover, Human Asset Specificity, Client MIS Experience, Contract, Rel Flex, $RF_{hat} \times Contract$ ) (9)

The results from this analysis are shown in column 5 of Table 5 and 6 for profit and quality respectively. As shown in Tables 5 and 6, the interaction effect is similar in sign and magnitude to the OLS results discussed earlier, while individual coefficients differ.

## Endogenizing Contract Choice

We then consider the situation where the contract variable is endogenous. The hat approach described above would not be feasible since contract choice is nonlinear; replacing the true values with fitted values leads to inconsistent estimates (Wooldridge 2002). Therefore, we first use the treatments effects model, where the contract is treated as endogenous but flexibility is assumed exogenous. The treatment effects model is used to estimate the effect of an endogenous treatment on a sample (Maddala 1983) and involves estimating a first-stage contract choice probit model and calculating the inverse Mills ratios (lambdas), which are then introduced into the flexibility and performance equations as an independent variable. We use the contract choice model estimated earlier to calculate the inverse Mills ratios. Since the coefficient of relational flexibility is insignificant in the contract model reported in the first column of Table 7, we omit it from the contract choice model here to retain consistency with the prior work (Gopal et al. 2003). The treatment effects results are shown in column 3 of Table 4 for flexibility and column 6 of Tables 5 and 6 for profitability and quality. Again, this analysis does not provide the interaction term but permits examining the robustness of the direct effects, in comparison to the OLS results. As seen in Tables 5 and 6, the results of the treatment effects are qualitatively similar to those from OLS.

Finally, an alternative method to estimate the interaction effect in the presence of contract endogeneity is to use a switching regression framework, wherein two separate contractual regimes are estimated, one each for FP and T&M contracts (Gopal and Sivaramakrishnan 2008):

$$
\begin{array}{c} \text {Performance (Profitability / Quality) P_{FP}} = \\ f \left(X _ {P, F P}, R F _ {F P}\right) + e _ {F P} \end{array}\tag{10}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{c} \text {Performance (Profitability / Quality) P_{TM} =} \\ f \left(X _ {P, T M}, R F _ {T M}\right) + e _ {T M} \end{array}\tag{11}
$$

In this case, the interaction effect of contract and relational flexibility is assessed using the coefficient of the RF variable in each contractual regime. This analysis provides a reasonable estimate of the interaction effect in the presence of contract endogeneity, as shown in Table 8 for profitability and quality respectively. In order to account for the endogeneity of flexibility, we can replace the RF variable in equations (10) and (11) with fitted values of RF (RF $_{hat}$ ) from equation (4); the results are very similar to those in Table 8 and are available on request from the authors.

## Results

As a first step, we conduct difference of means t-tests on the variables across contract types to understand the trends in the data (Table 1). FP projects have larger teams, more asset-specific human resources, and tend to be repeat projects for the same client, compared to T&M projects (p < 0.05). These results are consistent with prior research on software outsourcing (Ethiraj et al. 2005). In addition, quality is higher in FP than T&M, as shown in prior work (Gopal and Koka 2010). We note that the use of relational flexibility is similar across contract types, as is requirements instability, client MIS experience, and turnover. These results suggest that certain elements of projects that cause exchange hazards to increase appear to be systematic across the sample and not limited to one contract type. Therefore, it is unlikely that contracts are chosen purely on the basis of risks in the specific project. There are differences in the extent to which vendor and client decisions are taken across contract types (for instance, with asset specificity) which will have implications for the regression analysis, discussed next.

## Results of the Relational Flexibility Model

Table 4 shows the results of our model of relational flexibility and includes OLS, nonlinear 3SLS, and treatment effects models. Both OLS and the treatments effects model provide significant F-statistics and high $R^{2}$ values, indicating model strength. In addition, coefficients across the three models suggest some consistency in the sign and magnitude of the coefficients obtained across all three analyses. Hypothesis 1a pertains to the effect of requirements instability on relational flexibility and is strongly supported across all three models, with similar coefficients. Similarly, Hypothesis 1b addresses the role of employee turnover on relational flexibility and is strongly significant across all three models. Hypothesis 1c, with respect to asset specificity, shows consistent results, with the OLS and 3SLS results highly significant (OLS = 0.258, p < 0.05; 3SLS = 0.188, p < 0.05) but the Heckman result is less significant (0.223, p < 0.10), indicating less robust effects. In summary, the results show that flexibility is used in projects when exchange hazards are high.

Hypothesis 2a argued that prior interactions between client and vendor would increase the observation of relational flexibility; this is supported in the results across all three analyses. As trust can be built through previous interactions, current exchanges feature relational norms that may not be present in early interactions with the client. Similarly, client MIS experience is significantly associated with relational flexibility, supporting Hypothesis 2b. Client MIS experience represents an interesting variable in that an argument for a negative effect could also be made (i.e., experienced clients may choose to not allow flexibility but rather enforce contractual parameters rigorously, based on their superior capabilities). This would imply that experienced clients would forego relational flexibility in favor of formal governance. There are two reasons for why this may not be true here. The first reason emerges from relational theory: complex economic activities necessitate flexibility and, hence, adhering to strict formal governance actually backfires (Ring and Van de Ven 1992). Therefore, experienced clients would likely adopt a more flexible approach to the project itself but maintain monitoring and control, based on their capabilities. Second, the contract choice model shows that client MIS experience increases the probability of a FP contract (Gopal et al. 2003; Table 7 here), which pushes more risk onto the vendor. Therefore, the “portfolio” of formal and relational governance applied by experienced clients would entail greater risk exposure for the vendor through the FP contract, augmented with relational flexibility when necessary, managed through the experienced client MIS department. This combination of formal and relational governance pays dividends, as observed in the beneficial impact of both variables on quality.

It is noteworthy that the contract type does not affect the use of relational flexibility in this model. While counterintuitive at first blush, this follows from prior work on relational governance which argues that as exchange hazards increase in a project, the use of relational governance will increase. The contract type would therefore only influence relational flexibility if the contract type was chosen based solely on exchange hazards in the project. However, as Gopal et al. (2003) and Banerjee and Duflo (2000) show, bargaining power, prior relationships, reputation, and the competitive environment also influence contract choice, which suggests that the correlation between exchange hazards and contract type is not perfect. This is borne out in our sample: exchange hazards are not significantly different across contract types, based on the difference of means t-test reported above. Therefore, it is not surprising that contract is insignificant in predicting relational flexibility, especially when exchange hazards are accounted for in the regression.

## Discussion and Implications

In this study, we examined how and when relational governance, operationalized as flexibility, provides benefits to exchange partners in the presence of formal contracts. We argued for a more nuanced understanding of the complementarity between formal and relational governance by arguing, first, that relational benefits are contingent on how risk is apportioned by the contract and, second, that these benefits are higher, on the margin, on those dimensions that are of key interest to the risk-exposed party. We find strong support for our hypotheses of asymmetric benefits from relational flexibility to different contracting parties in an outsourcing relationship. Much of the literature, as noted earlier, has assumed symmetric benefits from relational governance, tested at the exchange level. As we show, this assumption of symmetric benefits warrants examination. When we limit our analysis to the direct effect models, we observe the direct and positive effect of flexibility, consistent with existing research. However, the interaction models and the resulting graphs presented in Figures 2 and 3 show evidence of the asymmetric benefits accruing to different performance dimensions across the contracts. It is on examination of the split-sample results (Table 8) that we notice much of the direct effect of relational flexibility is contingent on contract type. Our findings highlight the need to establish risk exposure first, and then examine the effects of flexibility on performance contingent on risk exposure.

Our analysis also highlights the implications of relational governance for the performance dimensions of interest. Since relational governance provides benefits by mitigating risk for the risk-exposed party, it perforce differentially affects the performance dimension that is most likely to be affected by the materialization of risk for the risk-exposed party. As a corollary of this reasoning, our analysis indicates that if a party experiences little or no risk even in a complex transaction, there is little value in relational flexibility on the relevant dimensions of performance for that party. Our study highlights the need to incorporate a more nuanced, limited, and contingent view of relational governance and its benefits in extant theory, in contrast to the more expansive view of relational governance that predicts value for all partners to an exchange.

These findings, however, raise an important question: if relational governance benefits mostly the party with higher risk, why does the other party acquiesce to its use in the relationship? As the consensus in the literature suggests, the enactment of relational governance is driven by uncertainty and exchange hazards. As noted earlier, the contract is a risk-partitioning mechanism. Therefore, while one party to the exchange is exposed to higher risk by the contract, the other party is not riskless. For instance, in FP contracts, where the vendor faces the greater part of risk in the project, the client is not riskless. The client faces the possibility of vendor opportunism. Literature suggests that when the vendor's profitability is affected by risk, especially in complex activities with low observability, the vendor has a strong incentive to shirk on quality (Baker 1992), which is the client's performance dimension of importance. In order to reduce the risk of opportunism by the vendor through shirking on quality, we suggest that the client acquiesces in relational flexibility within the exchange in order to allow the vendor to protect its profitability while maintaining the requisite quality for the client. This explains a significant effect on profitability in the FP regime but no significant benefits on quality from relational governance.

Similarly, in T&M contracts, the vendor might be motivated to extend flexibility to the client for two reasons. First, all projects carry within them the threat of client termination (Lacity and Hirshheim 1998). Since the client is risk-exposed in T&M contracts, signs of inflexibility by the vendor can be seen as evidence of lack of commitment to the project (Gulati 1995). Thus, project termination is always a credible threat if relational governance is not observed on the project. Second, relational governance is associated with repeat business, all else being equal (Artz and Brush 2000; Heide and John 1990; Poppo and Zenger 2000). Investments in relational governance, therefore, may pay off for the vendor in future business rather than direct value in the current project. In both cases, vendor investment in relational governance will be observed in T&M contracts. While we recognize that these arguments are speculative, they also point to an agenda for more research. What happens when relational governance is not used under high exchange hazards? Do clients get lower quality in FP contracts under such conditions? Correspondingly, will vendor profits be negatively affected in T&M contracts without relational flexibility? In addition, our arguments for T&M highlight the need to examine these issues longitudinally: do relational norms enhance the payoff from relational governance for different dimensions of performance?

In addition to the theoretical contributions, our paper also makes a methodological contribution. We use a triangulating approach to examine the endogenous interactions of relational flexibility and contract type on project outcomes. As discussed above, there is little guidance on how endogenous interactions should be estimated; we accordingly use a variety of analyses to establish the relationships of interest in this paper. Clearly, while these analyses provide evidence for the stability of our results, the impact of endogeneity (and the resulting biases) cannot be eradicated through econometrics alone. There are two possible cures to this that we hope will be addressed in future work. First, the development of appropriate statistical estimators will allow robust estimation of endogenous interaction terms (Harrison 2008b). A more suitable solution would be access to panel data on variables such as relational governance, outcomes, and contracts on repeated interactions between vendors and clients. This empirical strategy, though challenging, would provide clearer evidence of the interrelationships between these constructs.

## Limitations

We recognize that our analysis is subject to some limitations. First, we use data from one vendor firm; while this helps control some heterogeneity, it nevertheless limits generalizability. Second, our sample of 105 projects is small. However, the sample is similar to other papers like Carson et al. (2006), Ethiraj et al. (2005), and Goo et al. (2009). Our dataset additionally provides objective measures and more fine-grained data that provides greater veracity to our analysis. Third, our measure of contract type is limited to the two extreme forms of contracts. However, new and hybrid forms of contracts can easily be devised for different settings. To the extent that risk-sharing is monotonically related to these contractual forms, our reasoning would apply to hybrid contracts as well. Fourth, our measure of quality is perceptual and collected from the vendor. This is not unique to our paper (Nidumolu 1995; Ravichandran and Rai 2000) yet represents a limitation. Fifth, our theoretical arguments were based on the observed manifestation of relational governance in the specific exchange we study. Accordingly, we focused on relational flexibility as a critical dimension of relational governance. We recognize there are other forms of relational governance mechanisms such as joint action, trust, reciprocity, and forbearance that have been studied in the literature. While our theoretical arguments should extend to the instantiations of these other mechanisms of relational governance, more empirical work is needed to establish the asymmetric nature of these benefits in other contexts.

## Managerial Implications

Our work in this paper has several implications for managerial practice in outsourcing. Our work informs managers about the relative value of relational governance in settings where contracting parties bear risks. While conventional theory would suggest that relational mechanisms like flexibility, although costly to invest in, provide returns in terms of better exchange outcomes, our work shows that these benefits only materialize when the party bears risk. Therefore, for managers who have risk protection through the contract, a more nuanced calculus of costs and benefits from relational governance is called for. This does not imply that managers in risk-protected regimes should ignore relational governance; indeed, this would be myopic. Rather, our work suggests that managers should consider the impact of relational governance on the alternative performance dimension of interest to the other party in their calculus. This realization of asymmetric benefits can be leveraged in two ways. First, vendor and client managers can use our work to inform their contracting partners of the true value of relational norms. In fact, this sharing of knowledge could lead to closer relationships between clients and vendors. Second, as managers gain a better understanding of what the performance implications are for relational governance, it might be possible to endogenously change behavior to focus on the performance dimension that is not affected. For instance, vendor managers in T&M contracts may be able to find ways to enhance their profitability through relational governance instead of allowing the marginal value to only accrue on quality. In either contractual regime, a better informed manager should be able to make better judgments on project-related decisions.

As outsourcing evolves, there is a growing sense that both quality and cost savings will drive competitive dynamics in the vendor market (Couto et al. 2006). In addition, clients are moving from being contracting partners on discrete processes to long-term relationships with sourcing partners based on strategic alliances (Ethiraj et al. 2005; Gurbaxani 2007). In such circumstances, it is important for managers to understand the differences between which relational mechanisms provide value and, more importantly, to understand which performance dimensions are likely to reflect these benefits. As quality becomes more important and vendor profitability comes under competitive pressures (Banerjee and Duflo 2000), the trade-offs inherent in maintaining relational norms in a long-term alliance between risk, formal contracts, and relational norms will necessitate managerial thinking on the margin. Our work in this paper informs this analysis: managers should be able to better understand risk exposure and where to deploy relational norms in complex exchanges. More importantly, managers should be able to decide when to forgo such benefits so that their partners may enjoy, on the margin, the asymmetric benefits of relational norms in the interest of long-term returns. We hope our work in this paper leads to more work that provides managers with these nuanced tools for better decision-making in outsourcing engagements.
