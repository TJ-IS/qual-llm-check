## A B S T R A C T

This study examines the relationship between the background of an organization’s top information systems executive or chief information oficer (CIO) and what a CIO does. It provides a somewhat diferent picture of the much discussed relative merits of a CIO’s background. Findings indicate that a technical background has little relationship to what the CIO does once they become CIO. Where they are hired from, however, does relate to reporting line, business focus, and how and with whom they spend their time once hired. These findings will enable more efective CIO hiring and development activities, and inform future research

## 1. Introduction

A great deal has been written about the chief information systems (IS) executive or CIO<sup>1</sup> in organizations. Much of this has focused on their titles, roles, and where they reside in the organization’s structure [1–7]. These writings have informed our collective knowledge and helped shape our view of what an organization’s CIO does or should do, and how that role has evolved. CIOs today are often expected to balance ensuring information technology (IT)<sup>2</sup> availability, enacting technology strategy and innovation, and helping shape organizational strategy and transformation [7,8].

CIO background is another dimension of this discussion, particularly the relative merits of hiring these executives from outside the organization versus promoting them from within and of appointing CIOs who do not have a traditional IT background [9,10]. Hiring from outside is thought to bring new perspectives and ideas to the organization, and it may be done when radical change in an organization’s IT function is needed. On the other hand, promoting from within has the probable advantage of bringing someone into leadership who knows the company and its industry. A CIO who has a background from a functional area outside of IT may be hired to bring a more holistic, strategic, or transformational perspective to the C-suite. This discussion is generally not found for other C-level executive roles [11]. For example, it is rare to find a chief financial oficer with limited to no finance/accounting background. There is little discussion about whether the chief marketing oficer has a role in organizational strategy and direction. This anomaly in choosing top IS executives has given rise to concern for the IS professional career, the IS function, and the impact of IS on the organization [11].

Much of the literature about the source and background of a CIO, however, is descriptive or anecdotal. There is little empirical evidence about where the actual diferences lie or if there are significant diferences based on background [6,12]. Notable exceptions are Banker et al. [9] and Carter et al. [10]. They indicate that there is no one best background or reporting structure for a successful CIO. These researchers suggest that a good match between the organization’s IT focus and the CIO’s skills is key to CIO success. However, regardless where a CIO is hired from, if a major IS initiative fails, the blame is often placed on the CIO and the organization may seek a very diferent type of CIO in replacement [13]. There is more involuntary turnover of CIOs than other C-suite executives in many organizations [14], which may reflect an underlying misunderstanding of the CIO role and the transition into the role – regardless of background [13]. Does background matter? If yes, then how, and if not, then what does? The motivation for this study is to examine the role of a CIO’s background in a context that allows a comparison of the role across CIOs regardless of specific job assign ments.

The overarching research question addressed in this study is: How is a CIO’s background related to the way he/she approaches the job of being a CIO? Specifically, the study examines the relationship between CIO background (i.e., technical/nontechnical and internal/external hire) and to whom they report, with whom they spend their time, and at what they spend their time doing. It also examines the extent of CIO focus on the business and access to top management. These are examined through the proposal and testing of five sets of hypotheses related to the following specific research questions:

RQ1. To what extent does the CIO’s background in terms of nontechnical versus technical and whether he/she is hired from inside or outside the organization influence whether the CIO is more likely to report to the CEO or CFO?

R2Q. To what extent does the CIO’s background and reporting line influence whether he/she spends more time with non-IT-related or ITrelated stakeholders?

RQ3. To what extent does the CIO’s background and reporting line influence whether he/she is more likely to be engaged in business-related or IT-related activities?

RQ4. To what extent does the CIO’s background and reporting line influence whether he/she has a greater business focus?

RQ5. To what extent does a greater level of CIO business focus influence whether he/she spends more time on business-related activities and with non-IT-related stakeholders?

Findings provide deeper empirical insight into the relationship of these influences and a CIO’s job. They paint a picture of what the CIO job looks like in light of where he/she came from and provides insights that may enable more efective CIO hiring and development activities, as well as help those who want to become a CIO to better understand how to best develop and position themselves as their career progresses.

## 2. Literature review and hypothesis building

In this section, we provide literature support for five constructs and the proposed relationships among them. The constructs are CIO background (technical/nontechnical and hired from outside/inside the organization), CIO reporting line (CEO, CFO), CIO stakeholder interaction (primarily non-IT-related stakeholders/IT-related stakeholders), CIO activities (primarily business related/IT related), and CIO business focus. We propose relationships between CIO background and each of the others, including CIO reporting line. In addition, we propose re lationships between CIO reporting line and CIO stakeholder interaction, CIO activities, and CIO business focus. Finally, we propose that there is a relationship between CIO business focus and CIO stakeholder inter action and between CIO business focus and CIO activities.

## 2.1. CIO background and CIO reporting line

Although much has been written about the relative merits of where CIOs originate and their functional backgrounds, there is little agreement about which, if any, are best [10,15]. Historically, the position of CIO is one of the most dificult to fill, and perhaps one of the most controversial, partly due to the varying role of IT in organizations [6]. It is also one of the most dificult and demanding [16,17]. There are many pathways to the CIO position including technical or nontechnical backgrounds, and promotion from within the organization or hiring from outside. One view is that the role of CIO entails a transition across positions and roles, regardless of background, and that background is not a predictor of successful transition [6].

The lack of consensus seems to revolve around two basic arguments that are partly supported empirically and partly argued anecdotally. One argument, for example, is about what the CIO job is or should be, and another is about how much power or influence in the organization the CIO has or should have. Anecdotally, one perspective is that the CIO must have a strong technical background to do the job, and that the CIO should hold a position equal to any other C-suite executive. This is counter to many CIO positions in organizations [16–18]. Empirically, research indicates that CIOs with technical backgrounds may have more technical credibility than those with a nontechnical background, which is advantageous for managing internal IT resources and personnel, and that these technically experienced CIOs may have less of a strategic role in the organization and a lower reporting line with less access to top management [10].

Thus, some argue that a CIO’s background is related to the CIO reporting line in the organization. Some research, however, suggests that for organizations to best leverage IT, the CIO reporting line should be driven by the strategic posturing of the organization and that no one reporting line option is best for all firms [9]. For example, findings suggest that CIOs who report to the CEO are expected to be more strategic and transformational, and those who report to the CFO are expected to be more focused on reducing IT cost and improving eficiency [19,20]. Research also indicates that CIOs who report to the CEO, or who otherwise are able to leverage their access to top management, may have stronger formal power and thus are more likely to participate in strategic activities [9,10]. Lack of CIO access or influence has implications beyond the CIO and the functioning of the IS area. Rather, it may result in the organization being unable to make the informed strategic or operational decisions that increase its competitiveness. This is not to say, however, that CIOs with more technical backgrounds are not able to successfully influence top management [21]. It may simply require them to draw upon a diferent set of strengths or to learn a diferent set of skills or abilities to better communicate their vision to other key actors in the organization [22].

In addition, the type of CIO is often dictated by the needs of the organization. In an organization where IT is viewed as a support function to the rest of the organization, a functional CIO with a strong technical background may be more appropriate [23,24]. Here, the CIO role may focus more on operational efficiency and cost control. and thus, the CIO may report directly to the CFO. On the other hand, in an organization where IT is viewed as adding strategic value to the organization, the CIO may need a strong business background to efectively handle ambiguity and react to a rapidly changing business environment [24]. Indeed, some research indicates that CIOs with a stronger business background are paid more than those who lack this experience [25]. Thus, CIO reporting relationship may not only reflect what they are expected to bring to the table, but may also be tied to CIO background. Regardless of background, CIOs must find their path to success once they arrive in their new role [2,8,13], and this may be dictated more by where they find themselves than where they were before [5,12].

Another facet of a CIO’s background is whether he/she is hired from outside the organization or is hired from within. This may be driven by what the CIO is expected to do in their position [13]. Examples of expectations include startup CIOs, turnaround CIOs, realignment CIOs, and success sustaining CIOs. A startup CIO is expected to build an IT organization and create an IT infrastructure. The turnaround CIO is expected to take an IT organization that is struggling or in trouble and reset the direction. The realignment CIO, while not as drastic as the turnaround CIO’s job, is focused on realigning an IT organization with perhaps a changing business. Finally, the success sustaining CIO is expected to maintain and preserve a well-run IT organization. Not surprisingly, when the job involves a turnaround or realignment of IT, the new CIO often comes from outside the organization and reports to the CEO. However, when the job is one of sustaining and moving forward a well-aligned IT organization, the new CIO often comes from inside the organization and likely reports to the CFO [13]. ## 2.2. CIO stakeholder interaction and CIO activities

CIOs interact with a variety of people both inside and outside the organization, regardless of their reporting line [3,5,16–18]. They in teract with executives, senior managers, technologists, resource allo cators, leaders, liaisons, strategists, and general decision makers. In addition to relationships within their internal IT function, CIOs must also develop relationships with customers and suppliers outside of the business and their IT leadership [3,5,10,16–18]. Research suggests that to be successful CIOs must build internal business relationships and allocate their time to activities that enable them to leverage their re porting line and access to top management [10]. Thus, CIOs must decide how best to allocate their time among diferent stakeholders. CIOs with a primarily business background may be more strategic, transformational, and business-focused, and thus more prone to look for opportunities to use IT to impact performance. These CIOs are business strategists seeking to leverage technology for competitive advantage by using their knowledge of the market, customers, and the competition [10]. Research has found that the relationship between IS strategic leadership and the quality of an organization's IS is related to the vision for IS at the top, and that the quality of IS is significantly related to the relationship between IS strategic leadership and the benefits an organization realizes from IS [26]. This provides further support for the importance of a CIO who can leverage business knowledge in their management of organizational information systems.

Furthermore, CIOs that are hired from the outside are often brough in to bring about a turnaround or realignment within the IT organization. As such, their main focus is to address issues within IT organizations that are perceived to be struggling or failing. Thus, they often report to the CEO and spend a considerable amount of time interacting with other C-level executives working across functional areas to be successful [13]. CIOs that come from within the organization are often tasked to execute a sustaining strategy. The primary focus of these CIOs is on maintaining solid IT performance. This internal IT focus, leads them to work more with other IT management and staf [13]. ## 3. Method and data analysis

This research study was conducted in the positivist framework. This reflects an objective view of reality in which variables are quantified and testable relationships among them are specified a priori. In this approach, researchers view the phenomenon of interest as consisting of order, and thus they seek to identify patterns of ordered events or behaviors [28]. This indicates using an objective approach to data col lection and interpretation. In keeping with this framework, a quanti tative survey methodology was used to collect data that were then analyzed using quantitative statistical procedures. The data for this study were collected as part of the larger Society for Information Management’s (SIM) IT Trends Study [17]. The questionnaire was developed through a review of the literature, previous SIM IT Trends studies, and a multi-round Delphi method [4,5,16–18]. The Delph panel, a characteristic of these SIM studies since their inception in the late 1970s, consisted of academic researchers and SIM-member IT practitioners. The questionnaire was modified after each Delphi round. The final online questionnaire was distributed by emailing a personal link to 5332 SIM members across the U.S. during the spring of 2016 and asking them to complete the survey. The email addresses were obtained from the U.S.-based SIM membership list. One follow-up email was sent for each of nine weeks after the initial email encouraging them to respond. As an incentive, individuals were provided a personal and confidential benchmark report generated from the complete study that compared their responses to the aggregate responses. In addition, a SIM Chapter Challenge Contest was held that awarded cash prizes to the chapters with the highest response rates. A total of 1213 members completed the questionnaire, including the 490 CIOs (i.e., highestranking IT person in an organization, regardless of title) whose responses are utilized in this research.

## 3.1. CIO background and CIO reporting line

The CIOs in this study were largely hired from outside the organization (73.6 %) and held an IT position when they were hired (82.4 %) (Table 3). This is consistent with other research about CIO transitions across roles and organizations [6]. Over 60 % of those hired from another organization were hired from a diferent industry.

These CIOs largely report to the CEO (46.3 %), nearly 29 % report to the CFO, while less than half report to the COO/CAO (Fig. 1). Because of the low percentages of CIOs reporting to the Board/Board Member or Business Unit Executives, these were combined into COO/other; and thus, for the statistical assessments three categories of reporting line were used – CEO, CFO, and COO/other.

Logistic regression was used to examine the relationships between reporting line and (1) last job IT or Non-IT and (2) hiring from within the organization or external to the organization within the same industry or external to the organization from a diferent industry. A separate analysis was used for CEO and CFO. No other variables were included in these models. While other modeling tools could have been used, relationships between binary dependent variables are best addressed by logistic re gression [29] to overcome issues encountered with linear regression. The logistic procedure assumes a linear relationship between the predictor variables and the log odds. The listwise deletion procedure for missing data was used to ensure that only complete observations were used for analysis. Note that H1c is about external hires, in general because there is little to no prior research to support hypothesizing about differences based on whether the hire was within the same in dustry or a diferent industry. However, because this is exploratory research, the two types of external hires were included in the analysis to better explicate where diferences lie, if indeed there are diferences based on this distinction. These breakouts are used throughout the data analysis to provide deeper insight into the phenomenon of interest.

Findings for the tests of H1a-H1d are presented in Table 4a. There were no significant diferences in any of the analyses for reporting line based on whether the CIO’s background was technical or nontechnical. Thus, H1a and H1b were not supported. Findings do, however support H1c and H1d, and deeper assessment of where these diferences lie is provided in Table 4b as well as in Figs. 2 and 3. The beta coeficients are reported using the efect parameterization scheme, which is the default for the logistic procedure in SAS Institute Inc. software. This coding scheme allows for a categorical predictor’s efect to be compared to the average efect across the categories. Table 4b illustrates that the efect of an internal CIO hire is significant and negative (Chi-square = 15.07, p = .0001), and that the efect of an external CIO hire from the same industry is significant and positive (Chi-square = 11.89, p = .0006) in reporting to the CEO. However, the signs of these efects are reversed in reporting to the CFO. That is, the efect of an internal CIO hire is sig nificant and positive (Chi-square = 7.11, p = .0077) and that the efect of an external CIO hire from the same industry is significant and negative (Chi-square = 4.78, p = .0287) in reporting to the CFO. As will be discussed, the significance of these positive and negative efects supports hypotheses H1c and H1d.

CIO background had no significant bearing on the percentage of time the CIO spends with any stakeholder group. Whether a CIO had an IT or non-IT background, and regardless of whether they were hired internally or from outside the organization, had no statistically sig nificant relationship (based on individual Fs and their p-values) with the percent of time they interacted with any of the stakeholder groups. Thus, neither H2a nor H2b were supported.

Reporting line is significantly related to the percentage of time CIOs spend with some of the stakeholder groups. Thus, H2c and H2d are partially supported. Tukey’s Honestly Significant Diference (HSD) test was used to further assess these statistically significant relationships. This is a conservative test that examines all pairwise diferences among the independent variables and controls for Type I errors. It helps to determine the diference in means among the variables in the ANOVA to better understand exactly where the diferences lie. For example, the reporting line is significantly related to the time spent with stakeholders, but neither the model F nor the individual F values indicate which reporting line spends more time with which stakeholder group. For clarity and flow, we do not report each of the individual Tukey results. Rather, we provide the findings and discussion of them.

Findings from the Tukey HSD tests indicate that CIOs who report to the CEO spend more time with other C-level executives. The literature indicates that these CIOs are more focused on business strategy, thus it is not surprising that they spend more time with other C-level managers whose jobs are also likely focused in this way. Those who report to the CFO spend more time with IT colleagues outside of the organization. The literature suggests that the latter are likely to be IT-value- or costfocused. Thus, perhaps they look to their peers for best practice discussions, collaborations, and corroborations. Overall, however, these findings suggest that regardless of where the CIO originates and whether they come from IT backgrounds, once they are in the job, they largely allocate their time to stakeholders in the same way.

Time spent with a few of the stakeholder groups was significantly related to the control variables. Tukey’s HSD test results indicate that CIOs with a larger number of IT employees spend more time with their IT emplovees than other CIOs, and less with non-C-level, non-IT employees, and less time working alone. CIOs in the IT hardware industry spend more time than other CIOs with external customers, which is consistent with the nature of their business. In addition, CIOs in the retail sector spend more time with the IT employees of external customers than their counterparts in other sectors. This may be explained by the use of interorganizational value chain spanning systems (e.g., supply chain management or customer relationship management) that allow retail organizations to more efectively manage business transactions within their value chain. Another diference in time allocation across industries was that CIOs in the government sector spent more time with colleagues outside their organization than those in other sectors. Although the ANOVA indicates that there is also a diference at the alpha = 0.05 level across a primary industry in the time a CIO spends with their own IT employees, the Tukey’s HSD did not detect statistically significant diferences among the means of time spent for any specific industry. Outside of these findings, the reporting line has little relation with regard to how CIOs allocate time to stakeholders. Thus, while H2c and H2d are partially supported, findings suggest that at least this portion of the CIO’s job is largely consistent across back ground and reporting line. Furthermore, with only a few exceptions, diferences in who they spend their time with are not related to the industry in which they work nor the size of their own IT units. This empirical evidence reveals a picture of the CIO that is diferent than the one often postulated. CIOs who come from a particular background or who have a particular reporting line are generally presumed to have greater or lesser access to various stakeholder groups [10,15]. Our findings do not support that, and suggest rather a more consistent ac cess to stakeholders across background and reporting line, as well as across industries and the size of IT unit

CIOs with more IT employees (indicating larger organizations) spent more time on IT governance, IT operations/facilities management, and IT project management. Interestingly, those with more IT employees also spent more time as an evangelist for the organization. This may be because the organizational evangelizing is done as an outreach to IT employees, to ensure the alignment of a large IT organization with the rest of the enterprise. There were no other significant diferences on how CIOs allocate their time. Thus, again, these empirical findings paint a picture of a more consistent CIO job than is generally purported.

## 3.3. CIO business focus

We next examined the relationship between CIO background and CIO Business Focus as well as the relationship between CIO Business Focus and reporting line, with whom they spend their time, and at what they spend their time doing. Specifically, CIO Business Focus is measured by the CIO’s perception of five items: the extent to which IT enables business strategy, how well IT strategy aligns with the business, how credible IT is with senior business executives, the extent to which IT shapes business strategy, and the value of IT in business innovation. We expect a CIO under whom IT is highly focused on the business of the organization will perceive a higher level of each of these items than one under whom IT is less focused on the organization and more focused on IT operations and functioning. This taps a diferent construct than whether the CIO role is transformational, strategic, or functional because that construct typically taps expectations set for the CIO (either by others or themselves), whereas CIO Business Focus taps the CIO’s own perceptions of the extent to which IT is focused on and delivers results for the business of the organization. Furthermore, although one of the five items addresses IT alignment with the business, this measure of CIO Business Focus is broader than a singular IT alignment construct.

The psychometric properties of the items indicate that they provide a valid and reliable measure of the construct. All items were measured using a 5-point Likert scale. We used the exploratory factor analysis (EFA) to assess the validity of the items measuring the CIO Business Focus construct and to verify that the items did load on only one construct so that hypothesized relationships about the construct could be tested in subsequent analyses. EFA allows the assessment of whether theorized items converge together on a hypothesized number of factors (in this case one factor) and discriminate across multiple factors with minimal cross-loadings [29]. This is a powerful tool that helps us better assess the properties of sets of measures by assessing the correlation among items, and by using the correlation matrix to derive factor loadings that represent the correlation between an item and the construct it is thought to measure [29]. Although factor analysis does not directly constitute a test of construct validity, it does ofer a way of constructing an interrelated set of indicators meeting one of the conditions for construct validity – unidimensionality. If one or more indicators measure more than one construct (i.e., the measure is mul tidimensional), then it is dificult to establish reliability, which is also a necessary component of construct validity. EFA is also appropriate to identify and create a set of variables for subsequent regression analysis. Thus, EFA is appropriate for this portion of the research in this study. EFA using a Varimax rotation was used to test whether the five items measure one underlying construct with loadings of at least 0.60 as the cutof point for inclusion of an item [29]. Results provide support for the presence of one factor (Table 7). Cronbach’s alpha was 0.83, indicating strong internal consistency (reliability) of the items. It appears that this is a valid and appropriate measure of CIO Business Focus for this research.

Again, this was consistent across comparisons of the top 25 % to the lowest 25 % $( \mathbf { p } = 0 . 1 6 0 9 )$ , the top 25 % to the lower 75 % $( \mathbf { p } = 0 . 4 0 3 5 )$ , and the lowest 25 % to the higher 75 % $( \mathsf { p } = 0 . 1 4 0 9 )$ Thus, a CIO’s background appears to have little impact on the extent to which they focus on the business. However, the percentages were more equally distributed for the internal/external background (approximately 27 % internal, 28 % external in the same industry, and 45 % from a diferent industry).

The relationship between CIO Business Focus and reporting line was also examined using Chi-square tests. The top 25 % were statistically more likely to report to the CEO than either the lowest 25 % $( \mathbf { p } = 0 . 0 0 0 5 )$ or the lower 75 % $( \mathbf { \mathrm { p } } = 0 . 0 0 1 8 )$ . The lowest 25 % were statistically less likely than the top 75 % to report to the CEO $( \mathbf { p } = 0 . 0 0 6 7 )$ . Thus, H4c was supported. These findings are consistent with prior research that indicates that CIOs who report to the CEO are more likely to be business-oriented. Findings also indicate that the lowest 25 % are more likely to report to the CFO than the top 25 % $( \mathbf { p } = 0 . 0 0 0 2 )$ , and that the lower 75 % are more likely to report to the CFO than the top 25 % $( \mathbf { p } = 0 . 0 0 0 3 )$ . Thus, H4d is supported. Interestingly, the lowest 25 % were less likely to report to the CFO than the upper 75 % $( \mathbf { p } = 0 . 0 1 2 3 )$ . This suggests that the CIOs with the least focus on the business may be in a reporting structure that is removed from both the CEO and CFO. However, no statistically significant differences were found among CIO Business Focus for those who report to a level other than CEO and CFO.

Next, we assessed CIO Business Focus and its relationship with “at what” CIOs spent their time doing and “with whom” they spent their time. Recall that respondents were asked to provide their allocation of time to a general category called business-related activities, and one called IT-related activities in addition to their allocation of time to specific activities in each general category (Table 6a). Because CIO Business Focus taps a general approach to the job, we used the allocation of time to the general categories “business-related” and “IT-related” activities, rather than to the specific activities within each of these. Linear regression was used to assess diferences between the top 25 % and the lowest 25 % as well as between the middle 50 % and the lowest 25 % with regard to how they allocated their time to these two categories (H5a).

The regression coeficients in Table 8 can be interpreted as follows: the beta coeficient of the intercept is the average percent of time spent by the lowest 25 %. The beta coeficient for the top 25 % is how much time is spent by the top 25 % relative to the lowest 25 %, and the beta coeficient for the middle 50 % is how much time is spent for this group relative to the lowest 25 %. In other words, the lowest 25 % is the reference group for the three categories of top 25 %, middle 50 %, and lowest 25 %. These findings indicate that CIOs with a greater business focus spent significantly more time on business-related activities than those with less focus (Table 8). Thus, H5a is supported. Although these results are not surprising. they do provide additional confirmation of the validity of the CIO Business Focus construct and its operationalization herein.

Hypothesis 5b posits that CIOs with the greatest CIO business focus spend more time with non-IT stakeholders (e.g., C-level executives, external customers, and suppliers) than those with less business focus. There were some significant diferences between the CIOs with the greatest business focus (top 25 %) and other CIOs in terms of with whom they spent time (stakeholder groups listed in Table 5a). Only the items for which there was statistical significance are reported in Table 9. The top 25 % also spent significantly more time with external suppliers and external customers than the lowest 25 %. Furthermore, the top 25 % and the middle 50 % each spent significantly less time with internal IT employees and with IT contractors than the lowest 25 %. There were significant diferences in how they spent their time with other stakeholders such as non-C level non-IT employees, IT colleagues outside of the organization, or IT employees of external customers and suppliers. Thus, findings partially support H5b.

## 4. Discussion of key findings

A summary of hypotheses tested and the research question to which each set of hypotheses is related is provided in Table 10. The overarching research question in this study was about how CIO background is related to how he/she approaches the job. The first specific research question (RQ1) is about the extent to which background is related to reporting line. Our findings suggest that being hired from outside or inside the company is related to reporting line, yet whether they have an IT or non-IT background is not. The next research questions (RQ2, RQ3, and RQ4) go beyond the influence of background to include how reporting line is related to how the CIO approaches the job, and RQ5 addresses a specific aspect of the influence of CIO focus on the business. Although the specific organizational situation in which a CIO works certainly helps drive what they do, our findings paint a picture of the CIO role and focus that is more consistent across back grounds, industry, size of IT unit, and even reporting line than often believed. This study contributes to our collective understanding of what helps shape the CIO job and paints a picture of a job that is fairly consistent across backgrounds.

To whom the CIO reports, with whom they spend their time, and what they spend their time doing is largely consistent regardless of whether the CIO has an IT background or not, and irrespective of whether they were hired from within the organization or from another organization or industry. This is consistent with research that suggests that the direction and particulars of the CIO role is dependent on what they are hired to do [7,8]. While it is tempting to think that background influences the choice of who is actually hired into diferent roles, our findings indicate that this is not the case. Hypotheses related to background are not supported, with the exception of H1c and H1d. Findings about H1c and H1d indicated that CIOs hired from outside the organization are more likely to report to the CEO, and those from within are less likely to report to the CEO. This is consistent with research that suggests that CIOs hired from outside are often brought in to make significant strategic change (turnaround or realignment) and as such report to the CEO [13].

Background has no significant relationship with CIO reporting line (H1a and H1b) or the amount of time CIOs spend with IT or non-IT stakeholders (H2a and H2b). Neither is it significantly related to the extent to which they are engaged in business-related activities (H3a and H3b), or the extent to which they have a business focus (H4a and H4b). Although there is a widely held perception that background does influence these factors [6,12], our findings are consistent with empirical research that suggests there is no one best background for a CIO to have [9,10]. Findings suggest that where a CIO originates is only indirectly related to how they approach their job. It is, however, directly related to reporting line. Reporting line, which research indicates may be an indicator of what they were hired to do [9,10], has a direct relationship with who they spend time with (H2c and H2d), what they spend time doing (H3c and H3d), and the extent of CIO business focus (H4c and H4d). In addition, findings indicate that the extent of CIO busines focus is directly related to what they spend their time doing and with whom (H5a and H5b).

CIOs reporting to CEOs spent more time with other C-suite peers and on business-related activities (H2c and H3c). Because prior research suggests that access to top management is related to reporting line and how well CIOs leverage their reporting line [10], we also examined, in ex post facto analysis, how frequently CIOs meet with other executives. Although frequency of meeting does not indicate quality or content of the meetings, it does indicate access to top management. A graphical view reveals a stark diference in access to top management based on reporting line: CIOs who report directly to the CEO are much more likely to meet at least weekly with other executives (Fig. 4).

Although this also lends support to the notion that reporting line influences CIO access to top management (H1c and H1d), we believe that placed in the context of our other findings it goes beyond simply to whom a CIO reports, but rather points to what role the CIO is expected to fill and thus to what the CIO does. There are certainly examples of strategic and/or transformational CIOs who report to CFOs; nevertheless, these findings lend support to the notion that CIOs who report to the CEO are more broadly focused on the organization, whereas those who report to the CFO appear more narrowly focused on IT matters. This is consistent with research that suggests the latter are expected to be more IT-efficiency driven and the former more transformational or strategic [9]. It is also consistent with prior research that suggests that the CIO role is defined by the context into which he/she is hired. Moreover, it provides a deeper insight into the role of context.

CIOs who report to the CFO not only meet far more with that person than any other executive, they appear to meet more often with the CFO than CIOs who report to the CEO meet with the person to whom they directly report. Nearly 45 % of CIOs who report to the CFO meet daily with the CFO. Only around 30 % of those who report to the CEO meet daily with the CEO. This suggests that when a CIO’s role is value- or cost-focused, the CFO may have more influence over, or at least more direct monitoring, of the CIO function than a CEO does when the role is more strategic. It also suggests that even when the role may be more strategic, CFOs still have a great deal of influence. Around 90 % of CIOs reporting to CEOs meet weekly with the CEO, and around 84 % also meet that often with the CFO. Around 30 % of these CIOs meet daily with the CEO, CFO, and COO. Less than 10 % of CIOs who report to the CFO meet daily with the CEO and only 15 % meet that often with the COO. This suggests that if CIOs work in the context of cost management, they receive less input from executives other than the CFO than do their presumably more strategically focused counterparts or those who report to the CEO. While this may seem at first to be intuitively logical, it suggests that when the context of the CIO job is cost management and IT eficiency, there may be little input or influence from other business executives. This may color their perceptions of the CIO and the IT organization as being a silo that is not sensitive to the needs of their functional areas. It also suggests a context in which the CIO not only has limited communication with other executives, he/she may also have limited support in dificult situations.

Findings also suggest that organizations that are seeking to better leverage IT to position themselves more strategically or competitively in the marketplace, likely seek CIOs that can help them do so. Placed in the context of these prior findings, our findings suggest that they hire CIOs who are from outside their organization, yet are within the same industry as their organization. One explanation may be that these CIOs not only bring a diferent organizational perspective, but also that they may add broader knowledge about the industry in which the organization wishes to better position itself. Organizations that wish to derive greater eficiency from their IT (e.g., those where the CIO reports to the CFO) may be more likely to hire a CIO from within the organization. One explanation is that these CIOs know the organization, and can “hit the ground running” to focus on IT eficiencies with less of a learning curve than someone outside the organization does. Our findings, however, also suggest that CIOs hired from a diferent industry are more likely to report to the CFO than those from the same industry. We do not have information about which industries they are hired from, but one explanation of this might be that these CIOs are hired from industries that have demonstrated IT process eficiencies.

Although findings indicate that CIOs hired from other companies, and especially those from other companies in the same industry, are more likely to report to their new CEO, reporting line appears to have a stronger relationship with the CIO’s focus, relationships, and activities, especially for those with the greatest focus on the business of the organization (i.e., the top quartile in CIO Business Focus). Those with the greatest CIO business focus (top 25 %) were more likely to report to the CEO than all the rest (H4c); while the least business-focused CIOs (lowest 25 %) were less likely to report to the CEO or CFO than any of the others (H4d). Findings about H5a and H5b were mixed. Those with a greater CIO business focus are definitely more likely to spend time on business-related activities than those with less (H5a), yet only partial support was found for the hypothesis that they are also more likely to spend time with non-IT-related stakeholders (H5b). One possible explanation is that when a CIO has a strong business focus, they may also work to bring their IS organizations to this focus. Thus, in addition to spending time with non-IT-related stakeholders, they also spend time with the IT stakeholders to bring about more broad-based IS business focus. Further research is needed to explore this, but it is unrealistic to think that a CIO can bring about business-focused IS initiatives alone.

As with any study, the findings in this study should be interpreted in light of limitations. One is that it is based on the perceptions of respondents. Future research could benefit from comparing the perceptions of the CIO with those of the CEO/CFOs to whom CIOs report with regard to how they approach their jobs. Another limitation is that it is based on CIOs who are members of a single professional association. CIOs who belong to SIM may be more similar than CIOs who do not belong to the association. In addition, they are all in U.S.-based organizations. Future research that reaches a broader set of CIOs is needed to confirm and/or extend the findings of this study.

## 5. Conclusions and implications for future research and practice

Although our findings clearly indicate that a CIO’s background itself is not as important as to whom a CIO reports when it comes to at what or with whom a CIO spends their time or their business focus, we do not suggest that background is unimportant. Potential (and even current) CIOs should evaluate how well the strengths as well as the weaknesses of their backgrounds fit with the needs of the organization and the expectations of the role they are to fill. It is also possible that a CIO’s experience in, and presumably knowledge of, a specific industry may open the door to more business-focused CIO opportunities. This merits further examination in future research.

This research operationalized the construct CIO Business Focus, but it did not examine how successfully the CIO leverages this focus. Further research is needed to provide insight into how CIO Business Focus is related to actual CIO performance beyond at what or with whom they spend their time. This construct may reflect a strategic or transformational CIO role. Further research is also needed to examine influences on this role, as well as the role of the CIO more focused on IT performance, and the success they each achieve. In addition, insight into how these two kinds of CIOs compare to CIOs with a more balanced focus in terms of performance and other behaviors and characteristics may hold important implications not only for the hiring of CIOs, but also for shaping the CIO’s role more efectively

The lack of consensus in current thinking about what makes a good CIO is in part fueled by a tacit implication that one role is better or more desirous than another. Yet, it is more likely that many, if not most, organizations need one kind of CIO more than the others; and that this varies by the particular time and industry. It is our hope that the discussions move beyond which type of CIO is best CIO or how a CIO’s background impacts the IT function and its value to the business, to focus on other factors that may be tied to how and what successful CIOs do diferently.

Research suggests that CIOs are often expected to play a dual o even multipart role to balance IT operations, an IT turnaround, a par ticularly complex IT technical transformation, and IT’s value and contribution to business operations, strategy, alignment, agility, and transformation [19,20,32]. Future research could also extend this research to better define and understand CIOs who must enact and succeed at these various balancing roles.

There are several lessons in this research for those who are pursuing, or considering pursuing, a career path leading to CIO; as well as those who help educate and prepare them. It is important to note that non-IT background does not imply no IT knowledge or experience. It is not uncommon in organizations today for functional area managers other than IT to be closely involved with, perhaps even leading, large scale interorganizational IT implementations such as enterprise resource planning or business intelligence/analytics systems [33]. Thus, these lessons apply beyond people whose current job titles are within the traditional IT functional area and include managers in other functional areas that may work closely with, or have critical budget responsibilities, for IT in their own areas.

One is that the role of the CIO is largely consistent across a variety of factors. Therefore, learning more about what a CIO does and what it means to be a CIO, regardless of background, is key. Another lesson is the importance of understanding the organization-specific needs and expectations of the CIO role. CIOs today must balance many objectives (e.g., IT eficiency, strategic planning, business transformation, reduced time to market, business agility, and even nontraditional IT roles like revenue generation and facilities management) and roles (e.g., technologist, enabler, innovator, and strategist [32]. Moreover, there are research indications that suggest one of these typically takes precedence [2,3]. Thus, CIOs must understand what is expected and needed, as well as that organizations priorities and of course their technologies change. Our findings provide a framework within which an aspiring or current CIO can evaluate the role and the expectations associated with it, as well as new opportunities. These findings may also help organizations looking for a new CIO to do so more successfully and help educators better prepare future and current CIOs.

Many CIOs find themselves in frustrating situations when their ef forts to “bring IT to the executive table” are thwarted or when they find their access to top management limited. Our findings provide a broadly applicable framework that paints a diferent picture than is often pro mulgated regarding the influence of CIO background and reporting relationship on the CIO role. Our hope is that this research helps both current and future CIOs understand how to better leverage their backgrounds, strengths, and experiences to be more successful, not just for themselves, but for the organizations that employ them too.
