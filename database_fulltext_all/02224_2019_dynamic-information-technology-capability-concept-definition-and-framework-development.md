# Dynamic information technology capability: Concept definition and framework development

Ting (Carol) Li, Yolande E. Chan

The Stephen J.R. Smith School of Business, Queen’s University, K7L 3N6, Canada

## A B S T R A C T

In a digital world, information technology (IT) units routinely update their capabilities to cope with changing business requirements and frequent technology releases. Extending the dynamic capabilities literature, this article presents the concept of dynamic IT capability, a multidimensional first order dynamic capability that enables IT units to assist firms in appropriating business value from IT resources by influencing a set of IT-related ordinary capabilities. Scholars currently lack a dynamic capabilities framework that explains, from an IT unit’s perspective, how IT resources can be acquired, deployed, integrated, and reconfigured to fulfill business objectives. To bridge this research gap, we develop a high-level framework that highlights three constituent components of dynamic IT capability: dynamic digital platform capability, dynamic IT management capability, and dynamic IT knowledge management capability. Through an extensive literature review, we identify and summarize the set of ordinary capabilities that each dynamic IT capability component creates and reconfigures. We then ofer guidance on future instrument development. To encourage further exploration of this critical construct, we close by highlighting future avenues for dynamic IT capability research.

## Introduction

Dynamic capabilities have attracted much attention in the management literature since being first introduced (Barreto, 2010). The concept of dynamic capabilities was proposed to represent an organization’s capabilities to efect changes to its resource base (Eisenhardt and Martin, 2000; Teece et al., 1997). For organizations to survive and thrive in competitive environments, their resources and processes need to be periodically, if not continuously, adjusted and reconfigured (Malhotra et al., 2007). The core idea is that when facing a competitive landscape involving complexity and uncertainty, dynamic capabilities enable organizations to achieve and sustain competitive advantages by staying adaptive (Teece, 2007). Units providing information technology (IT) services within organizations face additional challenges because of relentless technology changes, readily available external (e.g., cloud) services, and IT savvy business personnel with high technology expectations and a range of technology devices (Bygstad, 2017; Vithayathil, 2018). Although the scholarly understanding of the building blocks of dynamic capabilities has advanced steadily in the past two decades (Helfat and Martin, 2015; Wilden et al., 2016), the roles played by IT resources in forming dynamic capabilities, in particular dynamic IT capabilities, remain less well understood (Benitez et al., 2018; Pavlou and El Sawy, 2006). Below, we expand on this more fully.

Scholars’ interest in analyzing the impact of digital technologies in supporting superior long-term firm performance in competitive environments has been growing steadily (Chakravarty et al., 2013; Lim et al., 2011; Wheeler, 2002). Digital technologies – combinations of information computing communication and connectivity technologies (Bharadwai et al 2013 p 471) – are pervasive in modern organizations and pose new dynamics for organizations to cope with. For businesses, digital technologies not only include long-existing enterprise information systems (IS) such as customer relationship management systems and enterprise resource planning systems, but also include cheaper and more readily available technologies that have emerged more recently such as cloud computing and social media apps (Bygstad, 2017; Nambisan et al., 2017). To embrace both newer and older information technologies, this study uses the terms IT and digital technologies interchangeably. In the face of the immense transformative impact of digital technologies, how the IT unit can enable an organization to recognize and appropriate value from IT is becoming an increasingly important issue (Bharadwaj et al., 2013; Sambamurthy et al., 2003).

During the past two decades, digital technologies have plaved new business roles, such as enabling new business processes (Pagani, 2013) and boundary-spanning activities (Lindgren et al., 2008), generating new products and services (Lucas and Goh, 2009), increasing customer engagement (Jarvenpaa et al., 2013), and facilitating innovation (Nambisan, 2016). However, IT alone cannot guarantee success (Carr, 2003; McGuigan, 2009); it takes certain leveraging capabilities to ensure that IT resources and practices do not fall short of expectations (Daniel et al., 2014; Daniel and Wilson, 2003). We define IT resources and practices as the combination of IT infrastructure and associated processes that an organization leverages to fulfill its business objectives. To make sure that the business value of IT is properly recognized and realized, the person, group or department providing IT services (i.e., the IT unit) needs to make the right IT functionalities available for use by the right people, at the right place and time (Pavlou and El Sawy, 2006, 2010; Tarafdar and Gordon, 2007).

The IS literature has predominantly viewed IT units as the source of IT capabilities in the firm (Pavlou and El Sawy, 2006). In the traditional view, IT units oversee the design, deployment, and maintenance of IT infrastructure and systems that automate and facilitate business procedures. As IT becomes increasingly integral to business processes, IT units undertake more strategic responsibilities – for instance, the service portfolio can be extended from managing backend IT functions to deploying up-to-date IT resources to accomplishing high-visibility, firm-level strategic objectives (Dhaliwal et al., 2011; Wagner et al., 2014). Instead of serving as isolated standby units that provide IT services as requested, IT units within organizations now engage actively in value co-creation activities with other units and ofer value-adding operations of a strategic nature (Choudhary and Vithayathil, 2013; Dhaliwal et al., 2011).

The growing adoption of cloud-based resources, despite their IT service enhancement and cost reduction potential, has created new challenges for IT units in organizations (Choudhary and Vithayathil, 2013). For instance, these units have to reassess the value of in-house infrastructural components, compare them with cloud-based alternatives (e.g., platform as a service or infrastructure as a service), design digital transformation plans, co-manage digital initiatives with external IT vendors if needed, and reconfigure existing ways of managing IT resources to include the new IT infrastructural components. In other words, today’s IT units need up-todate skillsets to address both changing internal (e.g., user‐related) and external (e.g., cloud-related) opportunities and challenges (Vithayathil, 2018; Yeow et al., 2017). Instead of becoming obsolete, IT units are even more critical.

In today’s turbulent business environment, which is characterized by changing customer preferences, shifting industry boundaries, fast-evolving technologies, and emerging forms of global competition, organizations are pressed to build dynamic capabilities that leverage the potential of digital technologies to facilitate diverse business activities (Chakravarty et al., 2013; Queiroz et al., 2018; Wheeler, 2002). There is, however, a dearth of research on the roles played by IT resources in the formation of dynamic capabilities. Studies on dynamic capabilities tend to omit discussion of the IT-enabled nature of the business capabilities being examined.

To our knowledge, there has been no discussion of the dynamic capabilities needed by IT units to support strategic business shifts. Only a few studies explore the roles of digital technologies in forming dynamic capabilities, and their findings are dificult to aggregate, as they have examined specific roles or types of IT in forming dynamic capabilities. For example, Benitez et al. (2018) conceptualized flexible IT infrastructure as a dynamic capability that enables organizations to sense and seize mergers and acquisitions opportunities; Queiroz et al. (2018) introduced IT application orchestration as a dynamic capability that explains how an organization refreshes its application portfolio through building, buying, and retiring processes. These specialized approaches to dynamic IT capability have led to diferent concept definition and measurement approaches, which have generated inconclusive, and mixed, empirical findings.

This study aims to develop a comprehensive and contemporary definition of the dynamic IT capability (DITC) concept, a first-order dynamic capability developed by IT units to stay adaptive to technological and market changes in modern business. In general terms, we define DITC as an IT unit’s capacity to acquire, deploy, integrate, reconfigure, and transform the organization’s IT resources for the fulfillment of business objectives. Adopting the hierarchical capabilities framework introduced by Winter (2003) and the typology of IT capability developed by Bharadwaj (2000), we propose a DITC framework that highlights key components of DITC, and the associated ordinary capabilities that they extend, modify, or create. Scholars frequently rely on the extant literature to present the cumulative understanding of a focal construct and determine its dimensions (Hong and Thong, 2013; Lewis et al., 2005; Ragu-Nathan et al., 2001). Following this approach, we critically review the related literature using an inductively developed coding protocol. To ensure the relevance and comprehensiveness of our DITC definition, we examine articles related to DITC from both academic and practice-oriented literature. Informed by the insights gained, we model DITC as a first-order dynamic capability that consists of three interrelated and interdependent constituent dynamic capability components, each with three associated ordinary capabilities

Below, we introduce the DITC concept and its component dynamic capabilities. Next, we present the extended DITC framework derived from our extensive literature review and discuss the ordinary capabilities associated with each DITC component. Recommendations for future studies to develop and validate DITC instruments follow. We then suggest other future research directions and outline important theoretical and practical implications

## The DITC framework

DITC is rooted in the dynamic capabilities concept. In fact, DITC is a specific set of dynamic capabilities, developed by IT units, that enable organizations to appropriate value from digital technologies (Wheeler, 2002). The DITC concept enables us to examine how IT units use three key dynamic capabilities – dynamic digital platform capability, dynamic IT management capability, and dynamic IT knowledge management capability<sup>1</sup> – to extend, modify or create related ordinary IT capabilities. In this section, we introduce the dynamic capabilities concept and present our high-level DITC framework.

## Dynamic capabilities

In the domain of strategic management, dynamic capabilities have been repeatedly discussed during the past two decades (Barreto, 2010; Eriksson, 2013). First introduced by Teece and his colleagues, dynamic capabilities are proposed to represent managerial capabilities that combine resources in new ways, gain additional resources, and dispose of superfluous resources (Teece et al., 1997; Teece and Pisano, 1994). The dynamic capabilities perspective originates from the resource-based view (RBV) (Peteraf et al., 2013). RBV suggests that organizations are fundamentally heterogeneous in terms of their resources and capabilities which can be leveraged to create competitive advantage (Peteraf, 1993). Extending RBV, the dynamic capabilities perspective also integrates theoretical aspects of evolutionary economics, behavioural theories, and organizational theories in order to explain the source of competitive advantage (Helfat and Peteraf, 2009; Vogel and Guettel, 2013).

The complexity of the theoretical underpinnings has led to several conceptual debates (Arend and Bromiley, 2009; Breznik and Lahovnik, 2014). As a very broad and complex construct, dynamic capabilities span the domains of strategy process and content – from managerial decision processes, to organizational routines, and competitive actions (Helfat and Peteraf, 2009, p. 91). As a result. various definitions have been developed in the management literature to accommodate diferent research foci, as summarized in Table 1. There are two definitions, however, that are very frequently adopted by dynamic capabilities studies (Peteraf et al., 2013) Teece and his colleagues (1997) suggested that dynamic capabilities are unique to individual firms as they are idiosyncratic in constituent details and are path-dependent. Eisenhardt and Martin (2000) proposed a second definition based on their observation that specific dynamic capabilities possess considerable commonalities across firms. In particular, they argued that there are similarities in efective ways of undertaking business activities, such as creating alliances and developing new products (Daniel and Wilson, 2003).

There has been a call for research to diferentiate among the types of processes that undergird dynamic capabilities (e.g., operational routines vs. emerging processes) (Cepeda and Vera, 2007). Winter (2003), for example, makes a distinction between ordinary (zero-order) and dynamic (first-order) capabilities. Ordinary organizational capabilities represent a set of highly patterned, repetitious, or quasi-repetitious routines that are geared to the operational functioning of a firm (Winter. 2003: Zollo and Winter. 2002). Dynamic capabilities, in contrast, represent routines and processes that extend, modify, or create ordinary capabilities (Collis 1994; Drnevich and Kriauciunas, 2011; Winter, 2003). When deploying first-order dynamic capabilities is insuficient, second-order dynamic capabilities are needed to help organizations create and implement changes to first-order dynamic capabilities, in order to adapt to novel situations (Daniel et al., 2014; Winter, 2003). However, when the changes no longer justify or sustain second-order dynamic capabilities, firms may step back to first-order or zero-order capabilities (Daniel et al., 2014; Winter, 2003).<sup>2</sup> This hierarchy of capabilities is increasingly being adopted in dynamic capabilities studies (e.g., Dong and Wu, 2015; Karimi and Walter, 2015; Zahra et al., 2006) and helps to eliminate the tautological flavour of earlier discussions (Cepeda and Vera, 2007; Laaksonen and Peltoniemi, 2016).

Following Winter’s approach, we present DITC as a first-order dynamic capability with three constituent components that IT units within organizations develop in order to plan, execute, and assess changes to IT-related ordinary capabilities (Drnevich and Kriauciunas, 2011; Winter, 2003). While an IT unit is actively involved in providing technological support to ensure that the IT infrastructural components function as expected, it also has the responsibility to innovate with, and execute changes to, IT resources and practices in a way that aligns with the organization’s strategic objectives and priorities (Choudhary and Vithayathil, 2013; Dhaliwal et al., 2011).

In this study, we refer to IT-related ordinary capabilities as the capabilities through which an IT unit deploys and supports IT resources – in Winter (2003)’s terms, how an IT unit “makes its living” in the short run. DITC is a first-order dynamic capability that IT units develop to extend. modify or create IT-related ordinary capabilities. IT units develop DITC to control the evolution of IT. related ordinary capabilities. As a first-order dynamic capability, DITC is itself governed by, and also contributes to the development of, second-order dynamic capabilities such as overall firm-wide sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring capabilities (Collis, 1994; Danie et al., 2014; Wilden et al., 2016). For instance, an IT-related ordinary capability is an IT unit’s ability to deploy a new customer relationship management system. DITC includes, but is not limited to, the IT unit learning how to more successfully deploy systems and adiusting future system deployments. An example of a second-order dynamic capability is the organization's ability to seize a business opportunity by rapidly adjusting the IT unit’s dynamic capability, for example by bringing on board IT consultants to accelerate the development of a time-sensitive, strategic system (Daniel et al., 2014).<sup>3</sup>

Most studies that contribute to our understanding of dynamic IT capabilities are in the IS literature instead of the strategic management literature (e.g., Benitez-Amado and Walczuch. 2012: Bhatt and Grover. 2005: Daniel et al., 2014: Lim et al., 2011). This is a limitation of this literature. Among the multiple strategic management review articles on dynamic capabilities in the recent decade, none has focused on the role of IT in forming dynamic capabilities, let alone dynamic capabilities held by IT units. The research foci of these articles are summarized in Table 2. As a result, the research findings that contribute to the understanding, development, and management of DITC have yet to be integrated, and our study aims to address this research gap.

## A high-level DITC framework

As IT resources become increasingly associated with business value creation. the processes and routines for IT innovation and adaptation are beginning to draw attention in dynamic capabilities research (e.g., Chen et al., 2008; Daniel and Wilson, 2003; Kim et al., 2011; Lim et al., 2011). Adopting the dynamic capabilities framework introduced by Winter (2003) and the typology of IT capability developed by Bharadwaj (2000), we propose a high-level DITC framework that links IT resources, IT-related ordinary capabilities, and DITC, as shown in Fig. 1. While IT resources are, in general, firm-specific assets that enable organizations to operate, capabilities need to be in place for organizations to perform coordinated sets of tasks and realize the value of IT resources (Helfat and Peteraf, 2003; Karimi et al., 2007).

Bharadwaj (2000) classified IT resources into three categories: IT infrastructure, human IT resources, and IT-enabled intangibles. Using this theoretical foundation, in a similar way, we present DITC as having three component dynamic capabilities that operate on ordinary capabilities to alter three categories of IT resources: one focused on infrastructure, a second on IT management, and a third on IT-enabled intangibles, in particular, on IT knowledge. Specifically, we identify the three constituent components of DITC as: 1) dynamic digital platform capability, 2) dynamic IT management capability, and 3) dynamic IT knowledge management capability. IT units rely on these DITC components to govern changes in IT-related ordinary capabilities (Bharadwaj, 2000; Santhanam and Hartono, 2003). In this way, IT units help organizations benefit from IT resources.

The first component of DITC is dynamic digital platform capability, which we define as the IT unit’s capacity to appropriate value from the organization’s IT infrastructural components. IT units rely on IT infrastructure to support the sensing and seizing of business opportunities, and to support IT-enabled competitive and collaborative actions. Dynamic digital platform capability is founded on the organization’s supporting IT infrastructure, and the IT unit’s ability to change the infrastructure as needed. Dynamic digital platform capability not only encompasses an IT unit’s capacity to plan, initiate and fulfill changes to ordinary capabilities that relate to the functioning of IT infrastructural components, but also enhances the eficiency, efectiveness, and flexibility of the organization’s IT infrastructure (Ray et al., 2005; Tan et al., 2015; Tanriverdi, 2005).

The second component of DITC is dynamic IT management capability, which refers to the IT unit’s ability to design and execute changes to business processes that control IT resources and practices in a manner aligned with the firm's goals and priorities. In turbulent environments, IT units need dynamic IT management capabilities to transform existing IT management capabilities (Lim et al., 2011: Zahra and George, 2002). Dynamic IT management activities involve the design and execution of strategic IT plans (Daniel et al., 2014; Kim et al., 2011) as well as the identification of solutions to address unpredictable and novel developments (Pavlou and El Sawy, 2010). The fusion of IT and business processes has given organizations increased opportunities to reap profits from IT through dynamic IT management capabilities. For example, assisting business personnel within an organization to use IT efectively not only enables the fulfillment of business operations, but also enhances an IT unit’s ability to innovate (Andersen, 2001; Tan et al., 2015).

The third component of DITC is dynamic IT knowledge management capability. We define dynamic IT knowledge management capability as the IT unit’s capacity to facilitate firm-wide IT knowledge creation, transfer, and retention. IT knowledge refers to the technology expertise and managerial IT skills that are held by organizational members to carry out daily operations and seek improvement as needed (Kim et al., 2011; Ray et al., 2004). As the foundation for organizational discovery and creation activities, knowledge management activities envision, exploit, and create value from knowledge resources with a view to furthering business obiectives (Chatteriee et al., 2015: Wu and Hu. 2012). To support firm strategic flexibility, an IT unit needs to continuously renew itself by updating its understanding of evolving IT resources and practices. Moreover, dynamic IT knowledge management capability also enables IT units to share useful IT knowledge with business partners (i.e., end users of IT) within and across organizationa boundaries (Karimi et al., 2007). Strong IT unit relationships and communications with business partners support reciproca knowledge sharing, and effective knowledge retention extends the service life and impact of the IT knowledge created.

## Review method

This study developed a comprehensive definition of DITC by carrying out an in-depth review of DITC-related articles in two literature streams: prestigious academic journals (AJ) in the IS discipline and leading practitioner-oriented journals (POJ). Each selected article served as an individual data point and was qualitatively coded. The research procedure was divided into four major steps: paper selection, coding protocol development, formal coding, and interpretation of findings. The research roadmap of this study is illustrated in Fig. 2.

Papers for review were identified using the procedures recommended by Webster and Watson (2002). Criteria and procedures for paper retrieval were first established. This was followed by screening and filtration processes. DITC-related papers that fully met the selection criteria were coded. Then the citations of articles being considered for coding were checked. Following the coding procedures adopted by Joshi et al. (2010), a formal coding protocol was developed. In this step, the contents of selected articles from diferent literature streams were categorized into DITC-related themes in a pilot coding process, during which the author coding agreement was examined, and it was confirmed that the inter-rater agreement was high, above 80%.

During the formal coding process, DITC-related statements were coded, and so were notable findings. In the interpretation stage, coding results were synthesized and reported, evolving DITC-related trends were summarized, components of DITC were identified, and research implications and future directions were highlighted. We coded a total of 79 articles in detail (50 AJ papers and 29 POJ papers), and the formal coding protocol converged at the middle point of the process. An additional 163 articles (114 AJ papers and 49 POJ papers) of lesser relevance, retrieved through keyword searches, were reviewed in a more cursory manner; their main sections were coded using the same protocols. The literature supported our theoretical modeling of DITC as a first-order dynamic capability with three components. No major components had been missed. The rest of this section describes more fully our research methodology, the selection criteria for data sources, the criteria for retrieving papers, and the development of the coding schema. Yearly and journal distribution, as well as the research foci of the DITC-related articles coded, are reported in Appendix A.

To ensure that the content coded captured the “nuts and bolts” of DITC, we examined two relevant literature streams. Articles in the most prestigious AJ and POJ on IT-related business topics were selected for coding. In the case of AJ, IS journals are most appropriate for studying complex constructs like DITC because the IS discipline has focused research on people, organizations, and technologies (Ghobadi, 2015). Among these journals, the “senior scholars’ basket of journals” refers to eight IS journals that are commonly accepted by IS scholars as very high quality outlets (Association for Information Systems, 2019). These journals were thus selected as the AJ source for this study

POJ are viewed as a credible and valuable source for studving the evolution of DITC for two major reasons. First, practitioners hands-on experience provides rich data for DITC research. How best to leverage IT to build competitive advantage in uncertain environments is described in both the academic and practitioner literature (Daniel and Wilson, 2003; Kohli and Grover, 2008). As a result, the practitioner literature is useful when analyzing DITC-related issues, as demonstrated by the study by Roberts and Grover (2012). Second, consistent with the strategy-as-practice perspective, IT researchers are often encouraged to study the activities performed by practitioners and incorporate pragmatic insights into their analyses (Whittington, 2003, 2014). This practice-based research approach is deemed to be mutually beneficial: while practitioners benefit from more tangible and accessible findings, researchers develop a deeper understanding of the components of DITC in action (Arvidsson et al., 2014: Daniel et al., 2014).

In selecting POJ articles, we recognized that both IT-oriented journals and general business journals can describe DITC-related findings. Accordingly, MIS Quarterly Executive (MISQE) was selected because it (1) examines IT from a practitioner’s perspective and (2) is closely linked to a senior scholars’ basket of eight journal (MISQ). In addition, three top-ranked, practitioner-oriented business journals – California Management Review. Haryard Business Review and Sloan Management Review – were selected based on (1) SCImago Journal Rank indicators and (2) the suggestions of experts in DITC-related domains. ## The extended DITC framework

Drawing on the insights gained from our in-depth review, we summarized the set of IT-related ordinary capabilities associated with each DITC component. DITC, a first-order dynamic capability made up of three components, governs changes in IT-related ordinary capabilities. While first-order dynamic capabilities shape existing ordinary capabilities and create new ones when needed, ordinary capabilities may in turn improve, or hinder, the development of these dynamic capabilities (Daniel et al., 2014). DITC, its associated IT-related ordinary capabilities, and their mutual influences are summarized in Fig. 3.

As the figure shows. DITC enables an IT unit to create or implement changes to IT-related ordinary capabilities to support specific business needs. Whether it be developing firm-wide digital discipline, or staying at the forefront of innovation, IT units need to update their ordinary capabilities regularly to maintain the vitality of their skillsets in the face of market turbulence (Woerner and Weill, 2019). The DITC components we have identified reflect three key dynamic IT capabilities possessed by efective contemporary IT units<sup>4</sup>. The firm needs to manipulate all three DITC components – dynamic digital platform capability, dynamic IT management capability, and dynamic IT knowledge management capability – in order to compete successfully. Together, these three DITC components help IT units make appropriate changes to important IT-related ordinary capabilities.

In summary, first of all, DITC allows IT units to appropriate value from IT infrastructure in the face of growing interdependence across organizations (Tan et al., 2015; Weill and Woerner, 2015). Second, DITC enables IT units to flexibly leverage digital technologies in existing and new competitive scenarios – for example, in the face of changing collaboration and competition dynamics (Ansari et al., 2016). Third, IT units can rely on DITC to successfully manage fast-evolving IT knowledge. Digital innovation has become the norm for many organizations, and the organization’s IT knowledge must be constantly updated and efectively harnessed. In a network of relationships with supply chain partners, technology vendors, customer communities, and other stakeholders, organizations need dynamic digital platform capability to maintain a healthy IT infrastructure portfolio and remain fluidly connected with the dynamic business environment. They also need dynamic IT management capability to efectively carry out digital transformation, and dynamic IT knowledge management capability to remain adaptive with a constantly renewed knowledge bank (Venkatraman, 2017).

We argue that dynamic digital platform capability influences ordinary capabilities related to the functionality, integration, and scaling needs of an organization’s IT infrastructure. Dynamic IT management capability afects an IT unit’s ability to systematically create and modify operational routines related to IT deployment, IT exploitation, and IT exploration. Dynamic IT knowledge management capability enables IT units to efectively govern the creation, transfer, and retention of IT knowledge in the organization. The three components of DITC act in concert to update the way an IT unit “makes a living,” and to support the fulfillment of strategic objectives at the firm level.

## Dynamic digital platform capability

Dynamic digital platform capability enables IT units to appropriate value from an organization’s IT infrastructural components when supporting firm-level sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring activities. Digital platforms, a key component of modern IT infrastructure, are increasingly recognized as the backbone of products, services, and operations of modern organizations. The pervasiveness of digital platform adoption has led to new market dynamics, which require changes to ordinary capabilities. Dynamic digital platform capability creates novel value creation paths, improves operational eficiency, provides access to external resources and capabilities, and enables more participatory forms of engagement (e.g., open innovation) (Busquets, 2010; Koch, 2010; Pagani 2013).

To stay adaptive to both evolving business requirements and technology shifts, IT units are expected to tailor the configuration of IT infrastructural components as needed while keeping an eye on the potential of emerging digital technologies. In today’s turbulent environments, IT infrastructure is expected to: (1) provide adequate IT functionality, (2) integrate disparate systems, including cloudbased and other external resources that complement in-house architectures, and (3) satisfy IT infrastructural scaling needs and be flexible enough to rapidly handle disruptions. Summarizing insights from our literature review, we identify three IT-related ordinary capabilities that are influenced by dynamic digital platform capability: IT infrastructure functionality, IT integration capability, and IT infrastructure flexibility.

## IT infrastructure functionality

For organizations, a primary goal of having an IT unit is to ensure the functioning of IT resources. We define IT infrastructure functionality as the capacity of an organization’s IT infrastructural components to support daily business activities. Today, digital platforms serve as the backbone of modern business operations, and supports IT-enabled processes and capabilities (Kim et al., 2011). Contemporary organizations make significant investments in digital platforms to obtain features and functions that, when properl leveraged, can shape the future of the business, including business strategies, customer relationships, and extended enterprise networks (Banker et al., 2006; Sambamurthy et al., 2003). Cloud-based resources, in particular, ofer IT units a pool of powerfu functions that provide on-demand IT capabilities (e.g., the ability to analyze customer churn rates) (Battleson et al., 2016). Given that business processes are highly reliant on IT operations, IT units with superior IT infrastructure functionality can help organizations to exploit business opportunities effectively and realize ambitious strategic objectives (Bhatt and Grover, 2005: Mithas and Rust, 2016).

## IT integration capability

IT integration capability refers to the capacity of an IT unit to make the existing IT infrastructural components compatible for intra- and inter-firm coordination (Henningsson, 2015; Rai et al., 2015). The advances of digital technologies ofer modern organizations the opportunity to synergize their IT resources, for example in-house and cloud-based systems, within and across organizational boundaries. IT integration eforts, in particular, facilitate the emergence of collective action supported by intra- and interfirm digital platforms (Kim et al., 2011). Internally, IT integration capability helps IT units break system silos in diferent functiona departments, connect these in-house systems, streamline cross-unit business processes, and develop enterprise-wide systems (Chatterjee et al., 2015). Externally, IT integration capability enables, for example, timely information exchange between an organization and value chain partners (Drnevich and Croson, 2013). Whether it be coordinated business value networks connecting customers, suppliers, and other business partners, or networks created during mergers or acquisitions, IT units need to develop an integration-friendly IT infrastructure that meets intra- and inter-firm connectivity needs (Barua et al., 2004; Henningsson, 2015; Straub and Watson, 2001). Moreover, IT integration capability provides both internal and external stakeholders with opportunities to exploit shared digital platforms eficiently and efectively in mutually beneficial ways (Baker et al., 2011; Roberts and Grover, 2012).

## IT infrastructure flexibility

Organizations are often pressured to adopt new, enabling technologies and transform or even terminate existing systems in competitive environments (Queiroz et al., 2018; Rai et al., 2010). IT units, as a result, have to extend the nature and scope of IT services they provide to stay adaptive. To help leverage these infrastructural changes, we define IT infrastructure flexibility as the extent to which an organization’s existing IT infrastructural components can readily be scaled, reconfigured, and aligned to meet diferent business objectives and technology requirements (Kim et al., 2011). Although the pervasive adoption of digital platforms may shift the value network of industry and contribute to market uncertainty (Pagani, 2013), they also have the potential to generate new business opportunities and introduce novel ways of value creation that are dificult for competitors to immediately imitate (Karimi and Walter, 2015; Park et al., 2017). Cloud-based resources, for example, ofer organizations the choice of reliable and costeffective infrastructural components that can scale up and down on a pay-as-vou-go basis (Choudhary and Vithavathil. 2013). In such circumstances, an IT unit needs to make sure that the organization’s incumbent IT infrastructure or digital platform does not become a roadblock when business changes are necessary and unavoidable (Daniel and Wilson, 2003). IT infrastructure flexibility, as a result, is needed to maintain an organization’s resilience to disruptions.

## Dynamic IT management capability

With an adequate IT infrastructure foundation, an organization’s competitive advantage can lie in its managerial and organizational processes (Teece et al., 1997). Dynamic IT management capability refers to the capacity of an IT unit to design and execute changes to work processes that control IT resources and practices in a manner aligned with the firm’s goals and priorities. In response to evolving customer demands and competitor strategies, organizations need to rapidly select, integrate, and reconfigure IT resources, processes, and capabilities (Zahra and George, 2002). Whether it be emerging technology selection, new system rollout strategies, or capability development prioritization, dynamic IT management capability is used by IT units to make tough decisions and trade-ofs to obtain desired firm outcomes.

Dynamic IT management capability enables IT units to regularly update their service portfolios to support new resources and practices such as, for example, establishing new online distribution channels, automating and eliminating paperwork, and reconfiguring collaborative business networks (Yeow et al., 2017). From incremental adjustments to radical alterations, an IT unit’s ability to efectively adjust how IT is managed enables the organization to make good adoption decisions, implement IT solutions at lower costs, rapidly reap benefits from newly assimilated technologies, and take advantage of emerging process improvement opportunities (Bharadwaj et al., 2010; Pagani, 2013; Rehm et al., 2017). Specifically, three IT-related ordinary capabilities are adjusted by dynamic IT management capability: IT deployment capability, IT exploitation capability, and IT exploration capability.

## IT deployment capability

IT deployment capability is defined as an IT unit’s capacity to assist business personnel in adopting, applying, and retiring IT resources and practices to carry out daily operations required by the business strategy and model (Daniel and Wilson, 2003; Queiroz et al., 2018). For successful technology deployment, IT units may need to customize newly adopted technologies to fit existing business processes and/or adiust these processes to deliver new cost-effective and efficient IT-enabled business operations. Effective use of IT by business personnel is an important link between IT infrastructure and business performance (Devaraj and Kohli, 2003). IT deployment, accordingly, involves IT units' efforts to inform, and/or respond to information from. business personnel about newly available IT (Wang et al., 2012). Apart from facilitating IT adoption and assimilation, IT deployment capability also enables IT units to “semi-automatically” refine IT-enabled business processes based on feedback from business partners who use the IT (Henningsson 2015). Ongoing IT improvement efforts may be hidden in business processes and be hard to replicate due to causal ambiguity (Lim et al., 2011), therefore providing firms with competitive advantages. Finally, an IT unit must monitor the performance of existing IT resources and practices, and when necessary execute the dificult but necessary decision to terminate these (e.g., legacy systems and outdated processes) to avoid IT ineficiencies and rigidities (Queiroz et al., 2018).

## IT exploitation capability

We define IT exploitation capability as an IT unit’s capacity to efectively use existing IT resources to support business activities, to improve operational efficiency, and to bring about IT synergies among internal and external stakeholders (Gregory et al., 2015: Wang et al., 2012). While IT deployment capability helps business personnel to use IT as designed, IT exploitation capability allows IT units to optimize this design by adjusting incumbent business processes to achieve better coordination, cost reduction, and organizational learning outcomes (Kim et al., 2011). Diferent functional units in an organization, as well as industrial partners, can participate collaboratively in exploiting IT business value in complex and/or comprehensive ways through digital initiatives (Morgan et al., 2013). For instance, the IT unit can streamline business processes with external partner organizations to overcome barriers of time and distance, work with internal business partners to increase the efectiveness of IT-enabled cross-firm processes (Wheeler, 2002), and redefine industry rules by weaving IT into the fabric of the business ecosystem (Krogh et al., 2005). IT exploitation capability enables an IT unit to eficiently allocate scarce IT resources, optimize IT processes, resolve IT-related conflicts, and streamline digitally-supported business collaborations (Leidner et al., 2011; Saraf et al., 2013).

## IT exploration capability

IT exploration capability refers to an IT unit’s capacity to experiment and discover, with business partners, innovations in IT resources and practices (Lee et al., 2015; Nambisan et al., 1999). In turbulent environments, simply ensuring that IT functions are used efectively to carry out daily operations is often not enough. IT units can innovate by actively seeking ways to improve existing routines and to further create new capabilities and opportunities (Roberts et al., 2016; Winter, 2003). IT exploration can be viewed as a strategic process that the IT unit adopts internally or fosters with its business partners to scan for emerging technologies, experiment with potential candidates, and dynamically adopt candidate technologies when opportunities or threats arise. Organizations with a greater emphasis on IT innovation are better able to address changes in their environments and have a greater likelihood of surviva compared with relatively conservative competitors (Leidner et al., 2011: Li et al., 2010). In the face of constant technological disruptions, realizing IT business value increasingly relies on IT exploration activities (e.g., scanning for new technologies and devising innovative uses of existing IT resources) by IT units (Venkatraman, 2017).

## Dynamic IT knowledge management capability

Knowledge is the systematic mix of individual, team, and organizational experience, skills, expertise, and know-how derived from processed information, and is a key source of strategic advantage and organizational capabilities (Li and Kettinger, 2006; Wu and Hu, 2012). In turbulent environments, IT units need to constantly update their knowledge sources and repositories. We define dynamic IT knowledge management capability as an IT unit’s capacity to facilitate firm-wide IT knowledge creation, transfer, and retention. IT knowledge includes (1) the technical expertise held by IT staf, (2) the managerial IT skills that enable IT staf to deploy, coordinate, and innovate with IT resources and practices, and (3) business personnel’s technical expertise and understanding of how IT can be used to carry out or refine daily operations (Bharadwaj, 2000; Kim et al., 2011).

IT units do not stand alone; they work collaboratively with business personnel, customers, and partner organizations to derive value from IT. Trust and understanding take time to develop but are needed for efective IT knowledge management. IT units are expected to establish and maintain supportive relationships as they share essential IT knowledge with business personnel and external stakeholders (Chakravarty et al., 2013). Dynamic IT knowledge management capability enables an IT unit to develop collaborative business partner relationships, continuously renew itself and its business partners, detect and seize IT-related opportunities, prevent knowledge loss, and align technical expertise with business needs (Jarvenpaa and Majchrzak, 2008; Peppard and Ward, 2004). To achieve this, an IT unit needs to efectively leverage three IT-related ordinary capabilities: IT knowledge creation capability, IT knowledge transfer capability, and IT knowledge retention capability.

## IT knowledge creation capability

IT knowledge creation capability refers to an IT unit’s capacity to create useful IT knowledge for the IT unit and its business partners. To create new knowledge, IT units first need to develop, leverage, and adjust various discovery mechanisms (e.g., feedback loops and system trial and error approaches) to maintain access to up-to-date and relevant information (Iyengar et al., 2015; Morgan et al., 2013). In addition to using traditional information sources such as IT industry forums and reports, IT units can learn from business personnel who contribute valuable insights on existing IT resources and practices based on user experience, and externa business partners who hold expertise that complements their existing knowledge base. With IT enabling most modern business operations, the IT unit also frequently is tasked with seeking progressive IT solutions for evolving business challenges (Bhatt and Grover, 2005; Kim et al., 2011). When new knowledge and information is acquired, IT units need to analyze and internalize it in ways that complement the existing knowledge base (Mitchell, 2006; Wakefield, 2013). IT knowledge creation capability not only enables IT units and business partners to stay informed about internal and external IT challenges and opportunities, but also helps them learn from their own and others’ mistakes, (Leidner et al., 2011; Mithas and Rust, 2016).

## IT knowledge transfer capability

We define IT knowledge transfer capability as an IT unit's capacity to make useful IT knowledge available to business partners within and bevond organizational boundaries in a timely manner. Business personnel frequently need new IT knowledge and skills to operate successfully in a digital environment (Hwang et al., 2015; Kim et al., 2016). To achieve efective knowledge transfer, IT units need to build and maintain healthy relationships with other business units and external business partners; this facilitates mutua understanding and knowledge sharing (Bhatt and Grover, 2005). IT knowledge held by diferent business units complements other knowledge in the organization and positively contributes to business performance (Baker et al., 2011; Chatterjee et al., 2015). Management teams, in particular, need basic, yet critical, IT knowledge – for instance, the functionality and performance of existing systems – to make informed IT-related decisions (Drnevich and Croson, 2013; Wang et al., 2012). With external business partners, an IT unit’s ability to transfer IT knowledge to the right audience grows more salient as problems of scale and coordination increase, especially in collaboration-intense contexts such as IT outsourcing and ofshoring, open innovation, and mergers and acquisitions (Prieto and Easterby-Smith, 2006; Rottman, 2008).

## IT knowledge retention capability

To sustain a solid knowledge base, IT units must take action to prevent IT knowledge loss. IT knowledge retention capability refers to an IT unit’s capacity to maintain the organization’s IT knowledge for future usage (Argote et al., 2003). Fast evolution of IT, staf or business partner departures, poorly designed codification approaches, and unexpected events (e.g., server failures) all pose IT knowledge retention challenges (Levallet and Chan. 2019). IT knowledge retention practices involve modular and standardized routines that codify and store newly acquired knowledge for reuse (Dedrick et al., 2011), as well as eforts to support the maintenance and retrievability of this knowledge (Gosain et al., 2004; Pavlou and El Sawy, 2010). Knowledge codification is the “compression of knowledge into a structure, involving the use of codes and models to translate rules and actions into procedures, guidelines, specifications, and documents" (Whitaker et al., 2010. p. 19). To actively manage the IT knowledge retention process. IT units need to brainstorm, and plan to mitigate, knowledge loss scenarios; they must devise codification and other personnel-based approaches that support knowledge reuse and expertise coordination (Kotlarsky et al., 2014).

Three DITC components have been discussed, together with their associated IT-related ordinary capabilities. In the next section, we ofer recommendations on the development and validation of DITC measures.

## Measuring DITC

Digital technologies are shaping how organizations do business. The DITC concept focuses on the IT unit’s capability to bridge the gap between IT resources and IT business value. The extended DITC framework we have developed enriches our scholarly understanding of how IT resources can be leveraged to meet changing business demands. As was earlier discussed, DITC is modeled as a first-order dynamic capability that has three components. Each component governs changes in IT-related ordinary capabilities. In the previous section, we summarized nine representative IT-related ordinary capabilities (i.e., three for each DITC component) discussed extensively in the literature. By mapping the connections between zero-order and first-order IT capabilities, our DITC framework paves the way for future exploration of DITC-related phenomena.

Although we have conceptually introduced and described DITC, detailed measures are needed in order to practically assess the construct (Lewis et al., 2005). We recommend that scholars develop and validate several sets of measures – for the three components of DITC, and the IT-related ordinary capabilities that these components extend modify or create (MacKenzie et al. 2011). Dynamio and ordinary capability specification in instrument development is needed to overcome current limitations in the DITC-related research (Laaksonen and Peltoniemi, 2016).

Over the years, scholars have tried to measure related dynamic and ordinary capabilities. For example, Mitchell (2006) developed measures of management’s integrative capability, which touches upon the essential elements of dynamic IT management capability such as efective deployment. The available measures, however, assess limited “parts” but not comprehensive coverage of DITC. We encourage scholars to develop a comprehensive DITC measurement instrument using the extant literature (MacKenzie et al., 2011). Some sample measurement items for the DITC components, and the associated IT-related ordinary capabilities are shown in Table 4.

The validation and use of appropriate instruments for DITC may be challenging because of the volume of items likely to be needed. To mitigate the risk of requiring a lot of measurement items when carrying out empirical DITC studies, we suggest that researchers consider using only those measurement items that capture the specific components of DITC required for the given research context. It should be noted that, for each firm, ordinary capabilities may be locally defined, and this can further complicate the measurement of firm-specific ordinary and dynamic capabilities (Winter, 2003). Measurement items, accordingly, need to be revised to fit specific research settings as may be appropriate. In addition to instrument validation, we also recommend that scholars specify and test key relationships between DITC and related constructs (i.e., the nomological network) (MacKenzie et al., 2011).

## Future directions

Adopting a dynamic capabilities perspective. this study introduces the DITC concept that captures the ability of the IT unit to acquire, deploy, integrate, and reconfigure IT resources and practices to fulfill business objectives. The pervasive adoption of IT has contributed to a climate of change and reinvention in business contexts, and has greatly increased the IT unit's salience in modern organizations. The definition of DITC and comprehensive framework presented provide a reliable starting point for future DITC studies. To move forward, we highlight five potentially high impact research areas: exploring the relationship between DITC and higher-order dynamic capabilities, using new analytical approaches to investigate diferent DITC configurations, adopting a practicebased view, exploring the microfoundations of DITC, and studying the IT unit as a digital age capability driver. ## The relationship between DITC and higher-order dynamic capabilities

Having introduced DITC, we recommend that scholars examine the mutually reinforcing relationships between DITC and higherorder dynamic capabilities such as firm-level and inter-organizational dynamic capabilities. When the current DITC held by an IT unit may not be suficient to support radically new competitive actions, higher-order dynamic capabilities are needed by the organization to adiust the DITC (Karimi and Walter. 2015). Accordingly, it will be important to study how DITC can be influenced and leveraged to facilitate firm-level sensing, seizing, and reconfiguration capabilities.

IT has empowered modern organizations to procure resources and capabilities bevond their organizational boundaries (Chuang and Lin. 2017). Organizations embedded in dense network structures can also benefit from understanding how DITC can assist IT units in supporting competitive actions that tap into available external resources (Chi et al., 2010). Meanwhile, how these inter-organizational dynamic capabilities efect changes to DITC for improved value co-creation outcomes also is a research area worthy of exploration.

## The use of new analytic approaches to explore diferent DITC configurations

Diferent configurations of the DITC components have diferent efects on the performance of IT resources and practices. For instance, successful IT units may have quite diferent configurations of dynamic digital platform capability, dynamic IT management capability, dynamic IT knowledge management capability, and supporting IT-related ordinary capabilities. For example, some IT units have superior IT integration capabilities, some excel at IT exploration, and some possess superior IT knowledge creation capabilities. When solutions are complex and there is equifinality (i.e., many ways in which to achieve the same desired outcomes), qualitative comparative analysis (QCA) can be a useful analytical tool; this is an empirical method that captures the holistic nature of configurations and enables researchers to examine how specific configurations are linked to outcomes of interest (El Sawy et al., 2010: Park et al., 2017). Future studies can use OCA to explore different configurations of DITC components that are linked with equally excellent outcomes.

## A practice-based view of DITC

In each organization, DITC is shaped by the IT unit along with stakeholders and their power relationships. Despite the clearly defined components of DITC and associated IT-related ordinary capabilities that we have identified, explaining how situated practices afect how DITC is developed, evaluated, and reconfigured remains challenging. What happens inside each IT unit is determined by firm specifics and the joint impact of decisions made in sequence by various participants (Peppard et al., 2014). A practice-based view is a viable approach to studying DITC because it tackles focal processes from within and takes the role of multiple actors into consideration (Whittington, 2014). Future DITC studies may wish to explore the nuanced understanding that can be generated by a practice-based view.

## Exploring the microfoundations of DITC

In search of more detailed understanding, a growing number of scholars have embarked on a journey to study the microfoundations of dynamic capabilities (Felin et al., 2015). These microfoundations represent the set of collective phenomena in need of greater explanation – specifically with respect to their origins, development, renewal, and management (Felin et al., 2012). Teece (2007), for example, has disaggregated dynamic capabilities into sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring mechanisms that are embedded in distinct skills, business processes, organizational structures, decision rules, and the like. When reviewing the dynamic capabilities literature, Wilden et al. (2016) developed an architectural model of dynamic capabilities that integrates resources and processes at individual, business, and corporate levels. DITC studies, as a sub-stream of dynamic capabilities, can benefit from microfoundationa thinking as well. Studying the microfoundations of DITC will help account for the various processes and structures across diferent levels of analysis. while simultaneously examining the organization's IT resource base and environment (Costello and McNaughton 2016; Wilden et al., 2016).

## The IT unit as a capability driver in the digital age

The DITC concept opens new research opportunities to explore the potential of IT units as capability drivers and leaders in firm value creation activities (e.g., strategic planning and customer engagement, Woerner and Weill, 2019). To cope with market dynamics in the digital age, modern organizations actively seek ways to improve and sustain competitive advantage. IT units, as a result, shoulder complex responsibilities to leverage the value created from IT infrastructure, human IT resources, and IT-enabled intangibles (Bharadwaj, 2000). However, the majority of dynamic capability studies we reviewed have analyzed dynamic capabilities associated with the overall organization. It is important for both scholars and practitioners to understand specifically the emerging dynamic capabilities and capabilities created by IT units (e.g., leading large-scale value co-creation initiatives) in digital business ecosystems.

DITC possessed by IT units serves as a viable starting point to fill this research gap. Future studies can examine in detail how managers shape DITC as they reconfigure DITC components which in turn initiate changes to IT-related ordinary capabilities. Researchers can study the efects of evolving DITC on firm competitiveness and performance.

## Conclusion

As the department that perhaps best understands the opportunities and challenges associated with digital transformation, IT units increasingly participate in decision making at the strategic level and lead firm-level initiatives (e.g., creating tiered customer engagement channels) (Woerner and Weill, 2019). Given the fact that IT is integral to contemporary businesses, an IT unit’s ability to collaborate with business partners to derive value from IT resources and practices can be a key determinant of superior long-term firm performance. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first study that explores how dynamic IT capabilities and ordinary capabilities can enable an IT unit to leverage IT resources and practices to meet changing business demands. A key aim of this study was to develop a comprehensive definition of DITC and generate insights into the IT-related ordinary capabilities governed by DITC components. Synthesis of this type provides a solid foundation for future DITC-related research.

In turbulent environments, a company’s strategic capabilities must continue to evolve in order to maintain competitive advantage. Using an inductively developed coding protocol, DITC-related studies published in two – academic and practitioner – literature streams were carefully selected and reviewed. Using the Bharadwaj (2000) typology, we proposed that DITC is a first-order dynamic capability consisting of three components: dynamic digital platform capability, dynamic IT management capability, and dynamic IT knowledge management capability. Using insights gained from our literature review, we presented the IT-related ordinary cap abilities influenced by each DITC component.

Like all studies, this one has a number of limitations. First, we reviewed only academic articles published in top IS journals. There are a great many other articles worth reviewing. Researchers interested in DITC might consider expanding the scope of the articles reviewed to verify and supplement our findings. Second, the selected practitioner journals varied in their themes and types of articles published. For example, MISQE is more technology-oriented and has a more formal structure (i.e., an identifiable abstract, introduction, etc.) than the other three practitioner journals. Nonetheless, many valuable insights were derived from all four journals.

A third limitation is that although the composition of DITC is presented simply in Fig. 3, we acknowledge that the interrelationships among the DITC components, and among the IT-related ordinary capabilities, as well as the ways in which these capabilities afect organizational processes outside the IT unit, are in fact very complex. More empirical work, such as exploring various configurations of DITC components or exploring the interactions among IT-related ordinary capabilities, and adopting a practice-based view, can help confirm the construct's validity and/or deepen our understanding of DITC. Finally, the high-level framework of DITC adopted in this study was derived from the dynamic capabilities framework introduced by Winter (2003) and the typology of IT capability developed by Bharadwaj (2000). We recognize that new DITC components and IT-related ordinary capabilities may be discovered. DITC will likely continue to evolve.

Our study makes four important theoretical contributions. First, we develop a fine-grained definition of the DITC concept that explains how an IT unit assists the firm in appropriating business value from IT resources and practices. Second, we conceptually diferentiate DITC from resources, ordinary capabilities, and second-order dynamic capabilities. This distinction allows several research questions – at multiple levels – to be generated to extend our understanding of DITC. Third, we outline the current scope of DITC and pave the way for scholars to further explore the construct, its components, its antecedents, its impacts, and its measures. And lastly, we identify valuable future research avenues for DITC researchers. Our study contributes to practice as well. The DITC components, and the identified associated IT-related ordinary capabilities, can serve as diagnostic tools when an IT unit evaluates its capabilities and performance. IT managers can use this assessment as a starting point to seek future improvement, and to cultivate DITC components as needed. Organizations at diferent digitization stages may need to nurture diferent DITC components. In this article, we propose five future research avenues that merit scholarly attention.

To sum up, DITC can present contemporary firms with a critical competitive advantage in turbulent, digital business environments. Practitioners can tailor DITC to address firm-specific needs. Academics can benefit from examining the DITC construct and its relationships with important antecedents and outcomes. The extant literature has not systematically analyzed dynamic capabilities possessed specifically by IT units. We invite academics to build on the DITC definition we have presented and expand our understanding and use of this critically important construct.

Pagani (2013) claimed that dynamic control over network reconfiguration can generate a high level of adaptability to cross-boundary industry disruptions, while Lee et al. (2015) proposed that superior IT exploitation and exploration capabilities are needed to achieve operational ambidexterity. Second, in some studies, dynamic IT capabilities were analyzed as multi-level constructs, with lower-level capabilities contributing to the development of higher-level ones. For example, Daniel et al. (2014) defined first- and second-order IS project portfolio management capabilities separately; while the former make changes to IT-related ordinary processes, the latter help firms stay adaptive to novel market conditions. Karimi and Walter (2015) proposed that first-order dynamic capabilities helped firms achieve digital platform capabilities.

## Appendix B

This appendix reports the justification of the research methodology we selected, the criteria for paper selection, and the development procedure of the coding schema.

## Selection of methodologies

The research methodology adopted by this study possesses two characteristics: (1) it examined the findings of articles from two literature streams, and (2) it adopted the coding procedures of content analysis to capture the “nuts and bolts” of DITC. Previous scholarly work ofered important guidance. First, there was no lack of precedents for comparison studies that used findings from diferent literature streams, and the sources of articles have greatly increased in the past two decades. For instance, Magzamen et al. (2001) incorporated newspaper, trade journal, and magazine articles into their content analysis; Foss et al. (2010) reviewed top AJ and POJ articles to test their propositions; and Schibrowsky et al. (2007) examined over 1,000 online marketing articles, regardless of their original sources, to explore the status quo of Internet marketing research.

Moreover, content analysis of articles is commonly carried out by scholars in various domains. For example, in the medical field. content analysis is used to code not only interview transcripts (Singer et al.., 1999) and astronomical gene information (Tringe et al.. 2005) but also articles from various sources (Datta and Petticrew, 2013; Partridge et al., 2011; Vantrigt et al., 1995). Similarly, content analysis of articles is conducted in marketing (Schibrowsky et al., 2007; Williams and Naumann, 2011), management science (Jordaan et al., 2013; Slack et al., 2004), psychology (Cascio and Aguinis 2008; Little et al., 2011), tourism (Ip et al., 2011; Ritchie et al., 2011). and many other disciplines

This practice is also commonly adopted in the IS domain. For example. Todd et al. (1995) explored the evolution of IS skills by analyzing the content of IS job advertisements from 1970 to 1990; Julien et al. (2011) conducted a content analysis of articles on information behaviour: and Joshi et al. (2010) explored the relationship between IT and innovation based on an extensive ex: amination of news articles from multiple data sources. In addition, the procedures of content analysis are continually refined and adjusted by IS scholars to address domain-specific research questions (Seidel et al., 2013; Stein et al., 2015). Conducting content analysis in our study was appropriate and effective.

## Criteria for paper selection

After identifying a list of high-quality source journals, paper selection criteria for each literature stream were developed, as shown in Table B-1. Since IT was already a default element in the eight IS journals selected, only “dynamic” and “capability,” as well as their corresponding synonyms, were used as keywords. To fully cover DITC-related studies in the top eight journals, articles that used “dynamic capability” and its synonyms in the abstract were also retrieved.

Papers in each literature stream were coded beginning with the most recent (published in early 2017). This ensured that when sufficient articles had been gathered to draw clear research conclusions. the dataset included the most recent articles. To ensure the appropriateness of the coding list, the selected articles were examined and agreed to by both authors.

## Development of the coding schema

NVivo 11 was chosen as the coding software for its powerful, multi-layer coding navigation and matrix coding functions. Each article that was imported into NVivo was assigned 14 paper-specific attributes, as shown in Table B-2. Four node folders were created to organize the coding: one paper summary folder and three theme folders to capture statements about DITC-related constructs, relationships, and emerging patterns in the literature, respectively.

Like the practice used by Bélanger and Crossler (2011), who coded privacy studies in the IS literature by topic area, key constructs, theoretical contributions, and methodologies for their in-depth review, this study developed a comprehensive set of nodes to capture the essentials of selected articles. Accordingly, the paper summary folder contained 15 important elements of the articles coded, as described in Table B-3. Most POJ articles did not have all the elements that were commonly seen in AJ papers due to their structural diferences. For example, while AJ papers often had clearly specified research questions, POJ articles usually just provided their research goal or briefly introduced the research gap they addressed.

The three theme folders were organized in the following ways. First, all the statements on DITC-related constructs that related to the nomological network of DITC were coded in the Nomological Network folder. Second, content that did not directly talk about DITC-related topics but shed interesting light on the research questions was coded in the Emerging Patterns folder. The coding in this folder was examined regularly to identify other constructs to be included in the nomological network of DITC. Third, relationships discussed in the articles were summarized in the Relationships folder; not only the antecedents, outcomes, moderators, and mediators in research models but also all DITC-relevant relationships were coded here. For example, “A constitutes B” represented the condition that B was a higher-order construct that was constituted by A(s); “A belongs to B” represented the condition that A was a type or subcategory of B.

To avoid duplicate coding, all the nodes generated were repeatedly scrutinized. To ensure the validity of the nodes developed and the appropriateness of the coded statements placed under them, the coding was regularly checked to ensure agreement between the two authors. By the end of the coding process, 53 nodes with 275 sub-nodes had been developed in the Nomological Network folder, 44 nodes with 134 sub-nodes had been developed in the Emerging Patterns folder, and 18 nodes had been developed in the Relationships folder. Approximately 12,000 coded statements were examined in these three folders as well as the paper summary folder.
