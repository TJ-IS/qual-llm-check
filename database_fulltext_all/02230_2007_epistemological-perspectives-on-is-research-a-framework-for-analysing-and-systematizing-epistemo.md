# Epistemological perspectives on IS research: a framework for analysing and systematizing epistemological assumptions

Jörg Becker\* & Björn Niehaves<sup>†</sup>

European Research Center for Information Systems, University of Münster, Leonardo-Campus 3, 48149 Münster, Germany, \*email: becker@ercis.uni-muenster.de, and<sup>†</sup>email: niehaves@ercis.uni-muenster.de

Abstract. Over the last three decades, a methodological pluralism has developed within information systems (IS) research. Various disciplines and many research communities as well, contribute to this discussion. However, working on the same research topic or studying the same phenomenon does not necessarily ensure mutual understanding. Especially within this multidisciplinary and international context, the epistemological assumptions made by different researchers may vary fundamentally. These assumptions exert a substantial impact on how concepts like validity, reliability, quality and rigour of research are understood. Thus, the extensive publication of epistemological assumptions is, in effect, almost mandatory. Hence, the aim of this paper is to develop an epistemological framework which can be used for systematically analysing the epistemological assumptions in IS research. Rather than attempting to identify and classify IS research paradigms, this research aims at a comprehensive discussion of epistemology within the context of IS. It seeks to contribute to building the basis for identifying similarities as well as differences between distinct IS approaches and methods. In order to demonstrate the epistemological framework, the consensus-oriented interpretivist approach to conceptual modelling is used as an example.

Keywords: epistemological framework, research paradigms, interpretivism, positivism, linguistic interpretivism

## 1. INTRODUCTION

Information systems (IS) research is multidisciplinary and multinational. Apart from IS, many other disciplines including business administration, computer science, sociology and psychology, contribute to studying the development, implementation and use of IS and information technology within organizations (see, for instance, Fitzgerald & Howcroft, 1998; Wade & Hulland, 2004). Furthermore, the contribution of many different national research communities to the discussion in IS research is very productive. An internationalization of IS research is conspicuous. Also because of the increasing shift of research emphasis from national to international institutions (see, for example, the EU framework research programmes), IS research projects involving researchers from more than a single country will become standard in the future. The publication of research results in worldwide read journals is establishing as a standard in alignment.

Thus, IS research can be seen as a rich tapestry of diverse research methods, paradigms and approaches (Benbasat & Weber, 1996; Wade & Hulland, 2004). Different academic disciplines and research communities tend to adopt distinct research methodologies and approaches (see, for instance, Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991; Chen & Hirschheim, 2004). Chen & Hirschheim (2004) conducted an empirical study analysing eight major IS publication outlets between 1991 and 2001. The examination of 1893 papers published in the American or European journals revealed that, at a methodological level, quantitative methods dominate US research on IS (71%), while 49% of papers published in the European journals apply qualitative methods. At the paradigmatic level, the vast majority (89%) of the US publications are characterized by a positivist paradigm. Although European journals also mainly publish research based on positivist principles (66%), they tend to be much more receptive to interpretivist research (34%) than US journals (see Table 1). Because of the fact that these paradigms are based on epistemological assumptions, the American-European differences at the paradigmatic level consequentially lead to an epistemological difference in alignment. (For an analysis of epistemological assumptions and especially those made by positivism and interpretivism see, for example, Hirschheim & Klein, 1989; Chen & Hirschheim, 2004)

Against the background of distinct and often inexplicit epistemological assumptions, working on the same research topic or studying the same IS phenomenon (Weber, 2003a) does not necessarily ensure mutual understanding. The potential lack of understanding in this context is a result of differing epistemological assumptions. In particular, the understanding of such concepts as validity, reliability or quality of research depends substantially on these epistemological assumptions. This is evident, for instance, in the controversy over positivist and interpretivist research within the IS field (see, for instance, Hirschheim & Klein, 1989; Walsham, 1995; Farhoomand & Drury, 1999; Weber, 2003b; 2004; Chen & Hirschheim, 2004; Dubé &

Paré, 2004). On the one hand, positivist researchers theoretically claim an intersubjective validity of their research results. The rigour imperative thus is to select and apply research methods aiming at minimizing the subjective influence of the individual researcher. On the other hand, interpretivist researchers focus more on the subjective propositions that are regarded to be relevant when individuals strive for knowledge. The rigour imperative is to select and apply research methods aiming at making the influence of the subject explicit (Klein & Myers, 1999). In respect of these fundamental differences, the discussion of epistemological assumptions of IS research is mandatory and essential.

Nevertheless, the lack of (systematic) epistemological analysis of IS research methods is apparent and discussed extensively within the discipline (see, for instance, Keen, 1980; Fitzgerald et al., 1985; Mingers, 2001). Here, a high degree of systematization in the debate over epistemological assumptions is prescribed for a number of reasons, for instance:

1 Different researchers likely focus on different epistemological aspects, in many cases as a result of the historical discourse in their discipline or research community.

2 The discussion of assumptions underlying a certain research approach may only address those epistemological aspects which seem most relevant to a given situation, for instance, at the beginning of a research project. However, in many cases, researchers other than those who commenced the project, participate in the research group which might also result in the application of additional or alternative research methods. This possibly renders the previous discussion of epistemological assumptions potentially incomplete or even obsolete.

3 The IS field is highly dynamic. This leads to constant methodological innovation. New research methods may require a consideration or analysis of other or additional epistemological aspects than those which have been addressed previously.

Thus, the systematic discussion of epistemological assumptions underlying IS research is a topic of concern to IS research both now and in the future.

For this reason, the aim of this paper is to provide a framework for analysing and systematizing the epistemological assumptions underlying IS research. After presenting the research methodology and surveying related work (Section 2), we develop the epistemological framework (Section 3). The framework consists of five epistemological questions and a discussion of selected possible answers to each question. Furthermore, selected interdependencies between certain epistemological statements will be discussed. In Section 4, the consensus-oriented interpretivist approach to conceptual modelling is discussed in order to demonstrate the application of the epistemological framework. In Section 5, we draw some conclusions and suggest possible future research directions.

## 2. RESEARCH METHODOLOGY AND RELATED WORK

The research method chosen to meet the objective of this paper is that of conceptual philosophical research. We refer to original epistemological and specific IS reference literature that is pertinent to the topic. In order to develop the epistemological framework, we thus conduct an extensive literature review which aims at collecting epistemological arguments and standpoints. Thus, the literature review does not aim at statistically analysing certain streams or trends of an IS epistemology.

• The basic literature is from the field of IS research, especially from IS journals, including MIS Quarterly (MISQ), Information Systems Research (ISR), Communications of the ACM (CACM), Information Systems Journal (ISJ) and European Journal of Information Systems (EJIS). These sources provide insight into the state-of-the-art discourse on epistemology and the philosophy of science in the specific area of IS research (for example, Hirschheim, 1985; Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991; Hirschheim et al., 1995; Weber, 2003b; 2004; Chen & Hirschheim, 2004; Wade & Hulland, 2004). Moreover, IS conference and books on IS research were taken into account.

• We furthermore conducted our literature review in the field of epistemology and philosophy of science which enabled us to take more general epistemological aspects into account. Thus, the discussion of epistemological questions is less dependent on current IS field circumstances. This has the advantage that the epistemological framework is open to future emerging research paradigms, approaches or methods.

We will therefore provide philosophical-logical, rather than empirical arguments. Nonetheless, where applicable, our arguments will also refer to empirical research results, such as those of Chen & Hirschheim (2004).

Several endeavours have been made also by IS researchers to systematically analyse different research paradigms while taking into account their different epistemological assumptions (for a selection of such efforts, see Table 2). One approach to analysing distinct ‘sociological paradigms’ can be found in Burrell & Morgan (1979). They differentiate between four paradigms: functionalism, interpretivism, radical structuralism and radical humanism. Several distinguishing criteria are given, but especially the ontological and epistemological aspects have been intensively discussed in the IS literature (for example, Hirschheim & Klein, 1989; Olaisen, 1991). We argue that Burrell and Morgan’s systematic criteria-based approach, especially the in-depth discussion of epistemological assumptions, should be taken up in this paper.

Accordingly, the epistemological framework developed in this paper takes into account this aspect. Here, the primary goal is not to classify distinct IS research paradigms, but to directly analyse the underlying epistemological assumptions (of paradigms and methods). It seeks to address a set of epistemological questions (including ontological and methodological aspects) that is more differentiated than the previous approaches. It is due to the nature of philosophy of science and epistemology that further aspects might appear in the course of time; thus, there can be no claim of completeness. However, an attempt is in fact made to address the largest possible spectrum of epistemological issues relevant to the field.

## 3. EPISTEMOLOGICAL FRAMEWORK

The discussion of epistemological issues must, at least for the time being, be considered as an open issue. For this reason, no theory based on a philosophy of science can be considered as binding for IS researchers. The individual or social-collective selection of an epistemological position, however, necessitates the extensive publication of the epistemological assumptions made. Here, epistemology can be understood as the science of analysing the way human beings comprehend knowledge about what is perceived to exist (Burrell & Morgan, 1979; Niehaves, 2005). It addresses the question of how a person can arrive at ‘true’ cognition. In order to do so, basic epistemological questions can be differentiated from one another and will be presented below in the form of an epistemological reference framework. The concept underlying this framework is the explicit breakdown of epistemological questions which are highly relevant to IS research and often discussed separately (see Table 3).

Question I (ontological aspect) of the existence of a ‘real’ world (see, for example, Walsham, 1995; Falconer & Mackay, 1999; 2000; Weber, 2004) as well as Question II of the relationship between cognition and the object of cognition, have been discussed intensively in the IS literature (see, for example, Fitzgerald et al., 1985; Hirschheim, 1985; Lee, 1991; Fitzgerald & Howcroft, 1998; Chen & Hirschheim, 2004). Both are fundamental to the discussion of positivism and interpretivism (for instance, Weber, 2004). Question III about the concept of truth has not yet been widely considered in the IS research literature. A brief mention can be found in Weber (2004) and Fitzgerald & Howcroft (1998). Nevertheless, this aspect becomes highly important in analysing, for instance: (a) the influence of language on research (Lyytinen, 1985); (b) conceptual modelling and modelling in general (especially Tarski’s semantic theory of truth); and (c) interpersonal validity/truth of research results, etc. Question IV about the origin of knowledge (see, for instance, Monod, 2003) and Question V about the means of achieving knowledge (methodological aspect) have also been discussed in recent literature (see, for instance, Boland, 1985; Hirschheim & Klein, 1989; Rathswohl, 1991; Butler, 1998).

## I. What is the object of cognition? (ontological aspect)

Ontology is the science, or the analysis of ‘what is’ and ‘how it is’ (von Foerster, 1996). In the context of this epistemological analysis, ontology reveals its relevance through analysing objects to which the process of acquiring knowledge refers. For epistemology in IS research, this means: What is the object of our research? (Monod, 2003) The process deals with the existence and nature of reality beyond the realms of pure imagination of the subject (Bunge, 1977; Decker et al., 1999).

a If, in their investigations, researchers assume a real world, one that exists independently of cognition, for instance, independent of thought and speech processes, they assume a position of ontological realism. Here, materialism can be understood as an extreme form of realism (Holbach, 1770). It assumes that all entities and processes are composed of or are reducible to matter, material forces or physical processes (Stack, 1998). This position is based on the object–subject paradigm.

b If researchers believe that abstract or mental entities have some sort of reality ‘independent’ of a ‘real world’, that is, if they perceive reality as a construct dependent on human consciousness, they assume a position of ontological idealism. Here, idealism was mainly influenced by Plato (Jackson, 2001) and can be regarded as any theory positing the primacy of spirit, mind or language over matter (see materialism, Question I, a). That can be, for instance, if they negate the existence of a real world which is independent of human thinking and speech.

c Kantianism attempts to overcome the differences between those two approaches. According to Kant, it is vital to distinguish between: (1) ‘things in themselves’ (‘Ding an sich’), referred to as noumena; and (2) the appearing of those things to an observer, referred to as phenomena (Kant, 1999). Following Kant, there exist both entities that are independent from human consciousness (noumena) as well as constructs that depend on human consciousness (phenomena). Thus, knowledge which can be acquired by an observer is restricted to phenomena. In contrast, the things themselves (noumena) are unknowable. Kantianism therefore holds that our mind forces the world we perceive (Kant, 1999).

## II. What is the relationship between cognition and the object of cognition?

This question, often regarded as the core of epistemology, deals with the relationship of cognition acquired by the subject to the object of cognition. The point is whether entities beyond human thoughts and speech can, at least in principle, be recognized as objective. There are two major answers to this question:

a In epistemological realism, objective cognition of an independent reality is possible. Epistemological realism claims the possibility of objective cognition and provides suitable measures for eliminating subject-dependent distortions of reality cognition (Loose, 1972). Because epistemological realism assumes that ‘objective’ cognition of an ‘objective reality’ is possible, it relies on the assumption of an ontological realism (see Question I, a). In IS research, such combination of an ontological and epistemological realism is often referred to as positivism (see, for instance, Banville & Landry, 1989; Hirschheim & Klein, 1989; Chen & Hirschheim, 2004).

b The understanding of cognition within the school of constructivism is subjective, i.e. ‘private (see Glasersfeld, 1986; 1987). The relationship between cognition and the object of cognition is thus determined by the subject (Lorenzen, 1987). Here, the continuum ranges from the assumption that the cognition of an ‘objective reality’ is ‘interpreted’ by the subject (interpretivism; see, for example, Burrell & Morgan, 1979; Walsham, 1995; Klein & Myers, 1999; Weber, 2004) to the assumption that cognition is ‘private’ because of the idea that there does not exist such thing as an ‘objective reality’ (radical constructivism; see, for instance, Glasersfeld, 1986; 1987).

## III. What is true cognition? (concept of truth)

A central topic in epistemology is the question as to how humans can achieve ‘true’ cognition. Expressed more intuitively, this refers to the extent to which ‘correct’ knowledge can really be obtained and how the ‘correctness’ of knowledge has to be verified. Major possible answers to this question are given by:

a Correspondence theory of truth. According to this theory, truth is a result of a correspondence which can be formulated as an analogy or equivalence between two relata. The first relatum is referred to as statement, the second is referred to as fact which is given by an ‘objective reality’ (see Question I, a). By comparing statements and facts, the former can be classified as either true or false. In the context of the correspondence view, facts thus act as truth inducers for statements, because of their assumed status as objective (Kirkham, 1992; Schmitt, 1994; Baumann, 2002). An attempt to further operationalize the term correspondence can be found in Wittgenstein (2001; see also Russell, 1956; Stenius, 1960).

b Consensus theory of truth. According to the consensus theory in its elementary form, truth results from the consensus of everyone (Habermas, 1973; Apel, 1979; Baumann, 2002):

i. A statement is true if, under ideal and optimal conditions, it is acceptable to everyone.

A variant of this concept can be, for example, that the range of truth is reduced by focusing on certain addressees. Statements about truth only involve a group of a certain size and should thus always be understood as relative to this particular group:

ii. A statement is true for a group, if it is acceptable under ideal and optimal conditions for the group.

This concept of truth implies that nothing exists or proves to be relevant in the context of a test of truth, which would not be apparent to the community/group doing the perceiving. For the search for consensus and truth, it is thus not a necessary condition that ‘objective’ facts or real world entities would exist (see Question I, a).

c Semantic theory of truth. The semantic theory of truth (see Tarski, 1944; Haak, 1978; Davidson, 1984; Kirkham, 1992) is based largely on linguistics and achieves clarity and precision of argumentation by using the compact instrument of modern semantics. Originally, the theory addressed the problem of self-referential statements that can result in logical paradoxa.<sup>1</sup> Tarski (1944; 1956) argues that truth is not possible, if statements, expressed in a certain language, can contain predicates of truth regarding the statement itself. Thus, the semantic theory of truth suggests solving the problem of self-referentiality by applying two distinct languages: (1) an object language (L) in order to express a statement (s); and (2) a meta-language (M) in order to analyse the correctness of that statement (see Table 4).

Tarski does not define the term truth. Instead, he expresses a condition for truth: the differentiation between object language and meta-language.<sup>2</sup> Furthermore, it becomes clear that truth always refers to a language, the object language (L), and thus can only be understood as relative linguistic truth.

## IV. Where does cognition originate? (source of cognition capability)

The question as to the origins of cognition, relates to the fundamental capability to perceive. In the context of IS research, the relevance of this question becomes clear if formulated as: Where does our knowledge derive from?

a Experience is regarded as one source of knowledge (impressions of senses). Experiencebased knowledge is called a posteriori or empirical knowledge (Alavi et al., 1989). The assumption of this source of cognition is often oriented towards natural science theory and is represented by the school of empiricism (see, for instance, Carnap et al., 1929; Quine, 1961; Berkley, 1975; Hume, 1978; Locke, 1982; Carnap, 2003).

b Intellect can also be assumed as a source of cognition. An object can become a matter of cognition through the conceptual efforts of the subject. Non-experience-based knowledge is referred to as a priori knowledge. The assumption of intellect as source of cognition is represented by supporters of rationalism, often also known as apriorism (see, for instance, Leibniz, 1962; Chomsky, 1965; Descartes, 1996; Bonjour, 1998).

c Kantianism can be regarded as a conciliating position that recognizes both experience and intellect as sources of cognition. According to Kant, none of these features can be preferred to another: Without a sensory element, no object would be given, and without intellect, the necessary categories for cognition would be missing. Thoughts are meaningless without content; cognitions are blind unless they are linked to specific terms (Kant, 1999).

## V. By what means can cognition be achieved? (methodological aspect)

The methodological aspect of epistemology deals with the question of how humans perceive. This question addresses the modes of acquiring knowledge.

a One way of cognition to be obtained inductively. Induction is understood as the extension from individual cases to universal phrases (Seiffert, 1996), that is, generalization. An inductive conclusion means the transfer from (observed, empirical) individual cases to a universal law (Rott, 1995). It is an a posteriori method which is often applied in the natural sciences.

b On the other hand, knowledge can be acquired through a deductive method. Deduction is the derivation of a statement (thesis A) from other statements (hypothesis A1, . . . , A ) with the help of logical conclusions (Gethmann, 1995). It is the derivation of the individual from the universal and is applied, for example, in mathematical axiom systems.

c The fundamental methodological aspect of hermeneutics is the ‘hermeneutic circle’ (Gadamer, 1990; Butler, 1998). The basic objective is to provide a way of understanding texts (Boland, 1985; Rathswohl, 1991). Here, the hermeneutic circle describes the process of reading texts against the background of previous understanding of the entire. This understanding influences the way in which a certain text is perceived. The reading of the text itself in turn shapes the understanding of the entire. Thus, according to hermeneutics, the process of gaining knowledge is influenced by a circle of (previous) understanding, gaining new knowledge, and then achieving a better understanding of the entire. Hermeneutics are often taken into account as a methodology, if both certain texts and ‘real world’ observations are not selfevident, but rather have to be interpreted appropriately (Hirschheim & Klein, 1989).

The set of questions presented here suggests a basis for the epistemological discussion of IS research approaches and offers the potential to support a comprehensive comparison of particular assumptions made in IS research.

## 4. CONSENSUS-ORIENTED INTERPRETIVIST APPROACH TO CONCEPTUAL MODELLING

In the following section, the consensus-oriented interpretivist approach to conceptual modelling (also referred to as consensus-oriented approach) is given as an example of applying the epistemological framework developed in Section 3.

Conceptual models are regarded as design artefacts which are, besides constructs, methods and instantiations, crucial elements in an IS design process (March & Smith, 1995; Hevner et al., 2004). One of the major difficulties in the context of IS design and implementation is the fit of IT artefacts designed and the requirements of the given problem situation. In practice, many IS development projects fail as a result of a miscommunication between business personnel and IS designers (Morgan & Soden, 1973; Keil, 1995; Standish Group International, 2001). Conceptual modelling is a commonly accepted approach to overcoming this communicational problem (Wand & Weber, 2002). Within the IS discipline, conceptual modelling is widely discussed (see, for instance, Batini et al., 1992; Hirschheim et al., 1995; Bodart et al., 2001; Wand & Weber, 2002; Shanks et al., 2003).

The term model (or conceptual model) is discussed by all epistemological doctrines. Here, the concept of conceptual modelling is seen in the light of the consensus-oriented interpretivist approach which is standing in the tradition of the critical linguistic approach (Kamlah & Lorenzen, 1973; Lorenzen, 1987). Based on the epistemological framework developed in Section 3, the consensus-oriented approach is based on the following basic epistemological assumptions (Table 5):

1 What is the object of cognition? (Ontological aspect). The existence of a real world is assumed, which is independent of human thoughts and speech and, for this reason, exists beyond human consciousness. Conceptual models are therefore intended to refer to a real world issue describing elements which are part of this real world. Furthermore, conceptual models are regarded as elements of a design science process which aims at solving a real world problem situation.

2 What is the relationship between cognition and the object of cognition? In the context of a consensus-oriented approach, specific importance is attached to the influence of subjects in the process of cognition: cognition is seen as subject-related. Following Kamlah & Lorenzen (1973), the main factor for this subjective influence is the particular language a subject applies. This means, on the one hand, that language is applied in order to reconstruct a real world experience; on the other hand, language also shapes the way that a certain real world situation is perceived. Here, language provides the categories and concepts which pre-construct cognition. Because of the fact that languages are regularly shared in a language community, acquiring knowledge can be regarded as a social process. In this sense, the consensusoriented approach follows the tradition of interpretivism (see, for instance, Burrell & Morgan, 1979; Klein & Myers, 1999; Weber, 2004). Here, not only the subjectivity of cognition, but also the existence of a real world, independent of human consciousness, is accepted. Against this background, a conceptual model can be understood mainly as a linguistic construction of a real world phenomenon.

3 What is true cognition? (Concept of Truth). The understanding of truth is relevant mainly with respect to the understanding of what are ‘true’ conceptual model statements. With the semantic theory of truth, Tarski develops a concept of truth which is always relative to a language (object language). Simultaneously, the existence of a meta-language is assumed, which contains the predicates of truth relating to statements of the object language. In this context, both languages ultimately emerge in linguistic communities. On the other hand, the consensus theory of truth confirms that for a group, a statement is true, if it is acceptable to this group. It becomes apparent that, both in the context of the semantic theory of truth and of consensus theory, truth is regarded as relative. In the first case, truth is relative to the language which is applied to formulate the statement. This languages is ultimately the property of a linguistic community (Kamlah & Lorenzen, 1996). In the second case, truth is relative to the community in which consensus was obtained with respect to the truth or non-truth of a statement. The foundation of this truth verification is, ultimately, the exchange of speech artefacts. Accordingly, finding a consensus within a group also requires the existence of a linguistic community.

In the context of the consensus-oriented approach to conceptual modelling, it should be assumed that truth emerges through the potential consensus of a linguistic community. Truth is thus regarded as relative to a language (semantic theory of truth) and relative towards a group (consensus theory of truth), in this case to a linguistic community. According to the consensus theory of truth (Habermas, 1973; Apel, 1979; Baumann, 2002), a statement is true if, and only if, it is acceptable for everyone. Focusing on certain business problems and IS solutions suggests that the reduction of ‘everyone’ to a group of smaller size is permitted. In this context, the concept of the consensus theory of truth, altered to this effect, might be: a statement is true (for a group), if and only if, it is acceptable for the group. This implies that truth is relative to the group in which consensus was obtained about the truth or non-truth of a certain statement. In order to express the statements within conceptual models (Wand & Weber, 2002), several modelling languages can be used, for instance, ERM (entity relationship model) or eEPC (extended event-driven process chain). By this means, models can be used as a formalized means of stating the consensus. Here, the formalized modelling language functions as an object language (L). A natural language, such as English, can be used to discuss whether the statements within a conceptual information model are ‘correct’. Accordingly, it contains the predicates of truth with respect to the statements based on object language and poses as a meta-language (M). Both languages are thus shared by the linguistic community.

4 Where does cognition originate? (Source of the cognition capability). Both empirical statements (Kamlah & Lorenzen, 1973) and a priori statements can be made, which may form the basis of conceptual models. Conceptual modelling therefore derives its results via theoretica reflection of the model contents, as well as from the implementation of the model in IS and through observation.

5 How does cognition emerge? (Methodological aspect). Conceptual models can contain both empirical and a priori knowledge. Both inductive and deductive conclusions can be accessed, firstly in the context of the model creation and secondly in the context of truth verification.

If, in the context of model creation, single statements are generalized on the basis of a set of individual tests, for example, in the context of reference modelling, the relevant process is that of induction (Becker & Schütte, 2004). Creating an information model can, however, be achieved deductively as well, for example, by attaching object-class-specific attributes to model elements on the basis of their linkage to certain object classes.

Truth verification is based on the procedure of interpersonal verification (Kamlah & Lorenzen, 1973). The formalized linguistic statements contained in a conceptual model are logically decomposed (deduction) until they are accessible as elemental statements for purposes of truth verification. This takes place by means of a group of experts who obtain a consensus. The main instruments are observation, experiments, interviewing and the interpretation of texts (Kamlah & Lorenzen, 1973). The validity of statements in the model can be confirmed, in the case of business-specific models, for example, with a single case. In the case of a pattern or reference model, however, the generalized abstraction of different individual verifications (induction) is necessary. This means that, in the context of the procedure of interpersonal verification, additional, mainly empirical, research methods are used.

Thus, the consensus-oriented approach is characterized by an interpretivist position, which is influenced mainly by the critical linguistic approach of Kamlah & Lorenzen (1973). In this regard, conceptual models contain formalized linguistic statements which are to be tested for validity in combination with additional empirical research methods. This is conducted through members of a linguistic community in order to obtain consensus. Therefore, elements of the semantic theory of truth and the consensus theory of truth are considered and used.

## 5. CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE RESEARCH

Information systems research is conducted within a multidisciplinary and international context. Consequently, the IS field can be described as a rich tapestry of different methodological approaches. Here, an epistemological framework for systematically analysing the epistemological assumptions of different research approaches and methods was developed. By offering a critical perspective on multidisciplinary and international IS research, the systematization of the framework is intended to inject new impulses into the discourse on epistemology in IS research. Furthermore, the epistemological framework was applied to the consensus-oriented interpretivist approach to conceptual modelling.

In IS research, epistemological aspects have been applied in order to define and differentiate between distinct research approaches, for instance, interpretivism vs. positivism (based on both Question I and II) or rationalism vs. empiricism (see Question III). These approaches, commonly referred to as paradigms, often coexist during the same period of time (Feyerabend, 1970; Laudan, 1977). Based on these epistemological differences, distinct approaches tend to develop distinct research focuses. They pose different kinds of research questions and suggest different ways of interpreting the results of scientific and theoretical investigations. Furthermore, they develop distinct understandings of such concepts as rigour, quality and validity. Against this background, with the help of the framework developed in this paper, we argue:

1 IS paradigms should be analysed (once more) against the background of epistemology. In this respect, we wish to pose the question: How do these approaches really differ from another? What are the logical differences? What differences can be or have been derived from the logical ones? By contrast, what differences have emerged as a consequence of historical developments and are thus not ‘logical’ differences? The epistemological framework is intended to support this analysis.

2 Epistemological and philosophical discussions are often conducted with a focus on certain specific epistemological aspects. Nevertheless, while we observe that distinct epistemological debates can be found in IS research, a widening of the focus can be very useful. What are the additional epistemological implications of a particular research approach, paradigm or method? What additional epistemological questions other IS researchers might pose with respect to a particular piece of research? The epistemological framework is intended to highlight the major epistemological perspectives relevant to the IS research field.

3 IS research paradigms often involve more than one epistemological aspect, for instance, positivism and interpretivism (see Question I and II). The set of questions presented in our epistemological framework is intended to take up this aspect. By differentiating between distinct epistemological questions, it seeks to create a transparency which invites further analysis of the interrelations between particular epistemological issues. In this paper, we could only provide examples of these interdependencies, so that the formulation of epistemological questions is regarded to be the first step.

4 Epistemology is customarily used to discuss IS research paradigms and approaches. Here, the implication is frequently made that particular research methods ‘belong’ to a certain paradigm and thus would have the same epistemological assumptions. While the latter argument is often based more on the discourse history than on philosophical logic, the following question arises: Are the implications of certain research paradigms really those of the ‘corresponding research methods as well? In searching for potential pluralism in IS research, this question arises: If particular research methods are less ‘epistemologically loaded’, does this open up potential for multi-method IS research across distinct research paradigms? In this respect, the presented set of questions in our framework is explicitly open to supporting the direct epistemological discussion of research methods.

5 The epistemological analysis conducted in this paper is intended to support and foster the quest for methodological pluralism in IS research. The explicit differentiation between particular epistemological positions is the first step. With regard to the imperative of refocusing the original and philosophical-logical differences, we suggest researching what the various approaches do and do not have in common in terms of their epistemological origins. While Kant considered the linkages between rationalism and empiricism, researchers (also in IS) currently already work, for instance, on explicating the influence of the subject on the process of gaining knowledge (Question II) in order to bridge the gap between positivism and interpretivism. With the help of basic, but differentiated epistemological questions, the presented framework seeks to bring transparency to distinct original epistemological concepts relevant to IS research.

We suggest the following as potentially constructive and viable future research directions:

1 The debate on interdependencies between certain epistemological questions and answers should be intensified. Here, a differentiation of distinct types of relations/interdependences – such as ‘logically necessary’, ‘product of the historical philosophical debate’ or ‘can be found in most dominant epistemological paradigms’ – can be very helpful.

2 In this paper, we did not discuss in depth the procedural aspects of our framework. Only by choosing the order of the epistemological questions and by discussing selected interdependencies, we could show that, for instance, first answering the ontological aspect can be useful. Nevertheless, we consider it helpful for potential applications to have procedural guidelines for using the framework.

3 An analysis of the consequences of epistemological assumptions on research evaluation is of great significance. In the current IS literature, several approaches can be found which discuss guidelines for sound IS research (see, for instance, Hevner et al., 2004). However, the discussion of epistemological aspects is frequently inadequate. Thus, the framework developed in this paper seeks to support an epistemological reflection, for instance, of those guidelines.

4 As discussed previously, the application of the epistemological framework for directly analysing the epistemological implications of research methods themselves is regarded as a productive avenue for future research. Such analysis could demonstrate how a disciplined pluralism in IS research could facilitate and support multi-method research across particular paradigms.
