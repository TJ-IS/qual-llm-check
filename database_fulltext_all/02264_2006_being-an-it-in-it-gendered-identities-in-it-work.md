# Being an ‘it’ in IT: gendered identities in IT work

Alison Adam<sup>1</sup>, Marie Griffiths<sup>1</sup>, Claire Keogh<sup>1</sup>, Karenza Moore<sup>1</sup> Helen Richardson<sup>1</sup> and Angela Tattersall<sup>1</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Information Systems Organisations and Society Research Centre, University of Salford, Salford, U.K.

Correspondence: Alison Adam, Information Systems Organisations and Society Research Centre, University of Salford, Salford M5 4WT, U.K. Tel: þ 44 161 295 3125; E-mail: a.e.adam@salford.ac.uk

## Abstract

This paper reflects on aspects of gender and IT work. The core hypothesis is that, if technical skill and masculinity are fundamentally related, then women working in IT jobs who are, in effect, challenging masculine skills by gaining them themselves, must develop a number of strategies to cope with the challenge that they feel is being made to their own gender identities and those of the men with whom they work. One strategy is for women to distance themselves from IT work; a second strategy is for women to distance themselves from their identities as women. Our results are drawn from a set of semi-structured interviews. We adopt the approach of critical research that seeks to expose asymmetric power relations in the organization and to let silenced voices be heard. This is related to the literature on silence in organizations. Within the critical approach, we chose a feminist methodology that looks towards identifying practices that are problematic for women and that acknowledges our biases and interests as researchers. Additionally, we draw upon the theoretical constructs of the gender and technology literature to theorize the relationship between gender and technical skill and how this impacts conceptions of gender identity.

Keywords: gender identity; women in IT; gender and technology

## Introduction

This paper considers aspects of gender and contemporary IT work, relating to women as IT workers and their experiences of working in IT-related jobs, focusing on the under-researched area of gender identity and its relationship to technological skill. The core hypothesis is that, if it is convincing that (technical) skill and masculinity are intimately entwined (Grint & Gill, 1995), then women in IT work who are, in effect, challenging masculine skills by gaining them themselves, must develop a number of strategies to cope with the challenge to their own gender identities and those of the men with whom they work. Such strategies involve distancing oneself from identifying as having IT skills and distancing oneself from identifying as a woman, which manifests itself in ambivalence over gender identity and raises issues of how far women speak up or remain silent in their workplaces.

The research is framed against the ‘women in IT problem’, in other words, the traditional view of women’s place in IT work, noting that women are leaving IT work in substantial numbers. Following a discussion of this, we describe the critical feminist research approach adopted in this study, which seeks to challenge the status quo, to reveal asymmetries in power relations and which actively looks to hear the voices of groups of people who are not in a dominant position in their organizations. In particular, we relate our research to the critical agenda set out by Alvesson & Willmott (2003), enhanced by studies of silence in the organization such as Bowen & Blackmon (2003). Although not usually brought to bear on a critical study, research on silence in organizations brings a useful, empirically based approach to theorizing questions of silence and speaking up.

The next section theorizes the relationship between gender and technology, and, in particular, the relationship between technical skill and masculinity. After this, we describe our research methodology and details of the interviewees who contributed to the study with an acknowledgment of the biases and interests of the researchers. The next two sections report the words of our interviewees in relationship to IT work and gender identity. Finally, a discussion section reflects on the critical approach used and the results obtained, relating these back to the critical agenda and a conclusion briefly outlines policy implications and the limitations of the present study.

## The ‘women in IT’ problem

Although a substantial literature on women’s work in IT now exists, much of it concentrates on the low number of women in IT and the continuing ‘problem’ of attracting women to the industry (Lander & Adam, 1997). This maintains a perspective where women in IT work may be regarded as problematic, and where the problem is often seen purely in terms of numbers. We do not regard low numbers of women in IT as a problem per se. However, we concur with Wajcman (1991) who argues that more women working in technological fields would make a difference not only to workplace politics but also to the type of technology that is designed, however, not necessarily in ways that we can predict. Additionally, IT offers interesting and well-paid careers and under any view of gender equity, IT opportunities should be open to women as much as to men.

Recent research suggests that retaining women in the industry is just as problematic as attracting them in the first place. Even taking into account the negative effect of the recession in the IT industry in the early years of the 21st century which saw a substantial drop in IT jobs nearly everywhere, a surprisingly large number of women are leaving IT work in the U.K. (Platman & Taylor, 2004). Writing before the recession in the IT industry, Panteli et al. (1999) noted the problem of women leaving IT, yet, despite campaigns to attract women and increasing adoption of equal opportunity policies, more than 5 years later, the situation has actually worsened. Indeed, a recent comparative survey of the IT workforce in Germany, the Netherlands and the U.K. indicates that women are leaving the U.K. IT workforce in disproportionate numbers compared to men (Platman & Taylor, 2004).

The numbers of women working as IT professionals in the U.K. doubled in the 3 years up to 1999, thereafter declining sharply in the following 4 years. Moving from 43,114 women in 1995, to a high point of 100,892 women in the U.K. IT workforce in 1999, Platman & Taylor (ibid., p. 8) report a drop to 53,759 by 2003. For men, the figures are 265,868 in 1995, progressing to a peak of 492,496 in 2000, thereafter dropping to 444,130 in 2003. As the IT industry was moving into recession anyway, the number of men in the industry has also declined, but by nothing like as much as the decline in women’s representation. Hence, the figures for women are especially stark, meaning that not only have women’s numbers declined in absolute terms, but also because the number of men in the industry has held up better, the proportion of women working in IT jobs has declined sharply. Platman and Taylor (ibid., p. 8) note the overall trend for male computing professionals is strong growth over the decade from 1995, despite the IT downturn in the early years of the 21st century. However, the peak in number of women appears short-lived followed by gradual losses after the IT downturn. They suggest that market forces may play a greater role in shaping gender representation in IT in the U.K. than has hitherto been recognized. Interestingly, although the situation in Germany and the Netherlands shows a drop in the proportion of women employed in IT, this appears to be confined to the year 2000 (ibid.).

It is difficult to attribute specific causes to this sharp drop in numbers of women in the U.K. As Platman & Taylor (ibid.) argue, the picture is clearly complex given that the reduction in numbers of women in IT is not necessarily observable to the same extent in other European countries. Nevertheless, given a fairly consistent message of low numbers coupled with barriers and campaigns to attract women yet which do not necessarily have a clear positive effect, we argue that there is still much to be discovered about the continuing situation of women in IT work. This lends support to arguments against the view of the women and the IT ‘problem’ as purely a question of campaigns to increase numbers (Henwood, 1993; Platman & Taylor, 2004), reinforcing concerns that there are aspects of women’s experiences of IT work that are not yet well understood, and that cannot be adequately captured by the traditional ‘low numbers’ approach.

Therefore, rather than casting women in IT as a problem to be solved, the complex picture of women’s involvement in IT work offers motivation for undertaking research that offers fresh theoretical and methodological insights to illuminate the situation of women in IT work, particularly in relation to the under-researched area of women’s views of gender identity in relation to IT skill. This suggests further that if we do not have to solve a numbers problem, we can obviate the pressure to seek causal links that are likely to be over simplistic in conception. Instead, we seek to build a model of women working as IT professionals, with empirical research reflecting a critical methodology resting on the theoretical constructs of gender and technology research in terms of the notion of technological skill and its relationship to gendered identity.

The focus on IT professionals, rather than on the IT industry, is appropriate given that the concept of an IT industry is becoming increasingly diffuse and difficult to define. This view is reinforced as studies vary quite substantially as to the number of IT workers quoted, suggesting there is considerable variation in what is taken to be an IT job and given that a number of women in the study did not regard themselves as being part of the IT industry. For instance, a recent report, while concurring with Platman & Taylor (2004) on the expansion of IT work, nevertheless reports higher figures, estimating that almost 900,000 people worked in ICT firms in spring 2003, and that there were over one million ICT workers, filling ICT roles in any sector (e-Skills, 2003).

The ‘women in IT problem’ first emerged as a reported phenomenon in the late 1970s and early 1980s, as it became clear that computing was not gender neutral and where the under-representation of women began to be regarded as significant (Woodfield, 2000). Since then, there have been a number of campaigns in Western countries to attract more women into IT, often part of wider campaigns addressing women’s relative absence from science and engineering (Henwood, 1993). However, as Woodfield (2000) and Henwood (1993) note, these campaigns have little effect on the poor representation of women in IT, as they ignore the structural reasons why women do not engage in technological work, concentrating, instead, on publicity campaigns and means for persuading women into the IT workplace. This is borne out by Platman & Taylor (2004, p. 8), who note that the downward trend of women in IT in the U.K. is particularly worrying when set against industry and Government-led initiatives to attract women into the profession in the U.K.

Hence, while we hesitate to make simplistic causal claims for the exit of women from IT work, we raise a number of issues that have yet to be aired, in detail, in the otherwise extensive gender and IT literature. The feminist stance adopted here is based on research on gender and technology where the relationship between technological skill and masculinity has emerge as a major factor in understanding the gender–technology relation. Such is the obduracy and strength of the link between technological mastery and masculinity that researchers argue that it is a major element in the construction of masculine identity (Cockburn, 1985; Wajcman, 1991; Faulkner, 2000; Woodfield, 2000). If technological skill has such a strong link with male identity, then we need to consider how technological skill links with feminine identity. If, as has been argued (Woodfield, 2000), masculine skills and attributes are accorded higher status than the so-called feminine attributes, one strategy that women could adopt would be to embrace technological skill as a part of feminine identity in order to gain prestige associated with a masculine attribute. Indeed, there are studies that demonstrate women explicitly embracing the gaining of skills in software engineering, at least in part because of the status that may accrue from gaining such skills (Henwood, 1991). This acknowledges, not only the pleasure in gaining status, but the pleasure in gaining technical mastery (Hacker, 1989) which women can enjoy as much as men. Nevertheless, ambivalence attends women’s technological mastery. In our study, such ambivalence manifests itself in two distinct ways; ambivalence over claiming ownership of technological skills, and ambivalence over being perceived as a woman with technological skills.

## A critical research agenda

In undertaking this research, we adopted a critical research framework that aims to understand the organizational and socio-cultural context in which the experiences of the women in this study are embedded. With such an understanding comes the possibility of change (Kvasny et al., 2005).

Research in the critical management tradition acknowledges that traditional management research tends to reproduce dominant institutions and interests (Alvesson & Willmott, 2003). A critical approach signals a disillusionment with management theory’s focus on management as rational action, preferring to focus on understanding the political nature of the organization. One of the guiding precepts of the critical approach towards management studies is the ideal of representing the interests and perspectives of groups other than those in a management position (Alvesson & Willmott, 2003, p. 203), particularly those who are currently marginal in management practice and theory.

Alvesson & Willmott (2003) note that the significance of gender relations has been neglected and that feminist voices have only recently begun to be heard only recently in the study of organizations. They offer five elements of a research agenda to guide a critical approach towards studies of organizational and management practice. We draw on these guidelines in our research, briefly outlining them here (in italics) and referring back to them in the concluding sections to consider them in the context of our empirical study. The first element (ibid., p. 16) concerns arguing for the adoption of a non-objective view of management and organizational processes. In this, we leave to one side the view that an objective reality can be mirrored in management techniques, instead looking towards the social construction of economic and social reality. Under this view, a major activity involves exposing asymmetric power relations, recognizing the privilege of senior management in setting agendas, defining reality and hindering emancipatory change. A related issue involves counteracting discursive closure where taken for granted assumptions freeze the existing social order allowing no space for challenging the existing social order. Revealing the partiality of shared interests points to Habermas’s (1984) concept of communicative rationality with the desire to bring conflicts into the open for rational discussion and resolution. While this remains something of an ideal, the concept of shared interests means that an existing social order may be opened up for discussion and critique not assuming that conflict is the only way forward and that consensus may be formed around the critique of an existing social order. Finally, appreciating the centrality of language and social action suggests that communication is key throughout the elements of this research agenda. Language not only carries historically established meanings but is crucial in constituting the identity of a social group.

Although the literature on silence within organizations has not been linked to a critical approach to date, we should note the significant congruence of silence studies, and their utility in offering empirical examples, with the will towards letting silenced voices be heard, which is central to the critical approach, given that significant numbers of empirical studies are only now beginning to emerge from the critical paradigm (Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2005). Morrison & Milliken (2000) argue that silence pervades organizations, with those below top management often afraid or unwilling to raise concerns. This is reinforced by the ‘spirals of silence’ model (Bowen & Blackmon, 2003), a dynamic, circular model where silence is reinforced by prevailing opinion, where that opinion becomes more entrenched which further reinforces silence suggesting that individuals are less likely to speak up against a majority opinion if they fear that their peers will not support them. Importantly, for the present study, among all the issues that may affect organizational voice or silence, Bowen & Blackmon (2003) point to personal identity as a crucial factor, particularly when personal identity differs in some significant way from the group norm:

An individual whose demographic attributes, social or personal identity differ from the group norm may not feel able to talk about important aspects of their personal identity. This may initiate an individual spiral of silence, where they feel inhibited in making meaningful contributions to the group and in turn the organization. Small personal silences are thus reinforced and subsequently escalate to inhibit voice on wider organizational issues. People who differ in ‘invisible’ ways such as sexual orientation must choose whether to speak out or remain silent about their difference. Thus, in this second spiral of silence, employees who initially remain silent about their personal identities may fail to exercise organizational voice even if they have valuable contributions that they could make (Bowen & Blackmon, 2003, p. 1394).

Critical theory emphasizes communication (witness Habermas’s (1984) concepts of communicative rationality and the ‘ideal speech situation’ where all voices have an equal right to be heard), yet critical theory gives us very few pointers, in practice, as to how communication may be hindered or promoted in organizations. Studies such as Bowen & Blackmon (2003) therefore, offer valuable empirical examples of communication, particularly among groups that are outside the dominant managerial group. This is especially important as they link silence and speaking up to aspects of personal identity. In their study, respondents were gay, lesbian or bisexual, which they regard as a form of ‘invisible diversity.’ As our study relates to gender, this is a visible, readily identifiable form of diversity, nevertheless Bowen and Blackmon’s findings as to the ability of marginalized groups to speak up or remain silent, resonates with issues that our respondents raised as we discuss in the concluding sections of the paper.

As gender is the central concern of the paper, we couple a critical framework with a feminist approach, which aims to identify and characterize gender inequalities (Oakley, 2000). By focusing on social and economic inequalities and calling for change, feminism and critical theory are natural bedfellows although, as Martin (2003) argues, for the most part, their synergies are yet to be explored. Hence, we are hopeful that the present study can act as an example of a nascent research approach which brings critical and feminist theory together in fruitful ways.

In undertaking this research, therefore, as critical feminist researchers it is incumbent on us to reflect on our own position as researchers. As Richardson & Howcroft (2006, p. 58) argue, for critical research, the traditional role of researcher as mirror is replaced by the metaphor of researcher as lens, where the researcher takes an active role. It is not just a question of seeing the world from the respondent’s point of view but also being aware of the political processes at work ‘behind the scenes’. Rather than reflecting exclusively individuals and the meanings they give to their social locus, the lens focuses on systems of meanings within which meanings arise. This is an important difference from much interpretivist research as it allows researchers to consider the conditions under which meaning is created by social actors.

Of the six researchers involved in the project, five have worked in the U.K. IT industry for various periods and at various times over the last 25 years. We have, ourselves, experienced some of the issues that our respondents describe. It could be argued, therefore, that we approached this research with a level of bias. However, in the spirit of critical research, we acknowledge our predispositions and biases. Rather than being a hindrance, we regard our own position as experienced women in IT as a factor which allowed our respondents to open up, when they had not done so before, and describe their views in much more detail, as we were clearly empathetic to their experiences. For some respondents, the flood gates opened and the experience was truly cathartic, as if no one had listened to their workplace concerns in this way before, thus striking a chord with Bowen & Blackmon’s (2003) findings on the way that marginalized groups may be locked into a spiral of silence on organizational issues. This, in turn, resonates with the arguments of researchers working on feminist research methodology (Oakley, 2000) who contend that we must acknowledge our own role in research, as privileged gatekeepers of epistemology, with a duty to let our respondents’ voices be heard, rather than adopting a pretence of impossible neutrality and objectivity. Furthermore, this recognizes that the researcher is not a neutral observer of subjects, rather that the researcher and research subjects mutually shape a shared reality.

## Gender and technology

Although aspects of women’s careers may be common across a range of disciplines and industries, nevertheless we argue that the gender and technology literature suggests a strong relationship between masculinity and technology that may present particular difficulties for women. Leaving aside domestic technology, such as washing machines and cookers, which has been linked to the feminine and women’s role in the home (Cowan, 1989), a wide range of authors has noted the strong link between masculinity and technology (Cockburn, 1983, 1985; Wajcman 1991; Henwood, 1993; Grint & Gill, 1995; Faulkner, 2000; Green & Adam, 2001; Margolis & Fisher, 2003; Adam, 2005).

Womens’ more general exclusion from technology may be seen in terms of the historical and socio-cultural construction of technology as a ‘masculine domain’ (Wajcman, 1991; Woodfield, 2000). We concentrate on a key aspect of that relationship for the present study, namely the link between masculinity and technological skill.

As so much of the rhetoric surrounding putative shortages in the IT industry involves the concept of a shortage of skills it is important to note the strong link between notion of skill, in particular technical skill, and the ways in which something becomes defined as a technical skill, and therefore a masculine attribute. Indeed, Wajcman (1991) and Cockburn (1985) identify technical skill and masculinity as mutually constitutive. Cockburn (1985) argues that power differences between the sexes and their relationship to technology were historically consolidated in the development of capitalism and the move to manufacturing production outside the home. Skilled working class men formed into trade unions from which women could be excluded and hence this served to exclude them from gaining skills that could earn them a living.

Cockburn’s (1983) groundbreaking research on the development of the print industry shows how the relationship between gender and technical skill is consolidated and maintained even through radical shifts in technology. The work of male craft workers in the printing industry underwent radical transformation with the introduction of computer technology. This lessened the requirement for physical strength and also constituted an acknowledged ‘deskilling’ of the work involved. However, by means of strong craft unions the male workers were able to argue that they were a distinct group with distinct qualities. This preserved the perception of their status as skilled workers but also served to contribute to the traditional marginalization of women in the print industry. Cockburn (1983) argues that this is an example of the way in which the official definition of skilled work always moves in line with the shifts of men’s role in the workplace.

As Grint & Gill (1995, p. 9) note:

It could be argued that there is a dialectical relationship between women and ‘skill’, such that women are concentrated in jobs which are deemed unskilled, and, conversely, that those occupations in which women constitute the majority of workers come to be seen as relatively less skilled than those dominated by men. ‘Skill’ is not some objectively identifiable quality, but rather is an ideological category, one over which women were (and continue to be) denied the rights of contestation.

Woodfield (2000, pp. 148–149) argues that the initial construction of ‘skilled’ status in a job, and its subsequent attribution to those who work in that job and similar ones has as much to do with the gender of the worker as it has to do with any objective measures of proficiency.

Putting all these aspects of the concept of skill together prompts the central hypothesis of the current project, namely that if (technical) skill and masculinity are as fundamentally entwined, to the extent that they shape each other, as the authors cited above suggest, then, women in the IT industry who challenge these masculine skills by gaining them themselves, must develop a number of strategies to cope with what is a distinct challenge to their own gender identities and those of the men with whom they work.

One strategy is not to enter the IT workforce in the first place – the ‘we can, we don’t want to’ approach (Siann, 1997). A second strategy is to leave the IT workforce altogether in the face of a working environment which continues to be challenging for women – this may relate to the drop in numbers of women in the U.K. IT workforce, a phenomenon that Panteli et al. (1999) recognize and that Platman & Taylor (2004) argue is accelerating. However, we acknowledge that it is not at all clear why the recent downturn in numbers of women in the U.K. should be quite so sharp. Finally, in recognition that women in IT may pose something of a challenge to masculinity, and indeed to their own femininity, when they acquire technical skills, a third strategy is to try render one’s gender invisible – the idea of becoming an ‘it’ rather than a woman. As we discuss below, it is this strategy of trying to deny one’s gender identity, which may be accompanied by distancing of oneself from IT and ‘technical’ skills, which marks out, working life for many of our respondents.

Although a small sample, there was a remarkable amount of commonality in regard to the emphasis the respondents placed on certain concerns, most notably the question of gender identity in relation to IT work. The interviews were typically of around 90 min duration, and conducted at either the participant’s office or a venue that was convenient to both interviewees and interviewers (such as a cafe) and were recorded and transcribed. Respondents were found through a snowballing technique, using a set of initial contacts already known to one or other of the researchers, and who, in turn, offered other contact names. Our research is informed by feminist methodologies of acknowledging our positions as researchers reflexively (Oakley, 2000) and incorporating the now substantial body of research on gender and technology (e.g., Cockburn, 1985; Wajcman, 1991), particularly on the link between masculinity and technological skill. A research approach based on these two theoretical vectors, namely a critical approach informed by gender and technology, was used, where categories were identified in the interview data and related back to the theoretical vectors.

We explore how women in the IT sector perceive their gender identities in relation to technological work and the coping strategies they use to maintain an identity with which they feel comfortable. This also reflects back on the nature of IT work which was defined by our respondents in a somewhat distant way. This suggests that, while they struggle with their own gender identities in relation to technology, they are also struggling with the nature of technology and how to define it in relation to themselves and their work, a struggle which reflects the mutual shaping of concepts of gender and technology (Grint & Gill, 1995).

## ‘I’m not in IT buty’

Our interviewees and questionnaire respondents did express a considerable level of job satisfaction, particularly with the opportunity to combine technical skills and knowledge with people and communication skills, yet they often downplayed their technical knowledge when talking about their work in general terms, emphasizing instead their role in using technology to manage and/or help people. A striking initial feature is the vehemence of women saying ‘I’m not in IT buty’ - ‘I’m in sales’, ‘I’m in the people part of the organization’, ‘I’m a manager’ – and overwhelmingly ‘I’m not in IT’.

Partly these responses reflect the changing nature of IT in the workplace and IT-related occupations. It is increasingly difficult to speak of a separate and clearly identifiable ‘IT industry’ or sector in the U.K. and other European countries. Occupations involved in design and creative technology are embedded in business processes and management generally and in the informatics and communications areas, in particular, and therefore not in traditional ‘IT’ as we historically have known it – programming, systems analysis and design, tool development and evaluation and so on.

Such a view, of not belonging directly to the IT sector, but to a wider work group which uses IT is reflected in Platman & Taylor’s (2004) research where they argue that many women are involved in wider employment rather than the IT industry itself, which, in turn becomes harder and harder to define as a separate function as IT permeates through many more jobs and sectors. Taking this on board, we acknowledge that the IT sector is diverse and that much of women’s engagement with IT may well lie outside the traditional IT sector. Nevertheless, we argue that a further reason for women failing to identify themselves as IT professionals lies in their ambivalence in identifying themselves in terms of technical skills. As our data reveal, women struggle to get comfortable in IT roles. Hence, distancing oneself from IT and identifying oneself as having hybrid technical–people skills in a sector other than IT many represent a very positive coping strategy for many women. They do not then have to confront, in quite such a direct way, issues of gender identity in a technical job which the following section describes.

## On being an ‘it’ in IT

The situation and, in fact, ‘problem’ of being a woman and issues of self and identity at work in male-dominated IT workplaces was revealed again and again in our interviews. As Tess put it:

I have almost forgot that I’m a female in this business, I’m not denigrating being a woman in any way but I’ve almost had to push aside being a female and get on with the job like any man would do.

Xena spoke of how certain characteristics that she perceived to be ‘masculine’ do not come ‘naturally’ to her:

I think I’ve come to realise that more and more recently as I get round more clients is that it’s all about confidence especially around men, sometimes I tend to take a back seat especially in my environment you have to see yourself. I think you almost have to have more arrogance. That doesn’t come naturally to mey.I think you’ve got to ‘fit in’, you’ve not got to be too girly, you’ve got to be quite tough and that’s not naturally me.’

Vanessa: ‘I’d say it’s a tough industry (IT), not many women would have entered into it and the ones that have entered will have shied away by the fact that they are women and they would have to adapt to be just like men.’

For many women, the demands they face in IT work may not, however, take the form of overt discrimination, but instead be a more subtle ‘demand’ to ‘fit in’ with male-dominated workplace cultures. Yvonne spoke of how she was able to ‘fit in’ to the IT industry given her previous ‘tomboy’ tendencies and drinking abilities, as the following quote indicates:

Well I used to be a bit of a tomboy when I was a kid but I don’t think that I’m one of the lads but when we have sales conferences and things like that, you know you have to do all the drinking and everything, really you have to fit in with the majority of men and not be upset by their banter. You can’t let it offend you. You wouldn’t last two minutes if you did.

Woodfield (2000) contends that belonging to a gender group is obligatory. We cannot step outside our genders; it is one of the most pervasive, if not the most pervasive, ways we have of ordering and categorizing our world. Individuals can no more be a gender less than an ageless self. Indeed, ‘social categories provide individuals with both a social location and a value relative to other socially categorised individuals.’ (ibid., p. 148) Yet, ‘women who choose perform outside their gendered scripts’ (Davies & Thomas, 2000, p. 1134), in this case working as a minority in an occupation regarded as ‘masculine’, as well as in managerial roles where women are also not commonplace, are often perceived as having masculine characteristics. Hence, if women who step outside the gender norm in the workplace are designated masculine, it may be preferable to mask one’s gender altogether.

Displays of ‘femininity’ in the IT workplace were deemed problematic by some women who have contributed to our fieldwork. Vanessa recounted how in the early stages of her career she ‘downplayed’ her femininity, becoming a genderless ‘it’ in an attempt to make herself less visible, even consciously adopting a masculine way of dressing, until she came to a point in her career at which she thought she would be recognized for her talents alone. Only then did she become more proactive in relation to her gender status, although her proactiveness led to jokes and comments about her sexuality. She recounts how:

When I first started work (as a vendor), before I started at Company X the dress code was still very formal. Then when I came back to work for Company X as an employee they changed the rules and all of a sudden it became smart casual, and very soon I would only be wearing chinos and polo shirts because I really shouldn’t be standing out. That took me a year and to the guys OK I was Vanessa, I was a woman but I was an ‘it’. I was not a woman, I was not a man I was a personyI dressed like I dressed (chinos and polo shirt) for a year until I attended one of the [WITI – Women In Technology International – a women in technology empowerment group] meetings on personal impact and how you should build your own confidence and I said ‘I’m too scared to show myself because I work in an all-male environment’. Every time I mentioned I was going to a meeting they always said ‘Oh you’re going to one of your lesbian meetings’ at work.

Tess adds to this:

I don’t know about my femininity but I have become more focused and self driven than quite a lot of men. I suppose I had a weird sort of compliment from my last manager who said to me ‘you know Tess you’ve got more balls than most men’ which I took to be quite a compliment.

The IT industry spokespeople often identify feminine characteristics that would be of benefit to the work involved. Wilma in fact identified some of these:

I’m a woman and I’ve got more people skills – patience – I’m better at listening and questioning – I don’t make assumptions as quickly – I don’t rule things out.

However, as Woodfield (2000) points out – women and traits associated with femininity are often significantly undervalued. Vanessa noticed this when she finally decided to ditch her polo shirts and chinos:

I wore a v necked shirt and they could physically see what was there and my guys really had trouble accepting that. I’ve had partners that come around and said oh and when you have got a minute could you rebook my hotel for me because they would automatically assume that I was part of the administration or a support person.

The discomfort that these women felt with their gender roles in an IT environment is palpable. So strongly masculine is the world of technological skills within which these women work that the only way they were able to achieve a level of comfort was by denying their own gender and becoming an ‘it.’ When Vanessa dressed in casual trousers and polo shirt she achieved a kind of invisible neutrality in her IT workplace. When she started to wear more feminine clothes she became visible but was mistaken for a secretary.

Clearly, it could be argued that the ambivalence these women felt about being women in a man’s world could apply to other areas of life in the organization. However, we argue that technology and technological skill, and especially IT skill, is so strongly associated with masculinity, it is extremely hard for women to ‘get comfortable’, to feel at ease, to feel that they belong in such a working environment. Yet the ease and confidence with which men identify themselves with IT, displaying no such ambivalence about their masculinity, feeling naturally at home in an IT environment and with no worries that what they would wear would identify them in a particular way, runs all the way through stories of men in the world of IT, whether in descriptions of hacking (Levy, 1984), the race to develop new computer systems (Kidder, 1981) and in more conventional IT workplaces (Tierney, 1995).

## Discussion

We now relate our empirical study to the five elements of the Alvesson & Willmott (2003) critical research agenda. First, we have adopted a non-objective view of management and organizational processes. We have deliberately avoided assuming that there is a readily identifiable IT industry within which to find objective views of a reality of labour processes. We explicitly looked to the views of our respondents on their working environments. Rather than explicitly presenting their views as non-objective and as contrary to the views of management, many of the women respondents expressed their ‘non-objective’ views in terms of an ambivalence as to their own positions in relation to IT and to gender identity. This resonates with research on silence in organizations where members of marginal groups may be reluctant to speak out in criticism of their organization (Bowen & Blackmon, 2003).

The second element of a critical research agenda involves exposing asymmetric power relations. Once again rather than explicitly arguing against a management view, our respondents gave many empirical examples of unequal power relations, such as Vanessa who adopted a male style of dress as she felt she should not stand out, or Yvonne who recognized the need not to be upset by masculine banter. A third element of the critical agenda involves exposing discursive closure where taken for granted assumptions freeze the existing social order. All the way through our research and the reported words of the women in the study, we witness their sophisticated awareness of the prevailing social order, where they feel they must not stand out visibly as women and where they must have considerable strength of will and tenacity to succeed. In terms of the fourth element, revealing the partiality of shared interests and the opening up of an existing social order for critique, we feel that this is problematic for the women in our study. For instance, Vanessa’s attendance at women in technology meetings was seen as threatening to her colleagues who called these her ‘lesbian meetings.’ While these meetings gave her confidence to challenge the existing order where she felt able to dress in a more overtly feminine way, rather than in the androgynous style she had adopted hitherto, she was then mistaken for a secretary and asked to make hotel bookings in a social order where the assumption is that women in the workplace must be in secretarial rather than technical roles.

The final aspect of Alvesson’s & Willmott’s (2003) critical agenda involves appreciating the centrality of communication and language. In this study, the question of remaining silent or speaking up is paramount.

Commenting on silent voices Law (1991, p. 2) makes this generalization:

First the dispossessed have no voice at all. Then, when they start to create a voice, they are derided. Then they are told that they are wrong, or they are told that this was something that everyone knew all along. Then they are told that they are a danger. Then finally, in a very partial form, it may be that their voices are heard and taken seriously. And it has been a struggle all the way.

This links in with our desire to have our research located as part of the critical research tradition. Although positive things were reported, nevertheless some of the things that these women told us are quite stark. As researchers who had worked for many years both in the IT industry and in education, we were, nevertheless, surprised by the level of difficulties that women continue to experience in IT. However, as part of a critical agenda it is important to uncover and report their actual words. We also note that it was difficult for us to report some of our findings to wider industry groups. As part of the project we held an industry workshop and although we were careful not to make our presentation hostile or critical, some of the findings we report above were not palatable to some of our industrial contacts. This points to a level of reflexivity about critical research. Where our respondents did not always feel they could speak up in the workplace, as critical researchers it was also sometimes difficult for us to speak up in certain forums where the critical nature of our research was viewed as hostile to the prevailing culture.

From our experiences through this research, we seem to be trapped in a ‘no voice-danger’ loop in terms of equality policy and the world of IT work still remains a place of struggle for many of the women in this study. The emphasis of the critical agenda on communication and the emphasis we place on silence vs speaking up has a strong resonance with the work of Bowen & Blackmon (2003), who argue that individuals in marginal groups can be trapped in a ‘spiral of silence’. Remaining silent about key aspects of personal identity feeds into silence about organizational processes. Although Bowen & Blackmon’s (2003) study related to potentially invisible aspects of personal identity, in that they were concerned with sexuality as part of personal identity, the marginality of our respondents feeds into concerns about their identities in relation to IT skills and in relation to gender, where some subjects appeared to make their genders invisible in order to fit into the prevailing culture.

Cyclical views on skills shortages and women’s relationship to them in IT work, the relationship between masculinity and technical skill and women’s threat to masculinity when they acquire these technical skills, their discomfort in relation to technical skills, and the response of male organizational groups to them all reflect in women’s continuing marginalization in IT work in the U.K.

Yet women find ways of coping with the obdurate link between masculine skill and technology. They may not enter the IT profession at all, and certainly they do not appear to do so in the numbers that policy makers would like them to, despite all the initiatives to recruit more women into IT. Second, they may leave the IT profession, and this seems to be happening to a surprising degree in the U.K. Third, they may try to cope at work by somehow negating their gender, by being an ‘it in IT’. But this is a high price to pay and tough to sustain. Women pay for it by struggling with confidence. They know that they have to adopt these strategies to fit in but are still excluded from masculine space.

## Conclusion: policy implications

In terms of policy making and managing diversity, given that IT-related work is losing more women than it is currently recruiting in the U.K., there is little evidence that organizational culture is changing (Panteli et al. 1999). There may indeed be an economic argument for creating a more diverse workforce, not least in terms of reflecting diverse customer interests, yet basing calls for the greater representation of women in IT primarily on the economic and business case leaves women vulnerable to any movement in the market and susceptible to shift in diversity discourses. The demand for an ethical perspective should also have a comparable voice in that individuals should be treated equally and fairly in the workplace because of legal, moral, social and ethical reasons not just because it is financially beneficial.

In conclusion, we acknowledge the limitations of our reported study in terms of size, although we hope to paint a much fuller picture of women’s participation in the U.K. IT workforce through a set of sister projects.<sup>2</sup> Additionally, the remit of our project did not include interviewing men, and we recognize that men’s experiences of IT is not always positive, despite the links between masculinity and technological skill, and may be counter-intuitive, for example, as in the Eriksson-Zetterquist & Knights (2004) study where older men proved more resistant to new technology than did women, suggesting that the gender–technology relation is far from fixed but is constantly remade and renegotiated. Additionally, for reasons of space, we have barely touched on some important features of work in the digital economy, notably the long hours culture and equal pay.

Admitting the interplay of these complex and interrelated issues, we nevertheless hope to have captured one of the significant factors in relation to women’s positioning in U.K. IT work, namely the question of gender identity and the way that women distance themselves both from claiming to be working centrally in IT and from being a women in relation to IT skills. By acknowledging our position as researchers in this work and drawing on the gender and technology literature for theoretical inspiration we hope, at least, to have accomplished part of the first task of the critical project, namely letting the voices of women in IT be heard.
