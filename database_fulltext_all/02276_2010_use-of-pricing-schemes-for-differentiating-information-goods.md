## 1. Introduction

Information goods have become a large and growing part of the U.S. economy. Information goods are commonly defined as products that can be digitized such as books, software, videos, music, telecommunications services, etc. Vendors of information goods offer many different pricing schemes. For example, Microsoft offers different pricing schemes such as Open License and Enterprise Agreement. On the other hand, Salesforce.com, a leading provider of customer relationship management (CRM) software, offers only one pricing scheme—subscription-based pricing. Similarly Apple’s iTunes music download service offers only one pricing scheme—a unit price for each song. Yahoo’s competing music download service also offers only one pricing scheme—subscription service with a monthly fee for unlimited downloads. How can we explain these outcomes where some firms offer multiple pricing schemes and others offer only one pricing scheme, but choose a pricing scheme that is different from their competitor? In this paper we examine this heterogeneity in pricing schemes and offer an explanation for this observed phenomenon. We posit that certain pricing schemes fit certain buyers’ usage patterns. For example, Sprint’s Simply Everything Plan provides unlimited voice and data usage and is targeted at high usage consumers, whereas AT&T’s Go Phone plan is targeted at users with low demand but higher average willingness to pay (WTP) per minute. We show that the use of different pricing schemes by undifferentiated or weakly differentiated sellers can enhance differentiation among competing sellers. Sellers that are well differentiated find it optimal to offer multiple pricing schemes. These findings support the commonly observed phenomenon of multiple pricing schemes offered by information goods vendors such as cell phone providers, software publishers and music download services.

To better understand the role of pricing schemes and differentiation, note that sellers prefer to be differentiated from their competitors because enhancing differentiation helps to alleviate price competition and

increases profit. Our finding that pricing schemes can be used to enhance differentiation can be viewed as the aggregation of a number of smaller steps. We model buyers that use multiple units of the information good. A firm may buy a number of single-user software licenses for their employees and an individual may need many “minutes” for cell phone calling. Buyers can be heterogeneous in their utility for the information good, both for the first unit as well as subsequent units. The first important effect of different pricing schemes is that they affect the number of units consumed by a buyer. For example, a rational cell phone user is likely to consume fewer minutes on a metered calling plan relative to consumption on a plan that offers unlimited calling for a fixed price. In a corporate environment, managers are likely to encourage wider adoption of software when it is purchased as a site license compared to when the manager has to pay for each additional license. The second point to note is that when the heterogeneity in the individual demands of different consumers is such that some consumers prefer to purchase fewer units but at a higher average price while others prefer to purchase more units but at a lower average price, then there exist a set of prices for the metered pricing scheme and the unlimited usage scheme such that the low usage consumers prefer the metered pricing scheme while the high usage consumers prefer the unlimited consumption scheme. For example, at typical prices, cell phone users who expect limited usage in emergencies such as senior citizens prefer a per minute calling plan whereas others (such as teenagers) prefer calling plans with large or unlimited minutes. Similarly small businesses often buy singleuser licenses while large corporations routinely purchase enterprise (site) licenses. Third, note that when vendors are undifferentiated or weakly differentiated from their competitors, they face intense price competition and earn relatively small profits. Combining the three previous observations, we can see that when one seller offers metered pricing while the other charges a flat fee for unlimited consumption, buyers will prefer different vendors depending on their individual demand functions. Thus the two sellers can enhance their differentiation based on their choice of different pricing schemes. Finally, such a strategy can form a Nash equilibrium when changing prices is relatively easier than changing pricing schemes. This assumption has been used in prior literature and is discussed in §2.2. When the sellers are already well differentiated they choose to offer multiple pricing schemes. In either case, whether we have weakly or strongly differentiated sellers, such markets will exhibit a variety of pricing schemes. We will demonstrate our findings for stylized demand functions and for pricing schemes that are versions of two part pricing—unitpricing scheme and fixed-fee unlimited-consumption pricing scheme.

## 1.1. Literature

Recent literature in marketing and economics has examined nonlinear pricing strategies in the context of duopoly competition (Armstrong and Vickers 2001, Miravete and Roller 2003, Yang and Ye 2008). Armstrong and Vickers (2001) offer an innovative approach by modeling competing sellers as selling utility to consumers. They use their framework to analyze the impact of sellers’ adoption of tariffs that enable price discrimination on consumer surplus and social welfare. Miravete and Roller (2003) use a horizontal differentiation framework to analyze competing providers of cellular services and to estimate parameters such as marginal costs. They identify a Nash equilibrium in which both sellers offer a twopart tariff with a symmetric structure based on their parameters. Yang and Ye (2008) study the impact of competition on the number of contracts offered by each seller finding that duopolists offer more contract variety relative to a monopolist. These papers focus on identifying symmetric equilibria where competing firms offer the same pricing scheme. In contrast our focus is on identifying the Pareto-dominant asymmetric equilibrium where competing sellers offer different pricing schemes.

Several researchers have analyzed the optimality of various pricing schemes using two stage games. Thisse and Vives (1992) seek to determine whether basing point pricing (BPP) can emerge as an equilibrium in static and dynamic games. They analyze a two-stage game where the sellers determine price policy in the first stage and prices in the second stage. Zhang and Sexton (2001) examine equilibria when a duopsony can offer either free-on-board (FOB) or uniform delivered pricing (UDP) in a two stage game. They use a horizontal differentiation model and find that an equilibrium where the two firms offer different pricing schemes can emerge only when the shipping costs are moderate relative to the value of the underlying product. In other words such an equilibrium is not obtained in the Bertrand setting because the equilibrium requires spatial heterogeneity and substantial shipping costs. Last, Lal and Rao (1997) have examined every-day-low-pricing (EDLP) and promotional pricing strategy for horizontally differentiated retailers in a two stage game. They show that competing retailers may offer different pricing strategies when consumers’ travel and search costs satisfy certain conditions.

Most pricing models can be classified as either vertical models where all buyers agree on a quality based rank-ordering of the product offerings (see, for example, see Bhargava and Choudhary 2001, 2004, 2008) or horizontal models where buyers have heterogenous preferences among products even when they are offered at the same price. Horizontal differentiation models are based on the work of Hotelling (1929) and refined by d’Aspremont et al. (1979), who proposed the principle of maximal differentiation. Whereas vertical models assume that consumers agree on a rank ordering of competing products based on their quality; horizontal models capture the fit between users with different requirements and products that may fit some users’ requirements better than others. For example, comparing The New York Times to The Wall Street Journal, it is likely that users who are interested in politics and culture may prefer The New York Times while financial analysts and investors are likely to prefer The Wall Street Journal. Another example is that of a bank and a small arts studio that want to buy database software. A bank is likely to require strong security features in the database software whereas an arts studio may focus more on ease of installation and use. Thus different users have different requirements and products may better meet the needs of certain segments of users. Thus when users do not agree on a rank ordering of competing products, a horizontal model is appropriate. Horizontal differentiation models have been used widely in information systems, marketing and economics literature. Dewan et al. (2003) use horizontal models to examine online product customization. They find that while product customization reduces differentiation, it does not aggravate price competition for the standard product. Dewan et al. (2004) study media concentration among websites that are horizontally differentiated. Telang et al. (2004) study horizontal differentiation among Internet search engines. Viswanathan (2005) uses a horizontal model to examine competition between firms selling across differentiated channels in the presence of network effects and switching costs.

Several papers have examined fixed fee and usage based pricing schemes. Danaher (2002) analyzes data from a field experiment of mobile phone subscribers who are offered contracts with different access (fixed monthly) fee and usage fees. They estimate price elasticities for the usage and access fees and report different price sensitivity for usage and access fee in their two segment model. Jain and Kannan (2002) analyze pricing of a library information service and show that the two sellers may offer different pricing schemes to exploit consumer uncertainty about demand but only when marginal costs are strictly positive. Masuda and Whang (2006) examine the performance of “fixed up-to” (FUT) plans and show that a monopolist earns at least as much profit with a simple FUT menu plan as with any other nonlinear pricing scheme. Sundararajan (2004) shows that a monopolist information goods provider will benefit from providing fixed fee pricing in addition to a nonlinear usage based pricing scheme in the presence of nonzero transactions costs. Among other pricing strategies, Desai and Purohit (2004) identify potential equilibria when a retailer can offer a fixed pricing scheme or choose to haggle over prices. In their model haggling imposes costs on both buyers and sellers and more price sensitive buyers incur lower opportunity costs of haggling. Chellappa et al. (2010) examine data from the domestic U.S. airline market finding support for their hypothesis that price formats are an important source of price dispersion.

In this paper, we show that sellers can use different pricing schemes to differentiate themselves when there is sufficient heterogeneity in buyer demand for multiple units of an information good (buyers need not have any intrinsic preference for any pricing scheme). We demonstrate that the adoption of different pricing schemes by weakly differentiated sellers is a Pareto-dominant Nash equilibrium. Contrary to prior literature (Jain and Kannan 2002), we show that such a strategy can be profitable for information goods with negligible marginal costs of production. We show that pricing schemes can help sellers avoid the Bertrand equilibrium when they are completely undifferentiated. We also show that it is optimal for sellers to offer multiple pricing schemes when they are well differentiated even in the absence of transactions costs. We discuss the similarities between differentiation based on pricing schemes and horizontal differentiation.

We begin in $\ S 2$ with a model of duopolistic sellers selling a commoditized information good. We extend this to a model with horizontally differentiated sellers in §3. We discuss our findings and their relationship to prior literature in §4 and conclude in §5.

## 2. The Case of Commoditized Information Goods

Our analysis focuses on two commonly observed and widely studied pricing schemes: site licensing or unlimited usage pricing and per unit pricing or metered usage pricing. These can be considered special cases of two-part pricing. To improve exposition, we will discuss our model in the context of buyers as firms that want to purchase software licences for some or all of their employees. The site-pricing scheme allows an unlimited number of users to access the purchased software while the unit-pricing scheme charges for each user.

We model two undifferentiated sellers with commoditized (perfectly substitutable) information goods with no marginal costs of production. Buyer are firms with multiple employees that could benefit from the software product. Buyers are perfectly informed about prices and there are no transactions costs. We assume that there exist two classes of buyers, one with a declining marginal WTP (D-type) for multiple units and the second with a constant marginal WTP (C-type) for a fixed number of units. For example, a firm may have declining marginal WTP for Adobe Acrobat software when it is used by individual employees for productivity improvements. On the other hand, firms that plan to use Acrobat as part of their enterprise system for process automation and work flow require certain employees to use the software and therefore need a fixed number of licenses.

## 2.2. Duopoly Competition

In this section, we determine the optimal pricing strategy for two firms selling a commoditized information good. We will show that under certain conditions the sellers will each offer a single-pricing scheme that is different from its competitor’s pricing scheme. We model a two-stage game where the sellers determine the pricing scheme in the first stage and their optimal prices in the second stage. Similar two-stage games where the pricing scheme is determined in the first stage and prices in the second stage have been used in prior literature (see, for example, Desai and Purohit 2004, Jain and Kannan 2002, Lal and Rao 1997, Thisse and Vives 1992, Zhang and Sexton 2001). This formulation is appropriate when sellers can change prices more easily relative to changes in their pricing scheme. Changes in the pricing scheme typically require substantial attention from senior executives, lengthy experimental trials, and substantial investments in advertising to inform buyers, in hardware and software to change MIS and accounting systems, and investments in employee retraining.<sup>1</sup> In comparison many sellers routinely change prices. For example, airlines and online retailers such as Amazon change prices every day at negligible cost.<sup>2</sup> The determination of pricing schemes is closely tied to the business model of the firm and is a higher level managerial decision relative to the determination of optimal prices. Nevertheless the ease of changing prices relative to pricing schemes is an assumption and our results should be viewed in this context.

In the first stage, each seller selects from three options among pricing schemes: (i) unit-pricing scheme, (ii) site-pricing scheme, and (iii) both pricing schemes. This yields a 3×3 matrix (see Table 1) and we need to determine the profit earned by each firm in the nine potential scenarios.

Lemma 1. When each firm offers one and the same pricing scheme, then the Bertrand equilibrium is obtained thus equilibrium prices and profits are equal to zero.

The proof is straightforward. If the firms select identical pricing schemes in the first stage then in the second stage the pricing game reduces to the Bertrand game. Buyers purchase from the seller that offers the lower price. Any positive price cannot be sustained in equilibrium as the firms have an incentive undercut each other’s price by a small amount. Hence, the Bertrand equilibrium is obtained.

Lemma 2. When one firm offers both pricing schemes, then irrespective of the choice of pricing scheme of the other firm, the Bertrand equilibrium is obtained.

Again the proof is simple: For ease of exposition, let firm A offer both unit- and site-licensing pricing schemes and firm B offer only the unit-pricing scheme. In the pricing subgame (second stage), the firms compete directly in the unit-pricing scheme (offered by both firms). Note that for any positive unit price offered by firm B, firm A can improve its profit by offering a unit price that is marginally lower. This leads to Bertrand price competition in the unitpricing scheme as each firm has an incentive to undercut the other as long as prices are strictly positive (Lemma 1). This causes unit prices to fall to zero, at which point all buyers can buy an unlimited number of licenses at no cost. Hence, none of the buyers purchase site licenses from firm A for a strictly positive price. Therefore neither firm earns any profits. The argument is easily extended to the case where firm B offers only the site-pricing scheme while firm A offers both pricing schemes.

Proposition 4 establishes the Pareto-dominant pure strategy equilibria for the two-stage game. It shows that when the sellers are highly differentiated they should offer both pricing schemes. This strategy is optimal for well differentiated sellers because the loss of incremental differentiation from the use of distinct pricing schemes is relatively small for sellers when they are already well differentiated. They can target both segments of buyers by offering both pricing schemes so that buyers can self-select the pricing scheme that is congruous to their usage pattern. In contrast when the sellers are weakly differentiated, the incremental differentiation provided by the use of distinct pricing schemes helps to mitigate price competition and increases profit.

Proposition 4 also extends the region over which an asymmetric equilibrium in pricing schemes has been shown to exist. Using a different model, Jain and Kannan (2002) find that sellers’ optimal prices fall to zero when marginal costs are negligible. In contrast, we have shown that asymmetric pricing schemes can be sustained in equilibrium even when marginal costs are zero. ## 4. Discussion

We seek to explain the observed diversity in pricing schemes for information goods and whether sellers can use pricing schemes as a strategic tool to position themselves in the marketplace. While much of the prior research in pricing of information goods has sought to determine the optimal pricing scheme, this paper shows that in a competitive environment, pricing schemes can be viewed as a strategic tool for differentiation and for targeting customer segments. To the best of our knowledge this is the first analytical paper that examines the selection of asymmetric pricing schemes for goods with negligible marginal costs and no transactions costs. Recent work has shown that it is optimal for a monopolist to offer multiple pricing schemes—usage and flat fee pricing schemes (Sundararajan 2004) in the presence of positive transactions costs. This leaves open the question of why competing sellers would choose to offer different pricing schemes for the same information good. For example, Apple’s iTunes music download service offers only one pricing scheme—a unit price for each song. In contrast, Yahoo’s competing music download service also offers only one pricing scheme—subscription service with a monthly fee for unlimited downloads. In the market for CRM Software, Salesforce.com offers only one pricing scheme— subscription-based pricing—whereas Microsoft offers a competing CRM solution (Microsoft Dynamics CRM), but it is not available under the subscription pricing scheme.

We showed in §2 that certain pricing schemes are better suited to certain buyer segments relative to other pricing schemes. When a seller offers a pricing scheme to a buyer segment that fits their usage pattern, then the buyers’ aggregate unit demand is higher and relatively inelastic. This causes the buyer’s aggregate WTP to be higher leading to higher revenues and profits for sellers. We show in Proposition 3 that undifferentiated sellers can sustain equilibrium prices that are strictly positive thus avoiding the Bertrand equilibrium. This is a novel use of pricing schemes and extends the list of previously known ways to avoid the Bertrand equilibrium (see §5.2 of Tirole 1988). We have shown that sellers may adopt asymmetric pricing schemes thereby earning substantial profits from commoditized information goods, even with perfectly rational, fully informed buyers, no marginal costs and no transactions costs.

In §3, we extend the model to the case of information goods that are horizontally differentiated and show that the level of product differentiation plays a role in the selection of pricing schemes. In particular, we showed in Proposition 4, that sellers will use different pricing schemes when their products are not well differentiated whereas in a monopolistic market or a market with a well differentiated duopoly, the sellers will use the same set of pricing schemes. We demonstrated the existence of an equilibrium with asymmetric pricing schemes when marginal costs are zero. The results in §3 also help explain the observed diversity in pricing schemes offered by information goods vendors.

In §2.2, we examine consumer surplus and deadweight loss to draw an analogy between differentiation based on pricing schemes and horizontal (spatial)

models of differentiation. Pricing schemes with higher congruousness with a buyer segment typically yield higher social welfare relative to a pricing scheme with lower congruousness. This is similar to the impact of transportation costs in spatial models of differentiation. The concept of congruousness may be generalizable to other forms of differentiation based on different buyer characteristics. For example, if buyers are heterogeneous in their level of risk aversion, competing sellers may be able to design different contracts for different segments and use them for differentiation. Buyer segments can also be heterogeneous when purchasing event tickets in advance because of heterogeneity in the likelihood of using the tickets. Future research can examine whether congruousness can be used effectively to identify appropriate contracts for different-buyer-segments.

Our results help managers understand the implications of modifying pricing schemes and the link between pricing schemes and the target market. For example, Sprint’s Incoming Free pricing scheme charges a higher per minute rate for outgoing calls but incoming calls are free. This makes it more attractive to users whose calling pattern involves mostly incoming calls. Similarly, T-Mobile offers an innovative pricing scheme called myFaves which allows users to make free unlimited calls to five people irrespective of their carrier. This calling plan makes T-Mobile more attractive to a segment of users who make a lot of calls to five or fewer people. Our results also show that the addition of a pricing scheme that does not focus on a niche segment of buyers is likely to intensify price competition. Thus pricing schemes need to be carefully crafted with a focus on the targeted buyers. Sellers with highly differentiated products and market power are more likely to be able to successfully offer a range of pricing schemes. New or smaller firms that lack a distinct brand and those who wish to target a niche market are likely to offer a limited set of pricing schemes. This can be tested empirically in the future. There is anecdotal evidence that supports our findings. For example, Oracle is the market leader in the CRM market and offers a number of different pricing schemes. Similarly Microsoft offers a range of pricing schemes for their dominant Windows operating system. On the other hand Salesforce.com, a smaller CRM vendor offers only one pricing scheme; iGate Corp. offers a single-pricing scheme for business process outsourcing; Websense.com, a software firm in the Employee Internet Management market offers only per-seat pricing while its competitor Apreo.com offers a fixed price for 100 licenses.

We conclude in the following section by listing some limitations of our study and directions for future research.

## 5. Conclusion

We have analyzed a duopoly market for a commoditized information good and then extended results to horizontally differentiated sellers. Our results offer a potential explanation for the observed heterogeneity in pricing schemes. While we model the degree of differentiation as exogenously determined, in the real world, firms may find it optimal to further tweak product features to fit the buyer segment that is selected based on their pricing scheme. Our results are derived in the context of information goods with negligible marginal costs. These results are robust to small marginal costs but for large enough marginal costs, the site-pricing scheme ceases to be viable. Buyers’ ability to resell physical goods limits the use of site pricing to information goods and services that cannot be arbitraged. Our results can be used to generate empirically testable hypotheses that can be the subject of future research. Future research can extend this paper to a broader set of pricing schemes. Given the stylized nature of the model, we examined a limited set of factors whereas many other factors are likely to affect the set of pricing schemes offered by a firm in the real world. Future extensions can examine the impact of other factors such as product line and the entry and exit of competing sellers. Future research can also develop necessary and sufficient conditions for asymmetric equilibrium using more general utility or demand functions.
