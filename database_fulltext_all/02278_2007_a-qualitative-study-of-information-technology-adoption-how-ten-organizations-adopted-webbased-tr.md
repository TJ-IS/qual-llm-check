# A qualitative study of information technology adoption: how ten organizations adopted Web-based training

Simon C H Chan\* & Eric W T Ngai<sup>†</sup>

Department of Management and Marketing, The Hong Kong Polytechnic University, Hung Hom, Kowloon, Hong Kong, \*email: simon.ch.chan@polyu.edu.hk, and <sup>†</sup>email: mswtngai@polyu.edu.hk

Abstract. Web-based training (WBT) has become prevalent in business organizations. This study used evidence from 10 organizations in Hong Kong to identify the significant factors that influenced their decision to adopt WBT. A qualitative field study was used. This study concluded the following three key factors significantly affect the adoption of WBT in Hong Kong: (1) the perceived benefits/costs of WBT, (2) organizational readiness, and (3) external pressures. The results and implications are discussed.

Keywords: Web-based training, qualitative study, IT adoption, perceived benefits, organizational readiness, external pressures

## INTRODUCTION

Web-based training (WBT) has rapidly developed in the business and academic fields over the past decade. It has been hailed as one of the fastest growing, knowledge-based industries with a transforming influence on education and corporate training and development across the globe (Sloman, 2001). Organizations are increasingly investing in the use of information technology (IT) to support all aspects of organizational work from group work to individual teaching, training and learning (Isakowitz et al., 1998). The opportunities for continuous improvement and lifelong learning suggest that WBT could be very important both to organizations and to individuals. There is already evidence that some organizations have benefited from WBT (Cone, 2000; Galagan, 2000). However, despite numerous studies on the implementation of WBT, there is a lack of qualitative research on the underlying factors behind the decision to adopt WBT.

Web-based training is a training method for distance learning that uses the technology of the Web, the internet, intranets and extranets (Hall, 1997). WBT refers to the communication of information via the World Wide Web (WWW) with the intention of providing instruction (Chamers & Lee, 2004). WBT provides a rich, collaborative learning environment to end users, which is available 24 h a day, 7 days a week, and with geographical independence (Davis, 2001). WBT allows individual learners who cannot undergo traditional training to attend classes in their homes or offices through the use of the internet or a company’s intranet (Levi et al., 1999). It reaches geographically dispersed employees quickly and at a low cost. Individual learners can learn what they want at their own pace (Davis, 2001). WBT allows the sharing of information and makes it possible to communicate worldwide, facilitating continuous access to data and allowing individual learners more control over their own learning. WBT is more cost-effective than traditional training methods in the long run (Beer, 2000; McDermott, 2001).

Web-based training allows organizations to provide training and information for employees across barriers of time and space. It is a medium that is flexible and can be changed quickly to keep pace with a changing business environment. WBT allows organizations to meet their immediate and strategic needs for a flexible, well-trained workforce. It promises lower distribution costs for just-in-time training at any time to any location (Simmons, 2002). However, while the potential benefits of WBT are substantial, they cannot be attained unless WBT is adopted. Considerations involved in the decision to adopt IT have been widely publicized (Lefebvre et al., 1991; Chau & Tam, 2000; Martin et al., 2003). However, relatively little empirical research using a qualitative approach has been undertaken on the adoption of WBT.

Based on the studies of Rogers (1983) and Damanpour (1991), the adoption of WBT can be viewed as an extension of IT adoption. Some factors are based on attributes of IT adoption, including the relative advantages of IT adoption, its compatibility, complexity, trialability and observability (Rogers, 1983). External pressures from an organization’s competitors are factors that encourage the adoption of IT. For example, Flanagin (2000) noted the importance of social pressures in decisions on website adoption. Surprisingly, the results indicated that social pressures were found to be more significant discriminators of adopters and non-adopters than organizational features, perceived advantages and perceived disadvantages.

Recently, there has been a dramatic increase in the number of organizations that have adopted WBT in the USA (Chamers & Lee, 2004). In understanding the phenomena of the adoption of WBT by organizations, it appears that the primary focus has been on its applications and benefits, with limited emphasis upon the antecedents of its adoption, which is a vital issue for organizations if they are to utilize its well-publicized applications and benefits. The main aim of this study is to identify factors that influence the adoption of WBT. There are three main reasons why this study deals specifically with the adoption of WBT. First, despite the popular and increasing significance of WBT in organizations, little effort has been made to identify the antecedents of WBT adoption (Chamers & Lee, 2004). The IT adoption literature provides a fundamental basis and rich conceptual framework for understanding the adoption of WBT, e.g. the adoption of the internet (Lefebvre et al., 1991) and the adoption of websites (Flanagin, 2000). Second, with the unique characteristics of WBT in the human resource management (HRM) field, it is fruitful to test whether WBT adoption is different from other types of IT adoption. Third, there is a lack of systematic research on the adoption of WBT in Asia. Given the competitive nature of the Hong Kong business market, insights regarding WBT adoption are of value for HRM practitioners.

This paper presents the results of a qualitative field study of 10 organizations in Hong Kong. The cases reflect the experience and concerns of HRM practitioners who have struggled with their decision to adopt WBT. WBT is defined as a kind of training method, which an organization starts to become aware of, searches for, selects and finally decides to adopt (Chamers & Lee, 2004). Sometimes, organizations start to become aware of WBT but are reluctant to adopt and invest in it. From an organization’s perspective, early adopters may be taking an unacceptable risk because of adoption uncertainties while late adopters may lose competitive advantage benefits through not providing novel services.

## LITERATURE REVIEW

Information technology has been emphasized as a strategic tool for enhancing training delivery and improving organizational performance, leading to optimized efficiencies. Over the last few decades, more and more researchers have been looking into IT adoption decisions from a both organizational and individual perspective. In the IT adoption literature, Rogers’ (Rogers, 1983; 1995) model appears to be the most widely accepted model for identifying critical characteristics in adoptions (Premkumar & Roberts, 1999; Thong, 1999). Rogers’s (1995) classical adoption model embraces relative advantage, complexity, compatibility, observability and trialability. Rogers (1995) and Bacon (1992) highlighted cost as an important determinant of IT adoption. Also, the image factor was found to be another determinant in IT adoption (Rogers, 1995). Even though Rogers (1995) highlighted the importance of the image factor in IT adoption, he suggested that IT adoption could be studied from the perspective of relative advantages.

Teo et al. (1997) examined factors affecting internet adoption among large businesses based in Singapore using a contingency model. The work concluded that organizational and technological factors were more important than environmental factors in internet adoption. More specifically, an aggressive technology policy, compatibility of the internet with organizational culture and infrastructure, and top management support were the most significant contingent factors affecting internet adoption. Relative advantage was also considered to be important, but to a lesser extent. Studies in the adoption of IT involving other forms of technology indicate that organizations are likely to consider numerous significant factors when contemplating IT adoption; for example, the adoption of the internet (Lefebvre et al., 1991), open systems (Chau & Tam, 2000) and electronic commerce (EC) (Grandon & Pearson, 2004). Top management support often plays a significant role in the judgement decision (McGinnis & Ackelsberg, 1983; Quinn, 1985). Also, large organizations that are most likely to adopt IT often have top management who have a positive attitude towards its adoption, and who are innovative and knowledgeable about IT (Thong & Yap, 1995). Culture and trust are other potential factors that influence decisions in IT adoption (Gefen et al., 2005).

Some studies have focused on particular types of IT adoption. Electronic data interchange (EDI) is an example in which EDI provides an electronic link with customers in small businesses (Kuan & Chau, 2001). A conceptual model using a technology-organizationenvironment framework was proposed as a useful approach for examining factors affecting the decision to adopt EDI. The findings indicated that, for small businesses, while the direct benefits are perceived to be higher by adopter firms than by non-adopter firms, the indirect benefits are not perceived differently by either adopters or non-adopters, contrary to the findings in studies on large businesses (Saunders & Clark, 1992). In addition, adopter firms perceive the financial costs of adoption to be lower and their technical competence to be higher that nonadopters firms do. Another example in the adoption of IT involves internet-based interorganizational information systems (IBIS), which are a promising platform for exchanging information along business channels (Soliman & Janz, 2004). In a field-based survey, the findings identified the factors that significantly affect the decision to adopt IBIS as pressures felt from trading partners, pressures felt from competitors, establishing costs, network reliability, data security, scalability, complexity, top management support and trust between trading partners.

Chau & Tam (2000) developed a model for the adoption of open systems, incorporating aspects of the external environment, organizational technology and the characteristics of the technology itself. Looi (2005) examined the effect of five factors on the adoption of EC among small and medium-sized enterprises in Brunei Darussalam. A review of the literature shows that owner characteristics such as lack of perceived relative advantage, lack of knowledge and lack of trust are significant inhibitors while environment characteristics such as competitive pressures and government support are significant motivators of EC in Brunei Darussalam. While Nolan et al. (1993) proposed a framework for IT adoption and organizational learning, Floyd & Zahra (1990) further evaluated the effect of fit between competitive strategy and IT adoption on organizational performance in small banks. Karahanna et al. (1999) compared the pre-adoption and post-adoption beliefs in IT adoption across time. From an individual perspective, Riemenschneider et al. (2003) used a ‘combine and conquer’ strategy with the theory of planned behaviour and the technology acceptable model applying a series of loosely to tightly integrated models to the IT adoption decisions of small business executives regarding the adoption of a website. Davis et al. (1989) examined the user acceptance of computer technology whereas Briggs et al. (1999) investigated the use of group support systems in a technology transition model.

Within the well-established IT adoption literature, however, WBT research has only been conducted in the past decade. Most studies have examined the applications and the role of WBT in organizations (e.g. Chamers & Lee, 2004; Long & Smith, 2004), and have focused on the principles of WBT design, the effectiveness of training delivered through the internet and the implementation process. Chamers & Lee (2004) explored the unique characteristics of WBT and discussed issues including considerations of the cost-effectiveness of using WBT and the impact that WBT has on both instructors and individual learners in an organization. Another main focus of WBT research has been on the benefits of its training methods. WBT is fairly easy for organizations to develop and can be adapted quickly to address the changing needs of an organization, making it an attractive complement or alternative to traditional methods of training (Hall, 1997). The internet has brought a new dimension to distance learning by providing a new mechanism to deliver training (Greenberg, 1998). One main goal for an organization in adopting WBT is to increase access to instruction and resources for employees (Gibson & Gibson, 1995). To conclude, the relative youth of this field means there is a limited number of studies on WBT adoption, although there are many IT adoption studies in the MIS literature. ## FRAMEWORK FOR IT ADOPTION

The framework for IT adoption used is to identify and describe the significant factors affecting the adoption of WBT as shown in Figure 1, which is mainly based on the internet adoption model developed in a study by Mehrtens et al. (2001), who identified the following three factors as being those that affect the adoption decision:

1 Perceived benefits/costs. This refers to the benefits/costs in terms of efficacy arising from the relative advantages/disadvantages of the internet over traditional methods of communicating with customers and others. This is because the internet is an effective way for employees to gather information and is also a business tool to build the firm’s image and to promote it nationally and internationally.

2 Organizational readiness. This includes the level of internet knowledge among non-IT professionals, and whether there are adequate computer systems within the firm to access and use the internet without major problems.

3 External pressure. This refers to pressure from existing internet users who expect the firm to be an internet user and want them to communicate electronically.

The adoption of WBT can be considered as an extension of the internet adoption model (Mehrtens et al., 2001), which has many of the same benefits as those claimed for the adoption of WBT. For example, WBT offers many perceived benefits, including instantaneousness, and allows the instructors and individual learners to interact via the Web in ‘real time’ (Hall, 1997). Kruse & Keil (2000) and Hall (1997) have discussed the advantages and disadvantages of using WBT. Furthermore, financial resources and technological readiness offer the opportunity for the organization to study WBT adoption. External pressure is another factor in the model by Iacovou et al. (1995) and Mehrtens et al. (2001) that has been argued to influence the IT adoption decision. For example, there can be pressure from competitors for an organization to adopt WBT (Klein, 1998).

Based on the above arguments, the framework of IT adoption is suitable for the case of WBT. The aims of this paper, which follows Mehrtens et al.’s (2001) internet adoption model, are (1) to identify the factors that facilitate the adoption of WBT, and (2) to field-test the validity of the concepts applied to the model with a qualitative approach.

## RESEARCH METHOD

The aim of this research is to identify the significant factors in the adoption of WBT by extending the IT adoption model to WBT contexts using a qualitative field study. The main aim can be stated in the form of the question ‘why do organizations adopt WBT?’. The contemporary nature of WBT favours the qualitative approach, which is particularly appropriate where the application in the area can be tested (Eisenhardt, 1989; Ryan et al., 2002). A qualitative study captures the reality in substantial detail, and is particularly useful when a natural setting or a focus on contemporary events is needed.

A qualitative field study can be used to generate, describe, or test a theory (Yin, 1994; 2003). As this study does not aim to predict future actions, the focus is on providing a description of the antecedents and on identifying the significant factors affecting WBT adoption using an extension of the IT adoption model. A qualitative approach has been criticized for lacking scientific generalizability (Scapens, 1990). However, it has been argued that the objective of qualitative study research is not to draw inferences about some larger population, but rather to generalize back to a theory or application (Yin, 1994).

Replication logic is composed of two components: theoretical replication and literal replication. First, theoretical replication occurs when cases are chosen because they produce contrasting results for predictable reasons. Second, within literal replication, cases are selected based on their similarities, and are chosen because they specifically support the theoretical explanation. This study follows the second approach in analysing IT adoption in WBT contexts. Benbasat et al. (1987) pointed out that the qualitative approach is suitable for investigating certain types of problems, especially those in which research and theory are at their early or formative stages; and difficult, practice-based problems, where the experiences of the actors are important and the context of action is critical.

## RESEARCH DESIGN

The research design is driven by the qualitative nature of the study. Conducting a qualitative study allows basic frameworks to evolve to the point of being able to identify those factors that affect the adoption of WBT.

## Sampling

This study used 10 organizations as the sample for a qualitative field study as recommended by Eisenhardt (1989). Table 2 summarizes the profiles of each organization. However, after each field study, an evaluation with respect to the necessity for further evaluation was undertaken. In accordance with Eisenhardt (1989), no new studies were added when the framework saturation had been reached.

After examining the sample size, the unit of analysis for this study was at the organization level in adopting WBT. As all decisions are made by individuals, the major method of collecting data was through semi-interviews with personnel who had been key champions in the decision to adopt WBT. The researchers decided to conduct ad hoc, semistructured and open-ended interviews. Interviews were face-to-face, one-on-one and in-person. The subjects were required to be senior respondents within the organization and to be informed about HR matters. Where possible, the interviews were recorded on audiocassettes and subsequently transcribed. Four out of 10 interviews were not recorded, so these raw data were written up immediately after the interview. Besides conducting semi-interviews as the core of the data collection, triangulation of evidence was achieved by examining each organization’s website, HR handbook, and by interviewing other participants who had been involved in the issues of WBT adoption. All these data were analysed based on the researchers’ judgement in order to prepare the background framework in each sample organization. This rich information provided for designing the interview questions, preparing for the interview, and analysing each organization for further discussion.

## Process of gaining access

There were two main stages in gaining access and obtaining a qualitative study from the 10 organizations. Figure 2 shows the sampling process of the study. The details of each stage are reported as follows:

## Stage 1

In a competitive business environment, such as in Hong Kong, it was difficult to contact organizations that were interested in the issue of WBT adoption for qualitative study. Because of this problem, it proved extremely difficult to gain access to explore how organizations went through the adoption decision process or even at what stage they were with regard to considering the matter. Stage 1 of the sampling process started with an informal meeting with one representative from a local WBT vendor in Hong Kong, which provides an e-platform system and service to its clients. The vendor provided the sample contact list in Hong Kong, which included organizations that had already adopted WBT or those that were potentially interested in adopting WBT. The researchers made a series of telephone calls and wrote a number of letters to invite these organizations to join the research study, but, unfortunately, received no response for 3 months. As a result, it was decided to develop other means of approaching the target informants.

The researchers started searching for a series of local conferences or workshops related to the topics of WBT, e-learning, training and development and IT adoption, on the assumption that these topics were related to identifying significant factors that influence the adoption of WBT. Fortunately, one of the Hong Kong universities organized an IT showcase and an exhibition e-learning symposium; and three conferences, organized by two different local HRM associations and one private training consultancy firm, were held in Hong Kong entitled ‘HRM Annual Conference: Creating an e-learning environment’, ‘Training Evaluation and Measurement in the 21st Century: Integrating e-learning’ and ‘Boosting Effectiveness in Corporate Training’. The researchers attended all of these conferences and workshops and actively approached the associations, presenters and attendees with their research interests. These associations gave an opportunity to the researchers to contact the presenters and attendees, as it was assumed those organizations that sent representatives to attend these events would have relevant experience in adopting WBT, the intention to adopt WBT, or an interest in WBT or e-learning practices. Over a period of 3 months, the researchers were able to establish contact and become acquainted with informed respondents, including general managers, senior HR managers and training and development managers, among the organizations. The target sample for the qualitative study was chosen through direct contacts. Twenty potential respondents were contacted by sending letters or e-mails, followed up with telephone calls inviting them to participate in our study. Ten organizations expressed a willingness to cooperate with the research.

## Stage 2

The issues to be explored in the interviews were developed, focusing on the adoption of WBT in Hong Kong organizations. Organizations with a well-established WBT system, or those that were in the midst of establishing or planning to establish such a system, or even those that were merely willing to establish WBT, were also included in this study. The organizations were ranked according to the nature of their businesses and labelled with letters from A to J. The first in-depth semi-interviews and collection of internal material regarding WBT adoption took place. Prior to the interviews, a set of open-ended questions (see Appendix A) with concepts derived from the internet adoption model (Mehrtens et al., 2001) was posted to each interviewee, allowing them time to consider and reflect on the issue, rather than having to respond in an ad hoc fashion. The aim was to allow participants to express beliefs that were based on their personal experience freely. All of the subsequent semi-interviews were held on a one-toone basis. The approach to conducting interviews and the topics were restructured and rephrased as the qualitative research progressed. The questions were open-ended and each ‘revelation’ was incorporated into an issue to be probed in the next interview. Each interview was accomplished by:

1 gathering information on the current status of the organization, how it got started adopting WBT and why it was interested in using WBT; or, if not, whether the organizations had the intention to adopt WBT;

2 identifying the significant factors in the adoption of WBT (perceived benefits/costs, organizational readiness and external pressures);

3 asking the participants to provide examples of WBT adoption activities; and

4 examining the main impact of adopting WBT.

The choices of informants and interaction were also driven by the intention to expose any similarities and dissimilarities, providing the opportunity to compare and understand key relationships in a WBT setting. Miles & Huberman (1984) have termed this as conceptual-driven sequential sampling. The interview process lasted for another 3 months before the data collection process was completed.

## Data analysis

The data across the 10 organizations were analysed to detect similarities and differences. Within Organization A, the iterative approach to analysing data was more open-ended than for the others. The focus was on the development of core and subthemes through the content analysis. The data were then categorized. Once all the data had been analysed, the core and subthemes were reorganized. These themes became a set of key common concepts that linked up with a number of related subconcepts. These preliminary sets of themes and subthemes guided the remaining cases, allowing the process of data analysis to be more precise.

Following the ‘constant comparative analysis method’ (Yin, 1994; 2003), data from the remaining organizations were contrasted with the core concepts of Organization A, and vice versa. This analysis employed Miles & Huberman’s (1984) method for across-case contrasts and pattern clustering, which involved matrix displays. The same process that was employed for Organization A was followed with the remaining organizations. The data were first grouped into the initial concepts and subconcepts generated by Organization A. It was found that the preliminary concepts generated by Organization A, however, did not necessarily conform to the results emerging from the remaining organizations. Emerging concepts were checked by examining them across participants, which further served to make the emerging themes, subthemes and concepts more rigorous. This analysis forced the confrontation of the emerging explanation with possible alternatives.

## THE TEN ORGANIZATIONS IN HONG KONG

Evidence from the 10 organizations in Hong Kong was used to test whether the case of WBT adoption generally follows the IT adoption model. The 10 organizations in Hong Kong consisted of a public sector department organization, an international retailing firm, an IT firm, a utility organization, an internet firm, an insurance company, a jewellery-retailing oriented organization, a Japanese-invested store, a construction organization and a printed circuit board organization. They ranged in size from 90 employees to approximately 14 000 employees. They were typically formed in a hierarchical structure, with systematic HRM practices. For more detailed descriptions of the 10 organizations, see Appendix B.

## APPLICATIONS OF WBT ADOPTION CASES

The 10 organizations were used to test the preliminary IT adoption model in Hong Kong. The multicase qualitative analysis showed that the framework of the IT adoption model could be used in a WBT context. The three components of internet adoption model (Mehrtens et al., 2001), which are treated as one in much of the subsequent discussion, are named: (1) perceived benefits/costs, (2) organizational readiness, and (3) external pressures.

## Perceived benefits/costs

Web-based training has recently come to be considered as one of the most effective training methods (Hall, 1997; Chamers & Lee, 2004). Based to a certain extent on reasons offered by the 10 organizations, two types of perceived benefits/costs stood out, which can be categorized as direct advantages/disadvantages and indirect advantages/disadvantages. Direct advantages/disadvantages refer to immediate and tangible benefits/costs that the organization or individual users would enjoy by using WBT. For example, users can benefit from receiving WBT anytime, anywhere. Indirect advantages/disadvantages are those benefits/costs that are less tangible and difficult to measure. For example, WBT improves communication between trainers and trainees. A summary of the findings is given in Table 4.

## Direct advantages/disadvantages

Organizations often gave examples of the relative advantages of WBT compared with traditional methods of training, such as the ability to deliver training anytime and anywhere (Hall, 1997). The results indicate that WBT can deliver training to any location, which was especially good for international organizations with different branches in different regions. For example, Organization B stated that, ‘Individual learners can access WBT courses 24-h a day; they can repeat the program any number of times until they meet their objectives. Courses can be taken at the office, at home, or anywhere individual learners feel comfortable’. Comments from other organizations included, ‘With the improvement of IT, training is no longer bounded by geographical location’ (Organization E). Organization A stated that ‘In rapidly changing businesses, employees must spend considerable time in training. WBT can help by providing custom-tailored training that requires less time to take by eliminating the time required to trave to the site of training and by breaking training into short segments that learners can fit among their regular work duties’. ‘From a trainers’ point of view, WBT eliminates the need for instructors to travel to the site of training’ (Organization I).

Furthermore, an important aspect of the decision to adopt WBT was the advantage of timeliness over traditional forms of training methods. For example, ‘The use of WBT can save more allocation time than traditional training methods. Employees can receive training when they are available’ (Organization G). Another direct advantage is that WBT is more convenient and flexible than traditional training methods. WBT can be deployed to the whole organization at once and does not require booking auditoriums, making travel reservations, or training large numbers of trainers and trainees. For instance, ‘Traditional training methods, whether in-house or outsourced are inflexible and can only be run in predetermined places and dates’ (Organization C). Based on these findings, WBT is a relatively timely training method that allows individual learners to avoid any changes of class arrangements and prevents them from missing learning opportunities. ‘Our organization would like to provide more learning opportunities while our trainees are eager to attend more training courses online (Organization B).

One favoured concept of WBT, which is the sharing of standardized information, also formed part of the direct advantages. Few organizations realize that the sharing of standardized information can be increased through WBT adoption. By providing universal access to uniform, high-quality training, WBT becomes a powerful tool for knowledge management. Half of the organizations (Organizations A, B, D, I, J) felt that among the perceived benefits of WBT was the ability to often share the same information in the same way, at the same time, between instructors and individual learners. For example, organization H stated that ‘Because selfdirected WBT does not require a quorum of learners to help targeted individuals acquire the skills and knowledge they need to advance rapidly, through WBT individual learners do not have to sacrifice the time on the job they need to gain the experience and credibility their new positions require’. WBT fulfils the individual’s needs but without the cost of travel time, which entails working overtime to complete backlogs of work.

## Indirect advantages/disadvantages

Besides direct advantages, the communication aspect of WBT was identified as being another perceived benefit over traditional training methods. Through WBT, instructors and individual learners can communicate with each other in a more efficient and effective way. Over half of the organizations (Organizations A, B, C, F, G, H) feel that WBT succeeds in providing online training services with asynchronous communication. Organization F stated that, ‘Instructors and individual learners had highly interactive discussions’. ‘From our trainees’ feedback, WBT improves the general communication with their instructors as they can directly share or leave the message on the Electronic Forum’ (Organization C). Furthermore, the opportunity to share information among instructors and individual learners was considered a good reason to adopt WBT. For example: ‘The use of WBT developed stronger communication links between instructors and individual learners. Individual learners, especially in Chinese society, are more willing to ask questions through WBT’ (Organization H).

‘By improving the knowledge, skills, attitudes, habits and methods of employees, WBT makes the organization more effective and more efficient’ (Organization B). WBT can be especially effective when it provides just-in-time, just enough, just-for-me learning that keeps individual learners on the job more of the time and enables them to quickly identify and fix problems that hold up production or lead to costly errors so as to improve employees’ performance.

## Organizational readiness

The 10 organizations helped to identify the following different forms of organizational readiness, which are highlighted in the IT adoption literature: (a) the level of IT knowledge among top management; (b) the level of IT knowledge of individual learners; (c) the level of IT use within the organization; (d) the size of an organization; and (e) its financial resources. A sum mary of the findings is given in Table 5.

## Level of IT knowledge among top management

Consistent with the internet adoption literature (Mehrtens et al., 2001), the evidence supported the idea that WBT was adopted by top management who were the key champions of the technology, who had IT knowledge, and who had the final judgement to make the decision on the adoption of WBT. Five out of 10 organizations (Organizations A, B, C, G and H) mentioned that the level of IT knowledge among top management and their support were important in the adoption decision. For example, ‘We organize WBT by using a top-down approach, which means that top management will make the main decision in adopting WBT for their subordinates’ (Organization A). Organization J considered that, ‘Different levels of IT knowledge among top management and different management styles will lead to different decisions in an organization’. If the level of IT knowledge among top management is high, it is more likely that the decision will be made to adopt WBT. If the level of IT knowledge among top management is low, it is more likely that traditional training methods, such as lectures and workshops, will be preferred.

## Level of IT use in organizations

‘The basic infrastructure of IT is one of the key elements in building WBT’ (Organization H). Nearly all organizations nowadays have sophisticated networks of computers, requiring no additions for the adoption of WBT. This evidence supports the view in the literature on IT adoption that organizations with a high level of IT infrastructure are more likely to adopt IT because the technology infrastructure is not a consideration. Most organizations gave the level of IT use and whether the adoption of WBT would involve hardware, software, computers and the network as basic concerns. For example: ‘Technology is critical in WBT adoption. Organizations should have sufficient support in their own technical infrastructure before the decision to adopt is made’ (Organization D).

In short, three main aspects of organizational readiness were identified within the 10 organizations: the level of IT knowledge among top management; the level of IT knowledge among individual learners; and the level of IT use within the organization. Furthermore, the evidence from the 10 cases indicated two additional types of organizational readiness: organization size and financial resources.

## Organization size

There is significant support for other aspects of organizational readiness, particularly organization size and financial resources. Attewell (1992) claimed that larger organizations tend to be more adoption oriented than smaller organizations in the same industry. This contention was supported in our study, as the larger organizations adopted WBT before the smaller ones. The larger the organizations in terms of size, the more likely they were to be willing to adopt WBT. Organization A stated that, ‘The number of staff in an organization may affect the likelihood of an adoption decision from top management’. In short, to some extent, the size of an organization is relevant due to the levelling effect of technology. This indicates that organization size should be taken into account as being one of the considerations in WBT adoption. However, Organization G mentioned that ‘Some small/medium-sized organizations are now outsourcing their training programs to third parties or training consultants. It is even more common to outsource the programs and deliver training through WBT rather than traditional training methods’. Small/medium-sized organizations also benefit from the usage of WBT. Both views indicate the importance of organization size as an indicator for WBT adoption.

## Financial resources

Financial resources were part of Iacovou et al.’s (1995) definition of organizational readiness in the EDI adoption model (Kuan & Chau, 2001). In the case of WBT, organizations need to invest financial resources to set up WBT. Eight out of 10 organizations (Organizations A, B, D, E, F, G, H and I) raised financial resources as an issue during the interviews. For example, the following comment was typical, ‘After we invest financial resources in building up the IT infrastructure of WBT, the adoption of WBT can save costs in the long-run’ (Organization C). WBT can save huge training costs, but usually only after a sizeable upfront investment. The low delivery costs of WBT are usually purchased with high development costs. ‘Financial resources in the adoption of WBT are a cornerstone of success. Even in the training and development field, we are facing the e-revolution’ (Organization H).

## External pressures

Organizations provided evidence of external pressures as one of the major considerations for adopting WBT (Flanagin, 2000). According to institutional theory, the actions of organizations are deeply influenced by other organizations within the same industry. There was pressure rather than merely an expectation that organizations would have WBT in delivering training and information to individual users. The issue of credibility compared with competitors was raised as an important motivator for adopting WBT. A summary of the findings is given in Table 6.

## DISCUSSION

This study provided a clear understanding of the influences on the adoption of WBT in Hong Kong organizations. WBT adoption was influenced by three significant factors: (1) perceived benefits/costs of WBT; (2) organizational readiness; and (3) external pressures. Organizations seek perceived benefits from WBT, which can come in numerous forms. Typically, organizations seek both the direct and indirect advantages of WBT over traditional training methods. Organizations need to be ready to adopt WBT by having top management support and individual learners who are knowledgeable about the internet, and by possessing the appropriate hardware and software technical infrastructure. Organizations are also influenced by pressure from competitors outside the organization who are willing to adopt WBT.

The model seems to adequately identify the three significant factors that influenced decisions to adopt WBT. With regard to perceived benefits/costs, the factors of direct and indirect advantages/disadvantages are similar to those in the IT adoption model. In addition, organization size and financial resources are other considerations in organizational readiness. The level of IT knowledge among top management, the level of IT knowledge among individual learners, and the level of IT knowledge in the organization are important. More specifically, the majority of the organizations highlighted the importance of external pressures. The extension of the IT adoption model in WBT contexts is a strength that should benefit other researchers, and also provide a more complete picture to HRM practitioners. Many prior studies on the adoption of IT have focused on perceived benefits/costs (Chau & Tam, 2000). The extension model shows that three very different types of influence exist. All three are significant and deserve both recognition and further study.

This study found the IT adoption model to be helpful in examining the significant factors influencing WBT adoption in Hong Kong. Many similarities were found as compared with internet adoption model (Mehrtens et al., 2001); however, the contexts were somewhat different, particularly in the area of organizational readiness. This study found further support for Iacovou et al.’s (1995) model, in which an organization’s size and financial resources are significant factors in WBT adoption. WBT adoption is also influenced by the expectations of individual learners. The transferable nature of the WBT adoption model is an addition to the body of knowledge, as it has not fully been used to explain the IT adoption model.

## Implications

The findings are of considerable significance for the HRM field, in terms of their preparation and procedures for WBT adoption. Many organizations in Hong Kong, even those with wellestablished and systematic training methods, have taken the time and effort to investigate WBT. HRM practitioners need to be convinced of the perceived benefits/costs before they fully adopt WBT, although perceived benefits/costs can take many different forms. Importantly, it seems that financial resources are a key factor in the decision on whether or not to adopt WBT. Also, IT expertise in developing WBT can be critical. Top management and individual learners with sufficient knowledge in IT are important in the adoption of WBT.

To devise WBT, organizations need top management support in recognizing the potential of WBT and the perceived benefits/costs that WBT presents for HRM practices. Although many academics and managerial practitioners are becoming more aware of issues regarding WBT, it takes time for the message to be translated into practical implementation in organizations. Other management consultants can help in developing WBT, but top management are unlikely to rely on third parties unless organizations lack the financial resources and experience to adopt WBT. This study suggests that there are opportunities to identify the significant factors and help organizations to adopt WBT.

Those organizations that have not adopted WBT must be convinced of the benefits/costs before they will do so. They are interested in several perceived advantages/disadvantages of WBT over traditional training methods, e.g. the delivery of standardized information. However,

HRM practitioners must consider how WBT will best fit in with their own HRM practices. To a large extent, WBT is an exciting IT adoption with HRM functions, but it has to be suited to the nature and size of the organizations and how WBT contributes to the field of HRM must be taken into consideration. The WBT adoption model has relevance to organizations that are already using WBT as a kind of training method. Different types of perceived benefits can be sought from WBT, so the model may suggest additional benefits/costs to pursue. The factor of external pressures provides evidence that many organizations expect their training methods to be delivered via the internet. Organizations may be able to exploit this as a comparative advantage, particularly if competitors have not yet adopted WBT.

Furthermore, an organization must be ready to adopt WBT. WBT requires an organization to acquire new IT infrastructure, for example, to upgrade hardware, software and its network. It is important that the top management within an organization is sufficiently aware of the perceived benefits/costs of WBT adoption and what it means for their HRM practices. Top management should seek advice, but they must decide on the training methods that are best for them.

## Future research

In future research studies, the WBT adoption model could be tested and extended with organizations in different regions. In future research it would be worth testing the IT adoption model with non-adopters of WBT or including the factors of perceived barriers to WBT. The model could be used in alternative research approaches to improve our understanding of the adoption of WBT by organizations. In-depth longitudinal observations of an individual organization could follow the process from the decision to adopt WBT through to its implementation. Furthermore, future research should re-examine the factors in the WBT adoption model, like the characteristics of individual learners. A study of organizational readiness could determine how an organization reaches the decision to adopt WBT. Alternatively, a survey could determine the importance of each factor and develop a predictive model, which may have to be industry specific. Another challenge in IT adoption research is in understanding the role of an organization’s culture. Culture plays a role in managerial processes that may directly or indirectly influence adoption decision (Leidner & Kayworth, 2006). Last but not least, the current study indicates that the IT adoption model is transferable to the adoption of other types of IT, WBT in this case, although some contexts were different. Future research should determine if the general form of the IT adoption model is applicable to other types of IT adoption apart from WBT, for example, knowledge management.

## Limitations

This study has several limitations. The experiences of 10 organizations formed the primary source of data for the WBT adoption model. It is possible that the focus on organizations in a specific region, Hong Kong in this study, was a source of bias. As WBT is a relatively new concept, some interviewees might have slightly different interpretations of WBT. The number of researchers and interviewees was kept to a minimum to ensure that the interpretation of terms and concepts was consistent among all of the interviewees. Much of the evidence was collected using interviews and was then interpreted by a researcher. The data analysis may have been influenced by the subjectivity of the researcher. Also, this study did not include non-adopters of WBT in the sample. They may have different views about the adoption of WBT. Importantly, the IT adoption model was used as a basis for the study, which may have influenced the interpretation of the WBT case evidence. Furthermore, it was not possible to interview more than one person in each organization because the decision-making process was controlled by only one person within the organization. This reduced the opportunity to triangulate the information with subsequent interviews within the same organization.

## CONCLUSION

The study used evidence from 10 organizations to determine their reasons for adopting WBT. Evidence from the 10 organizations was used to further test the preliminary IT adoption model. In fact, this study shows a qualitative field study being used to confirm the a priori relationship as outlined in Figure 1. This effort makes a contribution to the study of IT adoption and particularly to WBT adoption by an extension of the existing model of Mehrtens et al. (2001) with some subcategories. The study concludes that three main factors significantly affect the decision to adopt WBT in Hong Kong organizations: the perceived benefits/costs of WBT, organizational readiness and external pressures. This study adds value to the MIS field by confirming the IT adoption model (Mehrtens et al., 2001) in the context of WBT. This study also has added substantially to the understanding of decisions by Hong Kong organizations to adopt WBT.

# APPENDIX B . THE TEN ORGANIZATIONS

Organization A is a statutory body responsible for implementing Hong Kong’s public housing programme. Its eight standing committees, with a total of 43 non-official committee members, deal with the full spectrum of housing issues, ranging from strategic planning, building, commercial properties, human resources, finance, home ownership and rental housing to more day-to-day matters such as administrative complaints. Organization A has a workforce of over 14 000 categorized in more than 40 disciplines and located in over 200 outstation offices.

Organization B is the world’s leading retailer for international travellers. It operates more than 150 stores in 15 countries throughout Asia and the Pacific Basin, including the West Coast of North America. Its primary store formats include its anchor stores, duty-free and general merchandise concessions, and boutiques in leading hotels and resorts, and specialty stores. It has approximately 9000 employees in 15 countries. In Hong Kong, it has approximately 800 employees, including 600 front-line employees and 200 administrative office staff. There are a total of 11 employees in its Human Resource and Training Department in Hong Kong.

Organization C is a pioneer in the e-learning industry in Hong Kong. The organization aims to fully utilize its resources and deliver all training courses in an e-learning format. It employs over 90 experts in different fields of business. The organization has several teams working on different tasks, namely, a creative team, a instructional design team, a programming team, a marketing team and a support team.

Organization D is the oldest public utility in Hong Kong and the first gas utility in the Far East. It is a listed corporation, whose business mainly comprises the production and distribution of gas, the marketing of gas and appliances, and the supplying of gas. It has around 2000 staff, including workmen and administrative office staff. All employees have a chance to receive training

Organization E is one of the latest internet technology companies. By taking advantage of the Hybrid Fibre-Coax (HFC) network, its customers can connect to the internet without the delays caused by busy telephone dial-up networks. The company has nearly 2400 employees, 23 of whom are in the Human Resources Department.

Organization F is one of the first integrated financial service providers in the world, resulting from a full merger of the largest insurance company in the Netherlands with one of the country’s largest banks. It is active in more than 65 countries in the fields of banking, insurance and asset management. The company has over 2000 employees in Hong Kong. Organization F has its own intranet for employees to access information globally, for example, news and information on its competitors.

Organization G is a group of companies with operations in mainland China, Hong Kong and Taiwan. It is a leading trader in precious metals, dealing in gold and platinum bullion in various forms as well as palladium. The company has over 1300 staff in Hong Kong. They are the only jewellery company to have obtained an ISO 9002 certification. The company is focusing on training largely to ensure that their front-line staff provides quality services.

Organization H aims to provide a wide range of daily necessities at reasonable prices to local consumers. Its merchandise is broadly categorized into fashion, food, household items and catering services. The company has around 3200 staff in Hong Kong in 10 different branches, of whom about half are full-time staff and the other half are part-time. There are 13 members of staff in the HR Department, with four of them focusing on organizing training activities and materials.

Organization I is a construction firm, which imports heavy machinery and supplies construction materials to the local market. Its mission is to satisfy market demand and customer needs with quality construction materials and services at competitive prices. Its operations are mainly in Hong Kong and mainland China. It has a total of 400 staff, made up of 250 internal staff and 150 workers. There are only six members of staff in the Human Resource Department at headquarters

Organization J has evolved into a global leader in the printed circuit board (PCB) industry and today serves many of the most reputable and recognized companies in the world. It is principally engaged in the manufacture and sale of highdensity double-sided and multilayered PCBs. Its regional investment office is located in Singapore, and the company has eight plants across the region – two in Hong Kong, two in Thailand and four in mainland China – and a workforce of over 5000 experienced and skilled employees.
