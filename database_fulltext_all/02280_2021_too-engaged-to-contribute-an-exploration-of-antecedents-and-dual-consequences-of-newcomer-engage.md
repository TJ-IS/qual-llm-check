# Too Engaged to Contribute? An Exploration of Antecedents and Dual Consequences of Newcomer Engagement in Online Social Groups

Hsien-Tung Tsai<sup>1</sup>, Peiyu Pai<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>National Taipei University, Taiwan, hstsai@gm.ntpu.edu.tw <sup>2</sup>Corresponding author, National Chengchi University, Taiwan, p.pai@nccu.edu.tw

## Abstract

Both scholars and practitioners recognize the importance of newcomers’ contribution behavior in online social groups. However, extant research has largely focused on existing members’ behavior, leaving the issue of newcomers’ contribution behavior relatively unexplored. This research proposes a theoretical framework for understanding why newcomers engage in contribution processes, how group engagement leads to normative pressure, and whether normative pressure has curvilinear effects on information contributions. Drawing on the theory of engagement, we propose that newcomer group engagement, characterized by vigor, dedication, and absorption, exerts dual effects on information contributions. We also argue that newcomers who personally engage in contribution processes tend to reveal three key psychological conditions: meaningfulness (i.e., a sense of return on investments of the self in contribution processes), safety (i.e., a sense of being able to show and express oneself without fear of negative consequences), and availability (i.e., a sense of readiness to engage personally in contribution processes). We further investigate a focal antecedent for each psychological condition. Using multisource data collected at three points in time, this research finds that value congruence, perceived group support, and contribution self-efficacy positively influence newcomer group engagement, which in turn leads to greater information contribution behaviors. This study also shows that greater group engagement can initiate a spiral of social role expectations, leading to heightened levels of normative pressure. Moreover, normative pressure has an inverted U-shaped relationship with information contribution behavior. These findings offer both theoretical and practical implications.

Keywords: Contribution Behavior, Newcomer Group Engagement, Normative Pressure, Online Social Groups, Perceived Group Support, Value Congruence

## 1 Introduction

The past two decades have witnessed a substantial increase in studies investigating members’ voluntary contribution behavior in online social groups. Scholars have argued that this phenomenon is critical for organizational effectiveness because simply being customer oriented is not enough—firms must also learn from and collaborate with customers to create values that meet their individual and dynamic needs (Chan et al., 2010; Prahalad & Ramaswamy, 2000). For online social groups, scholars have argued that information contributed by members is a valuable resource for their long-term viability and prosperity (Bock et al., 2015; Goh et al., 2016). For customers, such user-generated content has become an important source of information in their search, evaluation, and choice of products.

Given the recognized importance of member contribution behavior in online groups, many studies have focused on identifying the individual, motivational, and contextual factors that promote or inhibit this constructive act (Bock et al., 2005; Chen et al., 2019; Chiu et al., 2006; Chiu et al., 2015; Faraj et al., 2011; Jeppesen & Frederiksen, 2006; Kankanhalli et al., 2005; Kuem et al., 2020; Ma & Agarwal, 2007; Ray et al., 2014; Tsai & Bagozzi, 2014; Wasko & Faraj, 2005; Wiertz & de Ruyter, 2007).

Despite the wide breadth of studies on the topic, previous research tends to focus on existing members’ behavior (for a detailed review, see Appendix A), shedding little light on newcomers’ contribution behavior. However, the latter topic is important because contributions by existing members alone are insufficient to ensure the continued viability of online social groups; instead, newcomers’ contributions introduce fresh ideas and perspectives and also replenish the loss of existing contributors leaving the group (Kraut et al., 2012; Ransbotham & Kane, 2011). Thus, in this study, we focus on developing a theoretically derived framework for understanding why newcomers engage in contribution processes, how group engagement leads to heightened normative pressure, and whether normative pressure has an inverted U-shaped relationship with newcomers’ contribution behavior. Specifically, this study makes the following contributions. First, we expand online social group research by examining key antecedents of newcomer group engagement. Leveraging Kahn’s (1990) theory of engagement, we argue that newcomers who personally engage in contribution processes tend to reveal three key psychological conditions: meaningfulness, safety, and availability. In turn, we examine a focal antecedent for each—namely, value congruence, perceived group support, and contribution self-efficacy, respectively. By examining these factors simultaneously, we advance the current understanding of how these factors uniquely influence newcomer group engagement.

Second, in contrast to previous online community research (e.g., Ray et al., 2014) that focuses solely on the benefits of engagement, we investigate both the positive and negative sides of engagement in newcomer participation processes. For the former, highly engaged newcomers focus their efforts on tasks that benefit the entire social group, while remaining cognitively vigilant to and emotionally connected with the endeavor (Rich et al., 2010). For the latter, these highly engaged newcomers also might be pulled toward the center of the group, which enhances “not only the expectations of other members for behavior but also the newcomer’s own perceptions of community expectations” (Algesheimer et al., 2005, p. 22). The experienced constraints imposed by fellow members’ expectations evoke a “have-to-do” versus a “want-to-do” mentality (Fu et al., 2010). In the long run, this perception may be detrimental to the relationship between newcomers and social groups.

Third, this study clarifies the extant online community literature by exploring the link between normative pressures and contribution behavior. Several studies on online social groups report positive relationships between normative pressure and information contributions (e.g., Bock et al., 2005; Cheung & Lee, 2010), others find no significant relationship (e.g., Bagozzi & Dholakia, 2002, 2006; Tsai & Bagozzi, 2014), and still others indicate that normative pressures have negative effects on relationship outcomes (e.g., Algesheimer et al., 2005). Drawing on activation theory (Gardner & Cummings, 1988), we propose that the relationship between normative pressure and contribution behavior is curvilinear rather than linear, as previous research suggests. We thus provide a more complete picture of normative pressure effects in online social groups and help supplement models derived from the theory of planned behavior (Ajzen, 1991) that ignore the curvilinear effect of normative pressure on behavioral outcomes.

The remainder of this article is as follows: in the next section, we present our conceptual framework and research hypotheses. We then describe our research methodology and present the empirical evidence, after which we discuss the findings and managerial implications. Finally, we conclude by outlining some limitations of our study and proposing future research avenues.

## 2 Literature Review

## 2.1 Research on Member Contribution Behavior in Online Social Groups

Member contribution behavior in online social groups differs meaningfully from passive participation (e.g., visits to the site, viewing activity) in that the former requires behavioral processes that involve distinct, complementary resource investments and intentions to benefit the overall group (rather than being rooted in personal gain). In this regard, member contribution behavior combines aspects of effortful decisionmaking (Bagozzi, 2006) and good citizenship (Organ, 1988), creating a form of effortful citizenship behavior.

Three primary theoretical perspectives help explain the process of member contribution behavior. First, many prior studies have used a social capital lens to assess social interactions in terms of the structure and content of network ties that affect members’ behavioral performance (e.g., Chiu et al., 2006; Wasko & Faraj, 2005; Wiertz & de Ruyter, 2007). These studies investigate, for example, how members with higher levels of network centrality contribute more helpful information to their online social groups (e.g., Wasko & Faraj, 2005). Yet, most social capital-based processes or mechanisms similarly require long periods of time, or at least repeated interactions, to take effect (Burt, 1992; Coleman, 1990; Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998). Newcomers to the social environment lack sufficient time to form and develop social ties with others, leading to concerns about the theoretical validity and empirical applicability of online group research pertaining to newcomer contribution contexts.

Second, some studies have used theories of motivation to propose a set of energetic forces that can originate both within and beyond an individual’s being to initiate member contribution behavior (e.g., Bock et al., 2015; Chen et al., 2019; Kuem et al., 2020; Teichmann et al., 2015). These studies focus on diverse motivational drivers, including hedonic benefits (e.g., enjoyment, fun, entertainment), altruistic motives, selfpresentation, and anticipated extrinsic rewards. Although these studies provide theoretical insights into intrinsic and extrinsic motives associated with member contribution behavior, they fail to account for the important social processes underlying such effortful behaviors across geographically distributed, electronically linked environments (Bagozzi & Dholakia, 2002).

Third, several studies have drawn on Kelman’s (1974) social influence theory to examine how three social influence processes affect members’ informationsharing behavior (e.g., Beck et al., 2014; Ray et al., 2014; Tsai & Bagozzi, 2014): normative compliance (i.e., influence based on reward or punishment aversion), internalization (i.e., acceptance of others’ beliefs), and identification (i.e., influence based on the liking or respect of another person). These studies have enriched the understanding of the social influence processes underlying members’ participation behavior in online social groups but they do not effectively explain the important phenomenon of greater group engagement initiating a spiral of social role expectations for newcomers, leading to heightened levels of normative pressure, which has implications for group members’ decisions to contribute. The current study aims to reduce significant knowledge gaps in the online social group literature. By integrating Kahn’s (1990) theory of engagement with activation theory (Gardner & Cummings, 1988) and using multisource data collected at three points in time, we shed more light on newcomer contribution behavior and unravel the complexity associated with the dual outcomes of newcomer group engagement.

## 2.2 Newcomer Group Engagement

Management scholars have long been interested in the concept of engagement and suggested that cognitive, attitudinal, and behavioral manifestations of engagement vary according to the specific nature of an exchange. For example, Brodie et al. (2013) develop a holistic view of community engagement, treating it as a multidimensional concept comprising cognitive, emotional, and behavioral dimensions. Recent research on online brand communities defines engagement as a behavioral manifestation and conceptualizes consumer community engagement as “customer activity types and patterns, specifically their contribution behaviors” (Wu et al., 2018, p. 259). In online community contexts, Ray et al. (2014, p. 531) suggest that engagement is “a heightened state of mind in which people are ready to completely and simultaneously invest their full range of energies in challenging tasks that are perceived to be socially important and personally meaningful.”

Within a work group context, Kahn (1990, p. 700) defines engagement as “the simultaneous employment and expression of a person’s ‘preferred self’ in task behaviors that promote connections to work and to others, personal presence (physical, cognitive, and emotional) and active, full performances.” Schaufeli et al. (2002) further provide a refined definition of engagement as characterized by vigor, dedication, and absorption. This conceptualization not only suggests a linkage between engagement and behavioral performance but also represents an inclusive view of an individual’s agentic self (Rich et al., 2010).

To complement existing conceptual approaches to information systems (IS) research and to more explicitly to reflect an affective-motivational state of engagement, we adopt Schaufeli et al.’s (2002) operationalization. Accordingly, we define newcomer group engagement as a positive, fulfilling, grouprelated state of mind that consists of vigor, dedication, and absorption. Vigor implies high levels of energy during interactions and cooperation with group members, which prompts a willingness to invest effort in group activities. Dedication entails being strongly involved in group activities and experiencing a sense of significance and enthusiasm. Absorption is characterized by being fully concentrated and happily engrossed while participating in joint activities (Schaufeli et al., 2002). In this sense, engagement is distinct from related concepts in management literature, such as commitment, satisfaction, or involvement. Specifically, whereas both commitment and satisfaction are concerned with an individual’s relationship with a social group (Meyer & Allen, 1991; Palmatier et al., 2006), engagement represents “the simultaneous investment of cognitive, affective, and physical energies into role performance” (Rich et al., 2010, p. 617). Moreover, job involvement is “similar to the involvement aspect of engagement with work, but does not include the energy and effectiveness dimensions” (Maslach et al., 2001, p. 416).

Kahn’s (1990) theory of engagement is applicable to online social groups for three reasons. First, both work teams and online groups are social organizations, and members in both contexts vary in their level of emotional and psychological investment, suggesting that Kahn’s (1990) theory is not limited to organizational contexts. Second, both Kahn (1990) and Schaufeli et al. (2002) conceptualize work engagement as an ongoing motivational state. Our research also treats newcomer group engagement as an affectivemotivational phenomenon, not a temporary, confined peak experience. Third, Kahn’s (1990) theory positions engagement as a key mechanism that explains the relationships between both individual and organizational characteristics and behavioral performance. This theory thus provides an informative perspective for investigating the antecedents and consequences of newcomer group engagement in online social groups.

In their study of online communities, Ray et al. (2014) build on engagement literature and Ma and Agarwal’s (2007) framework to develop a model of community engagement that is helpful for understanding the nature of members’ prosocial decisions. They suggest that community engagement is a function of knowledge self-efficacy, self-identity verification, social identification, and satisfaction. Although researchers implicitly agree about the significance of engagement in online social groups (e.g., Ray et al., 2014), the notion and consequences of newcomer group engagement remain relatively unaddressed. Moreover, various researchers recognize that attitude theory and the theory of planned behavior fail to consider how decisions become energized (e.g., Bagozzi, 2006; Fazio, 1995; Tsai & Bagozzi, 2014). That is, individual traits, group characteristics, and other commonly specified direct, behavioral determinants provide reasons for acting but do not necessarily incorporate the motivational content that is required actually to induce an act.

In the present study, we draw on previous research to propose that newcomer group engagement can provide a motivational impetus and perform a transformative function, such that the antecedents of decision-making transform into contribution behavior. To add depth to the online group research, we also delve into how newcomer group engagement relates to normative pressure and address whether normative pressure might have curvilinear effects on information contributions. In this sense, our research responds to calls by Ray et al. (2014) for more research on member engagement in social contexts characterized by virtuality.

## 3 Hypothesis Development

We develop and test hypotheses pertaining to newcomer contribution behavior in an attempt to complement and add to the findings of previous studies. We begin by reviewing engagement literature and then develop a theoretical rationale for the causal relationships in our proposed model, as depicted in Figure 1.

## 3.1 Antecedents of Newcomer Group Engagement

Kahn (1990) suggests that individual perceptions of social groups and individual characteristics foster psychological conditions that uniquely influence people’s willingness to engage personally in group roles. Recent self-regulation research echoes this view, indicating that the characteristics of social contexts activate psychological resources that influence goal-directed features of the motivations for acting (e.g., Locke & Latham, 2015). According to Kahn (1990), when people determine how much to engage with a social group, they ask themselves three fundamental questions: (1) How meaningful is it for me to put myself in this situation? (2) How safe is it to do so? and (3) How available am I to do so? Kahn finds that group members are more engaged in situations that offer them more psychological meaningfulness and psychological safety, as well as when they are more psychologically available. In the current research, we adopt Kahn’s (1990) theory of engagement to consider a focal antecedent for each of the psychological conditions: value congruence, perceived group support, and contribution self-efficacy. These three antecedents have been positively linked to behavioral outcomes in online contexts, thus enabling us to consider the degree to which newcomer group engagement serves as an important mechanism through which the effects of these antecedents are transmitted. In the next section, we discuss how the antecedent variables influence newcomer group engagement through activated psychological conditions.

## 3.1.1 Value Congruence

Value congruence refers to the similarity in the values held by individual newcomers and newly joined social groups (Edwards & Cable, 2009; Kristof, 1996). According to Kahn (1990) and Rich et al. (2010), congruence in values influences the experience of psychological meaningfulness, which in turn affects newcomers’ intrinsic motivation to interact and cooperate with group members. This theoretical logic resonates with group psychology research that suggests value congruence not only helps maintain the stability of individual selfconcepts (Ahearne et al., 2005; Bhattacharya & Sen, 2003) but also promotes individual feelings of authentic connection with the self, prompting greater experienced meaningfulness (Bono & Judge, 2003).

This is particularly true in a relatively autonomous setting, where newcomers enjoy considerable discretion in what they may choose to focus on and how they expend their energy (Bagozzi & Dholakia, 2002). Psychological meaningfulness elicited by value congruence serves as an internal guideline to engage newcomers more completely, so that they invest greater time and effort in social group interactions. A greater degree of value congruence with their social groups should cause newcomers to find their roles inviting, valuable, and worthwhile, such that they are more willing to engage fully (Kahn, 1992).

Organizational research likewise suggests that value congruence leads to feelings of trust (Edwards & Cable, 2009), which constitute a necessary ingredient for developing relationships between newcomers and the respective groups. Especially in new or unfamiliar environments, characterized by substantial uncertainty, trust mechanisms effectively increase the predictability of interactions and shift newcomers’ focus to future conditions and continuity (Doney & Cannon, 1997). Moreover, value congruence implies that newcomers have similar approaches as existing members to cognitive processing (Erdogan et al., 2004), such as how they interpret nonverbal social cues in online contexts. Such similarities reduce the likelihood of misunderstandings and facilitate the quality of communication, which in turn contributes to newcomer group engagement. Collectively, we hypothesize the following:

H1: Value congruence has a positive impact on newcomer group engagement.

## 3.1.2 Perceived Group Support

Eisenberger et al. (2001) define perceived group support as newcomers’ perceptions that the group values their contributions and cares about their wellbeing. Such perceptions influence newcomers’ group engagement through a sense of psychological safety, or a feeling of being able to invest oneself without fear of negative consequences (Kahn, 1990). Compared with existing members, newcomers lack a schema to make sense of other members’ expectations for them, have difficulty expressing their feelings, and experience uncertainty about the new setting (Jokisaari & Nurmi, 2009; Saks & Ashforth, 1997). Support from the group fosters newcomers’ feelings of psychological safety, which then influences their perceptions that the group is trustworthy, secure, and predictable, in terms of behavioral consequences (Yang et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2010).

Moreover, in online social groups, member interactions are geographically dispersed and relatively anonymous, so members’ behavior is only minimally regulated; fellow members can willfully ignore others or openly criticize their contributions (Ray et al., 2014). Thus, particularly during newcomers’ transition period into the group, they may worry about whether they will be criticized or ignored. However, if newcomers can establish trusting interpersonal relationships in supportive group environments, they should be more likely to take risks, expose their true selves (authenticity), and try and perhaps fail, without fearing negative consequences (Kahn, 1990; Zhang et al., 2010). This reasoning is consistent with organizational support research that shows that when members perceive strong group support, they develop positive, secure expectations about the group’s likely reaction to their contributions, as well as to their mistakes (Eisenberger et al., 2001). In addition, they regard their interactions with the social group as pleasant and satisfying, such that they display greater intrinsic task motivation and vigor in participating in group-oriented tasks.

H2: Perceived group support has a positive impact on newcomer group engagement.

## 3.1.3 Contribution Self-Efficacy

Self-efficacy has been found to be a critical determinant of behavioral performance in a wide variety of task domains (Bandura, 1986, 1997). Given our interest in contribution behavior and the domain-specific nature of self-efficacy (Bandura, 1997), we investigate contribution self-efficacy as the antecedent of newcomer group engagement. In this study, contribution self-efficacy refers to newcomers’ beliefs that they have the ability and resources necessary to engage in the contribution process (Bandura, 1997; Cheung & Lee, 2012). Newcomers with strong selfefficacy beliefs are more capable of handling challenging tasks and prone to be spontaneous and innovative in new settings (Bandura, 2001). Bandura (1997) suggests that people generally avoid activities that they believe exceed their own coping capabilities but undertake and perform with assurance tasks that they judge themselves capable of managing. Newcomers likely use judgments of their contribution self-efficacy as inputs to determine how well they are doing, how much effort to exert, and how long they will persist in the face of impediments to the contribution process (Bandura, 1997; Cheung & Lee, 2012; Kankanhalli et al., 2005).

Similarly, members’ judgments of their abilities to perform a specific task successfully influence their thought patterns and emotional reactions during anticipatory and actual exchanges with the social group (Gist & Mitchell, 1992). In online communities, Ray et al. (2014, p. 535) also argue that “highly efficacious online community members should find the opportunity to synthesize and contribute knowledge to others to be a very engaging proposition because it challenges their selfexpectations.” In their study of newcomer adjustment, Saks and Ashforth (1997) find that self-efficacy critically influences the development of newcomers’ attitudes and behaviors and enables them to function effectively after their entry. Consequently, we anticipate that more efficacious newcomers will be more capable and more willing to engage personally in group roles.

H3: Contribution self-efficacy has a positive impact on newcomer group engagement.

## 3.2 Outcomes of Newcomer Group Engagement

## 3.2.1 Contribution Behavior

Newcomer group engagement represents a critical determinant of contribution behavior for several reasons. First, engaged participants feel more vigorous, leading to more effective accomplishment of their contribution tasks. Feelings of vigor are particularly helpful for engaging in information contribution processes, which are cognitively effortful and typically demand additional effort, both to initiate and to persist (Tsai & Bagozzi, 2014). For example, writing a high-quality article for fellow members requires the contributor to focus on both usefulness and novelty; in turn, this demands greater effort over extended periods of time.

Second, for newcomers to engage in contribution behaviors, they need to care about the welfare of their social groups. When dedicated to and enthusiastic about their groups, newcomers are more likely to provide information contributions that benefit the group and maintain its positive status. Research indicates that the decision to undertake effortful, group-oriented behaviors requires shared mental models that emphasize the good of the collective and also help newcomers overcome inertia (e.g., to do nothing) or selfish impulses (e.g., Perry et al., 2016).

Third, when taking advantage of opportunities to contribute to their social groups, engaged participants often concentrate fully and remain happily engrossed in the contribution process (Kahn, 1990; Rich et al., 2010). Schaufeli and Bakker (2004) suggest that being fully absorbed in a task comes close to what has been called “flow,” which refers to a state of optimal experience that is characterized by focused attention, a clear mind, loss of self-consciousness, and intrinsic enjoyment (see Csikszentmihalyi, 1990). Greater engagement enables newcomers to become immersed in the process and to thereby exhibit greater information contribution behavior.

H4: Newcomer group engagement has a positive impact on information contribution behavior.

## 3.2.2 Normative Pressure

Group engagement might enhance newcomers information contribution behavior directly, though some researchers question the simplistic nature of models that emphasize relatively narrow aspects of participation processes and recommend considerations of a dual effect of engagement (e.g., Algesheimer et al., 2005; Halbesleben et al., 2009). Along similar lines, we posit that group engagement affects the formation of normative pressures, defined as a participant’s perceptions of the online group’s extrinsic demands to interact and cooperate with the group (Algesheimer et al., 2005). Such demands mainly involve implicit forms of coercion, requiring newcomers to adopt or embrace the group’s norms, rituals, and objectives. According to role theory, normative pressures reflect the influence of other people’s role expectations (Biddle, 1986), which consist of norms, beliefs, and preferences for the behavior of any individual. In online social groups, role expectations might be communicated by both regular and irregular participants with whom the newcomer interacts or observes in the social context (Biddle, 1986). In turn, we offer two arguments for why group engagement should influence the formation of normative pressures.

First, greater group engagement can initiate a spiral of social role expectations, leading to heightened normative pressures. A highly engaged newcomer will likely connect with various group members and might even take on leadership roles. Group behavior researchers argue that higher levels of engagement are accompanied by greater degrees of conspicuous participation in the group (Algesheimer et al., 2005) because greater engagement facilitates their access to broader sources of ideas and comments from other members, which increases the scope of the role expectations that group members share. Such effects should be especially prominent in social interactions in online group environments, where digital technologies enable interactive member feedback, and interactions can be archived automatically and retrieved easily. If highly engaged newcomers contribute articles, they might immediately receive comments and thoughts, reflecting the group’s expectations. Each of these comments or expectations might be reasonable independently, but collectively they may require more than the newcomers are able to achieve with their available resources (Katz & Kahn, 1978). Such circumstances likely induce normative pressures.

Second, group engagement encourages people to participate in social learning, which also influences the formation of normative pressure. A basic tenet of role theory is that people learn role expectations through their accumulated social interactions (Biddle, 1986). Highly engaged newcomers have more opportunities to choose insiders as social models whom they can observe and learn from (Köhler et al., 2011). Directly communicating with or observing these insiders helps newcomers get a sense of expectations for appropriate behaviors (Katz & Kahn, 1978) and also encourages them to gain exposure to the shared rituals and meanings embedded in the group, such that these expectations grow increasingly relevant, as a form of normative pressure.

H5: Newcomer group engagement has a positive impact on normative pressure.

## 3.3 Curvilinear Effect of Normative Pressure on Contribution Behavior

By leveraging activation theory (Gardner & Cummings, 1988; Scott & Erskine, 1980), we examine the possibility that both high and low levels of normative pressure restrict information contribution behaviors, whereas moderate pressure enhances it, resulting in an inverted U-shaped pattern. Activation theory is based on the concept of activation level that is defined as the degree of neural activity in the reticular activation system (Gardner, 1986; Gardner & Cummings, 1988). On the one hand, this theory posits that social pressure is positively related to activation (Gardner, 1990). On the other hand, extremes in activation level impede behavioral performance; only moderate activation levels result in maximum behavioral performance (Gardner & Cummings, 1988). That is, if normative pressure and activation are at moderate levels, participants are considered to be “optimally stimulated” (Gardner & Cummings, 1988). In online social groups, when participants are optimally stimulated, they should be fully engaged in information contribution processes, such as searching for related materials, generating alternative courses of action, or considering multiple explanations. In contrast, both high and low levels of normative pressure likely deviate from newcomers’ characteristic levels, leading to suboptimal stimulation and reduced contribution behaviors (Baer and Oldham, 2006; Zivnuska et al., 2002).

We find complementary curvilinear arguments in community behavior research (e.g., Algesheimer et al., 2005; Bock et al., 2005; Tsai & Bagozzi, 2014). Specifically, normative pressures increase cognitive stimulation and motivational arousal, which relate positively to behavioral engagement (e.g., spending more time dealing with information contributionrelated tasks). However, after a certain point, normative pressure may no longer exert positive effects on information contribution behavior because excessive pressures may produce prevention-oriented emotions (e.g., anxiety), with detrimental effects on relationship development. Fu et al. (2010, p. 65) even call excessive normative group pressures “a wolf in sheep’s clothing,” because the process engenders a have-to-do versus a want-to-do mentality and eventually may initiate an energy depletion process. Constrained or coerced interactions also can elicit defensive responses because participants receive situational cues that threaten their psychological resources (Eagly & Chaiken, 1993; Ryan & Deci, 2000). Feelings of autonomy are essential to participation in online social groups, such that newcomers who perceive high normative pressure may simply disengage. Thus,

H6: Normative pressure has an inverted U-shaped relationship with information contribution behavior, such that information contribution behavior is greatest at moderate levels of normative pressure. At very low or very high levels of normative pressure, information contribution behavior instead diminishes.

## 4 Methodology

To test our hypothesized framework, we used a twowave panel design and gathered data from two sources. Time lags give newcomers sufficient time to form and develop perceptions of variables of interest in online social group contexts while providing the temporal separation necessary to minimize potential common method bias and reduce respondent fatigue (Baer et al., 2018; Podsakoff et al., 2003).

## 4.1 Research Setting

We collected the study data with the assistance of an online social group platform, which granted us wide access to group members. This platform, established in 2003, hosts an interest-based website for members, as well as a mobile platform on which users can share opinions, photos, and experiences. The platform is well known in East Asia: most members come from Taiwan, Singapore, Hong Kong, and mainland China.

To become a member, a user must register by choosing a unique name and password and providing a valid email address for identity confirmation. Members can edit their own personal pages for different levels of self-disclosure, such as nicknames, contacts, and background introduction. Members’ personal pages are open to all, which can help everyone get acquainted easily. During their first visit, newcomers are asked to select topics that they are interested in (e.g., restaurants, traveling, parenting, culinary events), which immediately generates a customized front page for them and recommends relevant online social groups they can join.

During the participation process, all members are invited to provide reviews (ratings), reply to messages, post articles, and upload photos to increase their virtual badges or numbers of “stars.” Newcomers who opt to initially lurk (browse) rather than post can gain stars by signing in regularly (usually daily). Pop-up guidance encourages newcomers to participate in social activities such as browsing, rating, and replying to existing posts, as well as creating their own posts, to gain stars. Members are divided into five levels (from junior to expert) based on their accumulated number of stars. When members are promoted to an advanced level, they become eligible for exclusive benefits, such as a drawing to win an overnight stay at a 5-star hotel.

To facilitate member interactions, the platform emphasizes and promotes its interactive interface. Specifically, it offers online discussion boards on which members can communicate asynchronously with one another by posting and replying to messages. All information is visible to every other member in the various groups and accessible through the platform’s search engine. Some online social groups have editors who systematically collect and edit the articles that members have shared. Fellow members can thus easily read reviews by expert members who express various opinions and perspectives. In addition, to acknowledge member contributions, the platform announces the weekly rankings of the “hottest” and “most professional” social groups in terms of the groups’ publicity, rate of sharing, involvement of group members, and so on. The platform also maintains a database of each member’s daily participation activities, such as visit times, the number of shared articles, the popularity of posted messages, and the number of virtual badges (stars) earned. Data pertaining to members’ popularity, number of stars accumulated, and posted message ratings are open to all members.

It is worth noting that the platform also frequently hosts face-to-face gatherings. These meetings provide opportunities for members to share similar interests and enhance their ties to each other. In addition, according to interviews with the platform managers, many companies (e.g., restaurants, travel agents) regularly review ideas and feedback provided by group members to inform and accelerate their product and service innovations.

## 4.2 Data Collection Procedure

Our methodology and data collection design are consistent with Podsakoff et al.’s (2003) recommendations for minimizing the possibility of common method bias. First, we obtained measures of the predictor and criterion variables from different sources (i.e., self-reported and objective behavioral data). Second, for the self-reported data collection, to reduce evaluation apprehension, we informed respondents that there were no right or wrong answers and that they should respond as honestly as possible. Third, we created a temporal separation by introducing a time lag between the measurements of the latent variables of our framework. Specifically, at the start (T1), we used questionnaire items to measure the three antecedents of newcomer group engagement. Six weeks later (T2), we employed a second questionnaire to assess newcomer group engagement and normative pressure. Both surveys were accompanied by a cover letter that explained the purpose and importance of the study and provided assurance of confidentiality. We then collected objective measures of contribution behavior over a two-month period after T2.

To define which members were newcomers, we considered both prior research and expert opinions from online group cultivators and platform managers. In their study of online community designs, Ren et al. (2007) mention the importance of newcomers for sustaining online communities, as well as the features of newcomer participation processes in online communities, without specifying any timeline. On the basis of prior research and real-world input, Tsai and Pai (2014) use six months as a threshold in their study of newcomers’ motivation to contribute to online communities. In an investigation of how to build member attachment to online communities, Ren et al. (2012, p. 852) note that the “results remained the same when newcomers were defined as those with less than three or six months of experience.” Because there is no specific consensus about what constitutes a newcomer, especially in an online group context, and because this issue is context specific, we solicited expert opinions from five online group cultivators and platform managers. They noted that in an internet context, member interactions are not limited by geography or other interaction challenges, as are face-to-face groups. An online environment gives newcomers ready access to archived conversations between existing members, meaning that they can obtain practically unlimited information about group characteristics and easily learn the group’s languages, rituals, and traditions. Thus, their assimilation processes should be more efficient in online settings than they would be in face-to-face contexts. The increasing spread of smartphones and tablets also supports immediate, mobile access to online communities, which further facilitates community members’ learning and assimilation processes. As one community cultivator mentioned,

Three months in an online community could count as being a “newcomer.” My experience in online communities is that after three months, one is really a regular contributor, and that the most important period for newcomers to either become regular members or to disappear is those first three months.

In summary, drawing from both prior research and expert opinions, we specify three months as a reasonable duration for newcomer status.

The platform provider offered further assistance by forwarding our emails to members whose membership duration had been three months or less; in these messages, we explained the purpose of the survey and encouraged their participation. We sent the online version of the questionnaire to 1,000 newcomers, selected at random from the group platform’s mailing list. To ensure that less frequent visitors had a chance to be included in the sample, the provider helped us send emails to members’ alternative email addresses, which were required when they registered as members. Moreover, we offered a shopping voucher valued at approximately US\$6 to those who completed the twostage questionnaire. Overall, we received 284 completed questionnaires after two follow-up requests at each stage, a usable response rate of 28.4%. Approximately 95.8% of participants held at least a high school diploma, and the gender split of our respondents was roughly 58.5% female and 41.5% male, with an average age of 28.57 years. Our analysis of posting frequency indicated that 31.7% of the respondents did not post articles during the two-month period after T2. The average number of message posts was 4.20, with a standard deviation of 5.51.

## 4.3 Measures

We derived measures for the constructs in our model from existing scales and adapted them to suit the context of our study. Table 1 provides a detailed summary of the multiple-item scales. The survey instrument was administered in Chinese. Because the original scales were developed in English, two bilingual speakers with Chinese and English proficiency performed two-way translations to ensure semantic equivalence (Brislin, 1980). Specifically, the items were first translated into Chinese by a researcher in the IS field and then translated back into English independently by another researcher. Another bilingual English-Chinese researcher then compared the original and backtranslated versions, confirmed that they were comparable with a high degree of accuracy, and made modifications to resolve some minor discrepancies.

## 4.3.1 Newcomer Group Engagement

We considered several definitions of group engagement in this study, including Ray et al.’s (2014, p. 531) view that it reflects “the enthusiasm of members for contributing to their community” and Algesheimer et al.’s (2005, p. 21) assessment that it is “the consumer’s intrinsic motivation to interact and cooperate with community members.” However, as we noted previously, we ultimately adapted Schaufeli et al.’s (2002) definition of job engagement to define group engagement as a positive, fulfilling, group-related state of mind that is characterized by vigor, dedication, and absorption. We then used a three-item version of the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale (UWES-3) from Schaufeli et al. (2019), who derived this measure from the UWES from Schaufeli et al. (2002).

The UWES is the most widely used operationalization of engagement (Schaufeli et al., 2019). Schaufeli et al. (2019) also affirm the construct validity of this short, three-item scale across five national samples. We slightly changed the wording of the items to fit the online group setting. The coefficient alpha for the group engagement scale is 0.92.

## 4.3.2 Newcomer Contribution Behavior

In line with previous research (e.g., Tsai & Bagozzi, 2014; Wasko & Faraj, 2005), we assessed newcomer contribution behavior by retrieving archived data about the number of articles that newcomers had contributed to the group during the two-month period after they completed the questionnaire. Specifically, we considered the number of messages each respondent posted during the two-month period following the recording of the self-reported measures. It should be noted that we treated comments, reviews, and answers that newcomers created for the community as “contributions” if the messages provided useful information relevant to the issues that other members proposed. We did not include messages that contained only short, social messages (e.g., “Cheers,” “Thank you”), as these types of messages are not explicit examples of information contribution behavior. We retrieved these data after obtaining members’ consent. The archival data were skewed, which is typical of online group participation data (Bateman et al., 2011). To reduce the skewness, we used a logarithmic transformation of the number of articles.

## 5 Results

We applied a two-step approach (Anderson & Gerbing, 1988) to test Hypotheses 1-5. First, we conducted a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to assess the measurement properties of the latent constructs. Second, we performed structural equation analyses to test the research hypotheses. All analyses were performed on covariance matrices. We ran the models using LISREL 8.8 (Jöreskog & Sörbom, 1999). To confirm the goodness of fit of the models, we used chi-square tests, the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), standardized root mean square residual (SRMR), nonnormed fit index (NNFI), and comparative fit index (CFI). Satisfactory model fit requires nonsignificant chisquare tests, SRMR and RMSEA values of less than or equal to 0.08, and NNFI and CFI values of greater than or equal to 0.90 (Bagozzi & Yi, 2012; Hu & Bentler, 1999). We then performed a hierarchical regression analysis to test Hypothesis 6, as this technique assists in the examination of statistical associations for evidence of curvilinearity. In regression models, a curvilinear relationship is evident if the addition of the nonlinear predictor results in significant incremental variance after the linear relationship has been taken into account (Cohen et al., 2003).

## 5.1 Measurement Model Evaluation

We used two measures to evaluate the internal consistency of the constructs. The composite reliability (CR) is analogous to the coefficient alpha, and the average variance extracted (AVE) indicates the amount of variance captured by a construct measure, relative to random measurement error (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). The CRs range from 0.61 to 0.87, and the AVEs range from 0.53 to 0.69, above the recommended cutoff levels of 0.60 and 0.50, respectively (Bagozzi & Yi, 1988). Therefore, we determined that the constructs exhibit good internal consistency.

We evaluated the discriminant validity of the model constructs using three approaches. First, we checked whether the correlations among the latent variables are significantly less than 1. Our CFA model contains 8 latent variables and 17 measures, and the goodness-offit statistics for the model are within the acceptable ranges (χ<sup>2</sup>[93] = 222.96, p ≈ 0.00, RMSEA = 0.069, SRMR = 0.057, NNFI = 0.94, and CFI = 0.96), indicating good model fit. The 95% confidence intervals we constructed for each correlation coefficient do not include 1, in support of discriminant validity.

Second, we performed chi-square difference tests to compare a CFA model that constrains the correlation between a pair of constructs to 1 against an unconstrained CFA model. Evidence of discriminant validity requires that the unconstrained model fits significantly better than the constrained model. As we detail in Appendix B, the chi-square difference statistic is significant at the 0.001 level in each case, in further support of discriminant validity.

Third, Table 2 presents the means, standard deviations, and correlations for the variables. The diagonal elements (square roots of the AVE for each construct) are greater than the off-diagonal elements, so each construct shares more variance with its measures than with other constructs (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). Therefore, all construct measures in the measurement model achieve discriminant validity.

## 6 Discussion

The present study adds depth to online social group research by enhancing understanding of the determinants of newcomer contribution behavior. Using a unique data set that combines self-reported and objective behavioral data, we found support for the theory that newcomer group engagement serves as an important mechanism through which value congruence, perceived group support, and contribution self-efficacy differentially affect newcomer information contribution. Determinants of behavior within a social context can be categorized as distal or proximal (Bagozzi, 2006). Distal determinants influence an individual’s decision to engage in a specific behavior, whereas proximal constructs determine an individual’s maintenance of effort and persistence. In this study, value congruence, perceived group support, and self-efficacy were distal, while newcomer group engagement was proximal.

It is worth noting that previous empirical examinations of member contribution behavior in online communities have argued that social capital-based factors strongly influence participants’ decisions to contribute $( \mathrm { e . g . , }$ shared vision, reciprocity, or centrality) (e.g., Wasko & Faraj, 2005; Wiertz & de Ruyter, 2007). However, such approaches do not accommodate an agentic perspective, which involves simultaneously investing affective, cognitive, and physical energies into social role performance. Recent research also notes that social capital theory and its extension fail to consider how contribution decisions become energized and suggests that decision-making models should incorporate the motivational impetus necessary to induce constructive behaviors (e.g., Tsai & Bagozzi, 2014). In response, the current study posits and shows that newcomer group engagement can provide motivational content and convert the antecedents of decision-making into information contribution behavior.

In terms of distal determinants, an emerging body of management literature has argued that decision makers considering whether to enact a specific effortful behavior should take into account both internal and external factors that can foster crucial psychological conditions or resources (e.g., Halbesleben et al., 2014; Kanfer et al., 2017; Owens et al., 2016). Guided by Kahn’s (1990) theory of engagement, the current study supports the significant roles of value congruence, perceived group support, and contribution self-efficacy in newcomer group engagement through activated psychological meaningfulness, safety, and availability, respectively.

This finding provides a new lens for investigating the question of why newcomers contribute information to social groups characterized by virtuality. From an empirical standpoint, our study advances previous correlational evidence (e.g., Bock et al., 2005; Chiu et al., 2006; Ray et al., 2014) by providing a more rigorous and causal explanation of such links (internal and external factors → newcomer group engagement → contribution behavior). Future research on newcomer group interactions, whether within corporate online social groups or relatively autonomous group settings, can draw on and benefit from our conceptual foundation of newcomer contribution behavior.

This study also complements and contributes to online community research (e.g., Bateman et al., 2011; Ray et al., 2014) that focuses on the bright side of engagement by demonstrating that group engagement significantly produces normative pressure, which may have detrimental effects on the development of the relationship between newcomers and communities. For example, Ray et al. (2014) integrate theories from social psychology with existing models of online community participation (e.g., Ma & Agarwal, 2007) to develop a model of members’ intentions to enact prosocial behaviors. They find that engagement has direct effects on both knowledge contribution intentions and positive word of mouth. In addition, Bateman et al. (2011), adopting a commitment perspective, show that affective, normative, and continuance commitments exert differential effects on behavioral outcomes (i.e., reading threads, posting replies, and moderating discussions). These studies offer useful insights into how a motivational impetus or psychological state influences positive citizenship behaviors. Our research expands these studies by considering both positive and negative consequences, providing a new perspective on the process of information contribution decision-making.

Finally, this study extends prior research by demonstrating a curvilinear relationship between normative pressure and newcomer contribution behavior. The empirical evidence linking normative pressure and contribution behavior is equivocal. Some studies based on the theory of planned behavior (TPB; Ajzen, 1991) show that normative pressure is associated with higher levels of information contribution (e.g., Bock et al., 2005), whereas others report nonsignificant or negative associations (e.g., Algesheimer et al., 2005; Bagozzi & Dholakia, 2002, 2006; Tsai & Bagozzi, 2014). Drawing on activation theory (Gardner & Cummings, 1988), our study posits and supports an inverted U-shaped relationship between these two constructs. Specifically, moderate levels of normative pressure are likely to lead to the best behavioral performance in social contexts in which member interactions are geographically dispersed and relatively anonymous. This finding challenges the implicit assumption of TPB that normative pressure has a positive linear impact on behavioral outcomes. Our research also supports an emerging speculation by management researchers (e.g., Fu et al., 2010) that increases in normative pressure, in a relatively autonomous environment, can have detrimental effects on individual behavioral performance. Overall, our research provides a more comprehensive explanation of compliance social influences than either TPB-based research or social influence literature that emphasizes narrow aspects of normative pressure effects.

## 6.1 Managerial Implications

Members’ contribution behaviors in online social groups have emerged as notable topics of interest for both academic inquiry and managerial practice. In practice, a growing number of firms seek to leverage the information resources that existing members and newcomers contribute to social groups to inform their product design and development, customer relationship management, and other such activities (Nambisan & Baron, 2010; Wiertz & de Ruyter, 2007). Our findings provide a deeper understanding of the mechanisms that affect newcomer group engagement and information contribution behavior through the enhancement of value congruence, perceived group support, and contribution self-efficacy.

This study also finds that of the three factors examined, value congruence emerges as the relatively stronger predictor of newcomer engagement. There are two plausible explanations for this effect. The first pertains to the uniqueness of our sample, given that we tested our model in the Chinese context, in which values of in-group harmony and solidarity are strongly culturally prescribed (Wagner, 1995). An open question is whether value congruence plays a relatively stronger role in predicting newcomer group engagement in Western cultural contexts where individualism is highly valued. If this is the case, group cultivators would be well advised to adopt differential approaches when managing members across different cultures. A second plausible explanation is that value congruence plays an especially important role for participants in online social contexts because “individuals are likely to seek out and maintain membership in virtual groups with overlapping values” (Bagozzi & Dholakia, 2002, p. 11). Moreover, in online contexts, which are characterized by substantial uncertainty, value congruence effectively increases the predictability of interactions and shifts a newcomer’s focus to future situations and continuity (Butler et al., 2014). This finding provides useful insights for current practice.

Specifically, to enhance a group’s viability, many practitioners tend to post information on website banners to attract potential members who have not yet joined or heard about the group in the hope of converting them into members. Our findings imply that such approaches for new member acquisition may not work well if the group’s goals are to enlist engaged, active members and create a vibrant, self-sustaining online group. Instead, the impact of value congruence on engagement suggests that it would be more effective for platform managers and group cultivators to help potential newcomers correctly choose groups that share similar values with them (e.g., creating clear categories on their website). Then, after they formally join, groups should help newcomers acclimatize—that is, encourage senior members to engage with newcomers to help them learn shared narratives, rituals and traditions, or unique values already embedded in the groups. In addition, group managers should regularly meet face-to-face with a sample of members to obtain feedback on their interaction experiences and thoughts. Such high-context interactions provide opportunities for members to discuss group values, issues, and other important topics. In other words, although member interactions in online social groups facilitate the efficient exchange of resources during routine participation with little or no human contact, building or maintaining value congruence might require a human touch. As the group grows and heterogeneity increases, managers can use these face-to-face meetings to determine the group’s direction, including the possibility of spinning off the online group into new groups that better reflect shared values and interests. Such efforts provide opportunities for group participants to maintain value congruence and thus direct their group engagement.

We also find that newcomer group engagement is fostered when newcomers feel psychologically safe. Group support is particularly relevant for engendering psychological safety in online social groups where interactions lack verbal and physical cues. When perceived group support is low, participants are unsure of what to expect and fear that they may suffer for sharing information. They thus may choose to be on guard by withdrawing from the sharing process. By strengthening newcomers’ perceptions of group support, managers can cultivate a group atmosphere in which listening and openness are encouraged, leading to more frequent and free-flowing exchanges of information, both factual and emotional. In addition, supportive behaviors by existing members are particularly valued when they are voluntary, helping newcomers feel more like insiders than outsiders. It should be noted that when groups try to maintain a harmonious, supportive group atmosphere, counterproductive group states such as groupthink may arise (Janis, 1982; Park, 1990). Janis (1982) suggests many approaches to prevent or minimize the dysfunctional consequences of groupthink. For example, group leaders should encourage all members to freely contribute their ideas in a confident manner and adopt an impartial stance rather than initially stating their preferences. It would be worthwhile for future studies to systematically examine the issue of groupthink in online social groups.

To engender newcomer contribution self-efficacy, cultivators or organizers could design and execute reward systems that act as effective social persuasion. Reward tools in online social groups include giving virtual currency, awarding meaningful badges, promoting membership status, or offering public recognition. All reward mechanisms implemented should be easy to understand, accurate, and useful, rather than complex or manipulative. More importantly, the mechanisms should reflect the group’s values and reinforce positive expression of those values. Systems such as Stack Exchange allow group members to upvote member contributions while keeping track of how helpful members are through a scoring system. Effective contributors are rewarded with increased selfconfidence, which drives them to devote more effort to the process. However, online social groups must remain cautious when using rewards, as external-oriented rewards may promote “gaming the system” at the expense of group values.

Our findings bring increased clarity to the roles of newcomer engagement and normative pressure in newcomers’ decision-making regarding information contribution within online social groups. Specifically, we find that in trying to encourage newcomers to engage with their groups, cultivators unwittingly facilitate the formation of normative pressure because this process can initiate a spiral of social role expectations that other group members share. Although external, normative pressure may drive newcomers to contribute information to fellow members, as suggested by prior online group studies (e.g., Bock et al., 2005); however, excessive normative pressure in online contexts may produce negative emotions and elicit defense reflexes by potentially threatening individuals’ true inner selves or psychological resources (Eagly & Chaiken, 1993; Ryan & Deci, 2000). In other words, normative pressure acts as a double-edged sword: too little or too much normative pressure may stunt behavioral performance, while a moderate level may be optimal. In turn, our findings suggest that group cultivators should design and implement influence tactics that can buffer the negative effect of normative pressure on newcomers’ behavior by, for example, eliciting newcomers’ positive emotions (e.g., pride). This is an exciting possibility that merits additional research.

## 6.2 Limitations and Future Research

Although our findings are encouraging and useful, their applicability must be considered in light of several limitations. First, we use an objective measure of contribution quantity retrieved from a platform’s database, which usefully limits the scope of our analysis but also forces us to ignore the quality of information contribution behavior. However, our proposed model may apply even when we consider this type of contribution behavior. That is, contributing high-quality articles likely requires value congruence, perceived group support, contribution self-efficacy, and newcomer group engagement.

Second, we focus our discussion on an individual-level analysis, though members may contribute on behalf of a subgroup, such as when a small group of members arrange a social event in which everyone can participate. In this case, the spokesperson for the group acts as a representative and contributes information for the benefit of the wider group. This focus, however, may underestimate the contribution behaviors of members who do not serve as representatives. Future research should extend this scope to the subgroup level.

Third, in this research, we draw on Kahn’s (1990) theory of engagement to propose constructs that are important in newcomers’ contribution decisions. Further studies should investigate whether and how other factors might influence newcomer group engagement and information contribution behavior. For example, cognitive social identity might influence the formation of newcomer group engagement and the evaluative component of social identity might promote actions that produce in-group welfare (Ellemers et al., 1999). We thus call for additional research that integrates Kahn’s (1990) theory of engagement and social identity theory (Ashforth & Mael, 1989) to further advance understanding of newcomers’ contribution decisions in online social environments.

Fourth, we specified three months as the duration for newcomer status; the time frame may differ for other online group platforms. The length of the time it takes newcomers to adjust has been a consistent issue for socialization researchers (e.g., Bauer et al., 1998; Bauer et al., 2007). Bauer et al. (2007), in their metaanalytic review, suggest that researchers gather newcomers’ data at three-month intervals including at entry, three months, six months, and nine months following entry. This approach allows for more specific recommendations regarding what matters most at different points in the newcomer adjustment process. Moreover, additional research using multiple online group settings can increase the generalizability of the findings to other types of newcomers and social groups.

Finally, the theoretical arguments and research design both make it likely that the hypothesized relationships in the proposed framework are causal, but only the replication of these findings in experimental settings can establish this causality conclusively (e.g., by manipulating both the independent and mediating variables) (Spencer et al., 2005). As noted, the sample in this study came from a large online group platform that hosts a variety of groups. Although this context enabled us to rule out platform-related explanations for the observed findings (i.e., differences in reward effects were not caused by differences in the platform), it also creates questions about whether the results can be generalized to other online group platforms. Therefore, it is important to replicate our findings across diverse group platforms, especially in studies that investigate both boundary conditions and the potential moderators of our central findings.
