# Seizing the Commuting Moment: Contextual Targeting Based on Mobile Transportation Apps

Abstract. Despite the average daily commuting time of commuters increasing by the day, the way marketers can benefit from our commuting behaviors has not yet been examined. In collaboration with one of the largest global mobile telecom providers, this study investigates how contextual targeting with commuting impacts user redemptions of mobile coupons. The analysis is based on a rich field study in which 14,741 mobile coupons were sent to 9,928 public transit app users consisting of commuters and noncommuters. The key findings indicate that commuters are about 3× as likely to redeem their mobile coupon compared with noncommuters. However, a multiple-coupon distribution strategy is more effective in increasing redemption among noncommuters than commuters. Moreover, the redemption rate of commuters is higher for coupons with shorter expiration periods, whereas that of noncommuters is higher for coupons with longer expiration periods. On the basis of theories from psychology and physiology, we argue that stress, which is exacerbated by commuting, increases commuters’ coupon redemption rate. We provide empirical support for this argument and show that marketers can increase response rates by focusing on specific periods of the day when commuting stress is relatively high (e.g., rush hours). By carefully exploiting commuting, which is easily identifiable and occurs throughout the world, managers may improve their mobile marketing effectiveness.

## 1. Introduction

The mobile revolution has fundamentally changed the manner by which marketers reach and engage with consumers. This development has provided an unprecedented opportunity for researchers and practitioners to gain insight into the dynamic behaviors and preferences of customers. The advent of mobileenabled promotional coupons characterized by geographical and temporal features has enabled retailers to track and target consumers in real time on the basis of location, shopping trajectory, and “meaningful” moments (e.g., purchase). Recognizing the immense potential upside of disruptive advertising innovations in the emerging “mobile-only” era, marketers are poised to increase their investments in mobile promotions. One industry forecast projects that spending on mobile advertisements in the United States will exponentially grow to reach over US\$65 billion in 2019, and this figure accounts for 72% of all digital ad investments.<sup>1</sup>

Another survey reveals that 87% of retailers intend to invest heavily in mobile marketing to target on-thego shoppers.<sup>2</sup> Mobile-based promotion has become integral to mobile marketing initiatives, driving sales in online, offline, and omni channels. A study shows that more than 90% of all mobile users who are looking for great shopping deals actively search for mobile coupons.<sup>3</sup> The same study indicates that nearly 50% of mobile consumers want sellers to send locationbased coupons to them when they are near a store.

Despite the strong promise of mobile coupons, they have yet to live up to their much-vaunted potential. Mobile coupon redemption accounts for merely 2% of all coupon redemptions (Mobile Marketing Association 2013), and only 16% of mobile users regularly use and redeem mobile vouchers (eMarketer 2013). Although the rate of mobile coupon redemption is substantially higher than that of paper-based promotional incentives, this level remains at around only 10%.<sup>4</sup> Moreover, the privacy and security concerns of mobile consumers prevent marketers from vigorously capitalizing on geolocation-based tracking and targeting.

Confronted with these challenges, marketers seek accurate and context-relevant mobile-based targeting approaches that seamlessly integrate geographical features with temporal elements. The challenges are exacerbated by the infancy of research on context-based targeting in mobile environments (e.g., Andrews et al. 2016) and the minimal effort devoted to systematic inquiries into mobile applications (apps) that may accurately capture user behaviors, preferences, and contexts. Given that many mobile apps are routinely used by consumers, they offer marketers authentic information on the specific contexts that inspire customer engagement with mobile innovations. A number of scenarios can illustrate the value of data on mobile engagement. A game user may feel happy when he or she earns the highest rank or defeats friends in a close match. Similarly, a user feels accomplished when he or she creates a new music playlist or downloads a new album released only two hours ago. A mobile user who relies on navigation apps is likely to feel exhausted upon arriving at a rest stop after hours of driving. The patterns and tendencies captured by mobile apps with geographical and temporal features can serve as important vantage points from which marketers can infer consumer emotion, context, and purchase intent.

This study is motivated by the desire to expand current works on context-based mobile coupon targeting by examining a popular public transportation (i.e., bus and subway) app that tracks commuting behaviors. Commuting refers to the repetitive and routine travel between home and workplace. Commuters spend an average of 52 minutes every day traveling between home and work, and some workers spend three or more hours on their daily commute.<sup>5</sup> Given that individuals are exposed to various stressors, including time pressure and traffic congestion while commuting (Novaco et al. 1990), commuting is well recognized in psychology as a source of stress (Novaco et al. 1990, Schaeffer et al. 1988). Accordingly, commuting has been extensively studied in diverse academic disciplines as an important socioeconomic factor that influences public health (e.g., obesity (Lee et al. 2008), stress (Stutzer and Frey 2008), cardiovascular risk (Hamer and Chida 2008)) and urban planning (e.g., residential location (Cervero and Wu 1997), commuting duration (Gordon et al. 1991), worker mobility and unemployment (Simpson 1992), jobs–housing balance (Peng 1997)). Marketers have been equally interested in how they can reach and engage with consumers during the long daily commute. The traditional approach to reach and engagement is advertising in subway stations, subway cars, or free newspapers distributed at stations. However, people have become more engaged with mobile phones than with newspapers, books, or display advertisements during their stressful commute (e.g., Melumad and Pham 2017),<sup>6</sup> so numerous companies have attempted to take advantage of mobilecentric commuting. Two large grocers are among the first to target mobile-connected commuters and achieve success., Tesco<sup>7</sup> and Peapod<sup>8</sup> superimposed virtual grocery stores on the walls of subway stations in South Korea and the United States, respectively, thereby enabling commuters to shop for groceries using their smartphones and have their purchases delivered by the time they reach home. Despite these benefits, the potential of mobile-centric commuting as a marketing opportunity has yet to be fully exploited.

Commuting can serve as one of the most attractive contexts that mobile marketers can target not only because of commuters’ high level of mobile engagement during long daily commutes but also because of its effortless and accurate identifiability and potential for strong marketing effectiveness. Mobile marketers can simply use open APIs provided by map applications (e.g., Google Maps) to access and use information related to users’ residential and workplace addresses. Google Maps and Apple Maps, for example, have been collecting such information (Figure 1) to provide effective navigation services on users’ commuting routes (i.e., between home and workplace) (Figure 2). Alternatively, mobile app developers can easily adopt software development kits (SDKs) for this purpose. For instance, a free SDK for iOS and Android called Neura identifies critical locations (e.g., home and workplace) within a few days of learning phase and notifies the focal app when users arrive and leave.

The specific interest of this research is whether targeting a commuting context is associated with purchase-related behavioral patterns induced by mobile coupon offers. In collaboration with one of the largest mobile service platform providers in Korea, we conduct a field study on how contextual targeting with commuting is associated with responsiveness to mobile coupons. We first define commuting routes as routes between home and workplace and noncommuting routes as those other than commuting routes. We then send 14,741 mobile coupons to 9,928 public transit app users who travel over commuting or noncommuting routes (i.e., commuters or noncommuters). We analyz the relationship between targeting methods (i.e., commuter targeting versus noncommuter targeting) and response to mobile coupons and how this relationship is moderated by user attributes (e.g., demographics, previous redemption experience, and exposure to multiple coupons) and coupon characteristics (e.g., expiration dates). Dynamic and static matching mechanisms are adopted to corroborate the causal relationship and address potential endogeneity Finally, we conduct additional econometric analyses to confirm that potential confounding factors do not drive the obtained results.

The key findings indicate that unlike noncommuter targeting, commuter targeting increases the rate of mobile coupon redemption even after we control for the potential effects of users, coupons, and products. Enhanced receptiveness to mobile coupons is more perceivable when users obtain multiple coupons. Further analysis reveals that the multiple-coupon distribution strategy (i.e., repeated targeting) increases response rates of noncommuter targeting more effectively than that of commuter targeting. After controlling for unobserved heterogeneity, we find that coupons with short expiration dates more successfully improve the response rates of commuter targeting than those with long expiration dates. The reverse holds true for noncommuter targeting. For example, offering discount coupons with a day or a few hours remaining before expiration is an effective strategy for commuter targeting. By contrast, coupons with relatively longer expiration dates (i.e., one week) should be utilized for noncommuter targeting to enhance their redemption rates. Finally, commuter targeting exerts a significantly positive effect on redemption speed as well as the rate of mobile coupon redemption.

The contribution of our study to the growing body of literature on moment-based marketing lies in its examination of whether targeting commuting context stimulates high receptiveness to mobile coupons. Consumers may have unstable preferences for the same product over different time periods and contexts (Bettman et al. 1998). Although perceived as a negative socioeconomic component, commuting offers marketers opportunities that advance marketing success. The findings serve as a useful guide on who to target, when to send mobile coupons, how many coupons to distribute, which coupons are more attractive, and what products should be advertised on mobile coupons. Contextual targeting that is underlain by analyses of mobile apps with geographical and tempora components substantially increases users’ reception to mobile coupons. Our results provide managers with several fruitful directions in formulating effective distribution strategies for mobile coupons, implementing precise consumer segmentation, and achieving context-specific product positioning.

## 2. Research Background

## 2.1. Mobile Coupon Redemption

Given the portability and ability of mobile devices to locate and track themselves using global positioning systems (GPS), mobile coupons feature a distinctive capacity for targeting users using both location and time (Ghose and Han 2011). Accordingly, managers have utilized the geographical and temporal features of mobile coupons to increase their redemption rates. Surprisingly, only a few researchers have examined mobile coupons, as summarized in Table 1. Luo et al. (2014) are among the first to identify the geographical and temporal aspects of mobile coupons and examine their individual and interaction effects on redemption behaviors. By conducting a field study in which users at different locations (i.e., geographical aspects) are targeted at varying periods (i.e., temporal aspects), Luo et al. (2014) find that targeting proximal users using a product with short promotion lead time can lead to higher coupon redemption rates than targeting distant users and using a product with long lead time. They also identify the interaction effect between the geographical and temporal aspects; that is, the effects of promotion lead time show an inverted U-shape when targeting distant users but become negative when targeting proximal users. Other studies suggest diverse aspects of geographical and temporal targeting and examine their effects on mobile coupon redemption rates. For example, Danaher et al. (2015) employ the vertical and horizontal distances between customers and stores in a large shopping mall as an aspect of geographical targeting and find that horizontal and vertical distances exert a significantly negative effect on mobile coupon redemptions. Fang et al. (2015) and Fong et al. (2015) develop and examine the effectiveness of two geographical targeting concepts, namely geo-fencing and geo-conquesting, which target focal and competitive locations, respectively.

Earlier studies have focused mostly on the individual or interaction effects of geographical and temporal targeting, but recent studies have emphasized contextual targeting, which integrates both aspects of mobile coupon targeting. For example, Andrews et al. (2016) investigate how a context (i.e., physical crowdedness) affects consumer responses to the mobile coupons of a missed call alert service by using panel sets in the fields of subway trains. Using free food and beverage coupons, our study offers an extension of the studies that investigate the contextual targeting of commuters.

## 2.2. Commuting and Mobile Coupon Redemption

Among daily life experiences, commuting is associated with the lowest level of positive mean affect (e.g., happiness, warmth/friendliness, and enjoying oneself) and a relatively high level of negative mean affect (e.g., frustration/annoyance, depression, and anger/ hostility) (Kahneman et al. 2004). In other words, commuting between home and workplace is a source of stress (Schaeffer et al. 1988, Novaco et al. 1990). This problem has driven researchers from diverse disciplines to examine the determinants and consequences of commuting stress (see summaries by Koslowsky et al. 1995). Researchers have reported that commuting stress is induced by various factors, such as noise, time pressure, traffic congestion, and air pollution. Such a stress also stems from repeated exposure to the same stimuli—a situation engendered by travel over the same route at a uniform time of day. Prolonged exposure of this kind exacerbates commuting stress, pushing it to levels exceeding that experienced during other trips or journeys (Baron 1986). Commuting becomes even more stressful than normal when individuals cannot manage environmental factors (Stutzer and Frey 2008), which are often beyond their control (Koslowsky et al. 1995). Scholars have also indicated that diverse stress-related outcomes are significantly related to commuting. After a commute, individuals demonstrate not only lowered moods and reduced tolerance for frustration but also increased anxiety, heart rate, and hostility (Koslowsky et al. 1995).

Commuting stress is an equally important issue with respect to consumer targeting, exerting different effects on the redemption of mobile coupons, for instance. Stress occurs when an individual cannot cope with the demands of an environment (Lazarus 1966, Lazarus and Folkman 1984, Cohen et al. 1995). People who suffer from stress tend to believe that they have no control over their surroundings and the outcomes of their actions (Cutright and Samper 2014). Several empirical studies (e.g., Cutright et al. 2013) have demonstrated, however, that humans naturally crave the restoration of control (Fiske et al. 1996). For example, Durante and Laran (2016) argue that people under stress are willing to save money and spend only on necessities to recover control over themselves and their environments. Levav and Zhu (2009) reveal that customers are inclined to buy various products that can help them take charge of their environments. Regardless of the disagreement among research findings on how control is acquired, what is clear is the fact that the coping strategies typically implemented by people (e.g., exercise or buying) involve simple actions designed to reestablish self-restraint or environmental regulation (Koslowsky et al. 1995). Notwithstanding this aim for control, individuals are reluctant to follow through with their attempts for fear of wasting effort (Cutright and Samper 2014). They thus often exhibit limited initiative even if this means loss of the control that they desire (Glass et al. 1969, Lefcourt 1973). Put differently, the greater the predictability and producibility of the outcomes of actions, the stronger the propensity of individuals under stress to endeavor restoring control (Cutright and Samper 2014). In accordance with the clear outcomes of redemption behavior (i.e., acquisition of a product), we expect commuter targeting to stimulate a higher mobile coupon redemption rate than noncommuter targeting because redemption behavior poses no risk and allows people to control environments.

In physiology, stress is usually classified on the basis of stressors, which are agents that induce stress. The two categories are acute and chronic stress. Acute stress is caused by a specific, unpredictable event and short lived in nature, whereas chronic stress occurs on a daily basis (i.e., long-term stress). In other words, chronic stress is driven by a prolonged stressful situation (Koslowsky et al. 1995). Thus, commuting stress can be classified as a type of chronic stress because commuting is not a one-day event but a daily routine. Chronic stress brings forth physiological reactions and induces a desire to eat. In the physiology literature, the link among stress, food choice, and food intake is well established. Researchers (e.g., Takeda et al. 2004) have revealed that cortisol (a glucocorticoid class of steroid hormone) produced by chronic stress induces appetite. Specifically, chronic stress is highly associated with cortisol exposure, which poses a considerable effect on the reward system of the brain. When greatly sensitized, this reward system drives individuals to consume highly palatable foods, such as those containing sugar (Adam and Epel 2007). Given that commuting stress can be classified as a type of chronic stress and that we use food and beverage coupons in our study, we expect commuter targeting to result in high redemption rates of mobile coupons that enable individuals to satisfy the appetite and desire to eat induced by chronic commuting stress.

Despite the positive effects of chronic stress and cortisol on appetite, these may also pose adverse consequences on the normal functioning of the dopaminergic system, thereby increasing individuals’ impulsivity and unplanned behaviors. Impulsivity represents a multidimensional attribute wherein individuals attach more weight to immediate gain than to long-term future benefits (Moustafa et al. 2017). This natural human trait is known to moderate the relationship between chronic stress and coping actions (i.e., redemption behaviors). Previous studies (e.g., Inman and McAlister 1994, Krishna and Zhang 1999) have demonstrated that coupon duration is significantly associated with redemption likelihood, which, in turn, affects coupon profitability. Promotiona coupons with short-duration restrictions inspire a higher sense of urgency and a greater feeling of anticipatory regret (if economic savings are missed because coupons are left to expire) in consumers than those with long-duration restrictions (Inman and McAlister 1994). Regret theory is often adopted as a frame of reference for delving into the effects of time pressure on coupon redemption and purchase behaviors; the theory indicates that consumer regret increases as an expiration date approaches (Bell 1982, Inman and McAlister 1994).

Scholarly interest has been directed primarily toward across-time redemption patterns for coupons with and without time restrictions and toward anticipated regret. By contrast, limited attention has been paid to the effects of time pressure on redemption in a context where consumers may exhibit impulsivity due to chronic stress. As redemption of mobile coupons with short (or long) durations until expiration may stimulate less (or more) planned and more (or less)

impulsive behaviors, people experiencing chronic commuting stress are expected to be more responsive to coupons with short expiration durations than those with long ones. Given that highly impulsive consumers exhibit a greater sense of urgency and anticipatory regret than those with low proclivity for impulsivity, promotions with short-duration restrictions are more effective for the former than the latter. Conversely, noncommuters are less likely to exhibit impulsivity and anticipatory regret, so they are more responsive to coupons when they have sufficient time until expiration to exchange their coupons and obtain economic benefits.

## 3. Research Setting

## 3.1. Promotions via Mobile App

To test whether commuter targeting can increase the rate of mobile coupon redemption, we conducted a field study using a mobile app operated by one of the largest mobile service platform providers in South Korea. This mobile app, which has attracted more than a million active users, is developed for people who use public transportation, such as buses or subways. The key feature of this app is a public transit guidance function that provides users with several optimal routes (i.e., faster routes) and estimated times of arrival at their destinations based on real-time traffic conditions. After a user chooses a route from the available options, the app provides step-by-step guidance in real time during travel, accurately identifies the location of the user (i.e., on a bus, subway, or street), and alerts him/her about when and where to board, exit, and change public transportation modes.

The installation and use of the mobile app requires registration of users’ home and work addresses. As each user can register only one pair of home and work address, he/she is recorded as having two commuting routes, that is, from home to workplace and vice versa. To use the app function for commuting routes, users can simply tap the “guide me to workplace” or “guide me home” button. The app then guides users from their homes to offices or vice versa. When users wish to be guided on routes other than those through which they commute, they must add a temporal route by registering points of departure and destination. They can then activate the guidance function for the temporal route by tapping “guide me on the route.” These temporal routes are called noncommuting routes. However, the manner by which the user interface was designed ensures that users (commuters) have no incentive to add a temporal route and favor the respective function over that for commuting routes. The app provides exactly the same guidance function to commuters and noncommuters. In this study, commuters and noncommuters refer to users on commuting and noncommuting routes, respectively.

## 3.2. Field Study and Empirical Design

To validate the effectiveness of commuter targeting versus noncommuter targeting with respect to re demption rate, we conducted a field study using the data provided by our partner company. We randomly selected subjects who use the guidance function of the app, either for commuting or noncommuting routes, and sent them mobile coupons. When a user activates the guidance function for commuting or noncommuting routes, a popup window with the message “check the gift in your gallery” randomly opens, and a mobile coupon is automatically downloaded to the gallery application of the user’s mobile phone (Figure 3, left side). Immediately after the mobile coupon is downloaded, the user is requested to answer a short follow-up survey on his/her age group and gender on the popup window with no compensation offer. The survey is optional $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ a user can close the popup window that directs him/her to the survey) because requests for private information can negatively affect the redemption behaviors of users The mobile coupon is an image file containing the picture, name, expiration date, and unique barcode of a product (Figure 3, right side). Using this barcode, users can exchange their coupon for the product at no extra charge (i.e., free coupon) and at any selected store before its expiration. The barcode can also be used to identify whether, when, and where users exchange their mobile coupons. Five food and beverage products were provided by two national chain stores for the promotional campaign, and these products were allocated randomly to selected users (Table 2). Specifically, we chose the largest donut-selling (Dunkin’ Donuts) and convenience store (CU) chains in South Korea on the basis of their store count and availability across the nation. The selection was also based on the consideration that users should easily find stores where they can redeem their coupons

Our 21-day field study was conducted in South Korea on weekdays from August 24 to September 21, 2015. A total of 71,828 mobile coupons were distributed to 44,622 randomly selected users of the transit mobile app. Note that every user can receive one coupon a day. We identified the effectiveness of targeting people on commuting routes clearly by excluding users who received mobile coupons on both commuting and noncommuting routes and limiting our sample to users who received one or more coupons on either commuting or noncommuting routes only. After filtering, we had 9,928 users who received a total of 14,741 mobile coupons (i.e., 14,741 observations). We categorized 1,107 and 8,821 users into treatment and control groups, corresponding to those who received mobile coupons on commuting and noncommuting routes, respectively. Treatment and control groups represent users’ reception of mobile coupons through two targeting methods, namely, commuter targeting and noncommuter targeting, respectively.

A sensible approach to identifying the increase in incremental purchases driven by promotions (i.e., mobile coupons) may be to recruit another control group to which no promotions are provided and track their purchase histories. As with other studies that inquired into the effectiveness of mobile coupons (Luo et al. 2014, Danaher et al. 2015, Fong et al. 2015, Andrews et al. 2016), this study does not investigate the effects of promotions (i.e., promotions versus no promotions). Instead, we concentrated on two promotional treatments and ascertained which one more effectively elicited higher redemption rates.

The control group is approximately 8× larger than the treatment group because the reminder function is used more frequently on noncommuting routes. We adopted both dynamic and static matchings to alleviate the concern that the size difference between the groups and their varying traits could influence our results. Among the 14,741 mobile coupons distributed to users, 5,123 were redeemed, corresponding to a 34.8% redemption rate. The value is relatively higher than those derived of other studies (e.g., 3.22% by Andrews et al. 2016, 7.35% by Luo et al. 2014, and 2.6% by Fong et al. 2015). This difference may stem from the fact that our mobile coupons are free coupons (with a full 100% discount), whereas those offered in other studies are discount coupons (with small or very low discounts). Our findings are consistent with those of Ghose et al. (2019), who reach a redemption rate greater than 30% when they offer a 50% discount on coupons.

## 4. Econometric Analysis and Results

To directly identify the treatment effects on redemption rate, we initially focused on users who received only one coupon on either commuting or noncommuting routes throughout the study (one coupon users). We excluded users who received multiple coupons. We then included users who received multiple coupons (one or more coupon users) and examined the treatment effects over the multiple coupon approach to determine which mobile coupon distribution strategy is more effective.

In addition, the users in the treatment and control groups may differ in terms of traits and observability. Such differences, instead of the treatment, can lead to distinct redemption behaviors of mobile coupons. To mitigate the concern that the results are driven by user heterogeneity instead of the treatment effect, we adopt the propensity score matching (PSM) method. PSM helps address selection biases and establishes a causal link between commuter targeting and redemption behaviors by generating a matched control group that best resembles the observed characteristics of the treatment group (Rosenbaum and Rubin 1983, Rubin 2006).

4.1.1. Model Speci<sup>fi</sup>cations. In this section, we discuss the users who received only one coupon throughout the study period. We designed our model to estimate the unobserved probability or likelihood of redemption for each mobile application user. This variable is called redemption likelihood and was modeled as a logistic function of whether a user is on commuting routes (i.e., belongs to the treatment group). Other control variables were also included as follows:

$$

We carefully identified the commuting effect by controlling for time of day and day of week effects. We incorporated three Part of Day dummies (i.e., Morning, Afternoon, and Evening) and four Day of Week dummies (i.e., Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday, and Thursday) because commuters are expected to exhibit varying commuting patterns at different parts of the day and days of the week. Nighttime and Friday are employed as bases in the model, and Morning, Afternoon, Evening, and Nighttime represent 5:00–12:00, 12:00–17:00, 17:00–22:00, and 22:00–5:00, respectively. As redemption behavior can be affected by the number of days that are left before expiration (Inman and McAlister 1994), we controlled for the corresponding effects by incorporating the Days to Expiration variable, which refers to the number of days between the receipt and expiration of a coupon. Our model controls for the mobile app usage behavior of each user by incorporating three variables, namely, Usage Amount, Usage Frequency, and Avg Usage Amount. Usage Amount and Usage Frequency represent the number of times that each user has activated the application in the previous week and the number of days during which each user has used the app in the previous week, respectively. Avg Usage Amount indicates the ratio of Usage Amount to Usage Frequency.

We estimated the propensity scores or probabilities for the one coupon users to be assigned to the treatment group. Using a logistic regression, we modeled the likelihood of receiving a coupon on commuting routes as a function of the covariates, such as Days to Expiration and the Part of Day, Day of Week, and Product dummies. These variables were included to control the factors that may affect commuting patterns and redemption behaviors. We also included Usage Amount, Usage Frequency, and Avg Usage Amount to account for the mobile application usage patterns of users. Using the estimated propensity scores, we matched a control group user with a treatment group user to enable each treatment group user to have a corresponding unique control group user. In this way, we considered the biases that may arise from the different sample sizes of the treatment and control groups. After the matching procedure, 353 out of the 478 treatment group users were matched to control group users.

We identified the statistical differences of the covariates between the treatment and control groups before and after matching via a t-test to ensure the quality of the matching procedure (Table A.1). The results show that although the differences among several covariates are statistically significant before matching, all covariates are balanced and statistically similar after matching $( p > 0 . 1 )$ ). Figure 4 plots and compares the propensity score distribution of these covariates before and after matching. The gray solid line represents the matched treatment group, whereas the dotted and dashed lines represent the control group before and after the matching, respectively. Figure 4 shows that the propensity score distribution of the matched treatment group is more similar to that of the matched control group than to that of the unmatched control group. ## 5.1. Inclusion of Demographic Control Variables

## 5.2. Inclusion of User Location Control Variables

Stress levels may differ not only across users on different routes $( \mathrm { e . g . } ,$ , commuting and noncommuting routes) but also across those in different locations (e.g., metropolitan and rural areas). To validate whether the results remain robust even after controlling for the fixed effects of user locations, we obtained information on where users redeem their mobile coupons from the two participating retailers $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ Dunkin’ Donuts and CU). Considering that the information was collected when users redeemed their coupons, we could not identify the locations of users who did not exchange any of their mobile coupons. In addition, the locations where users redeem their mobile coupons do not al ways correspond to where their homes or workplaces are located. Nevertheless, we can infer where users live on the basis of the information that was readily available to us. Using such data, we classified users into four groups, namely, Metropolitan Users, Rural Users, Travelling Users, and Not-redeemed Users. Metropolitan Users are those who redeemed all their mobile coupons only in metropolitan areas (i.e., the capital and satellite cities near the capital that share a public transit system). Rural Users are those who redeemed all their mobile coupons only in rural areas (i.e., all locations, except the capital and the satellite cities near the capital). Travelling Users are those who redeemed their mobile coupons in both metropolitan and rural areas. Not-redeemed Users are those who have not redeemed any of their mobile coupons. We then employed Not-redeemed Users as a base group and included three dummy variables in the model for One or More Coupon Users. We focused only on One or More Coupon Users because the fixed effects can accurately predict the redemption behaviors of One Coupon Users. Table 6 shows that the results are largely consistent with those obtained in the main analysis thereby reinforcing the robustness of our results against location fixed effects.

## 5.3. Endogeneity of App Usage Behaviors

Previous redemption experiences can exert the positive effects on app usage. This possibility raises concerns regarding the possible endogeneity of app usage behaviors driven by previous redemption experiences (Pearl 2009, Morgan and Winship 2015). For example, if users realize that they can receive mobile coupons when using an app, then they may be motivated to increase app usage. To validate such possibility, we conducted additional analyses as a robustness check. For linear models, common approaches to addressing such endogeneity problems are to identify instrument variables, which are highly correlated with endogenous variables but uncorrelated with error terms, and to include the predicted values of endogenous covariates into the models. However, these methods may be inappropriate for nonlinear models, such as logit and probit models, which come with a binary dependent variable (Wooldridge 2010). As an alternative, a two-step residual approach is widely used in the marketing literature (e.g., Che et al. 2012, Danaher et al. 2015) and has been proven effective in reducing endogeneity biases in parameter estimation (Terza et al. 2008).

## 5.4. Assessment of Alternative Scenario

The treatment and control group users may experience systematically different events while they hold a coupon (i.e., from the day they receive a coupon until the day they redeem it), which can drive different redemption rates. ## 6. Conclusion

Despite the growing importance of contextual marketing in business (Salz 2015), relatively few studies have inquired into such a phenomenon given the difficulty of identifying and targeting consumer contexts. Research has centered on individual effects originating from the geographical and temporal features of targeting with efforts extending only as far as the investigation of these features’ simple interaction effects. Given that time and space are interrelated (Luo et al. 2014), however, each of geographical and temporal features cannot sufficiently demonstrate the contexts of customers and is insufficient as a basis for targeting the right people in the right (i.e., intended) context. Along with Andrews et al. (2016), who looked into how context (i.e., crowdedness) affects consumer responses to mobile coupons, to the best of our knowledge, our study is among the first to assess the effects of contextual targeting with commuting on mobile coupon redemption behaviors. The current research contributes to the growing body of the literature on contextual targeting in at least three key ways. First, we delved into a wide scope of contextual dimensions in an effort to deepen our understanding of contextual targeting. We extended the intriguing spatial context (i.e., crowdedness) that Andrews et al. (2016) explored to commuting, in which individuals dynamically interact with a given space. Although people in the same spatial environment are confronted with a similar level of crowdedness, their commuting statuses (i.e., commuters or noncommuters) can vary. Our findings confirm that people traveling over the same route and experiencing the same level of perceived crowdedness can differ in terms of redemption rates—a difference that is contingent upon commuting status. Second, commuting is a more easily identifiable context than crowdedness and thereby presents more wide-ranging opportunities for the implementation of mobile marketing. Mobile businesses can effortlessly distinguish between commuting and noncommuting consumers by using an API pro vided by map apps or adopting an SDK that is intended to detect users’ home and workplace addresses. Commuting, therefore, facilitates targeting via various mobile apps—a strategy that is not as achievable on the basis of perceived crowdedness given that such a context may be difficult to capture and leverage. Finally, we included the various elements of mobile coupons and corresponding treatment effects into our analysis, providing a holistic insight into designs and distribution-related strategies for mobile couponing (e.g., targeting contexts, number of coupons, and expiry lengths).

Several implications for research and practice can be gleaned from our results. This study expanded our understanding of the consequences of commuting and investigated such a phenomenon beyond its negative aspects. Extensive research on commuting has been devoted mostly to unfavorable effects, such as increased anxiety and hostility and decreased tolerance for frustration (Koslowsky et al. 1995). Carrying out a field study on the behaviors of 9,928 consumers who use mobile public transit apps, we, instead, identified how marketers can benefit from utilizing the commuting context, which is an easily identifiable routine that occurs throughout the world. The commuting context is considerably associated with a high likelihood of response to mobile coupons and at a fast redemption speed. Mobile marketers may capitalize on commuting to enhance the effectiveness of their mobile marketing strategies in different ways. For instance, mobile apps designed for food delivery (e.g., Domino’s Pizza) can expand the mobile marketing strategies of the business by limiting the coverage of mobile coupon distribution to commuters. Map applications (e.g., Google Maps) and local restaurant apps (e.g., Yelp), which have been evolving as marketing platforms, can also enjoy improved marketing effectiveness and profitability through the usage of the commuting context. Google Maps, which now allows local businesses to provide mobile coupons to its users, can supply commuter targeting functions at high bid prices. Online advertising services (e.g., Google AdWords) feature already differentiated bid prices according to various targeting methods.

Whereas most studies on mobile promotions restricted the number of coupons per individual to one, we allowed users to receive multiple coupons and then identified how the treatment effects change with multiple coupons. Furthermore, by adopting the dynamic and static matching procedures to generate matched control and treatment group users, we obtained the less biased estimates of the changes in treatment effects across multiple coupons (see Table 4). Accordingly, our results offer a number of constructive implications for mobile coupon distribution. The effect of Number of Coupons on redemption rates is significantly positive for the control group (p < 0.01) but is insignificant or significantly less positive for the treatment group (see Table 4). These results imply that, although marketers can increase the mobile coupon redemption rate of noncommuters by providing them with multiple coupons, such effectiveness does not extend to commuters. Therefore, for managers interested in directing their marketing strategies toward noncommuters, offering multiple coupons to each noncommuter can elevate their marketing performance. Conversely, marketing activities geared toward commuters must be restricted to offering only one coupon per commuter while increasing the number of targeted commuters to enhance the efficiency of the campaigns.

We also identified the different relationships between expiry lengths and redemption rates for commuters and noncommuters, providing fruitful implications for mobile coupon design. Mobile coupons with short redemption periods (e.g., expiry with a few hours or within a day) are more effective for commuters, whereas those with longer redemption periods (e.g., long exchange periods or regular redemption reminders) work more effectively for noncommuters (see Table 4). Marketers can also increase the mobile coupon redemption rates of commuters by utilizing coupons with short exchange periods.

Researchers and practitioners can likewise benefit from our explanation of why commuter targeting generates higher redemption rates than does noncommuter targeting. On the basis of psychological and physiological theories, we developed the argument that stress, which is exacerbated by commuting, increases commuters’ coupon redemption rates. We provided empirical support for this argument through additional analyses and validated that marketers can further increase response rates by focusing on the specific periods of the day when the commuting stress level is relatively high (e.g., rush hours) (Section 5.6). The results can be applicable to people facing similar stresses. People doing housework, for instance, are potential targets as housework is another important source of chronic stress (Kahneman et al. 2004). Despite the value provided by the study, however, the results should be interpreted with caution. Without further research, we cannot guarantee that the findings are indeed applicable to different types of stress (e.g., acute stress) and coupons of different products (e.g., clothes or movie tickets).

Similar to other studies, the current work has numerous limitations. The study involved food and beverage coupons given that products in such categories help diminish commuters’ stress. High response rates can be associated with the choice of product offerings available through coupon incentives. For the increase of the generalizability of the findings and control for potential product effects, products in alternative categories or brands should be offered in promotional coupon campaigns. Furthermore, the promotion literature suggests that consumers’ redemption behaviors may vary depending on specified or displayed savings (e.g., Chen et al. 1998, Della Bitta et al. 1981). Accordingly, future studies along this line of inquiry should vary the format of coupons in terms of discount rates and the presentation of savings (i.e., 50% versus \$10 off). Another limitation revolves around insufficient information about users’ demographic characteristics, such as income level and occupation. The availability of such demographic details would enable a highly comprehensive exploration of coupon redemption behaviors that may vary across diverse groups. Given the potential concerns about privacy, the public transit app studied in this research collects demographic data only from users who volunteer to supply such information. Finally, this study centered on the context of commuting-driven stress and its effects on coupon redemption propensities. Other stress-provoking situations must be tested to reinforce the reliability of the findings.

Given the prevalence of mobile devices and their arm’s reach availability all the time, mobile-based targeting is increasingly becoming an integral aspect of firms marketing campaigns. As we move close toward the app-based mobile economy, the importance of mobile apps as a bridge between online and offline channels is rapidly growing. Understanding consumers’ state of mind and satisfying their in-the-moment needs by analyzing temporal and spatial contexts will strongly encourage them to make offline purchases and enable firms to pave the way for successful marketing innovation.
