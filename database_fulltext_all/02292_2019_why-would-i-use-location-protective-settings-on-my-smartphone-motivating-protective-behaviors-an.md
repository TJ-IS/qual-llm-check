# Why Would I Use Location-Protective Settings on My Smartphone? Motivating Protective Behaviors and the Existence of the Privacy Knowledge–Belief Gap

Robert E. Crossler,<sup>a</sup> France Bélanger<sup>b</sup>

Abstract. The omnipresence of smartphones means that more and more personal information is accessed, transferred, or stored on these devices. Smartphone users struggle to control the release of their information when smartphones are always connected, close at hand, and the privacy settings for individual apps are difficult to access. To have meaningful privacy in this context, individuals must be knowledgeable about their devices and truly motivated to make use of the device’s privacy settings. We draw from extant privacy literature, the self-efficacy theory, and the information–motivation–behavioral skills model to understand usage of privacy settings on smartphones through data from 334 iPhone users. Our findings indicate that personal motivation is one of the strongest determinants of utilizing privacy-protective settings, and social motivation is not significant. Furthermore, privacy knowledge and self-efficacy constructs (i.e., knowledge specific to the device’s privacy settings) determine one’s use of privacy-protective settings, but knowledge and self-efficacy about smartphone technology do not. An interaction effect also exists between privacy knowledge and privacy self-efficacy such that people with high levels of privacy knowledge utilize less restrictive privacy settings when their confidence in protecting themselves is low, but as their self-efficacy increases, they are more likely to use more privacy-protective settings. We label this the privacy knowledge–belief gap.

## Introduction

The ubiquitous use of smartphones along with advances in accurately identifying and tracking smartphone locations, has resulted in organizations tracking people’s daily habits more intrusively than ever before. For example, as part of a \$21 billion locationtargeted advertising industry, some apps request updated location information more than 14,000 times per day (Valentino-DeVries et al. 2018). These location data, which often come from apps that provide local news and weather, are then analyzed and sold to advertisers and retail outlets (Valentino-DeVries et al. 2018). With more than two billion smartphones worldwide (Murphy 2017), often becoming the only device that a person uses to access the internet (Rainie and Perrin 2017), the amount of location-based information available to collect is enormous. Furthermore, people use their smartphones everywhere constantly, often without much cognitive effort such that they sometimes consider smartphones an extension of the self (Hein et al. 2011, Brasel 2016). Not surprisingly, many owners say “they couldn’t live without” their smartphones (Rainie 2016). Considering these facts, we argue that two key characteristics distinguish smartphones from other, earlier computing technologies (e.g., laptops, desktops): omnipresence and constant connectivity. Omnipresence is “the state of being widespread or constantly encountered” (Oxford Dictionary 2018). Most individuals keep their smartphones nearby when working, driving, socializing, relaxing in intimate settings, and even sleeping. Constant connectivity means that users may always be connected to digital services and content through their devices in a world of just-intime information, interactions, and decisions.

Findings from prior studies of privacy issues related to the use of technologies may not apply to the omnipresent and constantly connected nature of smartphones for several reasons. First, smartphones accompany users in their everyday lives and activities, becoming a device that can serve as a tool for guidance (e.g., GPS), memory, socialization, learning, exercise, and much more. The technology itself also differs from laptops and other computers in terms of informationsharing controls; smartphones utilize several informationsharing settings to control each app individually, and other computing technologies provide limited controls in fewer places. For example, with a smartphone, each interaction for activities, such as banking and driving, requires its own dedicated app with its own settings to control information sharing. These settings then predetermine the level of information disclosure allowed each time the app or smartphone is used regardless of where and when. Conversely, on a desktop or laptop computer, the main settings to restrict information sharing exist on selected applications, such as web browsers or communication tools (e.g., Skype).

An additional consideration about smartphones, especially iPhones, is the differential in ease of learning between the general device and the device’s privacy settings. The iPhone is designed to be easy to learn (Matyszczyk 2018) for both young children and seniors (Knapp 2018, Lieber 2018). However, iPhones have privacy controls and settings that are difficult to find; for example, to adjust some settings, users must click at least four times (Valentino-DeVries and Singer 2018). Despite this inaccessibility, settings that limit unauthorized or unintentional sharing of information must be turned on to protect data. This is why knowledge about privacy settings may play an important role in protecting the privacy of smartphone users (Crossler and Bélanger 2017). Lack of knowledge might explain the fact that even when individuals are concerned with their location information, 90% of smartphone users leave their location-based services (LBS) function turned on and use LBS for tasks that may not need location data, such as streaming movies or chatting (Anderson 2016).

A final factor affecting information privacy is that smartphones may contain significant amounts of information (Temming 2018), but their apps often use default settings that promote information sharing. These permissions can be problematic because individuals have limited awareness of which apps collect what information and how to protect their privacy (Klasnja et al. 2009). In fact, extant research has shown that many people do not take the simplest steps to protect their smartphones, such as using lock screens (28%), updating operating systems (14%), updating apps (10%) (Rainie and Perrin 2017), or managing privacy settings (28%) (O’Connell 2018). Other steps users could take to protect their location information is to delete rarely used apps (Valentino-DeVries and Singer 2018) or use a web browser (Corbett 2018) that has more consistent privacy-protective settings (Newman 2018), but few users do so. What, then, wil motivate users to enact privacy-protective behaviors?

To address this question, we extend prior research on privacy-behavior determinants by exploring the roles of both personal and social motivation as well as actual and perceived knowledge in affecting the use of privacy settings to protect personal information on smartphones. Our research framework integrates concepts from extant information-privacy literature and self-efficacy theory with the information–motivation– behavioral (IMB) skills model. The IMB skills model suggests that information and motivation influence behavior directly and through behavioral skills (Fisher and Fisher 1992, Fisher et al. 1994). We test the proposed framework with data from 334 iPhone users. We find that personal motivation is a very strong determinant of user privacy behavior, but social motivation is not: although the mobile device is used to interact with others, the influence of others has no effect on the use of location-protective settings (LPS). We also find that both privacy knowledge and selfefficacy are positively related to the use of LPS. However, knowledge and self-efficacy related to the device’s underlying technology make no impact on the use of privacy settings. Finally, we find, in a post hoc analysis, the existence of a privacy knowledge–belief gap: people low in privacy knowledge but high in beliefs (privacy self-efficacy) use less LPS. Similarly, individuals high in privacy knowledge but low in privacy self-efficacy also use less LPS.

The study’s findings provide novel insights into privacy and user-behavior research although the lack of effect from social motivation is inconsistent with several technology-adoption theories. The smartphone’s important role within an individual’s life, not what others think, drives that person’s decisions about information sharing via the device. This may apply to any technology that is omnipresent and constantly connected, such as wearables. The findings also reveal that privacy knowledge, in addition to privacy self-efficacy, is essential to engaging in privacy-protective behaviors. This suggests that studies examining technology-related user behaviors should also consider actual knowledge in addition to perceived knowledge. As society moves more toward technologies that have the potential to be invasive to personal privacy—such as augmented reality, in which information about others is easily accessible (Denning et al. 2014)—researchers must further examine the roles of motivation, knowledge, and selfefficacy in an individual’s enactment of privacy-protective behaviors.

## Background and Theoretical Foundations

One may define information privacy as “the claims of individuals that data about themselves should generally not be available to other individuals and organizations, and that, where data is possessed by another party, the individual must be able to exercise a substantial degree of control over that data and its use” (Clarke 1999, p. 60). However, smartphones omnipresence and constant connectivity allow constant tracking of users with LBS and facilitate increased information sharing—knowingly or not (Keith et al. 2013). Although some tools attempt to protect privacy on smartphones, mobile platforms generally are weak in offering information protection (Virvilis et al. 2015) even with built-in privacy settings in their operating systems such as iOS and Android (Apple 2017, Google 2017). Privacy settings may allow for applicationlevel controls that prevent unintentional information sharing, but only if the user enables them. Thus, mobile information protection relies on users properly utilizing information-protective settings for each app on their device. This has led to calls for more studies on “balancing of information privacy concerns with the advantages of location-based services” (Bélanger and Crossler 2011, p. 1022). Answering such calls, we study individuals’ actual use of privacy settings that limit sharing of location-based information on their smartphones. Because the research measures actual knowledge as a precursor to behavior, we use a theory that specifically includes knowledge and skills as antecedents to behavior: information–motivation–behavioral skills model.

## IMB Skills Model

The IMB skills model (Fisher and Fisher 1992, Fisher et al. 1994), presented in Figure 1, has mostly been used in the behavioral health and social psychology literatures to study and understand health-related behaviors. Examples include self-examinations to prevent breast cancer (Misovich et al. 2003), HIV risk–reduction behaviors (Fisher and Fisher 1992), and condom usage (Anderson et al. 2006). In the Information Systems (IS) literature, the IMB skills model has been suggested as a theoretical foundation to explore security and privacy behaviors (e.g., Khan et al. 2011 and Crossler and Bélanger 2017). The model assumes that individuals who are well-informed (information), motivated to act (motivation), and have the necessary behavioral skills to act are more likely to do so.

Information represents individuals’ understanding of the concepts related to the behavior of interest and of the means necessary to achieve behavioral change. Extant research in behavioral health has argued tha this knowledge must be context-specific, including the relevant facts and heuristics needed to enact de sired behavioral actions (Fisher et al. 2003). Examples in that domain include knowing about the risks related to HIV or cancer to ensure proper protective behaviors (e.g., Fisher et al. 2003). In most studies information is operationalized as knowledge abou the behavioral action (e.g., Anderson et al. 2006, Starace et al. 2006, and John et al. 2017) or awareness of the risks that the behavioral action will preven (e.g., Fisher et al. 2003). We refer to knowledge as actua abilities—a user’s actual knowledge about the tasks or behaviors of interest—compared with perceived abil ities, which are a user’s perceptions about being able to perform the tasks or behaviors. The IMB skills model suggests that both an individual’s actual abilities and perceived skills are needed to enact a protective behavior (Fisher et al. 2003). Perceived abilities are often operationalized as self-efficacy, which is “the conviction that one can successfully execute the behavior” (Bandura 1977, p. 193). Consistent with studies using this theoretical foundation, the behavioral-skills construct is operationalized as self-efficacy, and information is operationalized as knowledge. We explore two types of knowledge and self-efficacy in this research: those about smartphone privacy and those about smartphone technology. Privacy knowledge refers to contextual knowledge about privacy settings on smartphones while privacy self-efficacy is one’s belief that one knows how to use the privacy settings on one’s smartphone. Technology knowledge refers to actual contextual knowledge about the smartphone tech nology itself and how to use it. Technology self-efficacy is one’s belief that one knows the technology and how to use it. Consistent with the idea that information pri vacy refers to some form of information-sharing contro (e.g., Bélanger et al. 2002), we argue that, to exercise control, one must have knowledge of the object of con trol, in this case the smartphone and its settings

Motivation includes both personal and social motivation (Fisher et al. 2003). Personal motivation is related to personal attitudes or intentions tied to behavioral actions; social motivation is based on the social support system that can reinforce one’s motivation to perform the behavioral action (Fisher et al. 2003). Building on the IMB skills model, we examine both personal and social motivation, operationalized as sharing preferences and subjective norm. The IMB skills model provides support for motivation and information to have both direct relationships to behavior and indirect relationships to behavior through behavioral skills (Fisher et al. 2003). The direct effects of information and motivation are present when the behavior is not overly complicated to perform. Furthermore, when the motivation or information component is strong enough, it individually can affect the behavior without the other components being present (Fisher et al. 2003). In the IMB skills model, preventive behavior is the ultimate dependent variable. In information-privacy research, behavior is most often studied as willingness to disclose information (Smith et al. 2011); however, our dependent variable measures the user’s actual privacy protective behavior through the use of LPS.

To answer the research question, we focus our hypotheses on the role of motivation. However, to remain consistent with extant privacy literature and the theoretical foundation, we include in our tests the baseline constructs of privacy risk awareness and knowledge and the baseline relationships between awareness, self-efficacy, and privacy behavior as well as the relationships between knowledge constructs and their corresponding self-efficacy constructs and privacy behaviors. Those baseline constructs and relationships are shown as dashed lines in Figure 2, which presents the research model. The constructs and relationships are explained further in the remainder of this section.

## Social or Personal Motivation?

Extant research using the IMB skills model has considered various combinations of personal and social motivations with some studies including only personal motivation (e.g., Kalichman et al. 2001 and Mayberry and Osborn 2014) and others including both personal and social motivation (e.g., Anderson et al. 2006 and John et al. 2017). Although social motivation almost always is measured as a social or subjective norm, personal motivation is measured as either attitude (e.g., Starace et al. 2006 and Osborn and Egede 2010), intentions (e.g., Kalichman et al. 2001), or both (e.g., Anderson et al. 2006 and John et al. 2017).

We previously discussed the fact that smartphones have become a very personal device, sometimes viewed as an extension of the self (Hein et al. 2011, Brasel 2016) This concept is very important in discussing what motivates users to protect themselves. Thus far, it has been argued that smartphones’ omnipresence and constant connectivity have been fueling privacy issues; at the same time, these key characteristics may offer users many benefits. In several instances, individuals may be willing to share information. With smartphone apps, individuals do not need to memorize passwords or phone numbers; they easily can access knowledge needed to participate in any indepth discussion on any topic they have not mastered; they can find their way with maps, whether driving, walking, or bicycling; and they know when it is going to rain, or when packages will be delivered to their homes. Smartphones’ omnipresence offers instant access to digital content and services and even physical services (e.g., Uber, groceries, etc.). Along with offering these benefits, a smartphone stores a vast amount of information that users may or may not want to share. For these reasons, it is likely that the motivation to share information or use less privacy protective settings is more personal than social. In our study, consistent with the IMB skills model, we operationalize personal motivation as the attitude that the user has toward sharing the user’s LBS information (e.g., Anderson and Agarwal 2011 and Xu et al. 2011) through smartphone apps. With smartphones requiring access to information for apps to function and users experiencing benefits from sharing information, motivation to share information should reduce use of privacy-protective settings.

## Hypothesis 1a. Higher levels of information-sharing preferences lead to less mobile privacy-protective behavior.

The IMB skills model also suggests that social motivation affects behaviors and behavioral skills. Social motivation deals with social support systems that can reinforce one’s motivation (perception of support to engage in the behavior). Consistent with extant security research (Bulgurcu et al. 2010, Bélanger et al. 2017), we operationalize social motivation as the subjective norm, which is an individual’s perception of what important others would like them to do—in this case, protect their information privacy on their smartphone. Subjective norm represents “the perceived social pressure to perform or not to perform [a] behavior” (Ajzen 1991, p. 188). In the present context, it would suggest that if one’s important others believe an individual should protect information on one’s smartphone, this individual may be more willing to enact these protections. In the IMB skills model, there is a positive link between motivation and behavior. Our hypothesis, reflects this theoretical foundation. We note, however, that the unique context of the omnipresent and constantly connected smartphone, which stores very personal information and provides broad access to digital content and services, suggests that the smartphone is such a personal device that it is unclear how important others’ influence is in this case.

Hypothesis 1b. Lower levels of subjective norm lead to less mobile privacy-protective behavior.

## What Types of Motivation In<sup>fl</sup>uence Con<sup>fi</sup>dence in One's Abilities?

To explore whether personal and social motivation should lead to greater self-efficacy, we draw from self-efficacy theory (SET), which suggests that performance accomplishments, vicarious experience, verbal persuasion, and emotional arousal influence one’s perception of self-efficacy (Bandura 1977). The performance accomplishments construct refers to personal mastery of a related behavior or “past experiences with the specific task being investigated” (Staples et al. 1999, p. 759). The performance accomplishments construct is often the most influential antecedent to selfefficacy (Bandura 1977). As an outcome of using their technologies (past experiences) people realize the actualized net benefits of doing so (Rai et al. 2002). In the smartphone context, the actualized net benefits are the result of the information that an individual is willing to share via devices and apps, which develops the individual’s sharing preferences over time. As discussed previously, to take advantage of the benefits provided by the constantly connected and omnipresent smartphone, individuals must share information via this technology platform. Therefore, individuals’ sharing preferences should align with their confidence (selfefficacy) that they know what they want to share and how to control this sharing of information. As individuals gain experience using their device and control ling their privacy settings, they should develop more confidence in both privacy and technology skills. Thus, self-efficacy is part of a reciprocal relationship between behaviors, the environment, and cognitive factors (Compeau and Higgins 1995).

Hypothesis 2a. Higher levels of sharing preferences lead to higher mobile privacy self-efficacy.

## Hypothesis 2b. Higher levels of sharing preferences lead to higher mobile technology self-efficacy.

We previously argued that social motivation likely is less important in affecting individual privacy behaviors than personal motivation, so why would social motivation affect self-efficacy? We turn to the other two antecedents of self-efficacy posited by SET: vicarious experience and verbal persuasion. Vicarious experience is the experience one develops by observing others performing a task. Observing others allows individuals to build their confidence that they also can perform the task (Bandura 1977). For example, in a study in which managers modeled their best remote work practices to their employees, those employees’ confidence in using information technology to work remotely increased (Staples et al. 1999). Verbal persuasion, also called socia persuasion, refers to being persuaded by others that one can perform a task successfully (Bandura 1977). Both concepts are related to how “important others” affect one’s perceptions, which is the core of the subjective norm construct. Because of the omnipresent nature of smartphones, there are many opportunities for “others” to interact with smartphone owners, which allows for either observation of an individual performing tasks on a smartphone, including use of privacy settings, or reception of verbal persuasion about the importance of knowing how to properly use the smartphone and its privacy settings. Therefore, consistent with the IMB skills model, we propose that social motivation influences a user’s perception of the user’s abilities, or selfefficacy, for both the technology itself and its privacy settings.

## Baseline Constructs and Relationships

The IMB skills model and the privacy literature suggest additional constructs and relationships beyond our hypotheses, which are included for nomological completeness. For example, a significant amount of extant security and privacy research has explored the relationships between awareness and behaviors (e.g., Bulgurcu et al. 2010, Malandrino et al. 2013, and Parsons et al. 2014) and between self-efficacy and behaviors (e.g., Liang and Xue 2010 and Boss et al. 2015). Privacy risk awareness refers to individuals’ awareness of threats to their information privacy on their smartphones. Self-efficacy theory also suggests that past experience, during which knowledge is accu mulated, is an antecedent to self-efficacy (Bandura 1977). Finally, the links from knowledge to behaviors, previously discussed, are also supported by prior research. The omnipresence of smartphones means that information can be collected anytime and anywhere without individuals being clearly aware that their devices or apps are disclosing information, including the location and time-stamping data that many types of apps collect automatically (Xu et al. 2011, Keith et al. 2013). Although the collection of some personal data may seem unimportant to some individuals, the aggregation of different pieces of data can result in the creation of profiles about them (Bélanger and Hiller 2006). Individuals must, therefore, know how to enact practices to protect themselves (i.e., proper use of privacy settings). However, with the average user having more than 200 apps on the user’s mobile device and each app requiring an average of nine permissions (Wilson 2014), it is difficult to keep up with all settings. In fact, extant research suggests that a limiting factor to people performing protective behaviors is their lack of knowledge of technical tools to do so (Kang et al. 2015). Although privacy settings can offer protection from unwanted information sharing, knowledge of other features of smartphones, such as turning off Wi-Fi or Bluetooth, could offer additional information protection. Although not directly related to information sharing, improper Wi-Fi or Bluetooth settings can allow information to be leaked easily, especially when using public networks (Newman 2017). As such, general knowledge about technology should also be a determinant of use of privacy-protective behaviors.

## Methodology

We surveyed 334 iPhone users with an instrument created by adapting some items from extant studies and developing others. The focus was on the iPhone, as Apple has fewer operating system variants and does not allow different vendors to release their own versions of the operating system (which is done in the Android ecosystem; Google 2017). Considering that no measures for privacy behavior, privacy knowledge, or technology knowledge existed, we developed and validated new measures. Privacy behavior was operationalized as a ratio of the number of apps with LBS available as the denominator and the number of apps granted permission to provide LBS information as the numerator. To collect this information, it was necessary to provide users with step-by-step directions on where to retrieve it from their devices. Two iPhone users not involved in the study validated these steps. The researchers calculated privacy and technology knowledge scores based on correct answers to sets of knowledge questions. Items are provided in Online Appendix A, together with instrument-development procedures and measurement model testing.

We conducted two pilot tests. The first tested the instrument’s overall psychometric properties using data from 101 undergraduate business students, and the second tested measurement of the dependent variable with 228 iPhone users, collected from Amazon Mechanical Turk (mTurk). We used a granular LBS settings measure with detailed instructions. For example, respondents counted the number of weather apps in the LBS settings, then noted which ones used their location information. Such reporting was done for these categories of apps: games, maps, weather, camera, and shopping. The results confirmed the instrument’s overall psychometric properties, but in open comments, participants complained about the overall length of the survey and the LBS questions’ repetitiveness. We ran a correlation analysis between overall LBS and each granular LBS setting for each app category. All correlations between overall and granular LBS settings were significantly strong. Given the increased cogni tive efforts in asking for granular settings and given that the overall LBS privacy settings captured the granular settings, we included only overall LBS settings in our final data collection

The main data collection included 404 mTurk subjects that Amazon predetermined to be iPhone owners. Respondents were not involved in our pilot test. To ensure that data would be representative of the population of interest and avoid possible biases inherent in the use of crowdsourcing data identified by Steelman et al. (2014), we followed those researchers’ recommendations as described in Online Appendix B. Sixty-six responses were eliminated in the data-screening process explained in Online Appendix B, which also includes sample details, resulting in 334 valid responses.<sup>1</sup> Prior to testing the model, we calculated privacy and technology knowledge scores for each respondent using the same procedures from the pilot test. SmartPLS 2.0 (Ringle et al. 2005) was used to test the research model (1,000 bootstrapping subsamples) given the complexity of our model (Chin et al. 2008).<sup>2</sup> Figure 3 shows the results.

## Discussion

This research explores the determinants of privacy behaviors, focusing on the relative roles of personal and social motivation and taking into consideration the roles of actual and perceived knowledge in affecting those behaviors. Privacy behavior was measured as users’ privacy settings controlling the sharing of LBS information on smartphones. To the best of our knowledge, ours is the first study to measure the actual restriction of information disclosure through a technical feature (i.e., setting) on smartphones.

## Social or Personal Motivation?

Although our theoretical foundations suggest that both social and personal motivation should affect the enactment of protective behaviors (LPS), we argued that the omnipresent and constantly connected nature of smartphones suggests that personal motivation would be a stronger determinant. Our results not only support this claim, but also reveal that social motivation, operationalized as subjective norm, has no effect at all on the use of LPS on a smartphone. This lack of effect is inconsistent with prior research that draws on the theory of planned behavior in which subjective norm influences behavioral intentions (Ajzen 1991) or technology-adoption theories in which some form of social influence determines behaviors or behavioral intentions.

One potential explanation for the lack of effect of subjective norm was discussed in the hypothesis development section. We argued that the smartphone has become such a personal device that others’ opinions do not matter to one’s use of privacy settings. As technologies become increasingly personal—from the use of Amazon Alexa as a nanny (Schwedel 2018) to fitness trackers constantly monitoring users’ vital signs to augmented reality in which one can capture and visualize the information of others (Acquisti et al 2014, Denning et al. 2014)—researchers need to explore further how to motivate individuals to engage in privacy-protective behaviors. Furthermore, as today’s smartphones are omnipresent in individuals lives, these devices store information beyond that of the device’s owner (others’ pictures, chats, messages, etc.), making the user responsible to protect others data as well. In this context, in which people use and share information about others, studies are needed that would explore how to motivate users to also protect others’ information. One possibility is to consider the role of psychological ownership as it relates to motivation (Ho and Chung-Shing 2014, Baxter et al. 2015). Baxter et al. (2015) suggest that individuals become attached to objects, leading to psychological ownership when they have positive feelings of efficacy (control), when they self-identify with the tech nology, and when the technology makes them “feel like they have a place or home” (p. 143). Future research could study if it is possible to vary levels of psychological ownership to influence users to engage in privacy-protective behaviors. It is also possible that the lack of effect from social motivation is due to our focus on actual use of LBS settings on a device that may need to be revisited over time instead of general adoption of a technology at any point in time. In other words, subjective norm may be less likely to affect users over time compared with initial adoption. It is also possible that others’ opinions affect feature-level usage less when compared with holistic technologies. Future research is needed to explore how usage granularity may change adoption and continuance theories. It is also possible that in feature-level technological contexts, other types of social motivation become more relevant. In particular, researchers may wish to consider privacy behavior within the mobile environment using a layered perspective, in which, for example, social motivation affects privacy behaviors related to social media apps but not for nonsocial media apps. Another exciting possibility to explore is whether “important others” have changed as devices have become more personal. Is the device itself becoming an “important other,” or are social influence mechanisms being channeled through technologies? Again, as we move toward more devices that users may consider extensions of the self, it may be that social influence will become part of the device. Are one’s decisions about a device, such as a smartphone, driven by that device’s role in the user’s life?

The lack of effect from social motivation raises many questions as discussed, but the effect of personal motivation also provides avenues for future research. Personal motivation, operationalized as individuals preferences for sharing information, is the strongest determinant of privacy behavior, but it also affects behavior via self-efficacy. This is consistent with the theoretical discussion presented earlier. As individuals decide they like to share information, they feel competent managing their information privacy. To further explore the role of personal motivation, we conducted additional tests on the paths between sharing preferences, self-efficacy, and behavior and found that sharing preferences exerted a partially mediated effect on privacy behavior via privacy selfefficacy. The question is what happens first? Do individuals develop their confidence first? Do they acquire knowledge first? Do they decide to share information and then develop their actual and perceived abilities? Our cross-sectional data do not allow us to explore these questions. Future research is needed to investigate behaviors in a process model. Exploring changes in knowledge, self-efficacy, and behaviors over time could elicit the need to revisit SET as we better determine the sequence of events in individuals’ use of personal technologies. Given our results on motivation, SET also may need to be revisited to explain behaviors in contexts in which personal motivation far outweighs social motivation.

## Privacy or Technology Knowledge?

As discussed, smartphones are designed to be easyto-learn devices, but their privacy settings are often hidden or difficult to use. Our findings suggest that general knowledge related to smartphones makes no impact on utilization of LPS. However, contextual knowledge about privacy settings does make an impact with increased knowledge resulting in the use of more protective settings. Therefore, individuals who know more or perceive that they know more about mobile technology are not inclined to protect their privacy unless they also possess the privacy settings, knowledge, and self-efficacy to do so. Given extant research on self-efficacy’s effect on security and privacy behaviors, which were discussed earlier, our findings raise some key questions. First, how much contextualization is needed when exploring behaviors? The effect of contextual privacy knowledge and self-efficacy suggests that contextualization is very important indeed for both the behavior of interest and the measurement of knowledge.

Another key point of inquiry is whether prior studies have missed a key element of what affects individua behaviors by focusing only on perceived abilities and not measuring individuals’ actual abilities. The answer to this question is less clear, indicating a need for more research to explore the relative roles of perceived and actual abilities in influencing behaviors. In this study, privacy knowledge significantly affects privacy self-efficacy, but in the opposite direction that we would have argued for based on the IMB skills model: the more that users know, the less they feel “competent” at managing their privacy. In some ways, this resembles the concept of learned helplessness (Hansen and Thomsen 2013), in which, when users know more, they feel less competent performing a behavior. This also could explain why Marcolin et al. (2000) found that people generally had more confidence in their abilities than their knowledge indicated. This unexpected relationship, combined with the positive paths from privacy knowledge and selfefficacy to behavior, led us to explore further the relative roles of perceived and actual privacy abilities in this study. More specifically, we wondered what role self-efficacy (perceived knowledge) plays in the presence of varying amounts of actual knowledge. Therefore, we conducted a post hoc floodlight analysis using the Johnson–Neyman (JN) technique provided by the PROCESS plugin within SPSS (Hayes 2017). The JN technique is useful in identifying whether interaction effects are present between two continuous variables on a continuous dependent variable and the point at which the interaction effect changes from being significant to insignificant (Carden et al. 2017). In essence, this process is like shining a floodlight on areas in which the nature of the interaction changes. We conducted a JN analysis of the interaction between privacy self-efficacy and knowledge on privacy behavior. The interaction effect is significant and holds for all data points. To display the results of the JN analysis graphically, we created a mean split of privacy knowledge for respondents, placing people into high and low knowledge categories. Figure 4 displays the results of this interaction.

In Figure 4, a higher number on the privacy behavior scale represents a lower LPS or less protective behavior (more location information sharing). The interaction effect shown in the figure provides insights not identified when testing the direct effects alone. People with high levels of privacy knowledge utilize less restrictive privacy settings when their confidence in protecting themselves is low, but as their self-efficacy increases, they are more likely to use more LPS. People with low levels of privacy knowledge utilize less restrictive privacy settings (i.e., release more information) when their confidence in protecting themselves is high. This finding is consistent with the idea that a privacy knowledge–belief gap exists that influences privacy behaviors (Crossler and Bélanger 2017) with knowledge and self-efficacy at different ends of the spectrum. Crossler and Bélanger (2017) argue that two categories of people exemplify the privacy knowledge–belief gap: those with high privacy knowledge but low privacy self-efficacy and those with low privacy knowledge but high privacy self-efficacy. For the first group, they have the knowledge and, thus, the potential to use protective settings but do not believe they can do so. For the second group, they do not have the privacy setting knowledge needed but are overconfident in their perceived ability to use settings. This results in them using less protective privacy settings that may not be consistent with what they would prefer, potentially releasing more information than desired. For individuals with both low or high privacy knowledge and self-efficacy, no knowledge– belief gap exists, and they use settings consistent with either their high (use more LPS) or low (use less LPS) knowledge and beliefs.

The findings regarding the privacy knowledge– belief gap indicate that more research is needed to explore the relationship between perceived and actual knowledge. Potential experiments could examine how different training techniques—with some focusing on persuasive messaging aimed at increasing selfconfidence of privacy settings use or increasing actual knowledge of privacy settings—lead to the use of more protective settings. The results would potentially move us toward closing the knowledge–belief gap and provide insight on how best to offer appropriate customized training based on an individual’s knowledge and confidence. Researchers could use the new iPhone technology and privacy knowledge measures that we developed or adapt them to other plat forms by following the process described in Online Appendix A.

One intriguing implication of this research stems from the combined lack of effect from social moti vation and the importance of perceived and actual privacy knowledge. In the IMB skills model, information and motivation impact behavior directly and through behavioral skills: in other words, the information that individuals possess and their motivation affect their perceived ability to enact a behavior, which then affects their actual behavior. Therefore, we explored the roles of subjective norm and sharing preferences on self-efficacy. The lack of effect from social motivation on self-efficacy may be explained by some of the same elements discussed, or it may be that in dividual constructs (knowledge, awareness, and sharing preferences) overtake the social construct (subjective norm) in this context. Indeed, individual experience– related constructs are often the strongest indicators of self-efficacy (Bandura 1977). However, if others cannot influence users, how do we motivate people to learn about privacy and change their privacy behaviors? The results suggest that asking parents, teachers, or impor tant others to tell individuals how to better protect themselves is unlikely to give the intended results. Given the importance of personal motivation, experiential learning via games, self-evaluation quizzes, or educational apps, for example, might offer a better chance not only to provide necessary privacy awareness and knowledge, but also to give individuals the perception that they can perform the required actions.

A meta-analysis of 40 years of research showed that learning outcomes are significantly higher when experiential learning is implemented compared with other pedagogical approaches to instruction (Burch et al. 2016).

Several relationships in the framework provide additional insights consistent with our theoretical foundations. Drawn from the idea that past experiences affect self-efficacy in SET, two information constructs—privacy risk awareness and technology knowledge—affected their respective self-efficacy constructs. Because privacy risk awareness was linked positively to privacy self-efficacy but not to privacy behaviors, we ran a post hoc mediation analysis and found that the relationship between privacy risk awareness and privacy behavior was fully mediated such that, when privacy self-efficacy is not present in the model, privacy risk awareness significantly influences privacy behavior. This finding supports the theoretical foundation and further indicates the important role of selfefficacy in affecting behaviors. Future research should consider including individuals’ awareness and knowledge in models that measure behavior as both constructs lead to more protective behaviors, either directly or indirectly.

The societal implications of the research raise the question of who should be responsible for protecting users’ information. Given the results of this study about knowledge, we need a society in which technology providers design devices with privacy controls as easy to use and understand as the device’s general functionality. Smartphone providers appear to know how to provide easy-to-use designs, but they should apply this ability to privacy controls. In the Android ecosystem, privacy design decisions are starting to play a more important role because of recent updates that require developers to use updated application programming interfaces, which provide more privacy protections for users (Raphael 2018). As society moves more toward potentially privacy-invasive technologies, such as augmented reality (Roesner et al. 2014), in which information about others can be displayed to users (Denning et al. 2014), more effort must be devoted to the design of privacy controls. In fact, it would benefit citizens’ privacy if device producers simplified privacy settings across all apps on the device rather than requiring users to set privacy settings for each app individually. Otherwise, the task of ensuring individuals’ privacy with the use of these technologies will continue to rest with the individuals themselves.

## Limitations

One limitation of the study is whether mTurk participants truthfully answered survey questions. As discussed in the methods section, these concerns are lessened because of our study’s design, which captured factual information about settings. As a further validation, we compared 33 students’ self-reported settings with data we collected manually by examining the students’ phones. We found no statistical differences between self-reported and researcher-entered settings, illustrating that the instructions were clear and accurately reflected actual device settings. We also did not measure how often respondents review their settings. It could be that some people have spent more time evaluating each and every app and specifically controlling which apps they allow to access their location information, and others just limited the number of apps on their phone and used a minimal number of them. However, the outlier analysis indicates that using these data points did not change the significant paths in our research model, suggesting that this is likely not an issue. Future research could shed further light on this by comparing individuals who often change settings to those who do not. Online Appendix B presents participant demographics, which do not match the U.S. population perfectly, especially for household income because respondents reported higher incomes than the population. This is not surprising, however, as researchers have demonstrated that iPhone users are most likely to be in the top income quartile (Owen 2018)

## Conclusion

Smartphones have become essential for functioning in society (Economist 2015), but this added functionality has created an opportunity for people to be tracked simply by using their devices (Valentino-DeVries et al. 2018). With many users either unaware of privacy issues or not knowledgeable about how to handle them (Klasnja et al. 2009), there is a need for more research to understand how to ensure users enact privacy-protective behaviors, especially with the measurement of actual privacy behaviors (Bélanger and Xu 2015). Answering those calls, this study developed and tested a theoretical model to explore determinants of use of privacy settings that restrict LBS information disclosure on smartphones The implications of the study’s findings are that individuals need to learn about privacy settings on their devices to prevent unauthorized information sharing, and we need to appeal to their personal motivation to ensure that they learn and enact the desired behaviors. The study also demonstrates the importance of including actual and perceived knowledge in privacy behavior research.
