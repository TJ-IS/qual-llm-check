# Career Orientations of MIS Employees: An Empirical Analysis

By: Magid Igbaria
Jeffrey H. Greenhaus
Saroj Parasuraman

Department of Management
Drexel University
Philadelphia, Pennsylvania 19104

## Abstract

The career orientations of employees can have important implications for their job satisfaction, commitment, and retention within organizations. However, there is little empirical research on the correlates of career orientations held by managers and professionals in the MIS field. This study sought to address this gap in the literature and assessed the career orientations of 464 MIS employees, as well as their relationship with selected demographic characteristics, job type, and career outcomes. The most prevalent career orientations of MIS employees were found to be technical and managerial. Autonomy and lifestyle orientations were also found to be moderately represented in the sample. Women were more lifestyle oriented and less technically oriented than men. In addition, systems programmers, applications programmers, and software engineers tended to be technically oriented, whereas systems analysts, project leaders, and computer managers tended to be managerially oriented. The most significant finding was that employees whose career orientations were compatible with their job setting reported high job satisfaction, high career satisfaction, strong commitment to their organization, and low intentions to leave their organization. Firms need to recognize the diversity of career orientations so that appropriate reward systems and career paths can be developed. Research on this topic should continue to examine characteristics unique to MIS employees, as well as how these interrelationships change over time at different career stages.

Keywords: MIS personnel, career management, career orientations/anchors
ACM Categories: K.6., K.6.1, K.7.1

## Introduction

The sustained growth of career opportunities for management information systems (MIS) personnel (Connolly, 1988; Rifkin, 1985; Saks, 1989) has highlighted the need for increased research on career issues as they pertain to MIS employees (Bartol, 1983; Bartol and Martin, 1982; Baroudi, 1985; Ginzberg and Baroudi, 1988; Hartog and Herbert, 1986; Turner and Baroudi, 1986). Prior research on MIS careers has tended to concentrate on the “external career” of employees; that is, on the sequence of jobs and positions through which employees progress (Van Maanen and Schein, 1977). Little attention has been devoted to examining aspects of the “internal career” of MIS personnel—individuals’ aspirations, values, perceptions, and affective reactions to job experiences—which can have important implications for their satisfaction, commitment, and retention within the organization. Noting the dearth of research in this area, Ginzberg and Baroudi (1988) called for an examination of issues pertaining to the internal career of MIS employees, focusing on their self-concept and career values.

An important element of an employee's internal career is the career anchor (Schein, 1971; 1975) or career orientation (DeLong, 1982). A career anchor refers to a cluster of self-perceived needs, values, and talents that give shape to an employee's career decisions. It can be thought of as a central component of the self-concept that an employee is unwilling to relinquish, even if forced to make a difficult choice. Schein (1987) has identified eight career anchors that guide an employee's career decisions: (1) security/stability; (2) autonomy/independence; (3) managerial competence; (4) technical or functional competence; (5) entrepreneurial creativity; (6) sense of service/dedication; (7) pure challenge; and (8) lifestyle integration.

An employee's career anchor or career orientation $^{1}$ is significant because it influences the selection of specific occupations and work settings, and it affects the employee's reactions to his or her work experiences (Schein, 1975). The purpose of this study was to examine the career orientations of MIS employees and assess the relationships of employees' career orientations with their work experiences and job attitudes. Specifically, this study was guided by three research questions: (1) What is the relative frequency of each type of career orientation among a sample of MIS employees? (2) Are there differences in the demographic backgrounds and the types of jobs held by employees with different career orientations? (3) Are employees whose career orientation “matches” their current job setting more satisfied with their job and career, more committed to their organization, and less inclined to leave their organization than employees whose orientation does not “match” their job setting?

To our knowledge, there has been no prior empirical research that explicitly examined the career orientations held by MIS employees. However, there is reason to believe that the most prevalent career orientations of MIS personnel are either technical or managerial (Chesebrough and Davis, 1983; Lucas, 1989). The technically oriented employee strongly prefers to remain a specialist in the MIS function without taking on broad managerial responsibilities, whereas the mangerially oriented employee aspires to move increasingly toward administration and general management where different motives, talents, and interpersonal skills are critical to success. In fact, the concept of a dual-career path within the MIS function (Ginzberg and Baroudi, 1988) implicitly assumes that most MIS employees are oriented toward either technical or managerial careers. Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed.

## Hypothesis 1:

$H_{0}$ : MIS employees are as likely to hold technical or managerial career orientations, such as security, autonomy, entrepreneurial, service, pure challenge, or lifestyle career orientations.

H₁: MIS employees are more likely to hold technical or managerial career orientations other than security, autonomy, entrepreneurial, service, pure challenge, or lifestyle career orientations.

Since career orientations influence the selection of specific occupations and job settings, MIS employees with different orientations are likely to be found in somewhat different types of jobs within their organizations. The fact that programmers often prefer to remain in technical positions and systems analysts consider moves to a managerial career ladder (Carlyle, 1989; Ferratt and Starke, 1989) suggests that employees with a technical orientation are more likely to hold technical staff positions, whereas managerially oriented employees are more likely to hold administrative positions. Therefore, it appears reasonable to expect that employees with technical and managerial orientations will hold technical and managerial jobs respectively.

## Hypothesis 2:

$H_{0}$ : The career orientations of MIS employees are unrelated to the type of positions they hold.

$H_{1}$ : MIS employees with a technical career orientation are more likely to hold technical positions than non-technical positions, and those with a managerial career orientation are more likely to hold managerial positions than non-managerial positions.

Although no formal hypotheses were offered, we also explored the possibility that employees with other career orientations (e.g., challenge or lifestyle) will cluster in certain jobs within the MIS field.

The concept of person-environment fit, which has been examined in the context of work adjustment (Dawis and Lofquist, 1984), job stress (Caplan, 1983; Caplan, et al., 1980; Harrison, 1985), and job design (Hackman and Oldham, 1980; Hulin and Blood, 1968), is central to our investigation. Person-environment fit is reflected by the extent to which: (1) the resources and rewards provided by the job are compatible with the employee's needs and preferences; and (2) the demands and requirements of the job are compatible with the employee's skills and abilities. The potential consequences of a lack of employee job fit include anxiety, strain, job dissatisfaction, and other negative outcomes (Caplan, 1983; Caplan, et al., 1980; Dawis and Lofquist, 1984; Harrison, 1985).

The research described in this article examined the match or compatibility between an employee's career orientation and his or her current job setting. A job is compatible with a career orientation when it involves job duties and assignments that the employee finds interesting, when it requires abilities that the employee possesses and values, and when it provides rewards that the employee finds desirable. Because of these features, employees whose jobs are compatible with their career orientation should experience positive feelings about their work lives and should feel bonded to their organization.

## Hypothesis 3:

$H_{0}$ : The match between career orientation and job setting is unrelated to MIS employees' job satisfaction, career satisfaction, organizational commitment, and intention to leave.

H₁: MIS employees whose career orientation matches their current job setting are more satisfied with their job and career, more committed to their organization, and less inclined to leave their organization than employees whose orientation does not match their job setting.

In summary, this study sought to examine the distribution of different career orientations within the MIS field, identify background and job characteristics associated with each type of career orientation, and examine the impact of a match between orientation and job setting on several career outcomes. The findings of this research should provide valuable insights into the career orientations of MIS employees, as well as information relevant to the establishment of human resource strategies for managing the careers of MIS employees.

## Method

## Sample and procedure

A questionnaire was distributed to 2,548 members of the Association for Computing Machinery (ACM) in Pennsylvania, Delaware, and southern New Jersey. The sample was chosen because members of ACM represent a wide variety of jobs and organizational settings. Five hundred and seventeen questionnaires (response rate of 20.3 percent) were returned to the researchers. This response rate appears to be consistent with other mail surveys (George and Barksdale, 1974; McNamara, 1972). Fifty three questionnaires were eliminated due to missing data or subject unsuitability (retired employees and faculty at academic institutions), leaving a final sample of 464 employees (response rate of 18.2 percent). The participants in this study held a variety of positions within the computer field, reflecting considerable heterogeneity within the MIS profession. Table 1 presents a summary of the demographic characteristics of the sample.

In view of the somewhat low response rate to the survey, it was necessary to determine the representativeness of the sample to the population of ACM members (see Table 1). Fletcher, et al. (1986) reported that 85 percent of ACM members were men, whereas 80.3 percent of our sample were men. Furthermore, 94 percent of ACM members are reported to be 55 years old or younger, compared with 93.2 percent in our sample. With regard to education, Fletcher, et al. (1986) reported that 56 percent of ACM members had a master's degree (51 percent in our sample), while 92 percent of ACM members had at least a bachelor's degree (compared to 94 percent in our sample). In addition, we found that 49.5 percent of the participants had fewer than nine years of computer experience (versus 48 percent in the ACM report), 67.7 per cent had fewer than 14 years of computer experience (versus 68 percent in the ACM report), and 10 percent of the participants in our sample had more than 25 years of experience in computers (versus 9 percent in the ACM report). These data provide substantial evidence that the demographic characteristics of our sample are quite similar to those of the total ACM population.

## Measures

Gender and marital status were coded to create dichotomous variables. Age was measured in years. Respondents indicated their job title in an open-ended item. Education consisted of six levels from (1) some high school to (6) graduate or professional degree. Salary consisted of six tiers from (1) below \$25,000 to (6) \$65,000 or above. Organizational level consisted of two levels: (1) professionals and (2) supervisors and managers. Tenure in the current job and organization was measured by the number of years an individual had been employed in his/her job and organization, respectively. Participants were also asked to indicate how many years they had been in the MIS field. The demographic items were included in the background information section of the survey.

Career Orientation. Career orientation was assessed with the 41-item Career Orientations Inventory (Schein, 1985). Respondents indicated the importance of each of 21 items relating to their career (e.g., “to build my career around some specific functional or technical area”) on a five-point scale from 1 (of no importance) to 5 (centrally important). Additionally, individuals indicated the extent to which they thought that each of 20 items relating to career preferences (e.g., “During my career I have been mainly concerned with my own sense of freedom and autonomy”) was true of them, using a response scale from 1 (not at all true) to 5 (completely true).

Because the factor structure of the Career Orientations Inventory has not been firmly established, a factor analysis (with varimax rotation) was conducted. The initial factor analysis produced 11 factors with eigenvalues $\geq$ 1.0 that accounted for 68.8 percent of the total variance. All 11 factors were examined against the eight career anchors specified by Schein (1985). The criteria used to identify and interpret factors were that a given item should load .50 or higher on a specific factor and have a loading no higher than .35 on other factors. The homogeneity of the items within each factor was established further by computing their internal consistency reliability coefficient (coefficient alpha) applying the formula recommended by Cronbach (1951). This formula is widely used as a measure of internal consistency and represents a conservative estimate of the reliability of a scale (Armor, 1974).

Examination of the initial factor results revealed that five factors were identical to or corresponded very closely to five of Schein's (1985) career anchors. $^{2}$

\- One factor consisted of the five items developed by Schein to measure autonomy. According to Schein, autonomy-oriented individuals seek work situations in which they will be maximally free of organizational constraints and restrictions to pursue their professional competence. Responses to the five items were averaged to produce an autonomy orientation score. The internal consistency reliability (coefficient alpha) of the scale was .81.

\- A second factor consisted of five times identified by Schein as reflecting managerial competence. Managerially oriented employees, who wish to supervise, influence, and lead others, seek promotions to general manager positions as a vehicle to achieve feelings of success. Responses to the five items were averaged to produce a managerial orientation score. The internal consistency reliability of the scale was .86.

\- A third factor included four of the five items that Schein considered relevant to creativity and entrepreneurship. These individuals need to create something on their own by developing a new produce or service, by building a new business enterprise through financial manipulation, or by starting and building a business of their own. Responses to the four items were averaged to produce an entrepreneurship score. The internal consistency reliability of the scale was .91.

\- A fourth factor included five items identified by Schein as reflecting a dedication to a cause or sense of service. Employees scoring high on this factor are dedicated to serve other people and to make the world a better place in which to live and work. Responses to the five items were averaged to produce a service orientation score. The alpha reliability of the measure was .83.

\- A fifth factor consisted of five items related to lifestyle integration. Individuals with a high score desire to develop a lifestyle that integrates family concerns, career concerns, and concerns for self development. Responses to the five items were averaged to produce a lifestyle orientation score. The internal consistency reliability of the scale as used in this study was .73.

The other three career anchors identified by Schein (1985) were each represented by two closely related factors from our analysis.

\- Two factors were related to security, the first factor (three items) dealing with geographical security (e.g., putting down roots), and the second factor (three items) reflecting the desire for secure ties with the organization. Since both factors were related to an underlying concern for security (confirmed by a second-order factor analysis), the six items from both factors were averaged to create a security orientation score. The internal consistency reliability of the scale was .80.

\- Two factors (one containing two items and the other containing three items) tapped the technical features of the job situation, focusing on a primary interest in the intrinsic, technical content of the work and the functional area represented by the work. Because of the common focus on technical issues represented by these two factors (again confirmed by a second-order factor analysis), the five items were averaged to create a technical orientation score. The five-item scale had an internal consistency reliability of .74.

\- The final two factors (one containing four items and the other containing two items) assessed the preference for overcoming impossible obstacles, solving unsolvable problems, and winning against extremely capable opponents. Because of the two factors' common theme of challenge (reflected by the second-order factor analysis), the six items were averaged to create a pure challenge orientation score. The six-item scale had an internal consistency reliability of .72 in this study.

In summary, eight career orientation raw scores were created for each employee, one corresponding to each of Schein's eight career anchors. However, Schein (1985) has observed that the most faithful representation of an employee's career orientation is the anchor that is most salient to the employee in relative terms. Therefore, each employee was assigned a dominant career orientation on the basis of the orientation that received the highest raw score by that employee. For example, an employee whose highest raw score was technical was classified as technically oriented and was coded 1. It was this code (1 = technical, 2 = managerial, 3 = autonomy, 4 = security, 5 = service, 6 = pure challenge, 7 = lifestyle, 8 = entrepreneurship) that was used to identify the dominant career orientation of each member of our sample.

Satisfaction. Career satisfaction was measured by an eight-item scale adapted from Greenhaus, et al. (1990). Individuals indicated their agreement or disagreement with each statement (e.g., “I am satisfied with the success I have achieved in my career”) on a five-point Likert-type scale ranging from (1) strongly disagree to (5) strongly agree. Since a factor analysis revealed a single-factor solution, the six items with the highest loadings were averaged to create a career satisfaction score. The internal consistency reliability of this scale was .89. Job satisfaction was assessed with a three-item scale (Hackman and Oldham, 1975) reflecting overall satisfaction with the job. The internal consistency reliability of the scale was .77.

Organizational Commitment. This variable—defined as the identification with a particular organization, willingness to exert considerable effort on behalf of the organization, and the desire to maintain membership in the organization—was measured by a 15-item scale (e.g., “I really care about the fate of my organization”) developed by Porter, et al. (1976). The response options to the items ranged from (1) strongly disagree to (5) strongly agree, and responses to the 15 items were averaged to obtain an overall index of organizational commitment. The 15-item scale had an internal consistency reliability of .92.

Intention to Leave. This was assessed by a single item used by Parasuraman (1982) that asked individuals to indicate how long they intended to continue working in their current organization. The response options were anchored on a time-linked five-point scale ranging from (1) “one year or less” to (5) “eleven years or more, or until retirement.” The item was reverse-scored so that a response of “one year or less” would indicate the strongest intention to leave. Kraut (1975), Parasuraman (1982), and others have attested to the efficacy of a single-item measure of turnover intention.

Perceived Job Characteristics. Thirteen items were used to assess the extent to which employees' current job provided them with various opportunities and rewards. The response options were anchored on a five-point scale ranging from (1) not at all to (5) to a great extent. Eight items were adapted from Allen and Katz (1986), and five more items were added to measure different aspects of the job. A factor analysis (with varimax rotation) produced two factors with eigenvalues $\geq$ 1.0 that accounted for 58.3 percent of the total variance. Factor 1, labeled task-based rewards, included the following items: pursue your ideas, build a professional reputation, work with competent colleagues, work on technically challenging tasks, work on professionally important projects, have freedom to be creative and original, and be highly respected by your peers. Responses to the seven items comprising this factor were averaged to create a scale tapping the task-based component of the job. The internal consistency reliability of the scale was .88.

Factor 2, labeled organization-based rewards, included the following items: work on organizationally important projects, work on projects leading to advancement, be highly respected by top management, receive substantial annual salary increases, have a great deal of power and influence on the job, and receive a promotion within the next year or two. Responses to the six items were averaged to create a scale tapping the organizational component of the job. The internal consistency reliability of the scale was .84.

Boundary-Spanning Activities. In order to gain additional insight into employees' job duties, a 10-item scale developed by Miles and Perreault (1976) and used by Baroudi (1985) was modified to assess boundary-spanning activities. This measure taps several aspects of interdepartmental communications, including formal and informal written and oral communication. Each item was scored on a five-point scale ranging from (1) not part of my job to (5) a very significant part of my job. The internal consistency reliability of the scale was .91.

## Data analyses

In order to examine the prevalence of the technical and managerial career orientation (Hypothesis 1 $_{1}$ ), a chi-square goodness of fit test was conducted that compared the observed frequencies of managerial and technical orientations with the expected frequencies of these two orientations. In the interest of gaining knowledge regarding the distribution of other career orientations, another chi-square test was performed to examine the relative frequency of the other six career orientations, although no formal hypotheses were offered concerning the prevalence of these orientations.

In order to confirm the classification of individuals in terms of their dominant career orientation, an analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed using the dominant career orientation as the independent variable and the eight career orientation scale raw scores as the dependent variables. The purpose of this analysis was to examine the relative scores on the eight career orientation scales corresponding to each dominant career orientation.

Additional exploratory analyses were conducted to assess the relationships of selected demographic and background characteristics with individuals' dominant career orientation in order to develop a profile of MIS employees with different career orientations. First, an ANOVA was performed to assess whether individuals with different career orientations differed in age, organizational tenure, job tenure, and years in the MIS field (Ben-Horim and Levy, 1984). In a similar vein, chi-square tests of independence were used to determine whether the nominal demographic variables (gender, marital status, education and organizational level) were systematically associated with career orientations (Daniel, 1990).

Hypotheses $2_{1}$ , which predicted a correspondence of technical and managerial career orientations with the type of position held by MIS employees, was assessed through a chi-square test for independence. The intent of this analysis was to determine whether MIS employees who hold technical positions (such as systems programmers, applications programmers, and software engineers) are technically oriented and whether those who hold managerial positions (such as supervisors and managers, project leaders, and systems analysts) are managerially oriented.

Several steps were taken in order to examine the impact of a match between career orientation and job setting on job satisfaction, career satisfaction, organizational commitment, and intention to leave (Hypothesis 3 $_{1}$ ). First, we identified those employees whose dominant career orientation was either managerial or technical (N = 226) and determined whether they were in managerial or technical positions. Technically oriented employees who held technical positions (N = 68) and managerially oriented employees who held managerial positions (N = 82) were considered to represent a “match” between career orientation and job setting. Conversely, technically oriented employees in managerial positions (N = 37) and managerially oriented employees in technical positions (N = 39) were considered to represent a “mismatch” between career orientation and job setting.

Next, ANOVAs and chi-square tests were conducted to examine demographic differences between the match and mismatch groups. These analyses revealed no significant differences in age, education, marital status, organizational tenure, job tenure, and MIS tenure. However, the match and mismatch groups differed with regard to gender (members of the match group were more likely to be male than members of the mismatch group; $X^{2}=8.86$ , p ≤ .001) and organizational level (members of the match group held higher-level positions than members of the mismatch group; $X^{2}=8.19$ , p ≥ .001). Therefore, it was necessary to control for gender and organizational level in the examination of Hypothesis 3, so that our conclusions would not be confounded by gender and level differences between the match and mismatch groups. Thus, a multivariate analysis of covariance (MANCOVA) was conducted with match versus mismatch as the independent variable, gender and organizational level as the covariates, and the four career outcomes (job satisfaction, career satisfaction, organizational commitment, and intention to stay) as the dependent variables.

We also explored the assumption that employees whose career orientation matches their job setting are satisfied and committed because their job provides desirable rewards. Several ANOVAs were conducted to examine this notion. In each analysis, the independent variable was job type (managerial versus technical), and the dependent variables were task-based rewards (e.g., opportunity to pursue ideas, work on technically challenging tasks) and organization-based rewards (e.g., power and influence, receive a promotion).

## Results

## Frequency of career orientations

Hypotheses 1, predicted that MIS employees would be more likely to hold technical or managerial career orientations than other career orientations. The relative frequency of the eight dominant career orientations is presented in Figure 1. Consistent with our hypothesis, the two most widely held orientations were managerial (26.1 percent of the sample) and technical (22.6 percent of the sample). Collectively, the managerial and technical orientations were held by nearly 50 percent of the employees, a percentage that exceeded what would have been expected by chance ( $X^{2}=12,100$ , p<.001). Other career orientations represented in the sample include autonomy (14.7 percent), lifestyle (10.5 percent) and service (8.2 percent). The entrepreneurship orientation was least characteristic (4.7 percent of the members of the sample). Thus, the data confirmed our first hypothesis that the most prevalent career orientations of MIS employees are technical and managerial, although other career orientations were also represented.

The data presented in Table 2 provide interesting insights into the career orientation scale raw scores of individuals with different dominant career orientations. Table 2 shows that technically oriented employees scored very low on managerial orientation but relatively high on autonomy and service. On the other hand, managerially oriented employees scored very low on the technical and autonomy orientations and relatively high on pure challenge and entrepreneurship. Many of the other findings reported in Table 2 (e.g., the high orientation toward entrepreneurship among the autonomy oriented, the importance of life-style issues to the security oriented, the twin concern for autonomy and security among the life-style oriented, and the importance of autonomy to the challenge oriented) were consistent with our intuitive expectations.

The data show that career orientation was unrelated to age, education, marital status, and tenure in the job, the organization, and the MIS field. ## Relationship between career orientation and job setting

Hypothesis $2_{1}$ predicted that an employee's career orientation would be associated with the type of job he or she held within the MIS function. As shown in Table 4, there was a significant $(p < .05)$ association between career orientation and job type. Systems programmers were primarily technically or autonomy-oriented. Applications programmers and software engineers were similar to systems programmers in the predominance of a technical orientation but were less likely to be managerially or autonomy-oriented than the systems programmers. On the other hand, nearly 50 percent of the computer managers were managerially oriented, as were sizeable percentages of the systems analysts and the project leaders. Finally, consultants included a mix of technically and managerially oriented employees.

Autonomy-oriented employees, who place little importance on security, service, or managerial issues, were likely to be found in systems programming and project leader positions.

Additionally, lifestyle integration was moderately represented in the sample. These employees, who tended to be relatively unconcerned with challenge, service, and entrepreneurship, were more likely to be in software engineering and applications programming than other types of jobs. The finding that more than 20 percent of the women were lifestyle oriented is not surprising and reflects the primary responsibility for domestic and childcare activities that employed women continue to shoulder (Nieva and Gutek, 1981). As values related to lifestyles change among the wider population and more women enter the field of MIS, and as more men assume additional responsibilities for home and childcare, the lifestyle career orientation may be even more prevalent among MIS employees in the future.

There was a clear tendency for employees with different career orientations to gravitate toward different positions in the MIS function. The data suggest three clusters of positions within MIS: technically oriented systems programmers, applications programmers, and software engineers; managerially oriented computer managers, systems analysts, and project leaders; and technically or managerially oriented consultants. These findings are consistent with the view that career orientations influence employees' career decisions to pursue specific jobs and career paths (Schein, 1975).

The findings also indicate the importance of a match between career orientation and job setting. Managerially oriented employees in technical jobs and technically oriented employees in managerial jobs displayed a number of negative work attitudes, including low satisfaction and lack of commitment to the organization. This study showed that managerially oriented employees react positively to managerial jobs because they see opportunities for advancement, money, top management respect, and power in these jobs. Similarly, because technically oriented employees see opportunities for reputation enhancement, competent colleagues, peer respect, and challenging tasks in technical jobs, they are more satisfied and committed in these positions. These findings are consistent with the predictions of person-environment fit theory (Caplan, 1983; Caplan, et al., 1980; Davis and Lofquist, 1984; Harrison, 1985) and also support Ginzberg and Baroudi's (1988) proposition that compatibility between internal career needs and external career options can produce positive outcomes among MIS employees.

## Implications for Practice

Perhaps the most significant implication of this research is the diversity of career orientations held by MIS employees. The assumption that all (or almost all) MIS professionals are driven by the same set of values and goals is unfounded. Organizations need to recognize this diversity in their employee population so that appropriate reward systems and career paths can be developed. Employees should discuss their needs, values, and career orientations with their supervisors on an ongoing basis so that realistic feedback is provided, challenging assignments are given, and meaningful career goals are established. This places supervisors in the role of “career developer” of their subordinates (Greenhaus, 1987; Hall, 1976), a role for which they should be trained and rewarded.

Given this diversity of career orientations, organizations must realize that rewards and career opportunities suitable for one group of employees may be irrelevant or even inappropriate for another group. Therefore, organizational reward systems and career path opportunities must be flexible enough to accommodate a diverse work force within the MIS function. Since rewards must be valued by the employee in order for them to have incentive value (Porter and Lawler, 1968; Vroom, 1964), and since employees holding different career orientations value different types of activities and accomplishments (Schein, 1987), as demonstrated in our findings, somewhat different rewards may be necessary to motivate different types of employees. Rewarding a lifestyle-oriented employee with a geographical transfer, a technically oriented employee with a new managerial assignment, or an autonomy-oriented employee with a lucrative but constraining promotion may well detract from motivation and performance. At the very least, organizations need to understand and consider their employees' career orientations in the development of their reward systems.

The results of this study are consistent with the concept of a dual-career path system, which recognizes the diversity of career orientations and provides incentives that are consistent with employees' underlying career values. The activities and opportunities available in technical positions (technically challenging tasks, respect from peers, pursuit of ideas) are quite different from the activities and opportunities available in managerial positions (high visibility to top management, power and influence). Since turnover of MIS personnel has been attributed to both market demand factors and organizational factors (Bartol and Martin, 1982), an organization might be able to reduce a high turnover rate by providing career opportunities that match the internal career needs of the employees.

This study also has implications for university faculty preparing students to enter the MIS profession. Since career orientations do not seem to crystallize until individuals accumulate different kinds of work experience (Schein, 1985), having students (especially undergraduates) place extensive importance on their dominant career orientation may be inappropriate. However, faculty can introduce the concept of career orientation to students and can encourage them to explore their values and aspirations and to recognize the importance of a match between career orientation and job setting. Discussions of career opportunities in MIS can be organized around the notion of a match or compatibility with diverse career orientations.

## Implications for Research

This study has demonstrated the potential for negative work attitudes among managerially oriented employees in technical positions and technically oriented employees in managerial positions. The consequences of this mismatch were most easily tested among managerially and technically oriented employees because jobs could be identified (managerial and technical) that bore a close correspondence to the two respective career orientations. However, nearly 50 percent of the sample held autonomy, entrepreneurship, security, life-style, service, or challenge career anchors. It is likely that the traditional concept of a dual-career path, which focuses exclusively on technical and managerial paths, needs to be supplemented with a recognition of the possible variations that can occur within each path. For example, two systems programmers, one technically oriented and the other lifestyle-oriented, may have to be treated somewhat differently. In a similar vein, a managerially oriented project leader and an entrepreneurially oriented project leader may require different kinds of support, rewards, and career opportunities. Empirical research needs to examine the kinds of job experiences that are most compatible with a wider array of career orientations than the most prevalent managerial and technical orientations.

Research on the consequences of compatibility between career orientation and job setting should be extended in several other directions as well. First, the effects of incompatibility on job performance need to be examined because it is possible that incompatibility produces deteriorated job performance over time. Moreover, the stability of MIS employees' career orientations should be empirically examined. For example, the career stage model of Dalton, et al. (1977) may be a useful framework for viewing the career development of MIS employees (Ginzberg and Baroudi, 1988), and it would be interesting to determine whether different career orientations are associated with different stages of Dalton, et al.'s (1977) model.

Although the employees surveyed in this study represented a wide variety of organizations and functional areas, they were all members of one association and do not necessarily represent the entire population of MIS employees. Therefore, the generalizability of the results to employees in other companies and other geographical areas must be established through further empirical research. Finally, although bivariate analyses identified a number of relationships between career orientations and other variables, the observed relationships might be more complex than these findings indicate. Therefore, future research should investigate a network of multivariate relationships among a variety of individual characteristics, job performance, and career outcomes.

## Summary and Conclusions

The results of this study provided interesting insights into the career orientations of MIS employees. A central finding was that technical and managerial orientations are the most prevalent career orientations among MIS employees, with systems programmers, applications programmers, and software engineers likely to be technically oriented, and systems analysts, project leaders, and computer managers likely to be managerially oriented. The results also demonstrated that employees whose career orientations were compatible with their job settings reported higher job satisfaction and career satisfaction, stronger commitment to their organization, and lower intentions to leave their organization than employees whose career orientations were incompatible with their job settings.

Although these findings have important implications for the selection, placement, career development, and retention of MIS personnel, additional research is needed on a number of fronts. First, it is necessary to determine whether the career orientations of MIS personnel are unique and differ from those of personnel in other functional areas. Second, the stability of individuals' career orientations over time needs to be assessed, as well as possible variations in the pattern of relationships of career orientations with affective and behavioral career outcomes at different career stages. The accumulation of results from research in a wider spectrum of organizations and occupations should advance our knowledge of career issues as they relate to MIS employees. Researchers should continue working to refine the models of career development that incorporate variables uniquely relevant to the MIS field.

## About the Authors

Magid Igbaria is associate professor of management information systems at Drexel University. Formerly, he lectured at Tel Aviv University, Hebrew University, and Ben-Gurion University in Israel and acted as the administrative director of the Center of Management Information Systems (CEMIS) at Tel Aviv University. He received an M.A. in information systems and operations research from Hebrew University and a Ph.D. in MIS from Tel Aviv University. He has published articles on management of MIS functions, economics of computers, computer performance evaluation, charging of computer services, computational approaches in MIS, and microcomputers in business in several leading journals including Applied Statistics, Communications of the ACM, Information and Management, Information Systems and Operational Research (INFOR), Omega, and others. His current research interests focus upon economics of computers, management of information systems, career development of MIS professionals, and end-user computing.

Jeffrey H. Greenhaus is professor of management and director of doctoral programs in business at Drexel University. He received a Ph.D. in industrial-organizational psychology from New York University. He has published his research on human resource/career management in such journals as Academy of Management Journal, Academy of Management Review, Journal of Applied Psychology Journal of Management, Journal of Vocational Behavior, and Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes. He is the author of Career Management, published by The Dryden Press in 1987. His current research interests include career decision making, the career development of MIS professionals, the career experiences of women and minorities, and work-family dynamics.

Saroj Parasuraman is professor of management at Drexel University. She received her Ph.D. in organizational behavior from the State University of New York at Buffalo. Her scholarly work has been published in such academic journals as the Academy of Management Journal, Journal of Vocational Behavior, Journal of Management, Group and Organization Studies, and International Journal of Man-Machine Studies. She is a member of the editorial board of the Journal of Vocational Behavior. Her research interests include stress and coping, work-family linkages, work attitudes and turnover, the career development of women and minorities, and human computer interfaces.
