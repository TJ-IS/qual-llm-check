## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords. Crowdfunding Regular selling Price Advertising E-commerce

## A B S T R A C T

This paper studies a reward-based crowdfunding game wherein the creator first collects money through an Ecrowdfunding platform and then, if successful, invests the raised money to fund promotional activities via online retailing. We derive the creator's equilibrium decisions and the corresponding payof and show that they are significantly influenced by the potential demand and the gap between diferent types of buyer valuations. In particular, with the change in potential market size from low to high, the creator will choose investing none, part or all of the crowdfunded money to improve the advertising level. Meanwhile, when the market size is small, the creator tends to set a high price; when it becomes larger, the creator prefers menu prices or a low price to guarantee the success of the crowdfunding campaign. Comparing two scenarios, one with and one without considering advertising investment, creators may never choose the low price if they cannot raise enough money for advertising: however, that situation can never arise without considering the option of advertising investment These results provide implications for launching a successful crowdfunding campaign and product promotion/ advertising in this E-commerce era

## 1. Introduction

Reward-based crowdfunding refers to a business innovation mode that a firm uses to raise money for its new products via an online platform to support its production and advertising activities. With the rapid development of Internet technology and the prosperity of Ecommerce, reward-based crowdfunding has already become a new star in online retailing, attracting great attention for its importance in raising suficient financial resources in various forms, such as donations, or in exchange for other rewards [2]. For example, from its launch in 2009 until January 2018, “Kickstarter”, the biggest Ecrowdfunding platform in the world, successfully collected \$3.5 billion and funded 139,169 projects.<sup>1</sup> Reward-based crowdfunding has become increasingly popular [7,27] and been applied by many crowdfunding platforms, e.g., Indiegogo and Kickstarter. We use an online movie project to illustrate the typical process of a reward-based crowdfunding project and its subsequent selling activities.<sup>2</sup>

A movie design company first launches a movie trailer (or an idea) on an E-crowdfunding platform (e.g., http://Kickstarter.com) with a presale price, funding target and deadline. We call this movie design company the creator in such crowdfunding processes. Potential consumers surfing the crowdfunding platform can review this movie project and decide whether to make a pledge once they are interested in the project. We call these consumers the buyers. When the deadline approaches, if the funded amount reaches or exceeds the target, the creator receives all the raised money to support its filming, and all the buyers who have pledged funds will obtain digital copies of the movie afterwards. Otherwise, if the funded amount is below the target, the project fails and all the money will be refunded to the pledged buyers via online channels. More importantly, if the crowdfunding campaign succeeds, the creator can consider investing the crowdfunded money in advertising to promote the new movie, of which digital copies or DVDs will be sold via another E-business platform. For example, Amazon has had a Launchpad subsection since July 2016 to showcase products that were originally crowdfunded on the Kickstarter platform.<sup>3</sup>

Based on the above discussion, one can see that a standard online crowdfunding project normally consists of two periods: the crowdfunding stage and the regular (online) selling stage. In the crowdfunding stage, the creator has to determine a proper crowdfunding price to achieve a balance between raising enough money and promising a relatively higher success rate in the crowdfunding stage. After that, if the campaign succeeds, the creator will decide whether to invest some of the crowdfunded money in advertising in the regular selling stage and how much of it to invest. It is quite common to see that in practice, firms invest their crowdfunded money in the production and promotion process to further promote their products. One typical example is in the film market. Big Fish & Begonia<sup>4</sup> has been one of the most successful crowdfunding film projects in China, spending nearly \$6 million for production and \$4 million for dispersion and advertising. It finally took in nearly \$80 million at the box ofice.<sup>5</sup> Building upon these characteristics, this paper studies how crowdfunding facilitates funding raising and new product promotion in these online retailing activities. In particular, we are interested in the following questions:

• What kind of crowdfunding pricing policy should the creator adopt with an overall consideration of both crowdfunding and regular selling, and how much crowdfunded money should the creator in vest in advertising?

• Under alternative pricing policies, how do the equilibrium payof vary under various circumstances, especially for diferent potential demands and buyers' product valuations and fractions?

How will the creator's best choices for crowdfunding pricing policies be diferent between two scenarios, i.e., either considering or not considering advertising investment in the regular selling stage?

To answer the above questions, we build a stylized two-stage deci sion model for the creator to determine the pricing policy in the crowdfunding stage and the advertising level in the regular selling stage in that order. In particular, buyers on the E-crowdfunding platform make prepurchase decisions according to price, private valuations and the anticipated success rate. Notably, if a buyer pays a higher price, that buyer makes a greater contribution to the success rate. In this sense, buyers with diferent product valuations could pay diferent prices even for the same identical product. However, the buyers arriving at the regular selling stage only care about the price and their private valuations of the product. Therefore, the creator pays more attention to the total number of buyers who come in the regular selling stage, which can be potentially influenced by the advertising in the E-platform. We adopt the backward induction method to derive the crowdfunding pricing policies and the advertising levels in equilibrium as well as the creator's corresponding payofs.

Our conclusions shed light on the creator's best decisions about the crowdfunding pricing policies when facing diferent buyers and market circumstances. Intuitively, if foreseeing a small-end market in the reg ular selling stage, the creator will prefer a high crowdfunding price (H policy) in order to raise more targeted money, even enduring the risk of the failure of the crowdfunding campaign and thus the subsequent selling activities. On the contrary, if foreseeing a large end market, the creator will face a trade-of between setting a lower price (L policy) to guarantee a higher success rate for the crowdfunding campaign and setting a higher price (M policy) to raise more targeted money for the advertising investment in the regular selling stage. According to our result, the diference in product valuations between two types of buyers plays a pivotal role in the creator's choice in this dilemma. If the gap between the buyers' valuations is high, which means that the two types of valuations difer significantly, the creator will provide menu crowdfunding prices to screen diferent types of buyers with a higher target. Otherwise, if the gap between the buyers' valuations is low, the creator will first prefer the M policy and then the L policy as the market increases, indicating that the success rate becomes an increasingly important concern.

We also show a series of interesting trends for equilibrium adver tising levels and the payofs varying with the potential market size, regardless of which pricing policy the creator adopts. Specifically, when the potential market size is very large, the creator is willing to invest the full amount of the crowdfunded money in advertising to improve the end market size; otherwise, if the market potential is very small, the creator will be reluctant to invest more in advertising. When the po tential market size is medium large, the creator will only invest part of the crowdfunded money in advertising, and the investment level increases with the potential market size. Moreover, when the market size is medium large and keeps increasing, the creator's payof will exhibit a dramatic increase with the market potential, whose level is double that of the advertising level.

Our model also examines the impact of advertising investment on the creator's pricing choice in crowdfunding. To this end, we compare the creator's equilibrium strategies and payofs under two representative scenarios: with and without future investment in advertising. We show that under either scenario, the creator tends to set a high price only when the market potential is small but prefers lower prices (e.g., M or L policies) when the market potential becomes larger in order to guarantee the success of the crowdfunding campaign. Nevertheless, if giving consideration to advertising investment, the creator may never choose a low price (L policy) if the gap between the two types of buyers' valuations is large enough. This result can never arise if the creator cannot invest in advertising in the regular selling stage.

The remainder of this article is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the relevant literature. In Section 3, we lay out the model setup. The analysis of the creator's equilibrium decisions on prices and advertising investment are presented in Section 4. Section 5 investigates the creator's best choices for the crowdfunding prices under two different scenarios. Section 6 presents extensions from various perspectives, and Section 7 concludes the article. Proofs are presented in Appendix A.

## 2. Related literature

This research is closely related to two streams of literature: rewardbased crowdfunding and advertising investment. In general, crowdfunding can be classified into four categories, depending on the return type ofered for the funders' support [7, 12] : donation-based, rewardbased, lending and equity. One of the most prevalent types is rewardbased crowdfunding, which incurs an exchange of a monetary contribution from supporters for some kinds of non-equity rewards from creators or initiators [23,25].

The empirical literature investigating funders' behaviors in response to project attributes or product development provides interesting insights for further analytical studies. In particular, Kuppuswamy and Bayus [15] propose that people support crowdfunding projects financially when they believe that their contribution will make an impact on a particular target goal. Ryu and Kim [18] analyzes six motivations for sponsors participating in crowdfunding — interest, playfulness, philanthropy, reward, relationship, and recognition. This means that although buyers seek the reward of products, they may also be influenced by other motivations, such as interest, friendship or even kinship. Focusing on reward-based crowdfunding, Gerber et al. [8] and Davis et al. [5] reveal the deeper insight that sponsors will be happy to see that the new product they sponsored sells well in the future as long as they can first get their product reward as promised. Even in equity crowdfunding, supporters' investment decisions are associated with observable features such as funding targets and social networks, similar to reward-based crowdfunding, but not with typical criteria used by angel or venture capital investors [17]. In our research, the supporters concern for their own contributions to crowdfunding products and campaign features such as target setting will be incorporated, while other features, such as product delivery [24], are out of our scope. On the other hand, the pricing problem under crowdfunding [ 6, 26 ] or crowdsourcing [ 11, 19 ] has been explored empirically. However, scant research has analytically investigated the pricing of reward-based crowdfunding, especially combined with subsequent selling activities. Belleflamme et al. [2] discuss the pricing decisions in two stages, i.e., crowdfunding and regular selling, which is close to our setting. Nevertheless, they mainly compare two forms of crowdfunding, preordering and profit sharing, without considering the relationship be tween success rate and buyers' valuations, which is a major characteristic of crowdfunding [15]. Hu et al. [14] study a two-period problem of optimal product and pricing decisions in a crowdfunding mechanism. Chen et al. [4] investigate the joint decisions of production and pricing with strategic consumers for green crowdfunding products; Xu et al. [28] discuss the problem of financing target and resale pricing in reward-based crowdfunding. To the best of our knowledge, there are a lack of theoretical studies with regard to incorporating crowdfunding pricing and subsequent promotion strategies together.

After receiving the crowdfunded money for production, the creator should consider the promotion investment of new products in the end market. According to Soberman [20], advertising can be viewed as an instrument to probably increase either the intensity of demand or the amount consumers are willing to pay for a product. Bagwell [1] review some of the most influential articles on the economics of advertising, which provide explorations of persuasive advertising and informative advertising. Advertising is considered a marketing strategy to promote demand, either for a short-term or long-term business [ 9, 13, 20 ]. For example, Sogomonian and Tang [21] present a modeling framework for evaluating the benefits of coordinating promotion and production decisions over a finite planning horizon within a firm. Grossman and Shapiro [9] study the role of promotional expenditures by sellers in a model of product diferentiation. Moreover, other papers address the importance of joint decisions on pricing and advertising. Thompson and Teng [22] discuss three diferent pricing rules based on a monopoly case with linear advertising cost and exponential demand: optimal variable pricing, instantaneous marginal pricing, and optimal constant pricing rules. Lal and Matutes [16] study pricing and advertising stra tegies of competing retailers and find that firms advertise prices below marginal cost to attract consumers into the store and profit from other goods that consumers plan to buy at the store. These prior studies especially enlighten us about demand formulation and description with a joint efect of pricing and advertising.

This study is highly motivated by Hu et al. [14], while the significant diferences and contributions for our research should be high lighted as follows. First, we extend Hu et al. [14] by building a twostage game in which we not only consider the crowdfunding stage but also investigate the regular selling stage that is absent in Hu et al. [14]. This two-stage game fundamentally changes the dynamic of crowdfunding so that the creator needs to maximize its two-period profit rather than only focus on the success of crowdfunding stage. Second, we consider both production cost and advertising investment for regular selling. Given this premise, the crowdfunded money must first cover the production cost and then be considered for advertising investment to improve regular sales. As shown later, these two factors exert significant impacts on the creator's pricing decision in the crowdfunding stage. Third, the two-stage game also allows us to consider more pricing strategies, such as price commitment, in which the creator has to commit its pricing strategy over two periods. With this price scheme, the creator needs to further balance the crowdfunding target and its success rate, and this was not discussed by Hu et al. [14].

## 3. Model

Consider a risk-neutral creator who raises money via a crowdfunding campaign to support the subsequent promotion activities of the new product. We build a two-stage model consisting of the crowdfunding stage (t = 1) and the regular selling stage (t = 2), wherein each buyer makes a purchase decision according to his or her private valuation and the price(s) of the product. The buyers have diferent tastes or preferences regarding the product, which are captured by their valuations. In line with the vast literature, we assume that there are two types of buyers, those with high valuation and those with low valuation, whose value is consistent over two stages and follows the two point distribution:

$$
V _ {t} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} h & \text {with probability} \quad \alpha , \\ l & \text {with probability} \quad 1 - \alpha , \end{array} \right.
$$

where $h > l .$

The creator first announces price(s) p with corresponding target I, for a product in the crowdfunding stage, after which the creator considers investing in advertising (e) to promote sales in the regular selling stage. The campaign will succeed only if the total money raised from the crowdfunding campaign reaches the target (I). Otherwise, if the target is not reached by the deadline, the campaign will fail and all the raised money will be refunded to the pledged buyers. Note that regular selling occurs only if the crowdfunding campaign succeeds. In this sense, buyers with a high valuation may be willing to prepay more for an identical product to ensure the success of the crowdfunding campaign, but in the regular selling stage, all of the buyers will prefer the lower price. During a crowdfunding campaign, nobody knows which kind of buyers will appear and pay, which makes this the source of the most uncertainty for the outputs of crowdfunding campaigns.

If a crowdfunding campaign has succeeded, the creator determines the level of advertising (e) to invest in by incurring the corresponding cost, which is captured by a simplified quadratic function, $\cdot \frac { e ^ { 2 } } { 2 }$ . This form has been widely applied in the related literature $[ 9 , 2 0 ] .$ Because in practice [3] , crowdfunded money must first cover the production cost, we assume that the marginal production cost is c. For example, if there are N buyers who have prepaid at the crowdfunding stage, then at most, $I - N c$ can be invested in advertising once the crowdfunding campaign succeeds. We assume that the natural advertising level for the end market is $e _ { 0 } = 1$ , and thus, the promoted advertising level is $e \geq 1$ . To guarantee that the partly invested advertising level is higher than the non-invested natural level, we assume $\begin{array} { r } { h - c > 1 \ge l - c \ge \frac { 1 } { 3 } } \end{array}$ . In line with Gurnani and Xu [10], we let se denote the actual informative demand in the regular selling stage, wherein s represents the potential demand spurred by advertisement. Therefore, the final demand in the regular selling stage is determined by the end market size multiplied by the fraction of buyers who decide to pay with $p .$

$$

$$
s. t. \quad \frac {e ^ {2}}{2} \leq I (p) - N c,\tag{2}
$$

Note that $D ( e , \lambda ( \widetilde { p } ) ) = e s \lambda ( \widetilde { p } )$ , where λ represents the proportion of potential buyers will finally prepay for the product, and thus, $\lambda = \alpha \mathrm { i f } \tilde { p } = h$ and $\lambda = 1 \mathrm { i f } \widetilde { p } = l$ . In addition, considering that each crowdfunding policy is accompanied by a diferent monetary target, the monetary constraint for advertising investment varies among alternative policies. Moreover, the main uncertainty faced by the creator is whether crowdfunding campaigns with particular prices and targets will succeed, because one cannot be sure which kind of buyers will appear in the two substages during the crowdfunding campaign; to address this, we will discuss and introduce below a corresponding parameter, success rate $r ( p )$

## 3.1. Preliminary analysis

Consider a crowdfunding campaign in which there are two sequential substages, and only one representative buyer appears in each substage. In practice, three types of pricing policies are widely applied in crowdfunding campaigns, $\mathrm { i . e . , }$ denoted by H policy, L policy and M policy. Under H policy, the creator presells the product to high-type buyers by only pricing at $\begin{array} { r } { p \ = \ h , } \end{array}$ with the crowdfunding target as $I ^ { H } = 2 h ;$ under L policy, the creator prices at $p = l ,$ with the target set at $I ^ { L } = 2 l$ to attract both types of buyers to prepay for the product; under M policy, the creator posts a price menu such as $( p ^ { l } , p ^ { h } )$ , where $p ^ { l } \leq l \leq$ $p ^ { h } \leq h ,$ with the target set at $\overset { \cdot } { I } ^ { M } = p ^ { h } + p ^ { l } .$ To better understand various pricing policies, we crawled the data of 18,411 projects launched on Kickstarter from August 1st, 2016 to December 31st, 2016. The results show that 17,145 projects were launched with menu prices, while 1266 projects were launched with a single price.

Each buyer observes price p and voluntarily determines whether to prepay or to leave. The campaign will succeed only if both buyers in the two substages prepay for the product, enabling the raised money to reach the target (I). Preliminarily, we adopt the analysis method of Hu et al. [14] on crowdfunding success rates under diferent pricing mechanisms. Obviously, it is easy to determine that the success rates under the H policy and L policy are $r ^ { H } = \alpha ^ { 2 }$ and $r ^ { L } = 1 ,$ , respectively. How ever, under the M policy with a price menu such as $( \boldsymbol { p } ^ { l } , \bar { \boldsymbol { p } } ^ { h } )$ , where $p ^ { l } \le l$ $\leq p ^ { h } \leq h ,$ and a target set at $I ^ { M } = p ^ { h } + p ^ { l } ,$ , the scenario difers. Suppose buyer A arrives in the first substage, and buyer B arrives in the second substage. By the logic of backward induction, buyer B will always prepay at $p ^ { l }$ if buyer A has prepaid at $p ^ { h } ;$ otherwise, if A has chosen $p ^ { l } ,$ B will prepay at $p ^ { h }$ only if B’s product valuation is h, the probability of which equals α. Next, in the first substage, buyer A will certainly prepay $\mathsf { a t } p ^ { l }$ if he or she has low valuation l. However, if A is an h-type buyer, to motivate A to prepay at $p ^ { h }$ rather than $p ^ { l } ,$ the pricing menu should satisfy $\alpha ( h - p ^ { l } ) \leq h - p ^ { h }$ (IC condition). Based on the above analysis, the creator's optimal decisions and success rate under the M policy are $( p ^ { l } , p ^ { h } ) ~ = ~ ( l , h ~ - ~ \alpha h ~ + ~ \alpha l ) , ~ I ^ { M } ~ = ~ ( 1 ~ - ~ \alpha ) h ~ + ~ ( 1 ~ + ~ \alpha ) l ,$ , and $r ^ { M } = \alpha ( 2 \ : - \ : \alpha )$ , respectively.

In the following analysis, we study a two-stage game by considering both the crowdfunding stage and the regular selling stage. Under such a circumstance, the creator needs to maximize its two-period profit rather than focusing only on the success of the crowdfunding. We will first study the advertising investment under alternative crowdfunding pricing policies, i.e., H, L and M policies, as well as the corresponding exante payofs. After that, we compare the performances under diferent policies and give advice for the creator to make better choices among alternative pricing policies in the crowdfunding stage.

It is also worth mentioning that in the main text, we assume that the creator can set diferent prices between the two stages without any commitment in the crowdfunding stage. Later, we will also discuss the extended situation in which the creator has to make a price commitment between the two stages.

## 4. Equilibrium advertising investments and payofs

First, we analyze the equilibrium advertising levels at which the creator invests to reach alternative pricing policies. After that, we identify the combinations of pricing policies in the two stages and then present the expected ex-ante payofs in equilibrium accordingly.

First, when the potential demand (s) is very small, the creator will not invest the crowdfunded money to improve the advertising level (e) because that improvement will not benefit the sales much, and thus, $e ^ { * }$ remains constant as the natural level. Second, when s is large enough, the creator will be willing to invest as much crowdfunded money as possible in advertising, but the investment is constrained by the crowdfunding target, which was determined in the prior stage, so $e ^ { * }$ will not be influenced by s either. ## 4.2. Pricing policies

As mentioned above, we consider three alternative pricing policies $( \mathrm { i . e . , } L ,$ M and H policies) that the creator can choose for each stage. Although the menu prices (denoted by the M policy) work well for the crowdfunding campaign, they will become invalid in the regular selling stage because buyers will always choose the lower price. That is different from the crowdfunding stage, wherein buyers with high valuations might choose the higher price to increase the success rate of the crowdfunding product. Moreover, if the distributions of the buyers' product valuations are consistent between two stages, the creator's preference for either H price or L price in diferent stages will be analogous and straightforward. Combining the pricing policies in the two stages together, we carry out Lemma 1.

Lemma 1. Given the consistent distribution of buyers' product valuations in the two stages to maximize the profit,

## 6.3. Advertising: more consumers vs. higher valuations

According to Soberman [20], advertising can be an instrument to potentially increase either the intensity of demand at all price levels or the consumers' willingness to pay for a product. For example, consider a photographic equipment maker who aims to promote a new type of camera via advertising. One typical way to do so is to promote online shares on various social platforms (e.g., Facebook or Instagram), the main purpose of which is to increase the number of people who know about the new camera product. Alternatively, the maker can also disclose more technical information to improve consumers' valuations of the product. In short, a sales increase through advertising could be attributed by more informed consumers (informative advertising) or improved product valuations (persuasive advertising). Both informative and persuasive mechanisms of advertising have been widely adopted, and one can refer to Boyer [3] or Soberman [20] for more discussion on these mechanisms. In real life, the two mechanisms of advertising may interact with each other, though our analytical model formulates them separately for a clearer discussion and comparison.

## 7. Conclusion

Reward-based crowdfunding platforms have helped a substantia number of new product designers raise suficient money from consumers to support the production and selling activities of these new products. The typical process of crowdfunding begins with a creator launching a presale crowdfunding campaign to the buyers and then promotes the new product once the campaign succeeds. This paper studies the creator's optimal decisions regarding crowdfunding prices and advertising investments, which are supported by the crowdfunded money. We show that if the potential demand for the end market is large, the creator will prefer a conservative pricing policy (setting a low price) in the crowdfunding stage to guarantee a high success rate. Based on that, the creator should invest the crowdfunded money to improve the advertising level, which could probably bring a larger end market. Otherwise, if the market potential is small, the creator should set a higher crowdfunding price to gain a larger target while investing less or no money in advertising later. When the potential market size is medium large, the creator should only invest part of the crowdfunded money in advertising, and the investment level increases with the po tential market size. Moreover, when the market size is medium large and continues to increase, the creator's payof will exhibit a dramatic increase with the market potential, the level of which is double that of the advertising level. All of these results provide useful guidance for the firm to launch a successful crowdfunding campaign and efectively promote products in the E-commerce era.
