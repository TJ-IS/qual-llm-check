# Talking Up a Storm: How Backers Use Public Discourse to Exert Control in Crowdfunded Systems Development Projects

Abstract. Crowdfunding has emerged as a popular alternative to traditional funding, wherein project owners use web technologies to fundraise from members of the public (“backers”). The established models of control do not work for this new way of performing systems development, because of the number and diversity of controllers, the restriction of interaction to public online discourse, and the absence of defined hierarchical power structures. This study performs a critical discourse analysis of public online discourse from two famous crowdfunded systems development projects. It develops a framework for the study of crowdfunding control that incorporates the prominent role that power, informal control, and discourse play. The study shows that backers are empowered by their abilit to construct a positive or negative public image around a project, and they use this power to impose a diverse portfolio of formal and informal controls. Findings reveal a funda mental vulnerability in crowdfunding, as public perceptions of failure push backers to become increasingly assertive and owners to become decreasingly compliant

## Introduction

Systems development typically involves a wide range of contributors. Some act as “project owners” (Hartman and Ashrafi 2002, Hippel and Krogh 2003), assuming active responsibility for developing the system—for example, designers, software engineers, and project managers. Others invest infrastructure or financial resources to enable development (Kirsch et al. 2002). These investors apply controls to regulate or adjust the behaviors of project owners, to motivate participants, and to ensure capabilities are fully applied to advance an initiative toward its objectives (Ouchi 1977). The nature of such controls on systems development has been studied extensively over the years. This has produced numerous models to examine why and how specific controls are effective (e.g., Kirsch 1996); how individual controls are combined (e.g., Kirsch 1997), interact (e.g., Kirsch 1997, Maruping et al. 2009), and are “balanced” (e.g., Gregory et al. 2013) in a control portfolio; and how control can and should change over time (e.g., Harris et al. 2009).

New web technologies have challenged the relationship between project owners and investors with new crowdfunding models (see Belleflamme et al. 2013 and Mollick 2014). These models replace traditional funding structures with open calls for financial contribution over the web (Howe 2006). This has three increasingly important theoretical implications for control in systems development.

First is the reliance on public online discourse for control. Backers in a crowdfunded systems development project possess similar incentives to impose controls as traditional investor-controllers. After all, similar to traditional investors, backers’ desire to support a project typically stems from a combination of their interest in the project’s goals (Gerber and Hui 2013, Belleflamme et al. 2014), an interest in those performing it (Bretschneider et al. 2014, Zheng et al. 2014), and a belief that goals can be achieved to a satisfactory standard (Wash and Solomon 2014, Koch and Siering 2015). Thus, the performance and direction of a project is clearly important to its backers. Yet backers have limited access to project owners outside of public online discourse. Discourse has a well-established role in control (Dennis and Martin 2005, Schmidt 2008). However, discourse is typically discussed as one component in a larger set of control mechanisms (e.g., Ouchi 1977, 1979; Eisenhardt 1985; Das 1993; Kirsch 1997; Kirsch et al. 2010), many of which may not be relevant for one-off crowdfunding projects. Once the donation is made, backers cannot fire a project owner, renegotiate contracts, demand meetings, or contractually mandate reporting deliverables. Instead, their source of power appears to reside in their capacity to create publicly visible discontent. This creates new challenges for project owners, who may feel a strong pressure to maintain a positive public impression for backers and onlookers (Colombo et al. 2015, Gleasure 2015).

Second is the number and diversity of controls and controllers. Unlike traditional control environments where a small number of individuals impose controls on a larger number of controllees, crowdfunding technologies enable a wide range of backers to participate as investor-controllers. This may include backers with little experience, contrasting motivations, moderate interest in the domain, and minor financial contributions. Backers nonetheless often feel a strong sense of psychological ownership (Zheng et al. 2018), despite the fact they are often drawn to different ideological or material qualities than their peers (Gerber and Hui 2013, Gleasure and Feller 2016). This is a common problem for crowdsourcing in general—for example, tensions are common between newcomers and long-term contributors (von Krogh et al. 2003, Steinmacher et al. 2015), and between individuals with different backgrounds and philosophies (Berry 2004, Stewart and Gosain 2006). This leads to territoriality and competition between contributors, as each battles to control specific decisions and exert influence (Thom-Santelli et al. 2009, Kittur and Kraut 2010).

Third and most important is the uncertain distribution of authority. Control and power are intertwined, as the former represents purposeful enactments of the latter (see Das and Teng 1998, 2001; Wicks et al. 1999; Bachmann 2001; Sambamurthy and Jarvenpaa 2002; and Gallivan and Depledge 2003). Without the power to bargain with other parties, there is no means to impose controls in the relationship; hence, those controls become valueless (Argyres and Liebeskind 1999). The number and diversity of backers in a crowdfunding project, combined with the absence of traditional control structures, means it is not clear which backers individually or collectively possess the power to enforce controls, nor how that power is accumulated and reinforced. Thus, project owners may struggle to determine which controls should be prioritized and how adherence/nonadherence reinforces or challenges corresponding power structures.

The existence of crowdfunded systems development projects therefore exposes some fundamental limitations in our understanding of control in systems development, much of which assumes coordinated and knowledgeable controls, applied within clear explicit or implicit hierarchical power structures, and enacted within some mutually understood communicative/ disciplinary practice. This creates a significant theoretical and practical blind spot for project owners seeking to use crowdfunding, or indeed other forms of web-enabled systems development. More fundamentally, this blind spot demonstrates a need to expand our conceptualization of how power, control, and social discourse interact in systems development. We attempt this expanded conceptualization by drawing on Foucault (1977, 1981) and performing a critical discourse analysis of online discussion around such projects. Specifically, the objective of our research is to explore how backers of a crowdfunded systems development project use public online discourse to impose controls in their role as investor-controllers. This presents three individual research questions.

The first question explores the relationship between latent power structures and different types of goaloriented control in crowdfunding. This is a bidirectional relationship, as the act of control both leverages some assumed power to influence others and, through that influence, increases the perceived legitimacy of that assumed power (McGrath 2005, Stahl 2008). Thus, RQ1 asks the following: How do backers of a crowdfunded systems development project mobilize, reinforce or undermine the latent power structures necessary for control?

The second question explores the relationship between different controls and their situational operationalization in social discourse in crowdfunding Such social discourse is required to communicate controls and manage their impact in everyday life (Cecez-Kecmanovic et al. 2008, Brooke 2009). This often requires careful adaptation of generic controls into situation-aware communications—for example, where power relationships are tenuous, conflict is high, or adherence is difficult to ascertain (Bachmann 2001, Boss et al. 2009, Chua et al. 2012). Thus, RQ2 asks the following: How do backers of a crowdfunded systems development project enact specific controls and determine their adherence through public online discourse?

The third question captures the “transformation” element of critical analysis (Myers and Klein 2011), which seeks to improve on existing conditions. A project owner must often balance competing interests from participants, meaning they must frequently push back against specific requests that may undermine shared goals (Das and Teng 1998, Soh et al. 2011). A greater range and number of investors in crowdfunding projects heightens the need for project owners to maintain some degree of autonomy. Thus, RQ3 asks the following: Under what conditions do backer-related power structures, backer-imposed controls, and backers’ use of public online discourse in a crowdfunded systems development project become dysfunctional?

The next section examines extant literature on power, control, and social discourse to determine how this literature applies to crowdfunded systems development projects. Next, we describe our research design, which uses a critical discourse analysis (Fairclough and Wodak 1997, Wodak and Meyer 2009) to examine two notable cases of crowdfunding, followed by a series of semistructured interviews with project owners and backers. Findings are presented in the form of seven theoretical propositions, each tied to one of the research questions above. Finally, we relate our findings to the literature and discuss implications and limitations.

## Theoretical Background

Power, control, and social discourse provide the three distinct analytical layers for this research (see Figure 1). The following sections present a detailed discussion of these analytical layers.

## Power in Crowdfunded Systems Development Projects

Scholars of management and systems development have used the concept of power to make sense of organizational structures and behaviors for many years, particularly those structures and behaviors that appear collectively dysfunctional (e.g., Avgerou 2005, Brooke 2009). These studies typically apply a “critical” perspective that treats social interaction as active, agenda-driven efforts by individuals or groups to legitimize selfishly preferable versions of reality (McGrath 2005, Cecez-Kecmanovic et al. 2008). Critical information systems research is divided across three dominant epistemologies: (i) the Bourdieu lineage, which focuses on addressing inequalities in the distribution of symbolic and social assets; (ii) the Habermas lineage, which focuses on achieving emancipation through mutual understanding; and (iii) the Foucault lineage, which focuses on deconstructing social discourse to reveal evolving patterns of intersubjective influence and subjugation (see Myers and Klein 2011). This study aligns with the Foucault lineage, as that perspective is best equipped to deal with the uncertain power relations, the diverse perspectives, and the reliance on public online discourse that appear to characterize crowdfunded systems development projects.

The Foucauldian view places power and knowledge as interdependent coproducers of social reality, meaning knowledge can be gained from power and vice versa. Some individuals or groups are assumed to be more knowledgeable and therefore more suitable social leaders. These individuals become privileged by dominant power structures, which afford them greater capacity to shape society by determining what behaviors are prosocial or “normal” and what are harmful or “deviant” (Foucault 1975). Put differently,

knowledge linked to power, not only assumes the authority of “the truth” but has the power to make itself true. . . . Thus, there is no power relation without the correlative constitution of a field of knowledge, nor any knowledge that does not presuppose and constitute at the same time, power relations. (Foucault 1977, p. 27)

Hence, when some powerful individual or group successfully mobilizes their power to influence others (a power-subject), the observable deference of that power-subject reinforces the existence and legitimacy of the power relationship. Conversely, when some individual or group attempts to mobilize power and fails (i.e., the power-subject resists), this undermines the assumed status quo and challenges the knowledgeability of those in power. Hence, those with power continuously seek to reinforce established norms by punishing deviance, while disadvantaged individuals or groups seek to create a new social order by undermining prevailing norms (Foucault 1971). Should this new order come to pass, there is no guarantee it will be better or fairer. Rather, it will represent a collective shift in the types of knowledge (and knowledge bearer) perceived as superior. A Foucauldian analysis therefore seeks to understand who is attempting to reinforce dominant power structures and who is attempting to undermine them, not simply to pursue emancipation, but because it is the most effective way to understand what is “known” to those involved.

## Control in Crowdfunded Systems Development Projects

The Foucauldian view separates power from control by using “power” to describe the latent intersubjective force providing the backdrop for social interaction, and “control” to describe the different attempts to mobilize that latent force toward some particular goals and values (see Foucault 1975). Traditional managerial accounts of control focus on encouraging practices that ensure suitable priority is given to high-level organizational goals (Ouchi 1979, Eisenhardt 1985, Jaworski 1988, Das and Teng 1998). Comparable high-level organizational goals are less well defined for crowdfunded systems development projects. Many projects attract backers with different outcomes in mind and no clear indication of which should get priority (Pitschner and Pitschner-Finn 2014, Gleasure and Feller 2016, Hobbs et al. 2016). Indeed, it is a feature of crowdsourcing that the goals and interests of participants may not only differ, but may actively contradict one another—for example, some open source developers wish to keep a portion of code proprietary, while others wish to make it all open (West 2003, West and Gallagher 2006), and some firms using open innovation may wish to control the spread of valuable breakthroughs, whereas other contributors may wish to distribute them freely (Henkel 2006). It is therefore important to consider backer-imposed controls in the context of some assemblage of competing goals, rather than advancing the goals of a single overarching entity or organization. The definition of backer-imposed crowdfunding controls adopted in this study therefore refers to mechanisms used by backers to monitor, motivate, and amend practices, ensuring the capabilities of a crowdfunded project and its people are directed toward advancing their particular goals and values.

Seminal control literature makes a fundamental distinction (see Table 1) between “formal” and “informal” control modes (Ouchi 1980, Kirsch et al. 2002). Formal controls involve the written specification and evaluation of (i) behavior controls and (ii) outcome controls (Das and Teng 1998, Nidumolu and Subramani 2003). The potential for such formal controls appears limited in crowdfunded systems development, where backers are not organized into typical hierarchies and “top down” management structures, nor do they have scope to create formal documentation. Informal controls are socially enforced through norms and customs—namely, (i) self control and (ii) clan control (Ouchi 1979, Eisenhardt 1985, Jaworski 1988, Kirsch et al. 2002, Choudhury and Sabherwal 2003). These informal controls focus on the role that individuals (self) or groups (clan) play in the exercise of control (Ouchi 1980, Eisenhardt 1985, Jaworski 1988) and rely on common values, beliefs, and/or traditions (Ouchi 1980). The role of informal controls is therefore intuitive in crowdfunded projects, given many are characterized by common values and beliefs (Gerber and Hui 2013).

## Public Online Discourse in Crowdfunded Systems Development Projects

The operationalization of control relies on social discourse, which represents the enactment of control (and associated power) for some specific situation. The Foucauldian definition of discourse is not limited to explicit language per se; rather “discourse” describes all of the social actions that shape communi cation (Foucault 1971, 1972). The significance of social discourse is therefore vast, as corresponding controls and power structures are constantly being negotiated as they are translated from abstract notions into their real-world equivalents (Stahl 2008). The ability to influence social discourse means one can actively manipulate the fabric of social reality; hence, individual discourses are considered “practices that systematically form the objects of which they speak” (Foucault 1972, p. 49). Social discourse is thus essential to both enact different controls and to demonstrate social adherence. This ability to link complementary and competing social discourses is key to understanding which groups are dominant and why they may be suppressing the social discourse of others (Brooke 2009). However, unpacking different enactments of control and demonstrations of adherence/nonadherence in social discourse is a challenging process.

To complicate matters further, social discourse in crowdfunded systems development projects is largely reliant on single/limited-channel asynchronous public online interactions. This constrains social presence and inhibits one’s ability to layer communication with nonverbal cues and coercive norms (Trevino et al. 1987, Yoo and Alavi 2001). Despite this limitation, social necessity means individuals often find alternative means to embed “leaner” media with such contextual information, provided individuals can draw on some shared historic social context (see Lee 1994). Crowd funded systems development projects may contain multiple partially or nonoverlapping historic contexts. Thus, public online discourse is likely to be (i) rich in layered cues and norms (because other means to convey these are limited) and (ii) open to contrasting interpretations of these cues and norms (because the historic social context is divergent). These challenges demand extra vigilance to make sense of social discourse for crowdfunded systems development projects.

Two specific qualities that impact the enactment of control in social discourse have been identified in the form of style and congruence (Adler and Bory 1996; Gregory and Keil 2013, 2014; Wiener et al. 2016). Style describes a continuum of interaction types between members, varying between bureaucratic and collaborative (Gregory et al. 2013, Wiener et al. 2016). A bureaucratic style is top-down and power-centralized, with little subordinate input, meaning communication is largely unidirectional and adherence is ensured through enforcement (Adler and Bory 1996, Gregory et al. 2013, Gregory and Keil 2014). A collaborative style is more power-balanced, involves two-way communication, encourages deviation in response to contingency, and welcomes feedback. Further, a collaborative style provides a rationale for the control and seeks ways to improve it (Wiener et al. 2016). Backers in a crowdfunded systems development project appear ill equipped for anything but a collaborative style; however, it is not clear how such a style scales for a large and diverse crowd. For example, what happens when some backers want a deadline extended so a new feature can be included, while others do not? Compromise may be impossible, at which point a project owner must decide which group has more authority.

Congruence is the degree of similarity between members’ perception of an enacted control and the resultant consequences (Ouchi and Johnson 1978, Narayanaswamy et al. 2013, Wiener et al. 2016). Thus, congruence is a composite of (i) the shared discursive understanding of the enacted control (which assumes individuals and groups perceive similar social objects, and hence share some common power-knowledge heritage) and (ii) the level of discursive agreement as to the appropriateness of a control (which assumes the mobilized power-knowledge structures are dominant over others). Each of these components seems intuitively complex in crowdfunded systems development projects, given the diversity of backers. For example, some backers may use public online discourse to demand a project owner update them on the development. Based on their interpretation of this social discourse, the project owner must decide if these demands are representative of the largerbackercommunityorwhethermostbackerswould prefer they ignore them and focus on development. Should the project owner decide to respond, they must further discern from that discourse the kind of information sought—for example, technical progress, spending.

## Research Method

This study performs a “critical discourse analysis” (CDA) (Cukier et al. 2009, Wodak and Meyer 2009, Ross and Chiasson 2011) to explore how public online discourse can enable backers to accumulate power and impose controls in crowdfunded systems development projects. As a method, CDA seeks to infer a dialectical relationship between specific discursive events and the situation(s), institution(s), social structure(s), social identities, and object(s) of knowledge, each of which both shapes and is shaped by those discursive events (Fairclough and Wodak 1997, Phillips and Hardy 2002). Thus, CDA considers no just extreme or corrigible structures for dominance or discrimination, but also those structures that appear impervious or obvious (Wodak and Meyer 2009). This means that social discourses must be systematically deconstructed to understand how and why specific individuals or groups wish to control others, and equally importantly, how that effort at control reveals structural power differences.

Relating social discourse to control demands awareness that relationships between instances of discourse and specific controls are not one-to-one. Each control type can be enacted through one or more discursive mechanisms (Kirsch 1997, Choudhury and Sabherwal 2003). For example, in a crowdfunding context, it is conceivable that powerful backers would impose outcome controls for a specific feature, not only by explicitly requesting that feature, but also by increasing the intensity and positivity of discussion whenever that feature is mentioned. Equally, a single discursive mechanism can be used to enact multiple control types. For example, a backer’s friendly request for information acts as both a behavior control (because they require a specific action from the project owner) and a clan control (because they implicitly reinforce a low power-distance and peer-like relationship between backers and project owners).

Relating social discourse to power structures demands awareness that the intervening controls are typically interdependent. Indeed, while each of the four types of control enacted in discourse are distinct, and have their own respective strengths and weaknesses, each is rarely present in isolation—rather they are usually combined into a portfolio of controls under the direction of those in charge (Jaworski 1988, Jaworski et al. 1993, Kirsch 1997, Choudhury and Sabherwal 2003). This makes it difficult to interpret observations of adherence/nonadherence in social discourse to their corresponding power structures, as the same control may overlap in competing control portfolios (though perhaps with starkly different priorities in each). For example, business-minded backers may call for a specific feature because it has obvious commercial value, while technically minded backers may join in because the operational challenge in implementing that feature is interesting—whose power does a project owners’ adherence reinforce more? Control types may also complement one another as they appear in social discourse—for example, agile systems development principles advocate “light” behavioral control to empower developers and enable greater benefits from self control (Schwaber and Beedle 2002). The same may apply in crowdfunded projects—for example, those same technically minded backers may be encouraging “antiproprietary” clan controls both because of personal values and because it provides selfishly reusable code as outcomes.

## Case Selection

A case study (Eisenhardt 1989, Patton 2002) provided data for the CDA. Specifically, a comparative case study was used to allow theoretical replication and triangulation of emerging themes (see Orlikowski and Baroudi 1991). Two comparative cases were selected as the focus for the CDA. Consistent with the exploratory nature of the study, “index” cases were preferred to stratified or representative samples, as the former were more likely to accommodate rich empirical inspection and revelatory insights (Patton 2002). Further, we selected two cases. (i) A significant number of backers and public online discourse were involved. This was done to ensure sufficiently rich public online discourse data. (ii) Development activities broke down. This was because one is more likely to find visible, rich manifestations of power and control where conflict and disappointment are present (Ouchi 1979, Kirsch 1997, Choudhury and Sabherwal 2003).

The first case is the ZANO microdrone, a handheld Android-based drone that follows a user and takes pictures from vantage points that may not otherwise be possible. The project owner fundraised on Kickstarter from November 24, 2014, to January 8, 2015, during which the ZANO received £2,335,119 from 12,075 backers (far exceeding its initial target of £125,000). This made the ZANO the most successful crowdfunding campaign by a European business at the time. Despite early success, development faltered, promised functions went undelivered, and backers became restless. The company declared bankruptcy in December 2015, having reportedly only delivered 4 of the 19,000 preordered drones to Kickstarter backers. This case was selected for its notoriety, its contrast between early success and later difficulties, and the notably high levels of backer discontent, which included discussions of litigation against the project owner.

The second case is “Yogventures,” a video game launched by Yogscast, a gaming YouTube channel. The project owner fundraised on Kickstarter from April 6 to May 6, 2012, raising \$567,665 from 13,647 backers. This case differs from the ZANO in four key ways. First, unlike ZANO, where problems began to surface early in the project, development for Yogventures appeared to go well during the initial stages. This presents an opportunity to compare changes in public online discourse across cases and map those changes to differentiating events. Second, while the number of backers was similar (13,647), Yogventures was not overfunded to the same degree (the initial target was \$250,000). Thus, the volume and backgrounds of backers should have been less of a surprise for this project owner. Third, the primary output for the Yogventures project was software, meaning scalebased production issues were less relevant. Fourth, the Yogscast (a brand-owner) delegated development for Yogventures to an independent project owner, Winterkewl Games LLC. This creates a natural divide for backers looking to draw on historic power structures, allowing CDA more chance to observe the negotiation of new power relationships.

## Data Collection and Analysis

Two complementary stages of data gathering were employed. Phase 1 collected public records of online interactions between backers and owners. These records demonstrated public online discourse in action, including backers’ enactment of controls and subsequent changes in practices. Sampling focused primarily on Kickstarter, including campaign pages, comments, updates/videos, responses to updates, and (selectively) the personal profiles of owners and backers. Other sources included personal webpages/blogs, news/ technology websites, and social media, notably Reddit, Twitter, and Facebook (see Online Appendix D).

Phase 2 performed semistructured interviews with five project owners and seven backers. These were used to reinforce and enrich findings by triangulating observations and exploring the power-related perceptions and intentions behind public online discourse. Interviews also addressed limitations of analytical transferability by including project owners and backers from other platforms and different, successful projects (see Table 2 and Online Appendix E). This enabled a form of “member-checking” (Lincoln and Guba 1985) to triangulate and challenge the reliability and validity of findings (see Online Appendix A).

Data gathering and analysis incorporated several techniques from grounded theory (Charmaz 2006, Suddaby 2006). Specifically, this study adopted (i) theoretical sampling, whereby both Phase 1 and Phase 2 data were gathered and analyzed according to the needs of emerging theory (Glaser and Strauss 1967, Urquhart et al. 2010), and (ii) open, axial, and selective coding (Strauss and Corbin 1990). These grounded theory techniques were used to extend and refine preliminary theorizing (see McGhee et al. 2007). Such uses of grounded theory techniques with loosely formed a priori theory are common and generally accepted as a legitimate strategy for qualitative information systems research (Matavire and Brown 2013).

Preliminary emerging categories were the scale of individual investment and scale of group investment, both of which backers referred to often, particularly during the fundraising period and once projects began to struggle. Two further categories were identified as the accumulation of discourse and surges in discourse. These categories were explicitly referenced less frequently than scale of overall investment. However, they were clearly observable in the patterns of public online discourse sampled. The fourth emerging category was public perceptions of project failure. Discussion of possible failure was present early in both comparative cases. This discussion grew more intense as each project began to miss deadlines. A final emerging category was the individual right to impose control, as specific backers repeatedly referred to their personal contribution or status.

Axial coding occurred as authors continuously related emerging categories to other categories, as well as deeper theoretical/epistemological assumptions driving the research and competing explanations (Strauss and Corbin 1998, Charmaz 2006). This process collapsed accumulation of discourse and surges in discourse as subcategories of a single large category, the scale of overall discourse. This reflected a growing realization that backers and project owners tended to engage with public online discourse in ebbs and flows, many of which involved new participants from previous cycles. This meant it was the accumulation within surges that was important; hence, the boundaries between the subcategories became ambiguous. Similarly, the scale of individual investment and the scale of group investment were collapsed into one category called the scale of overall investment.

Analysis of “causal conditions” during axial coding (Strauss and Corbin 1998) presented two (largely anticipated) propositions suggesting that backers use interactions in public online discourse to impose controls on project owners and each other. Further propositions suggested the scale of overall discourse, the scale of overall investment, and the individual contribution to public online discourse each moderated backers’ power to impose controls. The moderating influence of public perceptions of failure on backers ability to impose controls was more complicated, as it appeared to increase backers ability to impose controls initially, then decrease it once perceptions became more substantial and discourse more frenetic. This was particularly useful for interview data, as it uncovered nonuniformities across projects and individuals. Interviews could then explore these nonuniformities and add nuance to explanations.

## Findings

An overview of findings is presented in Figure 2 and Table 3. Seven propositions emerged during data analysis. Propositions 1 and 2 address the second research question, which asked how backers enact specific controls and determine their adherence through public online discourse. Propositions 3–5 address the first research question, which asked how backers mobilize, reinforce, or undermine the latent power structures necessary for control. Propositions 6 and 7 address the third research question, which explored the conditions under which backer-related power structures, backer-imposed controls, and backers’ use of public online discourse become destructive in a crowdfunded systems development project.

Proposition 1. Backers impose controls on project owners through their interactions in public online discourse.

Public online discourse in both cases illustrated backers imposing each type of control on project owners. Further, many of these controls elicited some degree of adherence. Behavior controls pushed for regular updates, which backers requested often throughout each project. These requests were typically presented as suggestions, rather than demands. However, controls assumed more bureaucratic styles when dialogue slowed, often drawing comparisons to other projects—for example, “Other Kickstarter projects update their progress on their Kickstarter page until product is ready to ship. Do the same, it’s not asking much.”

Public online discourse-based outcome controls focused on desired game or drone features. For Yogventures, the desired outcomes were often whimsical and silly—for example, a “chocolatey banana sword” or bonuses for starting “dance parties.” This implicitly softened expectations of adherence, as it suggested backers were engaging with the creative process for hedonic purposes. Project owners in both cases encouraged backers’ suggestions, yet adhered to them only selectively, often making a point to highlight the corresponding game or drone features they had chosen to include—for example, “you asked, we listened.” This nurtured expectations for a collaborative style. Some controls tentatively deviated from this norm by suggesting at least some adherence—for example, “Thank you for your time and reading these ten suggestions I have for the Yogventures! And I hope you consider at least 3 of them in the game.” Project owners often chose to ignore these requests, further enacting and motivating the collaborative style in subsequent discourse.

Public online discourse–based calls for the project owner to exert self control were also observed in both cases, particularly in the earlier stages, when backers were making vast and diverging suggestions for game or drone features. Other backers frequently encouraged project owners to apply discretion—for example, one Yogventures backer remarked “I have faith that they filter 99% of the ridiculous suggestions out.” Discourse-based mechanisms for self control became particularly prominent when each project owner was criticized by external parties for lacking sufficient bureaucratic planning and reporting mechanisms. For Yogventures, a video-blogging peer of the Yogscast questioned the naivety of the project budget, while the project owner of the ZANO was accused of faking promotional videos. Discourse in both cases distanced external critics from the community and encouraged project owners to continue working as they saw fit.

Public online discourse–based clan controls became increasingly congruent for each project as backers discerned the priorities of the respective project owner. For Yogventures, clan controls initially built on shared experiences, values, and rituals from the Yogscast YouTube channel. This included multiple Yogscast-specific in-jokes—for example, “I am Dave! Yognaught” and references to “joffo cakes.” These injokes and rituals gradually decreased once backers realized that the project owner was a third-party developer and not inclined to respond in kind. At this point, discourse became more game oriented and humor more inclusive. For the ZANO, a series of technical updates fed into “techie” clan controls that focused on engineering goals over gimmickry or cosmetics values—for example, “The easy part of this project is painting the device. The challenging part is the tech.” These controls were exemplified when shipping delays were first announced. Bubbling expressions of frustration were routinely dismissed as impatient and short-sighted—for example, “I would rather wait another 6 months and receive the Gizmo I was hoping for instead of a part finished compromise.”

In terms of perceptions and intentions, there are suggestions that the desire to participate in public online discourse and the desire to impose controls were directly related. One possible symptom of this relationship was that the two interviewed backers who showed limited interest in imposing controls were also the two backers who did not participate in public online discourse. Project owners were aware of this relationship, meaning they were often reticent to engage with backers too often in case controls would become unmanageable. The interviewed ZANO manager recalled, “you had people pushing their luck. . . . The MicroSD card was never meant to be feature on the drone but [lead engineer] said it was happening because of backer pressure.” Project owners were nonetheless keen to nurture enthusiasm. The same ZANO manager recalled how the lead engineer spent “hours each day answering questions.” He noted, “Backers really liked this, they had a direct line to [lead engineer]. In a way it was like an open source project where there was collaboration and a real dialogue between backers and [the lead engineer]."

## Proposition 2. Backers impose controls on other backers through their interactions in public online discourse.

Public online discourse in both cases also showed backers imposing each type of control on other backers. Many behavior controls between backers acted to protect project owners from negative or distracting discourse. This makes sense, as such discourse damages the public image of the project. Nonetheless, backerto-backer behavior controls frequently created tension, to the point where project owners had to repeatedly intervene to encourage calm and diffuse escalating bureaucratic styles. An example for the ZANO occurred when several backers grew openly critical after a handful of preordered drones were shipped before those promised to backers. This prompted behavior controls such as “stop whining,” “quit complaining,” and “stop being so entitled.” The opposite behavior controls were observed once that project deteriorated, as defense of the project waned and critical backers encouraged others to spread punitive public online discourse in other media—for example, “Spread this scam. . . . Keep the momentum and make it big.”

Public online discourse-based mechanisms for outcome controls between backers were less common. Backers struggled to establish the necessary congruence to enact these controls in the absence of the project owner. One example occurred when a Yogventures backer attempted to establish an unofficial discussion forum. Several other backers challenged the individual proposing it, distancing them from the group and questioning the integrity of their intentions—for example, “we get it you got a s\*\*\*\*y forum up and you are trying to sell the domain for a quick buck.” That discourse effectively killed the forum, despite ongoing and repeated posts from the backer proposing it. For the ZANO, outcome controls between backers became prevalent after news spread of the project’s failure. Many backers felt a coordinated lawsuit could secure compensatory outcomes if enough backers participated. Others suppressed this outcome by citing a lack of evidence for malice or dishonesty, or by arguing the futility of suing a liquidated company.

Public online discourse–based calls for self control occurred most often to encourage retrospection and support collaborative discourse. A particularly interesting example occurred when a Yogventures backer suggested the game should hire a specific actor with a strong cult following to voice the sound effects. One backer reportedly contacted the actor’s agent to gauge their interest (despite having received no encouragement to do so from the project owners). This discourse culminated in a petition, for which the backer responsible explained, “I’m doing the petition to see if people are interested and if they are, I’m going to give it to [the first name of the project owner] and say: hey dude, I checked with the community and it seems people like the idea.” The idea was unceremoniously abandoned after that petition received little interest, suggesting backers placed high value on the self-determinism of other community members.

Public online discourse–based clan controls between backers illustrated evolving value/belief systems in each project’s discourse. Dramatic events tended to coincide with a heightened discursive focus on specific values and a slow growth in bureaucratic styles around the observed consensus. As one example, discourse in Yogventures grew dismissive of youthful backers after three \$10,000 pledges were withdrawn by a 15- year-old. This was further enacted by subsequent discussions of current jobs or university courses and, more subtly, by an increase in swearing and adult humor. In contrast, the devalued qualities among ZANO backers were maturity and even-temperedness. This shift occurred as discourse began to entangle commitment to the group with collective outrage—for example, “we have the right and power to start a class action lawsuit” and “For the people that say starting a class action lawsuit is a waste of time that’s bulls\*\*t.”

The perceptions and intentions behind these backerto-backer controls mirror those for controls imposed on backers. Interviewed backers suggested they felt a responsibility to manage the impact of public online discourse on project owners, as they felt bickering between backers could become a distraction. One individual explained, “there are the aggressive types of backers and they huddle together inside and outside the platform using social media platforms, blogs etc to bash the project owners.” Another backer lamented the “noise” created by peripheral backers: “sure, they can speak, but they also don’t have the right to be heard—especially if what they are saying contributes nothing to the discussion.” Interviewed project owners were aware that some backers were continuous jostling for control. Yet they were content to ignore it wherever possible, sometimes to avoid drawing the ire of specific backers, other times because they felt it was futile.

Proposition 3. Backers’ power is impacted by the scale of overall investment

Public online discourse in both cases showed backers’ implicit and explicit assumptions of power and control changed as the scale of overall investment increased. One implicit example was the requests for updates once projects reached fundraising milestones. Project owners typically adhered to such requests, reinforcing backers’ sense of entitlement by providing updates, acknowledgments, or additional commitments/goals. Other, more explicit examples occurred when backers referenced the scale of overall investment as a source of power over project owners—for example, one Yogventures backer claimed, “the more we raise, the more the [the project owners] have to make the game even better.” Interviews confirmed this link, as backers equated higher levels of funding with a deeper embedding of the community in the project. Project owners rein forced these perceptions in public online discourse—for example, the Yogventures project owner stated, “any extra money we raise will go directly to funding all the cool features the community wants this game to have.” Interviewed project owners also admitted a heightened pressure to show respect to backers during fundraising. One individual admitted, “when we were doing the fundraising I wouldn’t sleep, it was a matter of responding within minutes or hours, rather than leaving it too long.”

More complex was the significance of individual investment. Public online discourse during fundraising occasionally celebrated high-end backers, with rare incidents where backers touted their high-end contribution to gain status. Yet this link between power and individual investment faded as projects progressed and discourse developed. Interviews with backers found further evidence that the accumulation of interactions in discourse gradually buried any attention on individual investment as projects progressed. This was less evident for the low-participation backers interviewed, who maintained that each individual’s investment had at least some relevance as regards power. However, each of the others felt this link disappeared once targets were met, at which point only the collective fundraising mattered. One backer explained, “Because it was so overfunded, there was a general sense that expectations were a little bit higher . . . there was a general sense of ‘oh, we got so much more money than we were expecting. That must mean we can have more stuff in there than we thought previously.’”

Proposition 4. Backers’ power is impacted by the scale of public online discourse.

Public online discourse tended to come in surges, with backers assuming more power and imposing more controls as the scale of public online discourse increased. As with fundraising, the link between the scale of public online discourse and power was often explicit, as backers and projects owners referenced the level of discourse when reinforcing power relationships. This was particularly notable for the ZANO—for example, during fundraising they reassured backers, “We read all the messages and comments and in view of these, we are now delighted to inform you of the following updates.” They further referenced the scale of public online discourse when highlighting their adherence to more specific requests—for example, “We’ve had a bunch of requests from backers wishing to pledge and receive two of the Early-Bird ZANO’s complete with Standard case. We have now made that reward level available.” Backers praised the campaign for its responsiveness, further legitimizing and reinforcing their collective power as vocal backers to influence project owners.

At the level of intentions and perceptions, many backers perceived the scale of public online discourse as an indicator of their collective desire to share information. They therefore treated periods of intensive public online discourse as a chance to hold the attention of the project owner. One of the interviewed Yogventures backers summarized, “we thought we would be listened to if we all rallied behind an idea.” More intensive discourse also amplified the sanctioning power of backers if project owners undermined them. One backer described a coordinated effort in a different project to punish the project owner for not delivering rewards. This involved a long-term strategy of negative public online discourse for each of that project owner’s future business efforts. Project owners were aware of this growing reputational threat when the scale of public online discourse was high, because of both the increased public visibility among onlookers and the increased mobility of opinions among backers. One project owner explained their goal had been to “avoid enraged backers” by maintaining monthly updates that emphasized positive developments and ongoing progress. The ZANO manager agreed, noting, “if you turn your back on them for a minute the tide can turn."

Proposition 5. Backers’ power is impacted by their individual contribution to public online discourse.

Despite the collective nature of backers’ power over project owners, both cases illustrated that some backers had stronger power relationships than others, corresponding at least partly to their individual contribution to public online discourse. Part of this can be attributed to individuals adopting intermediary roles to intercept or redirect control requests, so limiting other backers’ access to the project owner. For Yogventures, this role was largely determined by the frequency and duration of participation—for example, one backer dismissed another’s comments by asking, “how long have you guys pledged for? Couple of hours maybe? A bit more? I’ve pledged on day 1, I’ve read every comment. . . . If you got a question after the guys at winterkewl and the yogscast themselves I’m one of the few who will probably know the answer." Other backers had higher individual contribution to public online discourse due to additional information channels. For example, one ZANO backer was given a tour of development premises and an early version of the drone. This prompted other backers to elevate powerknowledge structures around that individual in exchange for information, photographs, and personal reflections. Such disparities led to power struggles in both cases, as other backers undermined hierarchies, either in general terms (e.g., “we all have every right to ask questions and get answers from the creators”) or more specifically (e.g., “you need to learn your f\*\*\*\*\*g place”).

Interviews confirmed these sources of individual power and further highlighted knowledgeability as a key source of influence. One Yogventures backer explained, “I think there were people who, not necessarily because of their backing level, but because they had more of an understanding how stuff worked, they were listened to more . . . they were able to communicate with [the project owner] in a way that he immediately understood.” Similarly, the interviewed ZANO manager explained, “those that were able to converse with [lead engineer] at a high technical level, they approached the top of the hierarchy . . . those individuals then became leaders in that crowd.” Others gained status through intimidation. Another project owner explained, “there are people who have a shorter fuse so we have to respond to them more rapidly, or they’re just more prolific and if they got cross it would be more damaging or stressful than someone else.”

Proposition 6. Backers’ ability to impose controls on project owners has an evolving relationship with public perceptions of project failure.

The controls imposed by backers became increasingly bureaucratic with growing public perceptions of project failure. Behavior controls requesting interaction and updates were most common. These were initially collaborative (e.g., “A word from Zano would be much appreciated”), and project owners were typically responsive, acting to alleviate or manage collective and individual concerns. After some time it became clear that both projects would not deliver some or all of the intended functionalities. With project owners competence now in question, the enactment of controls became increasingly forceful—often presented as nonoptional calls for those project owners’ attention. Yet despite backers’ growing assertiveness, project owners in both cases appeared decreasingly compliant, each retreating from public online discourse to varying degrees up to the official termination of their respective projects.

Backers’ perceptions and intentions on this matter were intuitive. Most believed a collaborative style was more effective under normal circumstances—for example, one interviewee remarked, “being responsive to backers seems to be dependent upon how courteous and rational the community has been.” Yet many backers felt a growing desire for an explanation and a chance to scrutinize budgets and plans once proposed deliverables were compromised. Interviewed project owners were keenly aware how public perceptions of project failure affected discourse—for example, “the fact we were late meant we definitely felt a need to be more active because we felt at some point people were going to get p\*\*\*\*d off.” However, project owners also felt their efforts to save the project would be undermined if they disclosed issues too early; hence, they needed solutions in place before engaging backers. One interviewee described the “beast of project management” meant they often did not know why things were not working, an uncomfortable reality backers may not understand. More substantial issues therefore created longer periods of silence while sophisticated solutions were sought. Subsequent hostility led project owners to disengage and wait for discourse to return to a more manageable state. The interviewed ZANO manager recalled, “The public discourse consisted of rage, confusion, optimism. No one had control. . . . The backers had forced the project creator into a hole.“ Thus, changing enactments of controls became decreasingly effective at communicating or enforcing them.

Proposition 7. Backers’ power over project owners has an evolving relationship with public perceptions of project failure.

Project owners’ retreat from public online discourse had deep implications for assumed power structures. Perceived instability around the project led many backers to begin mobilizing their remaining power to salvage value while they still had means. This included refunds, information, and source code/ component parts. Power was typically mobilized by threatening the image of the project—for example, cost breakdowns were requested by insinuating that withholding such information was public admission of guilt or incompetence. Project owners largely ignored these requests while the fate of the project was uncertain, yet each returned to public online discourse once development had stopped to explain the reasons for failure. These included unforeseen technical challenges, costs, and personal/interpersonal issues. They also provided some of the requested project information—for example, financial breakdowns were provided in both cases. Other compensations were limited by dissolution of their respective companies. For Yogventures, this meant that remaining funds and source code were appropriated by the Yogscast. For the ZANO, this meant total liquidation of the company. Despite collective frustration, other backers in each case expressed gratitude to project owners for returning to explain what had happened, acknowledging they were not compelled to do so.

In terms of backers’ perceptions and intentions, project owners’ disappearance undermined the power relationships linking them to the project. This caused some backers to conclude projects were being covertly abandoned. The interviewed ZANO manager explained, “The discourse got to a level of mob mentality, there was definitely a witch hunt.” This escalated until ZANO employees and their families were receiving death threats. At this point, project owners motivation and sense of moral duty to save the project deteriorated as they felt it was already a lost cause. Another project owner described, “It feels very delicate . . . one of the questions we’ve been pondering and mulling over is—is there a chance this project fails, not because we can’t deliver . . . but because we’re exhausted . . . is there a tipping point?” A backer lamented, “it’s kind of a vicious cycle on that front, particularly when it’s a small company that can just fold and declare bankruptcy and move on.” Thus, the more backers leveraged their assumed source of power, the ability to create negative public image, the less meaningful power relationships became for project owners.

## Discussion and Conclusions

The most striking power-related finding from this study concerns the fragility of power structures in crowdfunded systems development. Backers’ power stems from their ability to damage public image around projects and project owners. Yet backers who wield this power may also undermine it, as significant negative public image reduces a project owner’s incentive to complete the project. Thus, crowdfunded system development is subject to a fundamental instability that makes it difficult to recover from setbacks.

The most striking control-related finding is the presence (and impact) of formal controls, the operationalization of which is limited in three key ways. First, formal controls are characterized as being “explicit” and “written in black and white” (Ouchi 1979). This typically refers to contracts, rulebooks, procedures, and so forth (e.g., Ouchi 1977, Das 1993, Kirsch 1997, Kirsch et al. 2010). Yet public online discourse often achieved similar results, despite the seeming mismatch with the types of “black and white” documentation described in seminal control literature. Second, for a control to be “formal,” it has to originate from someone with clearly identifiable authority—for example, a CEO or manager (Kirsch and Cummings 1996). In the cases studied, no backer identified such authority. Yet some backers not only assumed this role, they did so successfully— producing the desired response on multiple occasions. Third, formal controls are typically applied by someone who can choose when/how to monitor adherence (Boss et al. 2009). Opportunistic nonadherence weakens power and control, a problem traditionally managed with close interpersonal relationships (Kirsch et al. 2002, Dawson et al. 2010). Few backers possess such relationships, yet this did not prevent backers from evaluating control adherence with conviction.

Implications for Information Systems Development This study suggests that existing views of power and control may not be adequate to capture emerging models of systems development. Findings show a need to revisit how web technologies enable power/ control in systems development, including the oppressive or dysfunctional potential of those technologies. To achieve this, we return to the three fundamental principles of a Foucauldian analysis—that is, the genealogy of knowledge, the archaeology of knowledge, and the Panopticon (see Myers and Klein 2011).

The archaeology of knowledge acts to reconcile the first tension regarding the need for formal controls to be explicit. This concept of “explicit” is more complex in control research than it seems. Formal controls not only assume specific outputs and behaviors can be stated; they assume that these outputs and behaviors can be evaluated against overarching goals to determine their desirability (see Ouchi 1979). Thus, “explicit” formal controls require a clear set of goals to map to unambiguous instructions (Kirsch 1996). Such clarity of goals can become difficult when systems development projects involve large numbers of controllers from different backgrounds (Kirsch et al. 2002, Tiwana 2010). This becomes even more problematic in crowdfunding, where not only is the number and diversity of controllers amplified, but those controllers may operate in groups with significant opposing values (Gleasure and Morgan 2018). Public online discourse must therefore not only capture utterances of control to make them “explicit”; this discourse must be able to establish (i) which overarching goals are legitimate and (ii) how utterances relate to those goals.

The archaeology of knowledge asks researchers to examine the historical configurations and evolutions of social discourse that have grown from dominant discourse at a particular point in time—that is, to consider discourses “as practices specified in the element of the archive” (Foucault 1972, p. 131). This allows comparative decomposition of social discourse to identify which truth claims exist, how they vary, and which have become dominant. Public online discourse not only allows backers to make contrasting or complementary truth claims, it also creates a unilaterally visible archive showing how different claims have become accepted or marginalized over time. Thus, not only are specific backers claims explicit to project owners, they are explicit to other backers. Further, they are embedded within a legacy of historic public online discourse on crowdfunding websites that have been accumulating discourse-related norms for several years (Agrawal et al. 2015, Feller et al. 2017). This deepens shared expectations for priority goals and contextualizes requests within observable precedents from other projects.

The genealogy of knowledge acts to reconcile the second tension regarding the need for formal controls to come from someone with clear authority. The Foucauldian view assumes that knowledgeability and the power to impose control are inseparable—that is, deep control demands deep knowledge and vice versa (Foucault 1975, 1980; Townley 1993; Cutting 2003). This is also true in systems development, where the ability to link goals to specific outputs and behaviors depends, at least partly, on the controllers’ technical knowledge (Lyytinen and Robey 1999, Choudhury and Sabherwal 2003, Levina 2005). However, the specialized nature of systems development means technical knowledge is almost always higher among the development team than in clients or managers, meaning controllers must complement limited technical knowledge with institutional power relationships to create the types of “authority” required (Kirsch et al. 2002, Levina and Vaast 2008). Such institutional power relationships are less likely in crowdfunded systems development. This means backers must either possess equivalent technical knowledge to project owners or, more likely, find ways to create power relationships that mimic the institutional power structures in traditional projects.

Foucault introduced the genealogy of knowledge in Discipline and Punish (Foucault 1975) to explain not just what truth claims have characterized discourse, but also how new claims have emerged and become dominant. This principle links given systems of thought to accompanying historical events (Foucault 1980). Foucault (1972, p. 240) states, “the genealogical aspect concerns the effective formation of discourse, whether within the limits of control, or outside of them, or as is most frequent, on both sides of the delimitation.” Thus, the genealogy of knowledge reveals the “descent” and “emergence” of particular social discourses in relation to specific people and events (Garland 2014). Public online discourse in the selected cases shows that backers accumulated status by dictating priority norms and values. They then used this status to reinforce or undermine emerging hierarchies, until their authority became implicitly assumed in the community. This allowed them to then dictate outputs and behaviors in a quasi-managerial style. Project owners felt obliged to respond to avoid creating tension, further legitimizing those backers’ authority to onlookers (see Mitchell et al. 1997, Scott and Lane 2000, and Gleasure and Feller 2016).

Lastly, the Panopticon acts to reconcile the third tension regarding the need for controls to have adherence observed at the controller’s discretion. Systems development underwent a major transformation in recent decades that saw a decreasing role for procedural documentation (Agile Alliance 2001). This has the potential to limit the visibility of control adherence for clients/managers. However, this threat is mitigated by integrating clients/managers into the development process and by continuously releasing software for acceptance testing (Beck 2000, Lee and Xia 2010). Such integration substitutes predefined information channels/adherence measures with dynamic and evolving networks of information exchange, so allowing clients/managers to maintain formal control in a more flexible development process by identifying and evaluating new desired outputs as they emerge (Harris et al. 2009). Crowdfunded systems development projects challenge this by separating backers from developers and predefining many rewards and outputs.

The Panopticon is a metaphor based on Jeremy Bentham’s 19th century design for prisons, insane asylums, schools, hospitals, and factories, where members know they are subject to observation at all times but do not know when that observation is occurring (Mathiesen 1997). Foucault (1975) used this design to show how hierarchical observation controls individuals by pushing them to constantly imagine how they are being perceived by those in power (so creating a relentless normative self-surveillance, even when no actual surveillance is occurring). This resonates with findings in this study. Backers used public online discourse to demonstrate knowledgeability, either by showing they participated in large amounts of previous discourse, by showing that they hadadditionalinformation channels linking them to project owners, or by showing that they had expertise in the domain. Thus, knowledge able backers became gatekeepers for control, as (i) they were best placed to understand whether project owners were behaving as expected, (ii) they could challenge or support the legitimacy of controls imposed by other backers. In reality, these backers were still largely reliant on public online discourse to evaluate project owners adherence to controls. Yet their added perceived knowledgeability contributed to a collective sense that backers were operating within the information boundaries of the project; hence, they were still equipped to identify and evaluate new desired outputs as they emerged. This perception was shattered once projects deteriorated and project owners retreated from the reach of backers, at which point discourse suggested that many backers felt collectively powerless. Thus, many backers began scrambling to constitute new power relationships, dividing the community and creating conflict-ridden interactions and increasingly negative public image for the project.

The broader implications for systems development are that these findings collectively blur key distinctions used to make sense of control. For power hierarchies, the threat of negative public image means that project owners are always under duress, while backers’ knowledge is always restricted/restrictable. Thus, while traditional projects may assume that some controls are deeply embedded in explicit hierarchies and others are not, emerging hierarchies in crowdfunded projects appear to pervade almost all controls, albeit their impact is limited when compared with explicit managerial structures. Similarly, while controls in traditional projects can be separated into goal-oriented written/explicit instructions (formal) and value-based norms and customs (informal), almost all controls in crowdfunded projects combine these qualities. Finally, while controls can be presented as mandates or suggestions in traditional projects, the distinction is less clear in crowdfunded projects. Instead, discursive enactments almost always require some combination of compelling (bureaucratic) and convincing (collaborative). This means that the majority of power relationships, control types, and discourse styles in crowdfunded projects tend to sit somewhere on the spectrum between opposing classifications, as opposed to traditional projects where they may be more polarized. This requires that we revise how we think about control to avoid reliance on such polarized conceptualizations.

## Implications for Crowdfunding

This study brings two important issues into focus for web-enabled fundraising technologies. First is the role of backers as controllers in systems development. Most research to date has treated backers primarily as investors, whereby the challenge for project owners is to attract as many backers as possible to maximize funding (e.g., Griffin 2012, Mitra and Gilbert 2014, Colombo et al. 2015). Some research has acknowledged the role of backers in generating positive image and marketing “buzz” (Thies et al. 2014, Wessel et al. 2016). Other research has looked at how public online discourse bonds backers to project owners and outcomes (Beaulieu and Sarker 2013, Gleasure and Feller 2016). Yet discussion has largely neglected the controls imposed by backers, much less how these controls emerge and are enacted. This study offers a foundation to study these control behaviors further and to unpack control-related nuances in public online discourse.

The second issue concerns the additional challenges faced as larger numbers of backers are attracted to a project. Larger numbers of backers are needed to take advantage of many of the perceived advantages of crowdfunding, such as generating excitement, gathering ideas and feedback, and attracting media attention (see Gerber and Hui 2013, Gleasure 2015, and Summers et al. 2016). Existing research has addressed how attracting more backers may create additional production challenges (e.g., Mollick 2014) and market segment cannibalization (e.g., Belleflamme et al. 2014). However, the power/control perspective suggests a more fundamental issue: more backers means an increasing number and range of controls. This is not simply a matter of scale, as more backers means a growing number of controls from backers with different goals, interests, and levels of experience. Hence, unity among these backers is likely to fall as their number increases. Equally importantly, the capacity to unite backers in support of the project falls more quickly than the capacity to unite them in opposition. Crowds are generally better at identifying bad ideas than good ones, as good ideas must be good across a range of criteria, whereas bad ideas need only be bad in one recognizable way (Klein and Garcia 2015). As development proceeds and becomes more tangible, larger crowds of backers may therefore be increasingly difficult to unite in favor of specific practices or outputs but relatively easy to unite in opposition. This means a vocal and cohesive crowd is most likely if backers are dissatisfied. This size-related bias toward negative discourse means that the potential for destructive public online discourse grows disproportionately as more backers join a project. It may also mean that certain individuals grow strategically critical or outrageous in pursuit of individual power, if they perceive this as a way to preempt a sea change among other backers (Sia et al. 2002). Attracting large numbers of backers may therefore harm a project, even when due considerations are given to variations in production and market penetration. Such a problem clearly warrants attention, yet is overlooked by existing research.

## Limitations and Future Research

This study illustrates the need for renewed focus on power and social discourse in control research. Discourse is often referenced within holistic analyses of control mechanisms but rarely gets explicit attention. Those studies that do look at discourse focus on bounded, known groups (typically employees), in which such discourse acts as a vehicle for clan controls (e.g., Ouchi 1977, 1979; Eisenhardt 1985; Das 1993; Kirsch 1997; Kirsch et al. 2010). This study shows the importance of social discourse at a broader level, specifically how public online discourse can mobilize power and enact control in the absence of traditional control tools—for example, meetings, contracts, and reports. We thus call for greater emphasis on social discourse in control research, particularly where web technologies are replacing established tools.

We further call for crowdfunding/crowdsourcing research that explores the potential for more obvious authority structures and formal controls to complement those that emerge over time in discourse. Such structures and controls run the risk of rolling back some of the benefits of web-enabled participation, such as the speed and creative freedom associated with fundraising on the web. However, they may also limit the presence of power/control vacuums that could otherwise be filled in dysfunctional ways. For example, while set reporting times and structures for project owners may create some administrative burden, they may also diffuse backers’ escalating calls for interaction when projects are struggling.

Two key methodological limitations of our study must be acknowledged. The first relates to our selection of cases and the generalizability of findings We focused on cases where development activities broke down, as these were the cases in which controls were most likely to become visible. We also focused on large projects where levels of discourse were high This made sense for the purposes of theory building, as it presented more data from which to generate theory. Interviews allowed for some degree of triangulation, suggesting that core findings should apply to a range of crowdfunding contexts. However, future research is required to compare different platforms project sizes, creative domains, and outcomes.

The second limitation relates to the extrapolation of our findings to crowdsourcing and other open phenomena. This study focused specifically on crowdfunding, but we believe similar patterns may occur in other open areas such as crowdsourcing, innersourcing, and other nontraditional coproduction environments. The reliance on web technologies introduces complexities and nuances that impact on control (see Bonabeau 2009, Leimeister et al. 2009, and Brabham 2011)— specifically, (i) unknown composition of participant groups at the outset; (ii) abstract, knowledgeintensive, and often ill-structured work processes; (iii) diverse skillsets and backgrounds; (iv) diverse and sometimes competing agendas, including agendas that contradict the crowd system itself (e.g., power gained from destructive behaviors); (v) abstract, complex, often unknown final outputs and goals (and the resulting difficulty of their evaluation); (vi) uncertain, dynamic, fast-moving fashion waves and hype cycles; and (vii) unique power, trust, and bargaining dynamics. Similar to crowdfunding, the crowdsourcing and coproduction knowledge space suffers from a distinct lack of research on control. There is thus an opportunity for future studies to examine and compare our findings with other contexts that include multiple control dyads (see Soh et al. 2011), multiple control portfolios, multiple products and services, and multiple organizational units.

In addition to these limitations, this research shows a need to study control success in crowdfunded systems development. Control success is difficult to evaluate, as controls can be subtle, often evolving slowly over time. This means that long-term effects are difficult to isolate. Nonetheless, this study has created an opportunity to explore control success by identifying the different types of controls enacted through public online discourse, the mechanisms that enact them, and the power structures they mobilize. The obvious next step is therefore to examine the extent to which these controls act as a positive or negative influence on larger project outcomes. We invite such research to build on the findings of this study.
