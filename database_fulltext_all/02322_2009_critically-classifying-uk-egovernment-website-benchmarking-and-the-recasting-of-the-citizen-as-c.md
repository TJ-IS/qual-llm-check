# Critically classifying: UK e-government website benchmarking and the recasting of the citizen as customer

Benjamin Mosse\* & Edgar A. Whitley\*<sup>,†</sup>

\*Information Systems and Innovation Group, Department of Management, London School of Economics and Political Science, Houghton Street, London WC2A 2AE, UK, email benjaminmosse@mac.com, <sup>†</sup>email: e.a.whitley@lse.ac.uk

Abstract. In recent years, discussion of the provision of government services has paid particular attention to notions of customer choice and improved service delivery. However, there appears to be marked shift in the relationship between the citizen and the state moving from government being responsive to the needs of citizens to viewing citizens explicitly as customers. This paper argues that this change is being accelerated by government use of techniques like benchmarking, which have been widely used in the private sector. To illustrate this point, the paper focuses on the adoption of website benchmarking techniques by the public sector. The paper argues that the essence of these benchmarking technologies, a process comprised of both finding and producing truth, is fundamentally based on the act of classifying and draws on Martin Heidegger’s etymological enquiry to reinterpret classification as a dynamic movement towards order that both creates and obfuscates truth. In so doing, it demonstrates how Heidegger’s seminal ideas can be adapted for critical social research by showing that technology is more than an instrument as it has epistemic implications for what counts as truth. This stance is used as the basis for understanding empirical work reporting on a UK government website benchmarking project. Our analysis identifies the means involved in producing the classifications inherent in such benchmarking projects and relates these to the more general move that is recasting the relationship between the citizen and the state, and increasingly blurring the boundaries between the state and the private sector. Recent developments in other attempts by the UK government to use private-sector technologies and approaches indicate ways in which this move might be challenged.

Keywords: benchmarking, e-government, classification, Heidegger, Ge-stell; New Public Management, critical social research

## 1. INTRODUCTION

Governments play a key role in the lives of most citizens throughout the world. In the case of western, economically advanced economies such as those found in Europe and North America, there is a general sense of government acting on behalf of its citizens by providing or supporting various services. Although each country has its own particular form of government, with varying levels of government intervention in, for example, health care provision and regulation of industry, there is a general focus on public service inherent in all government activities.

Information technology (IT) has long played a major role in the support of government services. For example, large-scale computer systems were first introduced in government bodies around the world from the early 1960s, including in many developing economies (Odedra, 1990). More particularly, it was recognized from the early 1970s that computer systems had the potential to radically transform the ways in which governments provided services (Simon, 1973; Weizenbaum, 1976). However, for a variety of reasons outlined by Dunleavy et al. (2006, ch. 1), IT has remained relatively poorly understood and theorized in terms of its role on the transformation of government.

The emergence of internet technologies and networked computers in the late 1990s has seen an increasingly important role for technology in government in relation to the buzzword of e-government, and this effect has been heightened by experimentation with the ideas of New Public Management (Barzelay, 1992; Osborne & Gaebler, 1992; Hood, 1996; Bevir et al., 2003). Both of these factors have made government more sensitive and responsive to the needs of its citizens, often using the metaphor of citizen as customer to emphasize the need for government to provide a level of service that these ‘customers’ are familiar with in the private sector.

Of particular interest has been the widespread use of e-government websites to allow governments to inform and interact with their citizens. and there has been a marked increase in case studies reporting on the successful introduction (or not) of new, web-based, technologies by governmental agencies (e.g. Kaylor et al., 2001; Carter & Bélanger, 2005; Moon & Norris, 2005; Kumar & Best, 2006; Pan et al., 2006). However, most of these studies focus on traditional management concerns of effective systems implementation and adoption, rather than reflecting on the consequences that the introduction of these technologies may have on the relationship between the citizen and the state.

The purpose of this paper, therefore, is to reflect on the ways in which IT is changing the relationship between the citizen and the state (see. for example. Leith & Morrison. 2004: West 2004; Ciborra, 2005). It does this by focussing on one particular technique that has been adopted by the UK government, namely the benchmarking of government websites, in order to reach government mandated targets for providing services online for ‘customers’. That is, we argue that benchmarking government websites against the private sector is helping shift the relationship between citizen and the state to one of the customer and the state. Website benchmarking might be understood as the simple classification of various websites according to widely understood and accepted criteria; however, as Klein & Hirschheim (2001) note, there are inevitably competing design ideals underlying any such activity. As a result, the design ideal of the citizen as customer might not be the most appropriate one for particular policy or social welfare goals. Thus, this paper argues that the process of classification is a dynamic movement towards order that both creates and obfuscates truth and hides the processes by which this choice of design ideal came to dominate.

It makes this argument by drawing on Heideggerian phenomenology and in particular Heidegger’s concept of Ge-stell and the idea that the essence of technology contains both danger and opportunity for action. Heidegger’s work is often criticized for its poor conceptu alization of power and limited critique of the status quo. This paper demonstrates how a Heideggerian analysis can be applied to critical social research in an innovative way.

The structure of the rest of the paper is as follows. The next section introduces the motivation for this research and places it in the context of critical research found in information systems. This is followed by a section that reviews how the ideas of Martin Heidegger have been applied to the study of information technologies, highlighting his notion of the essence of technology before briefly reviewing questions of website benchmarking and classification more generally. The paper then introduces the empirical research method and the case study of a review of benchmarking processes for UK government websites. After reviewing the consequences of the benchmarking process and the design of websites the paper ends with a discussion of benchmarking technologies viewed through the Heideggerian lens and considers them within the broader move to recast the citizen as customer.

## 2. MOTIVATION FOR THE STUDY

It has become increasingly common for governments to present their ability to deliver services by comparing themselves to the best practice in the private sector. For example, during a debate in the House of Commons about the government’s proposals to introduce a large-scale, biometrically-based national identity cards scheme (see Beynon-Davies, 2006; Whitley et al., 2007), the then Home Secretary Charles Clarke told Parliament, in response to concerns about the government’s ability to deliver IT schemes on time and on budget, tha

only this year, the Comparisat benchmark survey, run by FDS International, surveyed a wide range of organizations based on their customer satisfaction and what was going on. Number one on that list was the UK Passport Service (the agency that would have responsibility fo introducing the ID cards), which was followed by Asda, eBay, Amazon, Virgin Mobile, Morrisons, Tesco, the Driver and Vehicle Licensing Agency, the Post Office, Boots and ScottishPower. I cite that example because it demonstrates that major public service projects, and even Home Office public service projects can, through investment in technology for massive schemes, beat the world – both the public and private sector – in the service that they offer to people. That is a tribute to what can be done. (Hansard 28 June 2005, Column 1170)

The research problem that this paper addresses is therefore to understand how IT is implicated in the changing nature of government and its relationship with the citizen such that it has become acceptable and accepted for politicians to compare themselves directly with the private sector using phrases like customer satisfaction.

The idea of being responsive to citizens, in a similar manner to which traditional businesses respond to their customers, has been around since the 1970s and has been proposed as an alternative to the traditional models of bureaucracy found in the public sector (Barzelay, 1992; Osborne & Gaebler, 1992; Dunleavy et al., 2006).

The initial logic was simply one of recognizing that government should become more ‘customer facing’ (Pegnato, 1997), adopting many of the techniques of business (Box, 1999). In the United Kingdom, despite (or perhaps because of) over a decade a Thatcherite reforms, the incoming Labour government published a report on modernizing government (The Cabine Office, 1999), which spoke of government departments and agencies needing to be ‘sensitive to their customers’.

Within public administration, a number of authors have questioned the extent to which such metaphors are appropriate for government and the limits of casting the citizen as a customer (Schachter, 1995; Fountain, 2001b; Ho, 2002); however, other authors have readily accepted this approach and encompass it directly into their own research (e.g. Layne & Lee, 2001).

One of the key insights offered by critical researchers has been the need to make explicit the assumptions that underlie all forms of life and, as appropriate, challenge these assumptions by making alternative perspectives visible and supportable. For example, Foucault has argued that

the real political task in a society such as ours is to criticize the working of institutions which appear to be both neutral and independent; to criticize them in such a manner that the political violence which has always exercised itself obscurely through them will be unmasked, so that one can fight them (quoted in Rabinow (1984)).

This need for careful understanding is particularly the case when dealing with assumptions and ‘operational’ decisions that are accepted without a due process of consultation and discussion (Latour, 2004). One case of such an assumption is the use of the metaphor of the citizen as a customer.

Similarly, Neil Postman (1992) argues that ‘embedded in every tool is an ideological bias, a predisposition to construct the world as one thing rather than another, to value one thing over another, to amplify one sense or skill or attitude more loudly than another’ (p. 13). In this case, IT is one of the most prolific tools. More particularly, technology ‘has usurped powers and enforced mind-sets that a fully attentive culture might have wished to deny it. Thus, an examination of the ideas embedded in computer technologies is worth attempting’ (p. 107).

This paper addresses the calls by Postman and Foucault by providing a novel examination of the ‘political violence’ hidden in recasting the citizen as a customer. It does this by exploring one small part of government IT, namely the means used to evaluate UK governmen websites.

The choice of benchmarking of government websites was empirically driven as it was in this area that the first author (BM) undertook an internship as part of his MSc studies. Indeed, the research presented in this paper did not originate as a traditional critical project; the authors were not expecting the work on website benchmarking to reveal as many insights about the recasting of government’s relationship with citizens as it did.

As a result, the fieldwork was not designed around, for example, explicit emancipatory principles that characterize much critical research (see, for example, Janson & Cecez Kecmanovic, 2005, for an exemplary case of a research project that was explicitly designed around principles of critical research and where this focus shaped the research design, data collection and analysis). Instead, this analysis of benchmarking of government websites emerged as a result of discussions between the authors and on reflection of the applicability of a particular theoretical perspective (taught on the MSc by EAW). Further details of the research approach adopted are presented below.

We feel that inequalities exist throughout the system and critical social research should not focus solely on the ‘bottom layer’ of society but rather can address many levels. Moreover, the dominance of private-sector evaluation methods for e-government websites also has direct implications on the users of public services, many of whom are the traditionally ‘less powerful in society and many of whom rely on the public sector to enhance their situation (e.g. Kvasny & Keil, 2002; Cushman & Klecun, 2006). For example, those seeking the best local statefunded school, those unable to avail themselves of privately funded healthcare or those who need local provision of welfare benefits are likely to require resources provided by the public sector and hence may draw upon information provided on government websites.

## 3. THEORETICAL CONCEPTS

## 3.1 Heidegger on technology

The theoretical perspective adopted in this research is Heideggerian phenomenology and, in particular, Heidegger’s work on the essence of technology. The choice of Heideggerian phenomenology is not an immediately obvious one for a piece of critical research. Heidegger’s relationship with the Nazis in Germany is unclear and hence problematic. Although it occurred before he wrote the main text we draw on in this paper, it has caused many to question the use of his philosophy for any emancipatory processes. There is no simple way to address both his relationship with the Nazis particularly while he was University Rector at Freiburg and his philosophical contribution (Lyotard, 1990). We note that Heidegger’s thinking has been incred ibly influential on much western thinking, and proved to be a major driver of the later work of Foucault (Willcocks, 2006), yet Heidegger himself is often chastised for not being critical of the status quo and of omitting power issues, including structural ones, except for warning of the influence of norms and the normal/appropriate way to do things. Heidegger’s discussion of the influence of norms is often translated in terms of ‘the They’, but Dreyfus (1991, ch. 8) suggests that this translation is misleading as it suggests that I am distinguished from Them. He prefers the appeal to normativity implied by the translation ‘one’, as in ‘one pays one’s taxes’ and the need to conform that is associated with it (cf. e.g. Foucault, 1977).

With Foucault himself becoming an increasingly important author in critical information systems research (Zuboff, 1988; Brooke, 2002; McGrath, 2003; Introna & Whittaker, 2004;

Klecun, 2004; Willcocks, 2006), it becomes apparent that Heidegger’s intellectual presence can be found implicitly in much critical research.

The aspect of Heidegger’s thinking that we draw upon in this paper argues against a simple means–ends theorizing of modern technology. This encourages us to move beyond simple instrumentality in our arguments and hence, as Foucault argued in the quotation given above, ‘to criticize the working of institutions which appear to be both neutral and independent (Rabinow, 1984).

Heidegger attempted a general account of the essence of modern technology (Heidegger, 1993). Heidegger was interested in the products and tools that came from the natural sciences and the new technocratic thinking and styles of practice that emerged from them (Willcocks, 2006), thus reflecting in part the social imagery of technology that existed at that time (Hughes, 2004).

Heidegger’s phenomenological approach has already been introduced into enquiries about information systems, management studies, electronic meeting systems, strategy, the internet and infrastructure (Dreyfus, 1982; Introna, 1997; Brown & Lightfoot, 1998; Ciborra & Hanseth, 1998; Whitley & Introna, 1998; Introna, 1999; Whitley, 1999; Dreyfus, 2001; Ciborra, 2002; Brigham & Introna, 2006; Chia & Holt, 2006) with most drawing on his analysis of the ‘essence of technology’.

In The Question Concerning Technology, Heidegger’s enquiry engages with technology on the following levels: (a) technology as instrument; (b) technology as a truth-revealing process; and (c) the essence of technology itself. We focus here on the essence of technology and its effect on the truth-revealing process. Heidegger rejects the essence of technology as being instrumental in nature. He redefines the essence of technology as a way of accessing truth, as ‘correctness of representation’ (Heidegger, 1993). Further, technology, as deriving from the Greek techne¯ ‘art’ or ‘craft’, implies this revealing in its own name. The artist or craftsman has the knowledge or skill to create an object; he gives it form. But technology is more than this simple form creation. It asks for a payback because rather than being a way of revealing truth, technology is also a mode by which knowledge is disseminated. Heidegger claims that the essence of technology is Ge-stell, a process of enframing that defines objects by their function (Heidegger, 1993). Heidegger often made unique uses of existing terms. Thus, Ge-stell is often hyphenated to emphasize the root (‘stellen’, to set down, to put) of the word alongside its links to bestellen (ordering), vorstellen (represent), nachstellen (to entrap) etc. (see Heidegger, 1993, esp. footnotes 14, 15). Although the unhyphenated version is commonly used in information systems publications, we use the hyphenated version here to emphasize the unique use Heidegger made of this term. In this process, man is not the master, controlling the technology, but finds himself conditioned by this enframing, a tendency that might be heightened further if coupled with the top-down authority attributed to government agencies.

However, for Heidegger, Ge-stell is not simply revealing and ordering; the essence of technology contains danger (Gefahr). By revealing selectively, the enframing process not only reveals but also conceals the truth. Ge-stell is then a revealing towards something other than truth. Each onion skin removed reveals the truth of the function of the onion skin to cover ye another skin, ad infinitum. According to Ciborra and Hanseth, the danger of Ge-stell is ‘in the fact that Ge-stell delivers representations of all that subsists, and these become the real world (Ciborra & Hanseth, 1998). The danger is that we live uncritically, conditioned by and playing a part in the truth that the essence of technology creates.

In this way, Brown & Lightfoot (1998) note that ‘The obvious danger here is that once under way, enframing is destined to also “set-on“ and reveal humans themselves as forms of standing-reserve’ (p. 300) or as ‘fuel’ for the process of enframing. They also highlight the ‘anxiety’ it can cause but argue that it is ‘something to be explored rather than managed out of existence’ (p. 303).

The idea of humans, as well as technology, acting as a form of standing reserve is highlighted in the context of nursing action by Benner (2000) who echoes these warnings and quotes Heidegger:

If man is challenged, ordered . . . then does not man himself belong even more originally than nature within the standing reserve? The current talk about human resources, about the supply of patients for a clinic, gives evidence of this (Heidegger, 1993, p. 323).

She uses this insight to ‘recover non-managerial, non-enframing ways of being with patients in order to reveal what was being lost by the informatics approach to medical planning and decision making’ (Benner, 2000, p. 298)

Brigham & Introna (2006) also emphasize that through Ge-stell a possibility can open up for acting otherwise, echoing Foucault’s call for action and noting that Ge-stell is a technological mood ‘that apprehends the world as something to be ordered and shaped in line with our projects, intentions and desires’ (p. 143).

In the next sections we review the literature on the process of website benchmarking and classification and then seek to understand it within the context of our understanding of Heidegger’s notion of Ge-stell.

## 3.2 Benchmarking and classification

With the increasing popularity of the internet, website-benchmarking technologies have become progressively more valuable statistical tools of analysis. No longer can a website justify itself merely by being a website – the bottom line is apparent: web projects must reach concrete goals and prove their investment. This sense of value is particularly apparent in the private sector where, in order to assess and evaluate websites, complex benchmarking methodologies have been developed (Smithson, 1999; Barnes & Vidgen, 2000).

Benchmarking is a process whereby an organization evaluates its operations by comparison to similar organizations. The aim of the benchmark is usually to establish best practice by examining and emulating other organizations’ strong points and generally does not readily change with the environment (Ansell et al., 2003, p. 340). One of the espoused benefits of benchmarking is the way it forces organizations to reflect on their own performance in relation to those of other organizations in the same sector, although it carries a danger of implying an ‘ideal form’.

According to Bowerman & Ball (2000), benchmarking style techniques have regularly been used by government bodies, although often under different labels (see also Dluhy & Swartz, 2006). However, more recent uses of benchmarking have allowed government bodies to compare their performance against alternatives. seeking out best value' solutions without the cost and complication of full competitive tendering for services (Bowerman et al., 2001). As such, it reflects on the inherent tensions between centralized control (there is a standard performance level that we should all expect) and localized improvisation (this level of service is sufficient for our purposes).

The public sector has lagged behind in developing its own methodologies for website benchmarking (Kaylor et al., 2001). In the United Kingdom, the development of website benchmarking was marked by a considerable sense of urgency that arose from the government’s pledge to make all government services available electronically by 2005 (The Cabinet Office, 2002b), a goal that was broadly met on time.

Benchmarking can be seen as one example of classification: an organization benchmarks its performance in relation to other organizations and is able to classify its performance as better than or worse than its comparators. Ever since Aristotle drew up his classification of nature based on dichotomies, mankind has been involved in a desire to reach an understanding of the perceived systems that order life. A tension exists between the perception of classification as discoverable entity and as created artefact. In so far as classifications can be understood to be human creations, they are inherently political (Suchman, 1994).

In the information systems literature, classification systems have, until recently, received scant attention although they are widely studied elsewhere. For example, librarians and information scientists have long studied ‘all aspects of bibliographic organization’ (Cataloging and classification quarterly. 2006). Within information systems ideas of classification have typically been based on the premise of classification as a sorting into a fixed structure. Typica criticisms of this approach (e.g. Janssen et al., 2004; Kunstelj & Vintar, 2004) focus on what elements should be within the classification scheme used, rather than on the nature of the classification scheme itself (Ansell et al., 2003). Other users of classification more broadly (e.g. the quality management literature) are moving towards more dynamic views of the classification process (e.g. through the adoption of concepts like continuous improvement).

Regardless of one’s perspective on the ‘realness’ of the ‘real world’, language plays a key role in how the world is broken up and identified, i.e. classified (Wynn et al., 2003), as there is ‘no natural set of categories’ (Kent, 1978, p. 13). As Searle (1995) usefully points out, there is a distinction between statements of fact, that require the institution of language, and facts stated which exist independently of any such institution (p. 27). Thus cats and dogs are different animals, but it is only through institutions such as language that we are able to differentiate between cats and dogs (where the categories might be feline vs. canine), or between tractors and cats-and-dogs (where the categories might be inanimate and animate objects).

Lakoff & Johnson (1980) also discuss the role of language and metaphors in classifying and understanding the world, drawing on, among others, the work of Wittgenstein and Sapir and Whorf. They note that ‘for human beings, categorization is primarily a means of comprehending the world, and as such it must serve that purpose in a sufficiently flexible way’ (p. 122). Their work on metaphors has been applied extensively in the area of systems development and implementation (e.g. Hirschheim & Newman, 1991; Kendall & Kendall, 1993), but even these authors still tend to see the resulting classifications as relatively stable.

Bowker & Star’s (1999) definition of classification has been widely used in the information systems literature (Andersen, 2001; D’Ambrosio, 2001; Sørensen & Lundh-Snis, 2001) and acts as a starting point for our enquiry. Here classification is ‘a spatial, temporal or spatiotem poral segmentation of the world’ which ideally exhibits three defining characteristics. These characteristics are the (a) uniqueness; (b) exclusivity; and (c) completeness of the classifica tion criteria.

Once a classification has been created, one has to distinguish the level to which it is standardized. Classifications may be either informal or formal (Bowker & Star, 1999). Informal classifications are the subjective, individual and dynamic means we use to operate in the world. In being dynamic, they are often visible, that is to say available and open to purposeful change. Formalized classifications, conversely, aim to ‘provide a means of access to informa tion across time and space’ (Bowker & Star, 1999), which is simply a means of decontextualizing a classification. Formal classifications may show degrees of institutionalization or standardization where they disappear into the background, turning into an invisible frame (Bowker & Star, 1999), or as Heidegger would argue, simply being ready-to-hand (Winograd & Flores, 1986).

## 3.3 Synthesis: using Heidegger to make sense of benchmarking and classification

This paper seeks to add a further dimension to our understanding of classification to that provided by Bowker and Star’s definition. We argue that classifying is an active process: it is an action and is performed with intent. It is this intent that stresses the dynamism implied in classification. The etymology of ‘classification’ may serve to illuminate this second dimension of the term. As was seen above, etymological analysis has often been applied by Heidegger to highlight distinctions that are perhaps not always obvious on first reading (see, for example, Introna’s [1997] discussion of the difference between being in-love and in-a-room, etc., and Ciborra’s radical reconstruction of the notion of finding oneself in a situation or a mood [Ciborra, 2002, ch. 8]).

According to the Oxford English Dictionary, classification belongs to modern terminology, dating to the 1790’s French word ‘classifier’, which, literally, describes the placing into classes. Class originates from the Latin ‘classis’, a word with which to divide the population of Rome fo taxation purposes. Both the Latin and the French seem to underline Bowker and Star’s position, as they stress the fixity of the classificatory divisioning system.

Instead of a static compartmentalization, classification turns into a process or movement towards order. As described above, according to Heidegger, the essence of technology is an ordering and a way of accessing the truth. That is, the essence of technology orders how we think and how we act.

The German terms die Zuordnung or die Gliederung point towards a further level of meaning implied in classification and the act of classifying. The preposition zu combines the ideas of ‘to’, ‘to add’, and ‘towards’ while Ordnung is similar to the English idea of an ‘order’, ‘arrangement’, ‘organization’, or ‘system’. The prefix zu adds the sense of movement and mobility to the idea of order. In comparison, die Gliederung literally means ‘the placing into a chain, or structure’. Similarly, it ascribes a level of agency or intention, instead of focusing on the static nature of order. We may therefore think of the act of classification as a dynamic arranging or ordering of data in order to make sense of the world and thus enable action and, subsequently, further arrangement.

Traditionally, a classification system is seen as ‘a set of boxes (metaphorical or literal) into which things can be put to then do some kind of work – bureaucratic or knowledge production (Bowker & Star, 1999). Underlying such a set of boxes are choices of how best to represen reality or truth. In contrast, in this paper we explore the action of classifying and do so with reference to Heidegger’s understanding of the essence of technology outlined previously. From this combination we see that these choices are seen as both the product of previous classifications and precursors to further classifications, highlighting both the dangers and opportunities of classification and benchmarking technologies as indicated by the notion o Ge-stell.

In thinking about the essence of technology, certain parallels between the functioning of classification and Ge-stell become apparent. A rare meaning of the Greek tecnologia as a systematic treatment (Oxford English Dictionary, 1989) points to this inherent linkage.

This highlights one of the key insights of Heidegger’s thinking for this paper. As technology is based on the act of classification, so too is the essence of technology dependent on classification to produce frames of truth (cf. Foucault, 1977). However, the act of classifying is necessarily imperfect, it is never just an access to the objective reality, but always implies the multiple revisions of knowledge that have gone into the creation of any classification throughout its past; it carries the infinite past of shifting categories and the potential of future realities it will create in each act of classification. It is the way the process of enframing that the website benchmarking process entails and how this affects the potential future realities that is of particular concern for our discussion of the changing relationship between the citizen and the state.

When we apply Heidegger’s insights with the discussion of classification and benchmarking, we can see how these two literatures complement each other. ## 4. RESEARCH METHOD AND CASE DESCRIPTION

The empirical work described in this paper draws upon the experiences of one of the authors (BM) who had the opportunity to do an internship during his MSc studies in 2003 with the main

UK government body responsible for, among many other informational operations, UK gov ernment websites. As part of this work, he was involved in a project (the 2003 review) that reviewed the benchmarking of government sites; thus, he was a member of the review group given the responsibility for reviewing the process of evaluating government websites (a meta-analysis) rather than the actual review of particular sites (low-level analysis).

As was discussed earlier, this internship was not intended as a critical research project. Nevertheless, the first author (BM) attended most of the meetings that took place on this issue between June and August 2003 and had access to both the official reports and studies (e.g. The Cabinet Office, 2001; Porter Research, 2002) as well as the thinking and perspectives provided by the staff actually undertaking the 2003 review and redesigning the websites. Thus, he had access to all the documentation associated with the process, including internal reports, minutes and presentations. By attending and participating in the meetings he had, as Fairclough (1992) suggests, knowledge of what was available and how to get access to it, as well as having a deep understanding of the institution he was researching and the processes of change it was undergoing (p. 227). At this time, following the dot-com boom and bust, a large proportion of the staff had previously worked for private-sector website developers and their mindsets were very different from those of traditional, career civil-servants. Through informal interactions during coffee breaks and after work, a real sense of these contrasting views on the role of public-sector website developers emerged.

The website benchmarking project was part of a programme of e-government which at that time was a key element of the UK government policy to promote and drive initiatives that aim to ‘ensure that the country, its citizens, and its businesses derive the maximum benefit from ICT’ (The Cabinet Office, 2001) and ‘Technology alone does not transform government, but government cannot transform to meet modern citizens’ expectations without it’ (The Cabinet Office, 2005).

Four guiding requirements or principles of UK e-government had been developed and feed directly into the benchmarking process that was being implemented. These are to (a) build services around citizens’ choices; (b) make government and its services more accessible; (c) work towards social inclusion; and (d) use information better. The government has an ongoing commitment to ensuring access to the internet, i.e. seeing the web as an important channe within which to develop online services (The Cabinet Office, 2002b). In this context, use and take-up were identified as key components to judge the success of the initiatives (The Cabinet Office, 2001). However, despite government spending about £14 billion a year on new and existing IT and related services by 2003, and directly employing about 50 000 professionals in the field (The Cabinet Office, 2005), take-up of websites had been disappointing and, when measuring individual usage of government websites across Europe. the United Kingdom lags behind many European countries (The Cabinet Office, 2002b; Dunleavy et al., 2006).

During the 2003 review of the benchmarking process, a number of example sites were discussed. This paper draws upon two of the cases that were discussed by the 2003 review group and is based on notes and internal documents from the meetings where these websites were discussed, plus later assessment of these websites by the second author (EAW). These cases were seen by the 2003 review group as being representative of the range of issues with e-government websites that the benchmarking process needed to address. One was a very successful website, which had clearly benefited from the existing website benchmarking processes, the second was less successful although the current version was far better than earlier ones and hence was felt to have benefited, in some way, from previous reviews.

While many student dissertations based on such internships would simply review the tasks that were undertaken against the extant literature on, for example, website design, this particular experience resulted in the first author (BM) reflecting more deeply on the process of evaluating government websites. Noting their use of criteria used by the private sector, he drew on Heideggerian phenomenology (that had been previously taught by the second author (EAW) on a specialist MSc option) to guide this process of reflection. The resulting hermeneutic process of understanding (Klein & Myers, 1999) led to the analysis that is presented in this paper and the realization that the process of government benchmarking of websites was moving beyond the task of improving the quality of the websites and was shaping potential future realities that could affect the relationship between the citizen and the state.

## 5.2 Analysis of benchmarking process in terms of Heidegger’s insights

Once the Department succeeded as the top-ranked website in the benchmarking exercise it embarked on a further benchmarking project in order to continue to meet the needs of their key professional audiences while attracting more usage of the site by the ordinary citizen. The aims of this further benchmark were to (a) independently evaluate their online offering in comparison to other relevant organizations; (b) develop an understanding of best practice in online areas, with special reference to their target socio-economic groups; (c) use this understanding to drive future strategy and planning; and (d) feed into and develop an implementation plan for recommended changes. The strands of the proposed research were recently expanded in a process of consultation to move beyond the purely website-oriented benchmark. The senior management wanted these strands to focus primarily on strategy in an effort to help guide and plan excellence. The website benchmark project would not only include an audit of ‘search, information architecture and navigation of benchmarked organizations’ but also aim to reach consensus about best practice in the strands identified. While the project is progressing slowly and at the time of the fieldwork was at agency tender stage, it relies on benchmarking the web offerings against a sample of 12–15 organizations drawn from the public and private sectors both in the United Kingdom and abroad. The benchmark organizations had been identified by senior management as a selection of their most admired sites (most of these being in the private sector).

## 6. DISCUSSION

This section investigates the unquestioned assumptions that inform the choice of criteria in the benchmarking process undertaken and relate them to the broader questions of e-governmen and classification.

The Department, in its benchmark criteria, aims to reach an understanding of excellence based on analysing other organizations’ online strategies. The classification is also based on a means of extending the benchmark into an exercise in evaluation in order to measure the organization’s website in strategic terms. In basing the choice of benchmark comparators on a list drawn up by the organization, the political realm of the project is revealed. In benchmarking against private-sector organizations, the implicit subtext is that government and private sector can be evaluated together and that meaningful comparisons can be made. The regulative effect of the classification is thus seen as a means of introducing insights gained in the private sector into the Department.

In thinking of benchmarking in this context as a creation or production of truth, benchmarking is similar to the fisherman’s net in Gunnar Olssen’s ‘Fisherman’s Problem’ (Olsson, 2002): that the fisherman’s net catches real fish is not in doubt, however the fisherman (whose nets don’t capture those creatures living below its lowest depth or those creatures too small to be caugh in the knots) is not in a position to discuss life in the oceans, only that life that is caught by the nets. The benchmark, like the net, plays a double function that reveals and also conceals the real world (Crampton, 2003).

The language of e-government has frequently been cast in the ideas developed by New Public Management (even if there is no single idea of New Public Management; Hood, 1996; Bevir et al., 2003) whereby management culture emphasizes the central position occupied by the individual (Fountain, 2001a; Ciborra, 2005). In the case of the UK government, this requires the adoption of many market mechanisms such as transparency of information and the supply of services according to demand. This is often problematic. For example, the concept of quasi-markets in the UK’s National Health Service was scrapped in the late 1990s as a result of the logistical and cognitive requirements of having to ‘price’ over 15 000 different hospital procedures which led to a huge relative expansion of back office administrators rather than frontline staff providing patient treatment (Dunleavy et al., 2005).

One of the noticeable consequences of this shift to the individual-centred state is the recasting of the citizen as customer. Thus, the Department’s website targets its advice according to the kind of customer you are: adult learners, children and families, higher education, local authorities, learning and skills, nursery owners, school governors, students, employers, head teachers, teachers, young people, learners, early years, parents and, perhaps surprisingly, the IT industry. The benchmark project reinforces this shift in citizen to customer both explicitly and implicitly (Steyart, 2004).

While the metaphorical recasting of citizens as customers is understandable in terms of the need to provide more responsive services, the consequences and dangers of this re-identification are manifold. Fundamentally, the idea of citizen as customer is embedded within the idea of government acting in a market. Laws of supply and demand and consumer choice are not possible in a governmental context (Ciborra, 2005). The idea of equality, sacrosanct in the public sector, is not possible in a market environment.

Similarly, the policy of e-government electronic service progression in the United Kingdom (the process of moving along the gradient from inform to interact and finally transact) assumes and makes explicit this customer model of democracy (Bellamy & Taylor, 1998; The Cabinet Office, 2002b). This becomes apparent in the choice of language throughout each of the reports at stake here. In the extreme case, the private sector’s web presence is equated with an ideal in efficiency and accessibility, while the public sector marks itself out by lengthy texts and obscure language. Thus, the 2003 review group drew on a report that declared that ‘few (government websites) seem to recognize that the vast majority of visitors to any Government site have experience of sites outside the Government arena, and are used to a professional, user-friendly, inviting and altogether satisfying Web site environment’ (Porter Research, 2002). The private-sector website environment is seen as the benchmark fulfilling the criteria that government should include on their websites. That is to say, the private sector is ‘professional, user friendly, inviting and altogether satisfying’ in their online services. The private sector becomes a model for the public sector’s web presence; it produces the truth for the classification of e-government websites.

As presented so far, benchmarking is consistent with aspects of new public management and the metaphorical recasting of the citizen as customer. However, Heidegger’s Ge-stell warns us against living uncritically, conditioned by and playing a part in the truth that the essence of technology creates. The benchmark under examination (Porter Research, 2002)

references the e-Envoy’s own guidelines (The Cabinet Office, 2002b). In defining which criteria to base the benchmark on, it identifies top performing websites and defines best practice recommendations for the future. The benchmark is the aspiration towards an ideal model of all a website should be, based on a set of unchallenged criteria, seeing the world as ‘something to be ordered and shaped in line with our projects, intentions and designs’ (Brigham & Introna, 2006, p. 143). Once agreed, the criteria for benchmarking become a formal classification: within the bounds of the report, these criteria are not available for discussion, questioning or justification. The report by Porter Research (2002) presents its criteria as unproblematic, as discovering the ideal format of website presentation, as discovering truth; there is no generic ‘best’ structure for a website and so any ideal is part of the production of truth. This does not sufficiently problematize the fact that criteria are not just created ad hoc but that they carry a historical frame within which the ideal criteria were developed, a frame that in this case is firmly rooted in the private sector.

Every act of classification towards benchmarking best practice is thus not a classification from first principles but rather a latest step, drawing to it elements from the classifications that came before. In the case of the Prime Minister’s website, for example, the site is reworked to be consistent with best practice norms based on a progression. Similarly, the classification making up the benchmark includes an understanding of what makes up a ‘good’ classification and the implicit basis of the classification is to inform the production of websites to meet this truth.

A nice illustration of this regulative effect is given in Steyart’s (2004) review of electronic government services. This survey adapts an existing private-sector model developed by Watson et al. (2000) and uncritically adopts five ‘performance indicators’ including ‘customer satisfaction’. The results are used ‘to help understand consumer behaviour at government sites’ and the paper proposes that ‘Government leaders should use a checklist in planning, designing, and maintaining their e-service sites to take into consideration the customer’s perspective’ (p. 374). In this way, Steyart illustrates Heidegger’s point about the enframing of the potential of future realities. What was once contentious now becomes an accepted input for future research and action.

The Department’s project also shows how the standards inherent in benchmarking have shifted from online to offline, from the mainly technical elements of a website to the strategy for management of the whole department. The Department’s project is a means to develop bes practice further and build on the high standards developed in the report. The relationship between the two projects demonstrates that while the classificatory principles at first are explici (The Cabinet Office, 2002b) they then become invisible as the principles become institutionalized and pass from the informal to the formal modes of classification. These invisible principles in turn act to create a frame with which the government benchmarks and hence drives innovation. The government is not immune from this rolling out of classification. No longer, for instance, is the Department intent on a benchmark of its website; the Department now aims to benchmark their management strategy in the same way. The Department classifies itself and is classified building best practice and becoming part of the framework of best practice based on a classification that draws upon assumptions that the citizen as customer is the priority. What started as a technical matter is now shaping the potential of future realities.

## 7. CONCLUSIONS

This paper has revealed assumptions about the introduction of website benchmarking that ‘a fully attentive culture’ might wish to challenge. In particular, it has shown how the technique of website benchmarking, drawn directly from the private sector, is being accepted and used in government, both for the design of websites and more generally in UK government. By using Heidegger’s idea of Ge-stell and applying it to the concept of benchmarking as a form of classification and truth production, this essence of technology reveals an intentional process of moving towards order which views the citizen as customer. This view, implicit in the benchmarking process, becomes more than a metaphor for improving the responsiveness of government services and shapes future realities in government practice far beyond website development This is not to claim that benchmarking is in and of itself wrong, but rather that benchmarking and the transformation of classification that it entails is more important and therefore more needing of fuller evaluation before acceptance than the simple goal of improving websites might imply.

## 7.1 Contributions to critical social research

This paper has made three distinct contributions to the application of critical social research within information systems. First, it has demonstrated the ways in which Heidegger’s notion o Ge-stell can be used in critical social research. With Heidegger often criticized for not address ing structural power issues, this paper shows how his insights can be applied to address power issues through the enframing effect of technology affecting the potential of future realities.

Second, it has demonstrated that critical social research can contribute to our understanding of all levels of society, not simply those ‘on the bottom rung’ where issues of emancipation and exploitation are immediately apparent. Inequality exists at many levels, as in this case between government and the private sector.

Finally, the paper has demonstrated the ways in which metaphors about treating citizens like customers can become wholesale mechanisms for recasting the role of the citizen in relation to the state. Such an analysis, however, is not necessarily as pessimistic as might be first thought and the opportunities that technology opens up for change are discussed in the next subsection.

## 7.2 Implications for change

Any research that seeks to contribute to the critical project must do more than simply identify problems with the status quo; it must offer alternative agendas for action. Here we step back from the particularities of website benchmarking to the broader debate about government’s use of IT. Rather than viewing IT deterministically, here we seek to use the very features of the technological Ge-stell as a means of change.

Former Conservative MP, Chris Patten has recently claimed that politicians have no grasp of technology (Espiner, 2006), and there is a growing evidence of a backlash against the use of external consultants by government to ‘implement’ and support whatever policies it wants.

For example, in a later Parliamentary debate about the identity cards proposals described above, rebel labour MP Frank Dobson suggested that

IT systems companies such as EDS and Siemens appear to be competing for the title of intergalactic rip-off IT merchant of the decade, and have ripped off the public and private sectors time and again through their negligence, incompetence and stupidity, usually resulting in massive costs increases for users and huge delays. (Hansard 13 February 2006, Column 1221)

Similarly, Craig & Brooks (2006) in a scathing book entitled Plundering the Public Sector, warn of the increasingly uncritical use of external consultants by government to build its computer systems. However, in a neat twist, the IT consulting industry is also becoming increasingly recalcitrant about being enrolled into the government’s plans. For example, although the government made much of its close relationship with Intellect, the trade body for the UK IT industry, Intellect later attacked the government for its lack of openness about its plans for the scheme (Williams, 2006), warning that the industry was concerned about its own reputation that might arise from poorly thought through government plans (Science & Technology Select Committee, 2006).

Thus as noted above, Ge-stell offers both dangers and opportunities for change.

## 7.3 Future research

This research can be taken forward in a number of different ways. Most straightforwardly, this concept of classification, drawn from Heidegger’s ‘essence of technology’ can be taken and applied to other areas, particularly where technology is shaping the classification processes in society. For example, many websites and online mail services offer targeted advertising based on, for example, the content of messages posted or other profile information (Introna & Nissenbaum, 2000). Similar processes are being implemented around passenger flight data, determining who is classified as safe to fly (Hosein, 2005). These systems are not using an existing classification but are instead producing classifications and the approach described in this paper can help reveal this process.

Further research can also be undertaken by seeking to study the discussions and deliberations that surround the introduction of new practices, like the use of benchmarking for both websites and management practices more generally. There is scope for intervention if such debates show limited consideration or deliberation of the consequences of these practices. The proposals in Klein & Hirschheim (2001) offer a starting point for articulating and considering such alternative design ideals.

Finally, empirical work can be undertaken to evaluate the ways in which the designs of websites, etc., change as a result of the adoption of benchmarking practices. In what ways are the websites changed, and how does this affect their usability and their relationship to their original ‘function’?

The insights offered by this paper have been particularly influential in related work undertaken by the second author (EAW) in ongoing work on the UK Identity Cards Scheme (Edwardes et al., 2007; Whitley et al., 2007). Thus, the enframing design ideals underlying the Scheme can be seen to be supporting the development of a surveillance society (LSE Identity Project, 2007) that many find unacceptable.

Benjamin Mosse has an MSc in the Analysis, Design and Management of Information Systems from the London School of Economics and Political Science. He is the

Services and Emerging Sectors Group. Benjamin also worked at the Columbia Institute for Tele-Information at Columbia University where he researched media ownership and concentration in the United States. He lives in New York. Further information about Benjamin can be found at http://www.benjaminmosse.com

Edgar Whitley is a Reader in Information Systems at the London School of Economics and Political Science (LSE). He has a PhD in Information Systems from the LSE and is the Co-Editor for the journal Information Technology & People and an Associate Editor for the journal MIS Quarterly. Edgar's research draws on his interests in socia theory and its application to information systems. His recent publications include work on Free/Libre Open Source Software (FLOSS), international students and aca demic writing, and the technological and political aspects of the UK Identity Cards Scheme.
