# Learning from other buyers: The effect of purchase history records in online marketplaces

Qiang Ye <sup>a,</sup>⁎, Zhuo (June) Cheng <sup>b,1</sup>, Bin Fang <sup>a,2</sup>

<sup>a</sup> School of Management, Harbin Institute of Technology, 150001, China

<sup>b</sup> School of Accounting and Finance, Faculty of Business, Hong Kong Polytechnic University, Hong Kong

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Available online 17 November 2012

Keywords: e-Commerce Historical sales record C2C market eBay Taobao

## a b s t r a c t

In this study, we investigate how a new element of information – historical sales records – affects buyers' behavior in online marketplaces.<sup>3</sup> A historical sales record is the previous sales information of a seller for a certain product. It is provided on an online shop's item description page and managed by the online transaction platform. It is more pertinent to the item at hand than the seller's overall feedback score or reputation rating, as the latter can be accumulated by selling many other items. Online buyers can use the information in historical sales records to learn from other buyers' purchasing behaviors. We propose that a seller's historical sales records for a certain item will directly affect the potential buyer's perception of the item's quality, making a purchase more likely when an item has desirable historical sales records. We use two approaches to test our hypotheses. First, we analyze purchase data from global.eBay.com and Taobao.com and <sup>fi</sup>nd that across a variety of products sampled, after controlling for price and a number of seller characteristics such as feedback scores/ratings, higher historical sales in a 30-day period are consistently associated with higher sales in the following seven days. This indicates that buyers are learning from other buyers. The second approach is a more direct examination of buyer behavior in which we run an experiment using an eye-tracking system to measure what information purchasers pay attention to on a website. We find that on the search result page, the area containing the historical sales record receives the longest <sup>fi</sup>xation length from the participants, and the participants who look at the historical sales record longer tend to choose the seller with the highest historical sales.

## 1. Introduction

Customers are often called on to make choices between different products, sellers and brands. With limited information on the value of these choices, customers often choose to follow other buyers. People observe the choices of other buyers in a variety of ways, including bestseller lists, the queue length in front of a teller, or the fraction of seats occupied in a restaurant. Despite these strategies, information about other buyers' choices can be noisy and hard to obtain in the of<sup>fl</sup>ine world. With the emergence of online marketplaces, it has become much easier to observe other buyers' choices. global.eBay.com, the world's largest online marketplace, provides links to the purchase history records of listed items. Taobao.com, the leading online platform for C2C trading in China, lists the historical sales records for each item for the previous 30 days on each item's description page and allows buyers to sort the search results based on historical sales records. We examine the effect that a seller's historical sales records have on customers' purchasing decisions to answer the question: to what extent do customers learn from other buyers?

We argue that an item's purchase history signals its quality and the seller's credibility to the buyer. In online marketplaces where buyers and sellers are physically separated and the products cannot be examined before purchase, buyers face a considerate amount of uncertainty regarding both the quality of the product and the credibility of the seller. Online marketplaces such as eBay and Taobao provide feedback mechanisms that help to reduce this uncertainty. Many previous studies have analyzed the feedback scores or reputation ratings and detailed feedback text comments of online marketplaces. However, as many sellers sell more than one product on marketplaces like eBay and Taobao, these feedback scores/ratings tend to be overall assessments of the seller and may not be directly related to the item that a customer is looking for. Historical sales, on the other hand, are associated with individual items and might directly affect buyers' perceptions of product quality. We argue that customers are more likely to purchase from sellers whose items have higher historical sales records.

We investigate the effect that historical sales records have on purchase decisions by empirically analyzing the online data and by conducting a lab experiment. In the empirical investigation, we collect “buy it now” purchase data from eBay and Taobao. Auction purchases are excluded, because only the bidding history record for the particular auction is available on eBay. In contrast, the majority of Taobao transactions are completed as “buy it now” purchases [30]. We control for price and for a number of seller characteristics such as feedback scores/ratings, ratio of positive ratings, and in the case of Taobao, we also include Taobao-backed institutional guarantees. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that across the variety of products we sampled, higher historical sales in a 30-day period are consistently associated with higher sales in the following seven days. In the lab experiment, we use an eye-tracking system to directly examine whether the participants pay attention to sellers' historical sales records.

## 2. Literature review and hypothesis development

Our study draws on previous research on online reputation, online word of mouth information cascades, and observational learning theories.

Many previous studies have investigated the role of online feedback mechanisms in building trust within online marketplaces [1,17,21]. Dellarocas [9] provided an excellent review of the research that has been done on eBay. These studies examined the effects of positive and negative feedback on price and probability of sale, with inconclusive results. Among the components of eBay's feedback pro-<sup>fi</sup>les, the overall number of positive and negative ratings has the greatest in<sup>fl</sup>uence on buyers' decisions, followed by the number of recently posted negative comments. Pavlou and Dimoka [25] went a step further and examined the role of detailed feedback text comments combined with numerical ratings in building a buyer's trust in a seller. They analyzed trust along two dimensions: benevolence (goodwill trust) and credibility (competence and reliability). Their results showed evidence of price premiums associated with extraordinary past seller behavior described in the detailed feedback text comments. Their assumption was that buyers have the time and the patience to explore the details in the text comments.

Studies on online word of mouth have explored the effect of word of mouth on sales and the prediction of sales. In general, the effect of word of mouth is asymmetric. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, negative word of mouth information plays a more vital role in a consumer's decision process than its positive counterpart [22]. Most previous studies have focused on online books or movies as they are homogeneous and generate easy-to-obtain online word of mouth data. Chevalier and Mayzlin [8] examined the effect of consumer reviews on the relative sales of books in the Amazon.com (Amazon hereafter) and Barnesandnoble.com marketplaces and found evidence that consumers do read review text rather than relying only on summary statistics. In contrast, Liu [20] found that most of the explanatory power of word of mouth for box of<sup>fi</sup>ce revenue comes from the volume of word of mouth and not from its valence. Dellarocas et al. [10] showed that the addition of online product review metrics to a benchmark model that included prerelease marketing, theater availability, and professional critic reviews substantially increased its accuracy in forecasting sales. Forman et al. [13] focused on the effect of reviewer identity disclosure on Amazon and found that online community members rated reviews containing identity-descriptive information more positively. Gu et al. [15] found that the rating of online reviews does not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly impact box of<sup>fi</sup>ce sales, while the volume does. Duan et al. [11] investigated the dynamics of online word of mouth and product sales and found that a movie's box of<sup>fi</sup>ce revenue and word of mouth valence signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uence word of mouth volume. Word of mouth volume, in turn, leads to higher box of<sup>fi</sup>ce performance. Ghose and Han [14] argued that an increase in content usage in the previous period negatively affects content generation in the current period. It has also been found that external word of mouth sources play a more important role in retailer's sales [16]. We, therefore, include both word of mouth volume and valence as control variables.

In their seminal papers, Bikhchandani et al. [3,4] de<sup>fi</sup>ned information cascade as the situation in which “it is optimal for an individual, having observed the actions of those ahead of him, to follow the behavior of the preceding individual without regard to his own information.” The rationale is that individuals have imperfect information about the true value of alternatives. Obtaining information and evaluating the alternatives are costly and time-consuming activities and, therefore, individuals choose to imitate others to take advantage of their hard-won information, hoping that others know best. Duan et al. [12] applied the information cascade theory to the online world and studied the effect of download rankings on the adoption of software. Observational learning is a similar theory that has been widely used in marketing research. It refers to the behavior of customers drawing quality inferences from observations of others' choices. This observational learning concept was <sup>fi</sup>rst proposed in psychology as “social learning” [2]. Although both word of mouth information and observational learning affect sellers' performance, there are some differences between them. Unlike word of mouth information, observational learning only contains the actions of previous consumers, without the reasoning that prompted the actions [4]. Zhang [31] found strong evidence of observational learning in the U.S. kidney market; patients drew negative quality inferences from earlier refusals in the queue, thus becoming themselves more inclined toward refusal. Adoption inertia occurs when social learning intensity, which is equal to observational learning, is reasonably high, but may vanish when the intensity exceeds a certain threshold [18]. Using a natural experimental setting that was the result of information policy shifts at Amazon, Chen et al. [6] tried to distinguish between the different effects of word of mouth information and observational learning. An observational learning effect can be also complicated by saliency and conformity effects. Cai et al. [5] distinguished between learning and saliency effects in a restaurant dining setting and found that when customers were given ranking information for the <sup>fi</sup>ve most popular dishes, the demand for those dishes increased by 13% to 20%. Other studies have suggested that observational learning effects are more conducive to niche products with a narrow or disproportionate appeal [27].

In this study, we investigate the effect of purchase history records, a speci<sup>fi</sup>c area that has not previously been studied in an online marketplace setting. We examine how this additional piece of information affects buyers' decisions. Our research contributes to the literature in the following ways.

We build on previous studies on online reputation and control for the seller's feedback ratings. These feedback ratings are an overall assessment of the seller's credibility. However, sellers typically list a variety of items simultaneously and the feedback rating may not be a direct indicator of the quality of particular items, as feedback ratings can be easily in<sup>fl</sup>ated by low priced items. In contrast, purchase histories combined with item descriptions may serve as a more credible signal to buyers of the quality of each particular item. In addition, the process required to understand historical sales records creates a lighter cognitive load than the processes required to understand detailed text comments or online word of mouth information. Online marketplaces such as Taobao.com facilitate easy comparison of purchase history records from different sellers by allowing buyers to sort the search results based on historical sales record. Therefore, customers are more likely to rely on historical sales record when making purchase decisions.

Duan et al. [12] posed a research question similar to ours, but our approach offers a number of new perspectives. First, Duan et al. [12] looked at free software downloads and we look at real purchases that require more involved decision-making. Second, in the study by Duan et al., customers made decisions about different products in certain software categories (product-oriented decisions). In our study, customers make decisions about different sellers who carry the same products (seller-oriented decisions). Third, our study is conducted in a much richer setting in that we collect data and test our hypotheses on a variety of products from perfume to computer memory to prepaid phone cards. Fourth, we supplement our analysis of online marketplace data with a unique experiment that uses an eye-tracking system to obtain a more direct examination of buyers' online purchasing behavior.

We propose that sellers' historical sales records have a strong impact on the purchasing decision of customers. Customers in online marketplaces are more likely to purchase from sellers with a higher amount of historical sales than from sellers with a lower amount of historical sales or with no historical sales. To test this proposition, we use data from Taobao and eBay to examine the relationship between a seller's historical sales for an item and the following customer's purchasing decisions. Furthermore, we design a lab experiment to provide additional validation for the proposition that historical sales records affect the customers' purchase decision making process.

We <sup>fi</sup>rst use data collected from Taobao.com and global.eBay.com to examine the relationship between purchase history and current sales. If customers are more likely to buy from a seller whose product shows more historical sales, then sellers with higher historical sales records will generate higher current sales. Therefore, our <sup>fi</sup>rst hypothesis proposes a relationship between purchase history records and current sales.

H1. In online marketplaces, sellers' current sales for a product are signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly and positively associated with the seller's historical sales of the product.

To gain a more direct understanding of the impact that purchase history has on customers' purchase decisions, we design a lab experiment using an eye-tracking system, in which we track customers' eye <sup>fi</sup>xation when they are choosing a seller. We record customers' eye <sup>fi</sup>xation area on the computer screen, <sup>fi</sup>xation time length, and customers' <sup>fi</sup>nal choice. The lab experiment allows us to directly examine the information set that is used in purchase decisions. We expect that the lab experiment participants will pay attention to the purchase history record and participants with longer <sup>fi</sup>xation times on purchase history record information are more likely to choose a seller with a higher purchase history record. Formally, our second hypothesis is stated as follows.

H2. The lab experiment participants with longer <sup>fi</sup>xation times on historical sales records are more likely to choose sellers with more historical sales.

We believe that this study makes important contributions to the understanding of buyer behavior in online marketplaces.

## 3.2. Research model

To identify the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of historical sales we use the following <sup>fi</sup>xedeffect, log-linear regression model for an online seller i on Taobao.com.

$$
\begin{array}{l} \ln (s a l e s _ {i}) = \alpha + \beta 1   \ln (H i s _ {S} a l e s _ {i}) + \beta 2   p r i c e _ {i} + \beta 3   \ln (R a t i n g _ {i}) \\ \qquad + \beta 4   R a t i o _ {P} o s _ {i} + \beta 5   D 1 _ {i} + \beta 6   D 2 _ {i} + \beta 7   D 3 _ {i} + \beta 8   D 4 _ {i} \\ \qquad + \beta 9   D 5 _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i}. \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

The regression model that we use for seller i in global.eBay.com is given in Eq. (2).

$$
\begin{array}{c} \ln (s a l e s _ {i}) = \alpha + \beta 1   \ln (H i s _ {S} a l e s _ {i}) + \beta 2   p r i c e _ {i} + \beta 3   \ln (R a t i n g _ {i}) \\ + \beta 4   R a t i o _ {P} o s _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i}. \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

The difference between Eqs. (1) and (2) is that the Taobao model includes the four dummy variables indicating whether the seller has joined one of the four types of customer protection programs.

To avoid the problem of taking the log of 0, we add 1 to the current sales, the historical sales and the seller's ratings in the above models.

We <sup>fi</sup>rst run our regressions with historical sales excluded (Model I) and then with the historical sales included (Model II) to observe the additional effect of the historical sales.

## 3.3. Results

The statistical results for each product are listed in the columns of Table 7. One of the eBay sellers did not have a purchase record and was thus excluded from the regression. The results produce a few in teresting <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

First, the inclusion of historical sales signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly increases the explanatory power of the model as measured by R<sup>2</sup>. This increase in $\mathtt { R } ^ { 2 }$ is most prominent for the prepaid mobile re<sup>fi</sup>ll card, (from 0.002 [insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant] to 0.243 [signi<sup>fi</sup>cant]), the only digital product included in our analysis. The Kingston memory product on eBay also demonstrates a large increase in R<sup>2</sup>, followed by the same product on Taobao.

Second, the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient on historical sales is consistently positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant across the four different products, indicating that sellers with more purchase history records are, indeed, more likely to generate higher current sales. Taking the Kingston memory product as an example, a 10% increase in historical sales is associated with a roughly 2.3% (1.10^0.237) increase in current sales. Thus, H1 is supported.

Third, the effects of ratings/scores and the ratio of positive ratings are mixed. For CK perfume, the rating is positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. However, the ratio of positive ratings is negative and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant for this product. The ratings and ratio of positive ratings for the other three products are insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant, except for the prepaid mobile re<sup>fi</sup>ll card in Model II and the Kingston memory product in Model I. These results are consistent with the mixed results reported in the literature. Our results provide additional evidence that merely looking at ratings or the ratio of positive ratings may not be suf<sup>fi</sup>cient.

Finally, if a product sold well in the past, it will probably continue to sell well in the future. The underlying assumption is that there must be a third factor (e.g., the quality of the product or the honesty of the seller) that is contributing to the sales performance in both the past and the future. Our result does not contradict this argument. In fact, on Taobao and eBay, this third factor is not directly observable to the buyers. Buyers must rely on other information to make inferences about the third factor. Historical sales information is readily available on Taobao and eBay and it may very well be used as an indication of the products' quality and the sellers' honesty.

## 4. Experimental investigation

## 4.1. Experiment task and design

We conduct two lab experiments to further examine whether consumers use sellers' historical sales record information and how this information in<sup>fl</sup>uences their purchasing decisions. The <sup>fi</sup>rst experiment is a treatment experiment. In the experiments we track participants' eye movements as they browse C2C websites in a university laboratory. Participants view a webpage listing 20 shops in Taobao, all of which sell the same style of Dove chocolate. These 20 shops are real Taobao shops, chosen from default search results.<sup>4</sup> Given the capability of the experiment's eye-tracking system and the time constraints, we shorten the default search results list by deleting every third shop. During the experiment, participants can click anywhere they want to obtain more information, including detailed information about products sold by speci<sup>fi</sup>c sellers, seller information, ratings and comments. The participants then make decisions about which shop they would buy from. Following the purchase decision, participants complete a paper-based questionnaire that collects their personal information and their previous online shopping experiences.

To control for possible confounding factors, a second experiment is run in which the historical sales records are removed from the pages.<sup>5</sup> The reviews and the number of reviews are also deleted, as volume of reviews indicates sellers' history sales [29]. This control experiment was conducted six months after the previous experiment, and four sellers from the original shop list no longer sold this product. Thus, the control experiment has only 16 shops. For these 16 shops, the product information is the same as in the <sup>fi</sup>rst experiment, except for the sellers' reputation; the numbers had changed, but the rankings were similar. The procedures in this control experiment are the same as in the previous experiment, i.e. participants can click anywhere they want during the experiment, and then make a decision. The participants in the <sup>fi</sup>rst experiment are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the treatment group, and participants in the control experiment are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the control group.

## 4.2. Experimental procedures

In the treatment experiment we use the Tobii T120 eye-tracking system, a screen-based system with a tracker integrated into a 17-inch TFT monitor. This system has a 120 Hz tracking frequency. Eye movements are processed using an infrared ray detector integrated at the bottom of the monitor, allowing for a large degree of head movement to ensure natural behavior.

The treatment group consists of 84 individuals (81 university students and 3 staff), and the control group consists of 32 individuals (30 students and 2 staff). Volunteers are from several schools within the same university. Tables 8 and 9 summarize the demographic pro-<sup>fi</sup>les of the two groups.

In the treatment group, individuals wearing contact lenses are not considered for the experiment. Participants arrive at the laboratory individually and are given instructions and noti<sup>fi</sup>ed that their activities in the experiment will be recorded for research purposes. The eye-tracking equipment is calibrated for each individual by having them look at a moving red point on the screen. The point moves to all four corners and to the center of the screen in a random sequence, stopping at each location. Participants gaze at the point when it stops. This process continues until there are no calibration errors. The process generally takes between 30 and 120 s. To ensure accurate calibration, the participants are instructed to concentrate their attention on the screen at the appropriate times in the appropriate places. Participants receive a small gift to thank them for their participation. The procedures for the control experiment are much easier. Instead of using the eye-tracking system, participants in the control group are asked to make a decision on a laptop.

The list page is the webpage used for the analysis. For the eye-tracking report, the areas of interest (AOI) are as follows: (1) history sales showing sellers' historical sales and rating product volumes and (2) products' prices with freight. We call the other areas of the screen Not\_AOI. Participants are allowed to view the webpages for as long as they desire, and make their decisions naturally.

The questionnaire administered to each subject after they have made a purchasing decision measures the participants' Internet sur<sup>fi</sup>ng experience, sur<sup>fi</sup>ng willingness, online shopping experience, online shopping willingness and some personal information as measured by Novak et al. [24]. The questionnaire appears in Appendix A.

Given these two results, it is reasonable to state that the association of higher historical sales with higher current sales found in the empirical investigation is the result of customers' rational use of the historical sales information.

## 5. Implications

The results of this study have implications for the understanding of online marketplace reputation mechanisms. With the spread of online shopping, people are more likely to purchase products via online marketplaces. However, online shopping does not allow customers to inspect products, and this results in information asymmetry. Identifying product quality is a crucial issue for online customers. One practical solution is to establish a seller reputation mechanism. In this way, customers can indicate a product's quality by evaluating a seller's overall reputation. Numerous studies have been conducted to investigate such reputation mechanisms, including studies of feedback, and ratings [1,17,21] or of online reviews [8,22], which are also called e-word-of-mouth. One problem with both feedback/ratings systems and online reviews is that they only re<sup>fl</sup>ect a seller's overall reputation. Sellers can establish a good reputation by deliberately selling a few things in quantity. Therefore, a seller's overall reputation does not help customers evaluate the quality of a particular product. This study identi<sup>fi</sup>es a new element – purchase history records – in the online marketplace reputation mechanism. Purchase history records signal the quality of the item to the buyer and the seller's reputation on the particular item and sometimes a more important piece of information than the sellers' overall feedback scores/ratings, as it represents choices made by previous customers. Social learning theory and herding theory have described a similar phenomenon in human decision making processes. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study open up numerous avenues for future research on online marketplace reputation mechanism.

This study also has valuable managerial implications for online marketplace platform <sup>fi</sup>rms. Online marketplace platform companies, such as eBay and Taobao, are always trying to ensure justice and to protect consumers in transactions. An online reputation system is one of the widely adopted measures these companies use to reduce information asymmetry. In this study, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that purchase history records have a strong effect on consumer's decision making. They help consumers reduce uncertainty by giving them an opportunity to learn from other consumers. Thus, online <sup>fi</sup>rms should display purchase history records. However, we realize that purchase history records are subject to seller manipulation, as sellers can easily create <sup>fi</sup>ctitious accounts or ask friends to make purchases that in<sup>fl</sup>ate the purchase history records. Online marketplaces must take measures to restrict this kind of behavior to protect buyers and maintain healthy market growth.

## 6. Conclusion

In our analysis of the transaction data from Taobao (the largest online marketplace in China) and eBay (the most popular online marketplace worldwide), we <sup>fi</sup>nd that historical sales have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on a seller's current performance. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, customers are more likely to choose sellers with more historical sales. This result indicates that buyers do take purchase history records into account when choosing a seller and making purchase decisions. Historical sales are consistently signi<sup>fi</sup>cant across the variety of items we sample when regressing current sales on historical sales and other seller characteristics. In contrast, feedback scores/ratings are not signi<sup>fi</sup>cant for some items, such as the Kingston memory product on both Taobao and eBay. Our explanation for this is that feedback scores/ratings, which are an overall rating of the seller, may not be directly related to the item the buyer is considering for purchase. Purchase history, in contrast, is directly pertinent to the item and it affects the buyer's perception of the item's quality. Our result helps to explain the mixed <sup>fi</sup>ndings on the relationship between feedback scores/ratings and the probability of sale, indicating that beyond feedback scores/ratings, customers put more weight on information that is more relevant to the item at hand. We supplement our empirical investigation with a unique lab experiment and con<sup>fi</sup>rm that buyers do pay attention to the historical sales records; furthermore, the buyers who pay the most attention to the historical sales are most likely to choose the seller with the highest historical sales.

This study has some limitations that should be addressed in future research. These limitations relate to both methodology and data. For instance, Dove chocolate, which is the target product in our experiment, is too familiar to customers. This in<sup>fl</sup>uences customer's decision, as they already know the product's quality. In future studies, the familiarity of products should be controlled. Additionally, although the $\mathbb { R } ^ { 2 }$ value increases tremendously with the inclusion of historical sales, due to the cross-sectional design of the study, we are not able to directly establish a causal relationship, as some underlying seller characteristics might contribute to both sales history and recent sales. We will try to collect time series data in our future studies to solve the possible endogeneity problem.

Furthermore, as sellers may manipulate purchase history records by creating <sup>fi</sup>ctitious accounts, a game that theoretically models sellers' opportunistic behavior, buyers' rational expectations, and online marketplace regulations would be an interesting and fruitful subject for future research. Another possibility would be to introduce an unbiased moderation or auditing service into an online marketplace. For example, Xu et al. [28] introduced an unbiased auditor to verify transaction outcomes on a random basis and to update the reputation according to the auditing results. Their main result showed the existence of a separating equilibrium in the reputation market in which high-type agents buy <sup>fi</sup>rms with good reputations and low-type agents buy <sup>fi</sup>rms with low reputations. Chen et al. [7] introduced moderation into a reputation system to ensure information and content quality. Future research could build on these studies to explore the effects of moderation or auditing in online marketplaces.
