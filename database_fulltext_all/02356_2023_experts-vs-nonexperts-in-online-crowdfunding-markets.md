# EXPERTS VS. NONEXPERTS IN ONLINE CROWDFUNDING MARKETS<sup>1</sup>

Mingfeng Lin Scheller College of Business, Georgia Institute of Technology Atlanta, GA U.S.A. {mingfeng.lin@scheller.gatech.edu}

Richard W. Sias Department of Finance, Eller College of Management, the University of Arizona Tucson, AZ U.S.A. {sias@eller.arizona.edu}

Zaiyan Wei Department of Management, Krannert School of Management, Purdue University West Lafayette, IN U.S.A. {zaiyan@purdue.edu}

The growth of crowdfunding markets that include both expert and nonexpert investors will soon accelerate due to recent changes in Securities Exchange Commission (SEC) regulations. Prior work has suggested that nonexperts (1) may benefit from experts’ participation via mimicking their trades, but (2) will also face a cost, as experts crowding nonexperts out of the best opportunities will ensure that nonexperts will suffer lower returns than experts. Traditional economic theory holds that the crowding effect means that the relative importance of nonexperts in the market will decline over time until they become unimportant. Exploiting a unique period in one crowdfunding market (Prosper.com) that allowed us to directly estimate the net cost of competing with better-informed experts, we found that the net negative effects of expert participation on nonexperts are small. We used simulations to both better understand (1) the market characteristics and crowdfunding platform choices that influence experts’ and nonexperts’ returns, their return gap, and the extent to which nonexperts are better or worse off relative to a market without expert participation, and (2) the factors that may contribute to the small expert/nonexpert Prosper return gap.

Keywords: Fintech, crowdfunding, experts, nonexperts.

## Introduction

Crowdfunding markets that allow suppliers and demanders of capital to match on online platforms arguably represent the most successful fintech example to date. Initially envisioned as peer-to-peer markets where nonexpert retail investors supplied capital, most crowdfunding markets now include both skilled and experienced institutional investors and individual retail investors who are tempted by the potential for higher returns and the low entry threshold even though they may lack sufficient expertise. Although there is variation in the mix across crowdfunding markets, experts and nonexperts both play important roles in most crowdfunding markets (Ziegler & Shneor, 2020).

It is not clear how the introduction of experts impacts nonexperts in these markets. On the one hand, by definition, expert investors should be able to better identify the best investments, implying that nonexpert investors will be left with the remaining less-attractive investments (the “crowding” effect). Economic theory holds thateverything else being equal, expert investors selecting better investments means that experts will garner larger returns and eventually dominate the market. On the other hand, the extant work on this topic suggests that nonexperts may benefit by mimicking experts’ decisions (the “learning” effect). Note that nonexperts do not need to intentionally follow experts (e.g., in many crowdfunding markets investors only use screen names and do not self-identify as experts or nonexperts)—as long as experts’ demand plays a meaningful role in driving aggregate crowdfunding demand, to some degree, nonexperts following aggregate demand means that nonexperts will follow experts’ demand.

Given the offsetting learning and crowding effects, it is not clear (1) if nonexperts benefit from or are exploited by expert participation, or (2) how market characteristics and platform choices impact the net effect. Thus, the overall research question for this study is: What factors determine the returns and difference in returns for experts and nonexperts in online crowdfunding markets? We take a two-step approach to better understand the potential costs and benefits of expert participation in crowdfunding markets. Our initial approach measures the returns earned by experts and nonexperts and their return differential, which can be attributed to experts crowding into the better investments. As far as we know, no previous study has attempted to do this because investorlevel data identifying experts and nonexperts is rarely available, and it is usually impossible to distinguish return differences that arise from better incorporating information (i.e., mispricing) from return differences emerging from differences in risk tolerance. We overcome these issues using data from a unique period on Prosper.com (a leading online debt crowdfunding market) that provides loan characteristics, investor type identification, and ex ante identifiable mispricing. Specifically, we study a period on Prosper when all loans are classified into one of seven groups according to the loan’s riskiness and all borrowers in the group are assigned the same interest rate. Because these groupings are coarse, loans in the same group have substantially different credit scores—and this information is available to all investors. For example, if two borrowers are charged 20% interest but the first borrower has a credit score of 700 while the second borrower has a credit score of 900, experts should overweight the second loan, as the borrower is paying the same rate but is less likely to default.

Consistent with ex ante identifiable mispricing, high credit score loans within each credit grade-interest rate group are less likely to default. Moreover, consistent with traditional economic theory, experts are more likely than nonexperts to overweight higher credit score loans within each constant interest rate group. For instance, our most conservative estimate suggests (holding the interest rate constant) that a 100-point increase in credit score is associated with a 4.9% increase in the fraction of the loan funded by experts.

Despite experts tilting their portfolios towards the best loans (holding interest rate constant), differences in returns garnered by experts and nonexperts are not large: experts garner monthly returns of 0.920% versus 0.878% for nonexperts. Thus, although consistent with economic theory that suggests that experts will eventually dominate the market due to their superior abilities, the small return difference means it will take a very long time to do so. For example, if experts and nonexperts each start with 50% of the market capital, the small return difference means that it would take 440 years for the nonexpert investors’ relative ownership of market wealth to fall from 50% to 10%.

One potential concern is that our analysis is based on a unique period in one market and may suffer from confounding factors. We ran three additional tests to further examine the relation between experts, nonexperts, information, and returns. First, consistent with the hypothesis that experts better use information to garner higher returns than nonexperts, we found a substantial decline in the expert/nonexpert return gap when Prosper changed their structure and reduced experts potential information advantage by moving to a much finer partitioning of loans. Second, we investigated how expert and nonexpert demand on day t relates to credit scores, Prosper score, and the aggregate demand on day t-1. These tests provided further evidence that, holding the promised rate constant, nonexperts tend to focus on the extent to which others (including experts) have already invested in the loan while expert investors tend to focus on mispricing signals. Third, we asked Amazon Mechanical Turk workers (MTurks) to select between a lower credit score/greater funding level loan and a higher credit score/lower funding level loan. The results provide further support for the hypothesis that naive crowdfunding investors are influenced by the choices of previous investors, as most respondents selected the lower credit score/higher funding level loan.

The second portion of our study employed a simulation model to (1) expand the generalizability of our analysis by considering how alternative market characteristics and platform choices impact experts and nonexperts and (2) to help in understanding the factors that contribute to the small return differential between experts and nonexperts in the Prosper market. Specifically, our model focuses on four factors that vary across crowdfunding markets—default rate, experts’ participation rate, how aggressively experts’ overweight the best loans (experts’ “weighting scheme”), and the signal-to-noise ratio of experts’ superior information (experts’ “signal quality”).

Our baseline simulations reveal four key results. First, even when nonexperts learn from experts’ trades, experts overweighting the best investments ensures that experts always garner larger returns than nonexperts. Second, although the crowding effect ensures that nonexperts garner lower returns than experts, nonexperts may be worse off (crowding effect dominates) or better off (learning effect dominates) in a market with experts relative to a market without experts. Third, a larger return gap does not necessarily imply that nonexperts are worse off. For example, when nonexperts benefit from following experts, increasing the signal-to-noise ratio in experts’ information advantage increases the returns for both experts and nonexperts as well as the return gap between them. Fourth, all four parameters— default rates, expert participation, experts’ weighting scheme, and signal quality—impact the returns garnered by experts and nonexperts, the return gap, and the degree to which nonexperts are better off or worse off relative to a market without experts.

Although platforms generally cannot directly control the four characteristics that we consider (e.g., experts’ aggressiveness in overweighting the best investments) in our baseline simulations, platform choices can influence the expert/nonexpert return gap and the extent to which nonexperts will be better or worse off relative to a market without experts. Thus, we also consider the impact of three platform choices—the level of platform prescreening, the separation of markets for experts and nonexperts, and the supply of investments that the platform selects for listing. Our analysis reveals that higher platform prescreening effectively reduces experts’ informational advantage and decreases the return gap. Although higher prescreening tends to lower experts’ returns, nonexperts’ returns may rise or fall. Similarly, nonexperts may be better off (when crowding dominates) or worse off (when learning dominates) in separate markets for experts and nonexperts, relative to a single market where experts and nonexperts compete. Finally, the ability of nonexperts to learn from experts depends critically on nonexperts avoiding the worst investments. As a result, in a market without excess supply, nonexperts are uniformly worse off when competing against experts— regardless of default rates, experts’ signal quality, experts’ weighting scheme, and experts’ participation levels.

Our simulations are also consistent with the hypothesis that two of the characteristics we consider—experts aggressiveness in overweighting the best loans and the signalto-noise ratio of experts’ information advantage—play important roles in explaining the small Prosper expert/nonexpert return differential. Specifically, inconsistent with experts’ fully crowding out nonexperts from the best loans, the vast majority of Prosper loans in our sample were funded by a combination of expert and nonexpert investors. In addition, although credit scores are strongly associated with default probability, default remains hard to predict. That is, the signal-to-noise ratio in our Prosper sample was relatively low as some low-default-risk loans default and most high-defaultrisk loans do not.

## Background, Research Questions, and Hypotheses

Crowdfunding market capital is approximately equally supplied by retail and institutional investors (Ziegler & Shneor, 2020). The relative importance of these groups, however, varies both across markets (e.g., consumer lending vs. real estate crowdfunding) and geographies (e.g., U.S. vs. Europe). Although some platforms (e.g., Prosper) now partition experts and nonexperts into separate markets, experts and nonexperts compete directly on other platforms (e.g., Wefunder). In fact, many platforms emphasize that nonexperts may benefit from participating in the same projects as experts. For example, republic.com advertises to potential investors that “the best venture capital firms, family offices, and high net worth individuals already invest in Republic companies” and encourages investors to follow, via the Republic app, “experts.” As pointed out by Vallée and Zeng (2019), even when experts and nonexperts are partitioned into separate markets, a subset of nonexperts using “expert tools” (e.g., LendingRobot) yields the same bifurcation of investors into expert and nonexpert groups.

In addition, recent SEC changes to the 2016 JOBS Act (implemented in March 2021) have yielded a nearly fivefold increase in Reg. CF offering limits (from \$1.07 million to \$5 million) and a \$25 million increase in Reg. A+ offering limits (from \$50 million to \$75 million).<sup>2</sup> This change “revolutionizes capital fundraising” (Perrone, 2020) and suggests that “regulation Crowdfunding will explode over the coming years” (Alois, 2021). Because the rule changes also remove investment limits for institutional and accredited individual investors and increase the limits for retail investors, the revised regulations suggest that the interaction between experts and nonexperts will increase dramatically. For instance, SMBx claims the new regulations mean that “it should be easier to raise money— more investors, including financial institutions, will now be eligible to invest, and many investors are now allowed to invest more money than before.” Similarly, Crawford (2021) claims the revised regulations will increase the interaction between experts and nonexperts, “the lack of limits for accredited investors means that institutional investors can more easily invest alongside everyday investors.” As of February 2022, there were already more than 65 U.S. companies that allowed expert and nonexpert investors to participate in online crowdfunding opportunities for equity, corporate debt, consumer debt, real estate, fine wine, collectible art, and numerous other assets.<sup>3</sup>

Platforms, investors, fundraisers, and regulators all appear to care about the expert versus nonexpert investor mix. For example, although borrowers in a consumer lending market would prefer as many investors as possible (as increased competition leads to lower rates), companies raising equity capital may prefer nonexpert retail investors because, among other reasons, (1) it is easier for founders to maintain control with a large number of atomistic (nonexpert) investors versus a few large expert investors, (2) nonexpert retail investors often serve as a marketing platform for these firms, and (3) there is a widespread view that although (Alios, 2016) “institutional capital is fickle, retail capital is sticky and it is enormous.” Theory (e.g., Vallée & Zeng, 2019) suggests that platforms benefit by attracting more investors (expert or nonexpert) as platform revenue is typically a function of volume. Given that expert investors usually control large amounts of capital, expert participation allows the platform to grow faster, fund larger loans/projects, and may add a positive reputational signal. Nonetheless, in practice, platforms hold diverse opinions regarding the attractiveness of nonexpert retail and expert institutional investors. For example, Lending Club shut down their nonexpert retail fractional lending market in late 2020 but maintained their expert institutional investor (i.e., whole loan) market. In contrast, upon the exit of Lending Club from the retail market, Prosper advertised that “Prosper remains committed to its notes platform.”<sup>4</sup>

Platforms, industry groups, and theory (e.g., Vallée & Zeng, 2019) have suggested that expert participation may discourage nonexpert participation due to crowding effects. In fact, the concern is so material that in 2015 the U.K. P2P industry group banned (Weeks, 2015), “the practice of institutional cherry picking” that “leaves the often timestrapped, resource-poor retail lender at a marked disadvantage, and is prevalent in the US market.” Similarly, in 2014, both Prosper and Lending Club installed “speed bumps” designed to slow institutional purchases of the best loans and allow retail investors to better compete (Alloway & Massoudi, 2014).

## The Costs and Benefits of Experts’ Participation

Economic theory (e.g., Alchian, 1950; Friedman, 1953; Cootner, 1964; Fama, 1965) holds that investors who have or better use information (for ease of exposition, we denote these better-informed investors “experts”) will outperform investors that do not have or fail to use information (less informed “nonexperts”). As a result, nonexperts will eventually become unimportant or extinct (Figlewski, 1978; Sandroni, 2000; Blume & Easley, 2006). The intuition is straightforward: experts overweighting the best investments crowd out nonexperts and ensure that experts will outperform nonexperts. These models hold regardless of the mechanism that allows experts to exploit their informational advantage. Specifically, experts will outperform nonexperts if (1) all investors have access to the same information but experts’ speed allows them to select the best investments prior to nonexperts, (2) all investors have access to the same information but experts better use the common information, or (3) expert investors have access to information not available to nonexperts.<sup>5</sup>

Consistent with experts having an informational advantage, Vallée and Zeng (2019) found that (1) loans with ownership by sophisticated investors (defined in their sample as retail investors who make their decisions with the help of the automated program offered by LendingRobot) are less likely to default than loans held entirely by non-LendingRobot retail investors and, (2) the negative relation between default and loans with LendingRobot investors is stronger when experts’ advantage is stronger, as evidenced by the fact that the relation is weaker when the platform (Lending Club) reduced the amount of information available to investors.

Both theory and empirical work, however, also suggest that nonexperts may benefit from expert participation by mimicking experts’ decisions. For example, in many models (e.g., Banerjee, 1992; Bikhchandani et al., 1992; Welch, 1992; Åstebro et al., 2020) of “rational herding” or “observational learning,” an investor may follow earlier investors’ trades if the investor believes that earlier investors are collectively better informed. Consistent with these models, later investors tend to follow earlier investors in equity crowdfunding (Vismara, 2016; Hornuf & Schwienbacher, 2018) and reward-based crowdfunding (Colombo et al., 2015) markets. Moreover, several studies have suggested that this pattern arises from later investors inferring information from earlier investors (Herzenstein et al., 2011; Zhang & Liu, 2012; Kim & Viswanathan, 2018; Åstebro et al., 2020; Tian et al., 2021).

Finally, regardless of the net effects of learning and crowding, crowdfunding markets may still represent an attractive investment to both experts and nonexperts; prior work (e.g., Morse, 2015; Bavoso, 2020) has suggested that crowdfunding markets can generate both higher returns for the average investor and better terms for fundraisers by eliminating much of the financial intermediation costs associated with traditional financial markets. For instance, Morse (2015) points out that consumer asset-backed securities yielded 4.4% over the 2007-2013 period, while the average first quarter 2013 return for Lending Club investors was 7%.<sup>6</sup> Thus, if experts earn 8% and nonexperts earn 6%, both are better off relative to the 4.4% alternative.

## Research Questions and Hypotheses

We take a two-step approach to investigating our overall research question. Our first set of tests estimate the returns garnered by experts and nonexperts in a crowdfunding market. This is usually a hard question to examine because experts are rarely identified in most datasets and it is typically impossible to differentiate differences in returns due to informational differences versus risk differences (Lamont & Thaler, 2003). We overcome these limitations by focusing on a unique period in the Prosper market, where loans with different credit risks were assigned the same interest rate; as a result, holding borrower interest rate constant, expert investors would be expected to tilt their portfolios toward loans less likely to default. Thus, we begin by testing whether, consistent with our proposition that the Prosper market exhibited material and ex ante identifiable mispricing during this period, variation in observable characteristics (e.g., credit score, Prosper score) are meaningfully associated with subsequent default for loans with the same promised rate. We thus hypothesize:

H1: Controlling for borrower rate, loans with higher credit scores and Prosper scores will be less likely to default.

We also propose that institutional investors proxy for experts and that these experts are more likely to use the borrower characteristic information to invest in better loans (holding promised rate constant), leading to our second and third hypotheses for our analyses of the Prosper data:

H2: Controlling for borrower rate, loans with greater expert participation will be less likely to default.

H3: Controlling for borrower rate, loans with higher credit scores and Prosper scores will attract greater expert participation.

We thus examine the first research question based on our Prosper data:

RQ1: What are the returns earned by both experts and nonexperts and, given that difference, how long would it take for experts to dominate the market?

While the unique period we examined on Prosper provided an ideal opportunity to estimate the return gap (RQ1), we next develop a simulation model to explore three follow-up research questions that can (1) help broaden the generalizability of our study by examining how market characteristics and platform choices impact the returns garnered by experts and nonexperts, their return gap, and whether nonexperts are worse off or better off relative to a market without expert participation, and (2) help us understand the magnitude of the Prosper return gap. Specifically, given the tension between crowding and learning effects, we focus on (1) factors that impact the ability of nonexperts to mimic experts, and (2) the value of the information.

Experts’ weighting schemes are central to our analysis because finance theory (e.g., Rock, 1986; Vallée & Zeng, 2019) has demonstrated that an increase in the aggressiveness with which experts overweight the best investments will mechanically increase adverse selection for the remaining investors. Analogously, as experts become a larger portion of the market, experts will (regardless of their weighting scheme) continue to crowd out nonexperts from the best investments. An extensive literature in finance and economics (e.g., Aboody & Lev, 2000; Bloomfield et al., 2009) has also suggested that experts abnormal returns will be a positive function of the value of the information they hold. The value of information in crowdfunding markets will, of course, depend on both signal quality and how often borrowers default, i.e., in a market where projects almost always succeed, experts’ relative advantage will be smaller than in a market where experts are both better informed and many projects fail.

Thus, we begin by focusing on four factors that vary across crowdfunding markets, which extant work suggests influence the returns of both experts and nonexperts. Specifically, we use simulations to explore the following research question:

RQ2: How do the four market characteristics (experts’ weighting, experts’ fraction of market wealth, default rate, and signal quality) impact experts’ and nonexperts’ returns, the return gap, and whether nonexperts are better off (learning effects dominate) or worse off (crowding effects dominate) with expert participation?

We next extend the simulation model to investigate three variables that platforms control (at least to some degree) and, as above, either impact the ability of nonexperts to mimic experts or the value of the information. First, Vallée and Zeng’s (2019) model demonstrates that higher platform prescreening is associated with a decline in experts’ informational advantage. Second, some platforms have developed separate markets for experts and nonexperts. Given the potential trade-offs of crowding out and learning, however, it is not clear under what conditions nonexperts may be better or worse off in separate markets. Finally, our simulations consider the importance of excess supply (i.e., whether any projects on the platform remain unfunded) because without excess supply, nonexpert demand is a residual demand and nonexperts end up with everything experts do not buy (as in adverse selection models such as Rock, 1986)—even when nonexperts can mimic experts. In sum, our third research question asks:

RQ3: How do (a) platform prescreening levels, (b) separate markets for experts and nonexperts, and (c) excess funding supply impact experts’ and nonexperts’ returns, the return gap, and whether nonexperts are better off or worse off with expert participation?

Our final set of tests links the Prosper empirical results with the simulation analysis. Specifically, using insights garnered from the simulations, we ask:

RQ4: What factors may help explain the magnitude of the expert/nonexpert return differential for the Prosper sample?

## Prosper

Between late December 2010 and early July 2012 (our sample period), Prosper classified all loans into one of seven “Prosper ratings” (AA, A, B, C, D, E, HR [HR = “high risk”]) based on the “estimated loss rate,” which is a function of a custom “Prosper score” and a credit score. The Prosper score ranges from 1 (highest risk) to 10 (lowest risk) and is based on Prosper’s risk model, which uses information from the potential borrower’s credit report (e.g., total inquiries), as well as potentially unverified information provided by the borrower (e.g., stated income). The credit score and Prosper score are combined in a matrix to generate the estimated base loss rates. For example, at the end of 2011, a Prosper score of 4 and a credit score of between 600 and 664 had an expected base loss rate of 19.9%. Prosper considered two other factors when generating final loss rates—base loss rates are adjusted downward for previous Prosper borrowers and for shorter-term loans.

Prosper defines the “lender yield” as the “borrower rate” minus the 1% Prosper service fee. The lender yield is adjusted downward by the expected impact of uncollected interest on charge-off loans and upward by the expected impact of collected late fees generating the Prosper “effective yield.” As a result, the “estimated return” for a group of loans with the same borrower rate is the group’s effective yield minus the estimated loss rate.<sup>7</sup>

During our sample period (at a given point in time), all loans with the same Prosper rating, the same term (12-, 36- or 60- month), and the same previous borrower characteristics (new borrower or previous borrower) were assigned the same borrower rate. Thus, for example, at a point in time, all 36- month C-rated loans by new borrowers had identical promised interest rates even when they had substantial variation in credit scores. For instance, in their 2011 annual report, Prosper reported that borrowers with Prosper scores of 7 were assigned C Prosper ratings for credit scores ranging from a minimum of 650 to a maximum of 900. Analogously, borrowers with different Prosper scores can be assigned the same Prosper rating (and promised rate).<sup>8</sup>

Figure 1 provides an example of a Prosper loan listing. Lenders are provided with the borrower’s credit profile (second panel), including information from the credit bureau (left two columns), the borrower’s Prosper score, and the borrower’s credit score in a 20-point range. Although they have the same borrower information (i.e., the data in Figure 1), we propose that some of the information—such as credit score—are obvious indicators of credit quality that should be considered by experts. Of course, the additional information (e.g., dollar amount currently delinquent) may also contain signals regarding the default likelihood. As discussed above, experts’ advantage in the Prosper market may arise because both experts and nonexperts recognize the value of the information, but experts move faster or because experts better use the information than nonexperts in forming their portfolios.

## Descriptive Statistics

Our primary sample includes funded loans between late December 2010 and mid-July 2012.<sup>9</sup> We obtained detailed data about the listings, loans, borrowers, and lenders from Prosper via its daily data export.<sup>10</sup> Prosper lenders are required to complete a lender registration agreement and identify themselves as either an individual investor or an institutional investor. Thus, we used their Prosper identification to classify each lender as an expert (institutional investor) or nonexpert (retail investor). Importantly, there is only one contract between Prosper and every lender—retail and institutional. All investors were charged the same fee (1% of every borrower payment for our sample period) and all loans were subject to the same servicing standard.

Prosper investors funded 19,697 loans for a total value of \$141.9 million during our primary sample period. As detailed above, loans with the same Prosper rating, loan term, and previous borrower characteristics listed at the same time were assigned the same borrower rate.<sup>11</sup> Thus, for our sample, at any given point in time, there were 38 possible borrower rate groups.<sup>12</sup> Although the terms tend to be stable for substantial time periods, Prosper periodically updates borrower rates and estimated loss rates. Thus, we formed loan characteristic “bins” with identical Prosper ratings, loan terms, previous borrower characteristics, and borrower rates over contiguous periods where those values did not change. For example, 36- month AA-rated loans by a repeat borrower had a rate of 6.25% between February 16, 2012 and April 10, 2012, at which point the rate increased to 6.49% until July 17, 2012 (thus generating two AA, 36-month, repeat-borrower “bins”). We required bins to have at least 10 loans to be included in our sample. Our final sample consisted of 19,243 loans in 210 bins with a total value of \$138.3 million, of which expert investors funded \$49.2 million and nonexpert investors funded \$89.1 million.

Table 1 reports descriptive statistics for 12-month (Panel A), 36-month (Panel B), and 60-month (Panel C) loans both overall (first column) and by Prosper rating. As expected, borrower rates and expected loss rates increase monotonically across credit grades. Mean (actual) default rates exhibit a similar pattern. Because credit scores determine, in part, credit grades, credit scores decline monotonically across the credit grades. That is, although credit scores do not impact rates within a given credit grade (holding loan term and previous borrower constant at one point in time), credit scores do impact interest rates across credit grades.

Table 2 reports descriptive statistics for the default indicator, the fraction of the loan funded by expert investors, and the information contained in the Prosper listings across the 19,243 funded loans in our sample. All variables, except indicator variables, credit score, and Prosper score, are winsorized at the 99% level to minimize the impact of outliers.

We define the “credit score” as the mean value within the reported 20-point credit score range in the borrower’s listing (see Figure 1). The third column in Table 3 reports the average difference between the top and bottom credit scores within each bin. The fourth and fifth columns report the minimum and maximum credit score differences across bins. For instance, across all 210 bins that have identical within-bin borrower rates, the difference in the credit score between the borrower with the highest credit score and the borrower with the lowest credit score averages 131 points. The remaining rows report similar within-bin credit score variation for bins within every Prosper credit grade.

Consider a simple stylized example to demonstrate the crowding issue: Assume that experts and nonexperts each have \$150 to invest and a bin has three loans for \$100 each at the same 10% promised rate. However, assume that the loan applicants have substantially different credit scores— Applicants A, B, and C have credit scores of 640, 740, and 840, respectively. If experts use the credit score information but nonexperts do not (or if experts use the information before nonexperts have the opportunity to do so), then experts will invest \$100 in Loan C and \$50 in Loan B. Nonexperts will then be left with \$50 in Loan B and \$100 in Loan A. Because experts overweight the better-quality loans, nonexperts, by definition, overweight the lower-quality loans. Finally, assume that credit scores have value, and while Borrowers B and C with better credit scores pay off their loans, Loan Applicant A immediately defaults. Thus, expert investors earn the 10% promised return, and nonexperts lose 63% of their total initial investment.

## Empirical Tests

## Borrower Characteristics, Expert Demand, and Default

We first tested whether, controlling for promised interest rate, credit scores are associated with default likelihood consistent with the hypothesis that Prosper experienced ex ante identifiable mispricing during this period (H1). Specifically, we estimated an unconditional fixed effect (for each of the j = 210 bins) logistic regression of the default indicator variable on loan credit score. Since the fixed-effects model accounts for variation across bins, the coefficient associated with the credit score examines whether within-bin credit score variation predicts default likelihood.

Model 1 in Table 4 reports that the coefficient associated with credit score (divided by 100) is negative and statistically significant at the 1% level. Marginal effects (reported parenthetically) suggest that a 100-point higher credit score is associated with a 2.5% lower default likelihood. Given that the mean default probability is 22% (see Table 2), a 100-point increase in credit score suggests an 11% decrease (=0.025/0.22) in the default likelihood from the mean value. Consistent with mispricing, the results reveal that despite identical promised (borrower) rates, lower credit score loans within the same bin are more likely to default.

The borrower listing also reports the proprietary Prosper score. Consistent with the Prosper score adding value, Model 2 in Table 4 reveals that loans in the same bin (with the same promised rate) with a higher Prosper score are less likely to default, even when controlling for variation in credit scores. The magnitude is substantial—the marginal effect suggests that a one-unit higher Prosper score is associated with a 2.1% decline in default likelihood. Adding the Prosper score to the analysis also increases the marginal effect associated with the credit score.

Although the Prosper score uses the remaining data available to lenders (i.e., the other variables in Figure 1) and the credit score presumably (credit score models are proprietary) uses most if not all of these variables, it is possible the remaining 16 variables reported in Figure 1 still have value in capturing the default likelihood beyond what is captured by the credit score and Prosper score. Given that these variables are highly correlated (e.g., the correlation between “amount delinquent” and “number of current delinquencies” is 0.54), we conducted a principal component analysis on the 16 other variables.

We identified four principal components that we denote (based on factor weights) as credit lines, delinquencies, debt/income, and bankcard use, respectively.<sup>13</sup> The results in the third column in Table 4 suggest that additional information beyond the credit score and Prosper score may help predict variation in within-bin default risk. In sum, the results in the first three columns of Table 4 support the hypothesis that the Prosper structure in this period resulted in meaningful mispricing, as investors could use credit score and other variables to select lower default-risk loans within a pool of loans with identical promised rates.

If experts better use the available information to overweight the best loans, then greater expert ownership should predict lower default likelihoods (H2). To examine this question, we repeated the fixed-effects logistic regression, replacing the credit score with the proportion of the loan funded by experts. The results reported in the fourth column of Table 4, holding the promised rate constant, reveal a meaningful (statistically significant at the 1% level) negative relation between expert participation and default likelihood. The marginal effect suggests that a onestandard-deviation higher level of expert ownership (0.38) is associated with a 4.5% lower likelihood of default relative to the mean value (i.e., -0.026 × 0.38/0.22).

Since lenders cannot identify borrowers (i.e., lenders can only view the screen name), experts’ advantage must arise from information in the listing. If experts use the credit score, Prosper score, and the other listing information to select the best loans, then the relation between expert ownership and default should be weaker once the listing information is accounted for.<sup>14</sup> The final column in Table 4 presents the results of the fixed-effects logit of default on both borrower characteristics and expert ownership. Consistent with the hypothesis that experts’ advantage arises from information in the listing, the relation between default and expert ownership is no longer statistically meaningful once the information in the listing is accounted for.<sup>15</sup>

## Borrower Characteristics and Expert Demand

Although the test in the last column of Table 4 is consistent with the hypothesis that the relationship between expert ownership and default is driven by experts overweighting high credit score loans within each bin, we next examine this more directly by regressing the proportion of the loan funded by expert investors on the credit score and bin fixed effects.<sup>16</sup> The results in the first column of Table 5, holding the promised rate constant, reveal that a 100-point higher credit score is associated with a 4.9% higher fraction of the loan funded by experts.

Consistent with the hypothesis that expert investors recognize that the Prosper score has incremental value in predicting default, the results in the second column of Table 5, holding the promised rate constant, show that experts overweight higher Prosper score loans even when controlling for credit score. Again, the point estimate suggests that the impact is material—a one-unit higher Prosper score is associated with experts accounting for 2.0% more of the loan funding. The final column in Table 5 adds the four principal components used in the previous section. These variables have opposite signs in Tables 4 and 5 if experts use this information to avoid higher default risk loans (holding the promised rate constant). Further, consistent with experts crowding into the better investments, when the coefficients in Tables 4 and 5 are both statistically significant, they have opposite signs.

## Expert and Nonexpert Realized Returns

The results in Tables 4 and 5 support the hypothesis that expert institutional investors better use the listing information to overweight better loans (holding the promised rate constant); therefore, experts should garner larger returns than nonexperts. As a result (assuming no new investors enter the market), economic theory holds that the relative wealth of nonexperts will decline over time and experts will eventually dominate this market. Thus, we estimate the returns earned by experts and nonexperts and, given the return differential, we calculate how long it will take experts to dominate the market (RQ1).

We begin by estimating the total cash flows from defaulted and non-defaulted loans within each bin.<sup>17</sup> Specifically, we use the Prosper reported effective yield to infer net monthly cash flows to investors for each non-defaulted loan. For defaulted loans, we infer from the principal repaid: (1) the number of months prior to the borrower stopping payments and (2) the associated monthly cash flows.<sup>18</sup> We compute the cash flows to experts and nonexperts for each loan from the fraction of the loan funded by experts and nonexperts and the loan’s aggregate cash flows.

Loans within each bin have identical lengths (e.g., 36-month loans). Thus, we aggregate cash flows across each event month for all loans within each bin to generate the total cash flows associated with all bin j loans, the total cash flows to expert investors from bin j loans, and the total cash flow to nonexpert investors from bin j loans. Given the initial cash flow from expert investors (Expert\_ $\_ F _ { 0 } )$ and the subsequent monthly cash flows to expert investors (Expert\_CF<sub>1</sub>, Expert $C F _ { 2 } ,$ Expert\_ ${ \underline { { C F } } } _ { 3 6 } )$ from both non-defaulted and defaulted loans in each event month (e.g., t = 0 to 36 for 36-month bins), we compute the monthly internal rate of return (IRR) for experts in bin j loans by solving:

$$
\begin{array}{l} E x p e r t \_ C F _ {j, 0} = \frac {E x p e r t \_ C F _ {j , 1}}{\left(1 + E x p e r t \_ i r r _ {j}\right) ^ {1}} + \frac {E x p e r t \_ C F _ {j , 2}}{\left(1 1 + E x p e r t \_ i r r _ {j}\right) ^ {2}} + \\ \ldots + \frac {E x p e r t \_ C F _ {j , 3 6}}{\left(1 + E x p e r t \_ i r r _ {j}\right) ^ {3 6}}. \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

We analogously compute the monthly IRR earned by nonexpert investors in bin j loans:

$$
\begin{array}{c} \text {Nonexpert\_CF} _ {j, 0} = \frac {\text {Nonexpert\_CF} _ {j , 1}}{\left(1 + \text {Nonexpert\_irr} _ {j}\right) ^ {1}} + \\ \frac {\text {Nonexpert\_CF} _ {j , 2}}{\left(1 + \text {Nonexpert\_irr} _ {j}\right) ^ {2}} + \ldots + \frac {\text {Nonexpert\_CF} _ {j , 3 6}}{\left(1 + \text {Nonexpert\_irr} _ {j}\right) ^ {3 6}}. \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

Because our goal was to examine the return difference for experts and nonexperts that resulted from experts’ better (or faster) use of the information in the listing rather than from differences in investors’ willingness to bear risk (i.e., differences in bin weights), we estimate the total return earned by expert investors as the sum of the products of aggregate bin weights (across the j = 210 bins) in the Prosper data and expert investors’ estimated return from their investment in each bin $\cdot ^ { j : 1 9 }$

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text {Estimated return}_{\text {experts}} \\ = \sum_{j = 1}^{J}\left(\frac{\text{$value bin j$}}{\sum_{j = 1}^{J}\text{$value bin j$}}\right)\big(IRR_{\text {experts},j}\big). \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

Analogously, nonexpert investors’ estimated return is given by:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text {Estimated return} _ {\text {nonexperts}} \\ = \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {J} \left(\frac {\mathbb {S} v a l u e b i n j}{\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {J} \mathbb {S} v a l u e b i n j}\right) \big (I R R _ {\text {nonexperts}, j} \big). \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

The first two columns in Table 6 show that in our primary sample period, expert investors garnered returns of 0.920% per month, compared to 0.878% for nonexpert investors. The difference—0.042% per month (0.56% annually)—although statistically meaningful (at the 5% level), is surprisingly small.<sup>20</sup> Although the results in Tables 4 and 5 suggest that experts crowd out nonexperts from the best loans, the cost to nonexperts is not high. Finally, we assume that experts and nonexperts enter the market with equal wealth and compute the number of months, $n ,$ for expert wealth to rise to 90% of the market wealth (our ad hoc definition of dominating the market) by solving the following for n:

$$
(0. 1 0 / 0. 9 0) = \left(\frac {1 + E s t i m a t e d r e t u r n _ {n o n e x p e r t s}}{1 + E s t i m a t e d r e t u r n _ {e x p e r t s}}\right) ^ {n}.\tag{5}
$$

The fourth column in Table 6 reveals that, during our primary sample period, it would take 5,280 months (440 years) for nonexpert investors’ wealth to fall from 50% of the market to 10% of the market.<sup>21</sup>

## A Natural Experiment: Changes in Prosper Market Structure

In our primary sample period, Prosper gave all loans with the same Prosper rating, loan term, and borrower (new or previous) classification (at one point in time), the same promised interest rate. In mid-July 2012, however, Prosper began further prescreening loans such that loans—within the same Prosper rating, loan term, and previous borrower characteristic—were assigned different interest rates. For example, in the last week of our primary sample period (7/13/2012-7/19/2012), Prosper assigned all 41 newly listed C-rated 36-month loans by new borrowers a rate of 20.99%. In the subsequent week (7/20/2012- 7/26/2012), Prosper assigned the 30 newly listed C-rated 36- month loans one of four different interest rates ranging from 17.74% to 20.99%. Because the partitioning of loans into smaller risk groups (i.e., range restriction) caused a reduction in correlation (Meade, 2012), additional prescreening effectively reduced signal quality (we quantify the effects of prescreening in our simulations).

## Daily Expert and Nonexpert Demand

Since the vast majority of loans have ownership by both expert and nonexpert investors (see Table 2), the results are consistent with the hypothesis that nonexperts follow expert investors’ trades. To examine this issue more directly, we limited the sample of all 41,238 listings (i.e., both funded and unfunded requests) during our primary sample period to the listings that fell into one of the 210 bins (eliminating approximately 3% of listings), had at least one bid (eliminating approximately 8% of listings), and took more than one day to fund (eliminating approximately 22% of listings), leaving a sample (for this analysis) of 27,691 (funded and unfunded) listings. For each day and each listing, we computed the total amount bid by experts and the total amount bid by nonexperts (both scaled by the loan request amount). We also computed the cumulative fraction of the loan request funded (total bid value/loan request value)—both overall and by experts—as of the end of the previous day, creating a panel of 161,594 listing-bid-day observations. We then estimated regressions of the day t expert demand, or the nonexpert demand on credit score, the Prosper score, the day t-1 cumulative fraction of the loan funded, and the fixed effects for each of our 210 bins. That is, controlling for identical borrower rates (i.e., the fixed effects), we examined how day t demand by experts and nonexperts related to the credit score, Prosper score, and previous investors’ cumulative demand for the loan.

The first two columns of Table 7 provide further support for the hypotheses that expert investors are attracted to higher credit scores and Prosper scores while nonexpert investors are attracted to loans with greater funding. Since experts’ lag demand is positively correlated with cumulative lag demand (for our sample of 161,594 listing-bid days, the correlation between lag expert demand and lag cumulative demand is 47%), nonexperts following previous investors’ trades means that nonexperts tend to follow experts. The final two columns repeat the test but replace the regressor lag cumulative aggregate demand with lag cumulative aggregate expert demand. The results continue to suggest experts are attracted to higher credit scores and Prosper scores while nonexperts are attracted to loans with high lag demand by experts.

## Do Naive Investors Value Other Investors’ Choices? An Experiment

As a final test of the hypothesis that nonexpert investors are attracted to listings with higher demand from previous investors, we ran an experiment using Amazon Mechanical Turk where we presented respondents (MTurks) with two Prosper listings and asked them to select between a loan with a higher credit score but lower funding and a loan with a lower credit score but higher funding. Specifically, as shown in Figure 2, the listings were identical in every way, except that the first loan had a credit score range of 700-719 and was 75% funded while the second loan was 10% funded and had a credit score range of 730-749. The listings were presented side by side (and randomly reshuffled such that each loan was on the left 50% of the time). We collected a total of 299 valid responses. Inconsistent with the hypothesis that previous investors’ choices play no role in naive investors’ decisions, 54.2% of respondents (statistically different from 0.0 at the 1% level and from 0.5 at the 5% level based on one-tail tests) selected the lower credit score/higher funding level loan.<sup>23</sup>

## Market Characteristics, Experts, and Nonexperts: A Simulation

We next develop a parsimonious simulation model to help us understand (1) how market characteristics and platform choices impact the returns of experts and nonexperts, the return gap, the trade-offs between crowding and learning effects, and the extent to which nonexperts are better or worse off relative to a market without experts (RQ2-RQ3), and (2) what factors may contribute to the small return difference for our Prosper data (RQ4). Our initial specification considers four parameters that vary across crowdfunding markets: (1) experts’ aggressiveness in overweighting the best loans (weighting scheme), (2) experts’ relative participation, (3) the default rate, and (4) the signal-to-noise ratio in experts’ superior information (signal quality). We assume a debt crowdfunding market with 2,000 loan requests, each for \$10,000 with a 36-month term, but only half the loans are funded. We further assume that all borrowers are charged 21% interest (i.e., the average effective yield in our Prosper sample). Experts observe a standard normal signal of default likelihood (e.g., -1 × credit score) for each loan:

$$
X _ {i} \sim N (0, 1).\tag{6}
$$

Default risk is a function of experts’ signal and other unobservable (e.g., borrower’s intent to default) or random (e.g., subsequent events in borrower’s life) variables. That is, although experts’ signal for loan i, X<sub>i</sub>, is positively correlated with default likelihood, it is a noisy signal. Specifically, we assume the asymptotic correlation (where higher correlation generates higher signal quality) between experts’ signals and actual default risk is v such that the indicator of true default risk (d) is modeled as:

$$
d _ {i} = \nu X _ {i} + \sqrt {1 - \nu^ {2}} * z _ {i}, z \sim N (0, 1).\tag{7}
$$

Because d is the (weighted) sum of two normally distributed variables, it is a normally distributed indicator of default risk. As detailed above, experts’ advantage may arise because all investors have the same information but experts respond more quickly to the information, because investors have the same information but experts better use the information, or because experts have access to information unavailable to nonexperts. For the initial simulations, we focus on the latter two cases, which we operationalize as only experts observing the signal.<sup>24</sup> Nonexperts in our simulation recognize that experts’ decisions reflect their superior information. We assume that experts first make their selections and then nonexperts invest. As a result, nonexperts will follow experts into the same loans when possible and select loans (within the remaining unfunded pool of loans) randomly when they can no longer follow experts.

We assume that experts account for E share of the total funded market (e.g., if E = 60%, experts account for 60% of the total loan funding) and consider three different weighting schemes for experts. In the “full crowding” scenario, we assume experts sort loans by descending default signal, X<sub>i</sub>, and purchase 100% of the best loans until their wealth is fully invested. Since expert investors fully fund the best loans, nonexperts do not have the opportunity to follow experts into the best loans. As a result, nonexperts invest in a random sample of the remaining unfunded loans. Thus, for example, given two thousand \$10,000 loan requests of which 50% are funded, the total funded loan market is \$10,000,000 (\$10,000 $\times 2 , 0 0 0 / 2 )$ . Under this scenario, if expert investors accounted for 30% of the funded loan market (i.e., E = 30%), experts would hold 100% of the 300 best loans, nonexpert investors would hold 100% of a random sample of 700 of the 1,700 loans not selected by experts, and 1,000 loans would remain unfunded.

In the second weighting scheme, we assume that experts, knowing their signal is noisy, while overweighting better loans, invest some portion of their wealth in the “best” 50% of loans to diversify their risk. This “learning” scenario allows nonexperts to mimic, to some degree, experts’ decisions. Specifically, we assume that experts sort loans by the descending default signal $X _ { i } ,$ limit the sample to the 1,000 listings (of the 2,000 listings) with the lowest default signal, and hold the following fraction of each loan $i ! ^ { 2 5 }$

$$
\begin{array}{l} \% \text {Expert} _ {i} = \frac {\left(\text {inverse default rank} _ {i} | \text {top 50\% of loans}\right) ^ {(1 - E)}}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {1 0 0 0} \left(\text {inverse default rank} _ {i} | \text {top 50\% of loans}\right) ^ {(1 - E)}} \times \\ (E \times \text {No. loans / 2}). \end{array}\tag{8}
$$

This weighting scheme recognizes that the largest amount that experts can invest in a given loan is \$10,000.<sup>26</sup> Nonexperts follow experts into the best 1,000 loans by purchasing the portion of the loan unfunded by experts (i.e., %Nonexpert<sub>i</sub> = 1 - %Expert<sub>i</sub> for the top 1,000 loans). Under this scheme, experts’ total investment in the best 1,000 loans will sum to their wealth (i.e., E × \$10,000 × 2,000/2) and nonexperts’ total investment in the best 1,000 loans will sum to their wealth $( \mathrm { i . e . , } ( \mathrm { l . } E ) \times \mathbb { S } 1 0 , 0 0 0 \times 2 , 0 0 0 / 2 ) . ^ { 2 7 }$ Thus, under this scenario, nonexperts learn from experts’ trades and thereby avoid the bottom half of loans.

Finally, we consider a third scheme where experts still account for E% of the total wealth but (1) 1,300 of the 2,000 loans are funded (implying the total funded loan market is \$13,000,000 $= 1 , 3 0 0 \times \ S 1 0 , 0 0 0 )$ , (2) experts limit their investment to the top 50% of loans (i.e., the top 1,000 loans with the lowest default signal), and (3) experts invest in the top 1,000 loans following Equation (8), as if they held $1 . 3 \times E$ of wealth (i.e., replacing E with 1.3E).<sup>28</sup> Under this scenario, nonexperts will follow experts into the top 1,000 loans but will also be forced to purchase an additional 300 loans that have no expert ownership. Analogous to the full crowding scenario, we assume that nonexperts select these additional 300 loans randomly from the 1,000 loans with no expert participation. Thus, we denote this the “hybrid” scenario where nonexperts can follow experts into some loans (the top 1,000 loans), but due to experts’ larger weights in the top 1,000 loans, nonexperts are also forced to select (a random sample of) 300 of the less attractive bottom 1,000 loans.<sup>29</sup>

Since the default risk (Equation 7) is normally distributed, we compute the top k quantile value and assume that all loans with default indicator d<sub>i</sub> are above that value default (e.g., when k = 10%, approximately 10% of the loans default). We also assume that if a loan defaults, it is equally likely to default in any month of its 36-month term. The initial cash flow by expert investors in this market is given by:

$$
\text {Expert\_CF} _ {0} = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {2, 0 0 0} \% \text {Expert} _ {i} \times 1 0, 0 0 0.\tag{9}
$$

Note that Equation (9) represents an outflow (i.e., expert investors are funding the loans) at time $t = 0 . { } ^ { 3 0 }$ The total monthly inflow to expert investors for month t is given by:

$$
E x p e r t \_ C F _ {t} = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {2, 0 0 0} \% E x p e r t _ {i} \times P M T _ {i, t},\tag{10}
$$

where PMT is the monthly payment on a 36-month \$10,000 loan at 21% (specifically, PMT = \$376.75) if the loan did not default by month t and \$0 in all months greater than or equal to t for each defaulting loan. We analogously compute the initial and subsequent cash flows for nonexperts and, dropping the bin j subscript, use Equations (1) and (2) to solve for the return (IRR) earned by expert and nonexpert investors in each simulation.

For our initial simulations, we let (1) default rates (i.e., k) vary from 0% to 40% by 5% increments, (2) experts’ role in the market (i.e., E) vary from 30% to 70% by 10% increments, and (3) the correlation between experts’ signal and true default risk (i.e., v) vary from 0% to 40% by 5% increments. For each of these parameter values, we considered the full crowding scenario, the learning scenario, and the hybrid scenario, generating 1,215 scenarios (9 values of k × 9 value of v × 5 values of E × 3 weighting scenarios) that were each simulated 100 times for a total of 121,500 simulations.

## Simulation Results

Figures 3, 4, and 5 summarize our simulation results for the full crowding, learning, and hybrid scenarios, respectively. Each blue dot represents the average (over the 100 simulations) return (IRR) earned by experts, while the vertically corresponding red dot represents the average return earned by nonexperts for that set of parameter values. Darker dots indicate the return gap (i.e., the difference between the blue dot and the vertically corresponding red dot) is statistically significant at the 5% level or better.<sup>31</sup> Default rates vary from 0% to 40% across the horizontal scale. Thus, as shown on the horizontal axis, the first (nonshaded) region includes all the scenarios where there are no defaults, while the first shaded region includes all the scenarios where 5% of the loans default, and the last region represents the scenarios where 40% of the loans default.<sup>32</sup> Within each default region, each of the five pairs of blue and red “lines” represents the level of participation by expert investors (E). As highlighted in the figures for the 10% default region, the left-most pair of lines represent scenarios where experts account for 30% of the market, while the right-most pair represent scenarios where experts account for 70% of the market. Finally, each dot that forms the “line” represents the variation in signal quality. For example, as highlighted for the 25% default region, when the signal quality is zero (i.e., v = 0), the blue and red dots are essentially identical (as experts have no informational advantage) regardless of default likelihood or expert investors’ role in the market. In contrast, the last dot in each line represents the highest signal quality (where the correlation between experts’ signal and default is 40%).

The results in Figures 3, 4, and 5 yield four important insights. First, regardless of the weighting scheme, expert ownership levels, signal quality, and default rates, experts uniformly outperform nonexperts, i.e., the blue dots (in all three figures) are above the vertically corresponding red dots (except when experts have no information advantage) and the difference is almost always statistically meaningful. Thus, even when nonexperts can mimic experts’ trades, experts’ overweighting of the best investments ensures that experts garner larger returns than nonexperts.

Second, although experts overweighting the best investments means that experts always outperform nonexperts, nonexperts may be better off or worse off in a market with expert participation, relative to a market without expert participation. To clarify, recognize that the return earned by nonexperts in a market without experts is equivalent to the return earned by all investors in a market where no investor has an informational advantage—as a result, the apex of the “V” shape in these figures captures nonexperts’ returns in a market without experts. Thus, nonexperts are uniformly worse off in a market with experts when they are fully crowded out of the best loans (i.e., the red lines in Figure 3 slope down from the apex); nonexperts are uniformly better off in a market with experts when nonexperts can always learn from experts (i.e., the red lines in Figure 4 slope up in the learning scenario); and, holding the signal quality and default rate constant, nonexperts may be better off with expert participation when experts account for a relatively small share of the market (i.e., the red lines slope upward for the first few lines within each default region in Figure 5) but worse off with expert participation when experts account for a relatively large share of the total market capitalization (i.e., the red lines slope downward for the last few lines within each default region in Figure 5).

Third, a larger return gap does not necessarily imply that nonexperts are worse off relative to a market without experts or absolutely. For example, in Figure 4 (the learning scenario), increasing the signal quality increases the return gap but also increases the returns of both experts and nonexperts, i.e., although experts capture most of the benefit of higher signal quality, nonexperts also benefit (albeit to a lesser degree) when the learning effect dominates.

## Simulation Extensions: Platform Prescreening

Prescreening partitions loans into risk groups thereby reduces the signal range within each group (relative to the signal range in the population of listings). Since range restriction causes a reduction in correlation (e.g., Meade, 2012), prescreening reduces signal quality. To quantify the effect, we assume that there are 2,000 loans and the platform can view the noisy default signal (X<sub>i</sub>) where, as before, the asymptotic correlation between the default signal and actual default risk for the 2,000 loans is v (as in

Equation $7 ) . ^ { 3 3 }$ The platform begins with no prescreening, so all 2,000 loans are in the same group. We then sort loans from high to low default risk based on the noisy signal and partition loans into n equal-sized groups where n = 1 (i.e., no prescreening), n = 2 (low prescreening), up to n = 10 (high prescreening). We let signal correlation on the pool of 2,000 loans vary from 10% to 40% by 10% increments and, based on 100 simulations, compute the mean correlation between signals and true default risk (i.e., the empirical correlation between $d _ { i }$ and $z _ { i }$ in Equation 7) for the population of 2,000 loans as well as within each of the n subgroups. Figure 6 plots the relation between average withingroup signal correlation and group size for the number of prescreened risk groups ranging from one to 10.

The results demonstrate that prescreening quickly deteriorates signal quality. For instance, if the asymptotic correlation between the signal and true default risk is 40% for the population (top yellow line), prescreening into two groups reduces the correlation between the signal and true default risk, measured within each group, to 25% (on average). Prescreening into seven groups (as in Prosper) reduces the 40% correlation to 9% (on average). As shown in Figures 3, 4, and 5, regardless of other parameters, the gap between experts’ and nonexperts’ returns falls with lower signal quality.

In sum, prescreening generally reduces signal quality, resulting in a smaller return gap. As verified in our simulations (untabulated to conserve space), experts are generally worse off with greater prescreening, consistent with the evidence in Figures 3, 4, and 5 showing that lower signal quality hurts experts’ returns. Nonexperts, however, may be better off (i.e., a reduction in signal quality helps nonexperts in the Figure 3 crowding scenario) or worse off (i.e., a reduction in signal quality hurts nonexperts in the Figure 4 learning scenario) in a market with greater prescreening relative to a market with less prescreening.

## Simulation Extension: Dual Platforms

In 2012, Lending Club began their “whole loan” market. Soon after (in April 2013, after our sample period ended), Prosper started their whole loan program. These programs selected a random sample of loans that were offered to experts to fund in full. In both programs, if no institutional investor chose to fund the loan, the loan was then moved to the fractional pool. In this section, we consider the impact of partitioning markets for experts and nonexperts both without spillover (i.e., unfunded loans in the expert market are not transferred to the nonexpert market) and with spillover (i.e., unfunded loans in the expert market that move to the nonexpert market). Specifically, we randomly partitioned the 2,000 loan requests into equally sized expert and nonexpert pools. Because the partitioned market structure means that loans in the expert market must be 100% funded by experts, we assume that experts will fund the best loans in their pool. For example, if experts account for 30% of the market, then experts would select the 300 best loans (lowest default signal) from the 1,000 listings randomly assigned to their pool. Given separate markets, nonexperts cannot mimic experts; therefore nonexperts randomly select loans from the 1,000 listings randomly assigned to the nonexpert pool. Figure 7 reports the results of the simulations. Experts are worse off (relative to the full crowding model in Figure 3) as half of the “best” loans are randomly allocated to the nonexpert market (and therefore cannot be funded by experts).<sup>34</sup> Nonexperts—who simply earn the return on the average loan in the pool—are better off with separate markets if the crowding effect dominates (e.g., nonexperts’ returns in Figure 7 are higher than those in Figure 3) and worse off with separate markets if the learning effect dominates (e.g., nonexperts’ returns in Figure 7 are lower than those in Figure 4). Also, because experts cannot use their informational advantage in the half of the listings assigned to the nonexpert pool, markets are less efficient, i.e., more poorquality loans get funded in the separate markets scenario than in the corresponding scenarios in Figures 3 and 4.

Figure 8 reports the results for separate markets with spillover. For example, when experts account for 30% of the market, they fund the top 300 loans in their pool and the remaining 700 unfunded loans are then moved to the nonexpert pool. Nonexperts in this example would randomly select 700 loans from the combined pool of 1,700 loans. Because the worst loans from the expert market are added to the nonexpert market, nonexperts’ returns decline with increased signal quality (i.e., the adverse selection increases and the red lines thus slope down in Figure 8). Once again, whether nonexperts are better or worse off in separate markets depends on the extent to which they are crowded out in the pooled market. Separate markets with spillover help nonexperts (and hurt experts) when crowding effects in the pooled market dominate (e.g., nonexperts’ returns are higher in Figure 8 than Figure 3) but hurt nonexperts when learning effects in the pooled market dominate (e.g., nonexperts’ returns are lower in Figure 8 than Figure 4).

## Simulation Extension—No Excess Supply

Our simulations, reflecting the reality of most crowdfunding markets, are based on a market with “excess supply,” i.e., 1,000 loans are unfunded in the simulations for Figures 3, 4, 7, and 8, and 700 loans are unfunded in the Figure 5 simulations. Our final extension examines what happens when supply and demand are equal. Although platforms cannot perfectly match supply with demand, platforms can control the parameters that determine which requests turn into listings. Specifically, we repeated the learning and crowding simulations but assumed that there were only 1,000 loans available for investment (and therefore all loans are funded). In the crowding strategy, experts select the best loans, and nonexperts are therefore relegated to selecting all the remaining loans. In the learning strategy, all loans are funded (via the weights in Equation 8), but experts overweight the best loans. The results of the no-excess supply simulations are reported in Figures 9 (full crowding scenario) and 10 (learning scenario).

When the supply of loans matches total demand, nonexperts are uniformly worse off, relative to a market without experts (i.e., the red lines slope down in both Figures 9 and 10). In the full crowding scenario, the remaining loans are uniformly the worst loans (Figure 9), relative to a random subset of better and worse loans selected by nonexperts in the market with excess supply (Figure 3). Even when experts do not purchase 100% of the best loans, supply matching demand ensures that nonexperts are worse off relative to a market without expert participation. Given excess supply (Figure 4), nonexperts avoid the worst loans by mimicking experts. Without excess supply (Figure 10), however, nonexperts are forced into the worst loans (despite having some presence in the best loans).

## Simulations and Understanding the Prosper Results

The simulation results can help illuminate the factors that may contribute to the small return difference reported for our Prosper sample. As shown in Table 2, experts account for 35% of average loan defaults and 22% of Prosper loan defaults. ## Discussion and Limitations

Because the simulations focus on the characteristics that vary across platforms and markets in practice, they have important implications for other (i.e., non-debt) crowdfunding markets. For example, signal quality may be stronger in equity crowdfunding because information asymmetries are greater. Moreover, nonexperts’ learning opportunities may be greater in equity crowdfunding markets, given that experts are less likely to fund an entire equity issuance.

Although both our empirical tests and simulations assume that investors are either informed or uninformed, in practice, the extent to which an investor (retail or institutional) is informed is a continuum. In addition, as pointed out by Vallée and Zeng (2019), even when markets are partitioned into retail and institutional submarkets, there is a continuum of investor sophistication (e.g., retail investors using LendingRobot versus others). It is possible, for example, that the small return gap for our Prosper tests results is, at least in part, because some institutional investors do not use the available information while some retail investors do.<sup>36</sup>

As noted in the introduction, if experts’ lag demand is positively correlated with their lag aggregate demand, nonexperts following previous investors’ decisions means that experts’ decisions impact nonexperts’ choices. That is, even if nonexperts cannot identify experts, the “crowd” will reflect, experts’ choices to some degree.

Finally, although we estimated how the return gap eventually impacts the relative importance of experts and nonexperts in crowdfunding markets, clearly many other factors (that are beyond the scope of this study) impact the relative growth of experts and nonexperts across geographies and types of crowdfunding markets. For instance, most growth in institutional funding in U.S. debt crowdfunding has arisen from additional institutions entering this market due to their attraction to higher yields, asset class diversification, and low return volatility (Johnson 2018).

## Implications for Research and Practice

The crowdfunding literature on better-informed and lessinformed investors can be partitioned into studies primarily focusing on the costs of expert participation faced by nonexperts (e.g., Vallée & Zeng, 2019) and studies primarily focusing on the benefits to nonexperts by expert participation (e.g., Åstebro et al., 2020). Our study ties these two streams of research together and demonstrates that both factors can play a substantial role in driving the returns of experts and nonexperts, as well as the return gap. For example, in a market where the learning effect dominates, nonexperts may not require compensation for participating with experts (as in most adverse selection models). Moreover, both market structures and platform choices greatly impact the costs and benefits of expert participation. Although experts should garner larger returns than nonexperts, the net impact on nonexperts can have a wide range of outcomes—ranging from nearly no effect (e.g., when signal quality is low) to large negative effects (e.g., high signal quality and high crowding) to large positive effects (e.g., high signal quality and high learning).

Our analysis also has implications for both platforms and regulators by demonstrating that market structure (e.g., separate markets, level of prescreening) and conditions (e.g., signal quality, experts’ weighting scheme, experts’ participation level, and project success rates) impact both market outcomes (e.g., efficiency) and whether nonexperts are better off or worse off when experts are introduced to a crowdfunding market. That is, there is not a one-size-fits-all solution for the optimal crowdfunding structure. For instance, because experts (by definition) use information better to select investments, expert participation helps ensure that crowdfunding markets are efficient and properly allocate capital (Wurgler, 2000). Moreover, if experts do not fully crowd out nonexperts from the best investments, nonexperts can potentially benefit from expert participation, even if experts garner larger returns. Thus, platforms and regulators concerned about the expropriation of wealth from nonexperts to experts should first consider whether nonexperts face a net crowding cost or net learning benefit from expert participation. For example, whether nonexperts may be better or worse off in partitioned markets will depend on whether experts’ advantage arises from speed or because nonexperts do not have (or ignore) the information used by experts. If nonexperts’ only disadvantage is speed, then nonexperts could benefit from partitioned markets (i.e., in Figure 7, nonexperts’ returns from their pool of loans would match experts’ returns). If nonexperts, however, are truly nonexperts (i.e., they fail to fully exploit the available information or do not have access to the fundamental signals), then whether nonexperts are better off in separate markets depends on whether nonexperts face a net crowding cost or a net learning benefit in a nonpartitioned market. That is, relative to Figure 7 (partitioned markets) nonexperts returns are higher in Figure 4 (pooled markets, with net learning effects) and lower in Figure 3 (pooled markets with net crowding effects).

Our results also demonstrate the important role of excess supply—without excess supply nonexperts are worse off with expert participation regardless of whether experts fully crowd out nonexperts (Figure 9) or allow nonexperts to mimic their trades (Figure 10). Thus, all else being constant, experts and nonexperts may both be better off on a platform encouraging more listings (even if many projects remain unfunded).

Our results also contribute to work in finance and economics (e.g., Black, 1986; Aboody & Lev, 2000; Kogan et al., 2006; Bloomfield et al., 2009) that focuses on the interaction between better-informed (i.e., experts) and less-informed (i.e., nonexperts) investors. To a large degree, however, this literature focuses on secondary markets (e.g., stock trading) where experts’ gain is, mechanically, nonexperts’ loss (e.g., a better-informed expert purchases an undervalued security from a less-informed nonexpert). That is, the nonexpert is the counterparty to the expert’s trade. Crowdfunding markets, however, are unique in that they are typically primary markets where a large portion of projects remain unfunded. As a result, experts’ gain need not equal nonexperts’ loss. Our tests demonstrate, for example, conditions where experts’ informational advantage not only benefits experts but also benefits nonexperts and market efficiency (i.e., higher-quality projects are more likely to receive funding). In short, the relations between experts, nonexperts, and market efficiency are more nuanced in the burgeoning crowdfunding industry, relative to markets considered by most finance theory and empirics.

## Conclusion

Although recent regulatory changes portend tremendous growth in crowdfunding markets, there is substantial debate regarding the potential costs and benefits that nonexperts face when competing with experts. For example, two of the five SEC commissioners voted against the recent changes to Regs. CF and A+ because they believe these markets are not suitable for most nonexperts.<sup>37</sup> Similarly, platforms, regulators, and retail investors are concerned about experts crowding out nonexperts from the best opportunities. Conversely, theory and empirical evidence suggest that nonexperts may benefit from expert participation by mimicking investors’ trades.

Leveraging a unique period on a crowdfunding platform where one can ex ante identify relative mispricing, we provide the first empirical estimate of the difference in returns that can be attributed to experts overweighting better investments. Consistent with traditional economic theory, the results support the hypothesis that expert investors are better at using the available information and, as a result, earn larger returns than nonexpert retail investors. Surprisingly, however, the return differential is small, averaging just 0.56% per annum.

We use a simulation model to better understand how market characteristics and platform choices impact the returns garnered by experts and nonexperts. Our simulation demonstrates that (1)

experts garner larger returns than nonexperts, (2) nonexperts may be better off (when learning effects dominate) or worse off (when crowding effects dominate) in a market with experts, relative to a market without experts, (3) a larger return gap does not necessarily imply that nonexperts are worse off, and (4) all four parameters—experts’ weighting scheme, experts share of the market, default rate, and signal quality—impact the return gap and the extent to which nonexperts are better or worse off, relative to a market without experts. Moreover, platform choices—the level of prescreening, joint or separate markets for experts and nonexperts, and the listing supply—can have large meaningful effects on the returns earned by experts and nonexperts. For instance, although sometimes viewed as a way to prevent the exploitation of nonexperts, creating separate markets for experts and nonexperts is not a panacea because, depending on the parameters, nonexperts may be better off or worse off and pricing may be less efficient.

Our simulations can also help in understanding the factors that may contribute to the small Prosper return difference. Specifically, the results are consistent with the hypothesis that experts’ weighting strategy (i.e., most Prosper loans are funded by both experts and nonexperts) and low signal quality play an important role in explaining the small Prosper return differential.
