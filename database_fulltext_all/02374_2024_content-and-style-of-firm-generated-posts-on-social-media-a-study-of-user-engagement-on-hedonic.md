# Content and Style of Firm-Generated Posts on Social Media: A Study of User Engagement on Hedonic and Utilitarian Product Pages on Facebook

Scott Schanke,<sup>1</sup> Gautam Ray,<sup>2</sup> Gediminas Adomavicius,<sup>3</sup> Mihir Wagle<sup>4</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Lubar College of Business, University of Wisconsin-Milwaukee, USA, schanke@uwm.edu <sup>2</sup>Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota, USA, gautamr@umn.edu <sup>3</sup>Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota, USA, gedas@umn.edu <sup>4</sup>Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota, USA, wagle008@umn.edu

## Abstract

Social media platforms are increasingly crucial in corporate engagement, but there is limited research delineating how page characteristics influence firm behavior on these platforms. This paper examines the relationship between firm-generated content and user engagement on product pages and whether a page’s characteristics and content motivations moderate this relationship. Analyzing a sample of 29,267 posts on 85 Facebook product pages of 49 Fortune 1000 firms, we studied the relationship between the content (i.e., informational vs. emotional) and style (i.e., formal vs. informal) of a given post and the level of user engagement. The key finding is that in the content dimension, “incongruous” posts, i.e., content not traditionally expected for a product page of a given type, generate more favorable engagement. Also, informal style posts achieve more favorable engagement irrespective of the product page characteristics. The secondary discovery is attributable to Facebook’s function as a social media network where acquaintances engage with one another. The platform’s unwritten protocol of communication largely emphasizes informality, which is likely the driver of this observation. These findings contribute to the burgeoning social media strategy literature illustrating how product page characteristics moderate effective social media content strategies.

Keywords: Social Media, Content Strategy, Content Motivations, User Engagement, Utilitarian vs. Hedonic Products, Informational vs. Emotional Content, Formal vs. Informal Communication Style

## 1 Introduction

There is significant corporate interest in leveraging social media platforms like Facebook, Instagram, and Twitter to support corporate engagement efforts, and unlock business value (Castillo et al., 2021). Corporate outreach on these channels is expected to grow by 3.86% a year in the U.S., becoming a 255.8 billion dollar industry by 2028.<sup>1</sup> Given that social media is mainly consumer-oriented, the presence of consumer goods manufacturers (e.g., Coca-Cola) and retailers (e.g., Target) on these platforms is to be expected. However, it may seem surprising that companies that sell industrial equipment, like Caterpillar and Lockheed Martin, are also utilizing social media in their corporate engagement efforts.

While a select group of industrial goods firms have chosen to use social media, a recent study found that 85% of firms that produce these products fail in their marketing and communication efforts.<sup>2</sup> These failures are troubling, as the customer base that makes the purchasing decisions is now moving away from conventional sales interactions like cold calls and mailing lists. Instead, consumers’ first interactions with an organization are increasingly mediated through social media. As the sales process is changing, it behooves organizations to adopt social media platforms like Facebook in their user engagement strategies. That said, many organizations are unsure about which content strategies will work best to engage their users, as utilitarian products bought for their functional value, like industrial equipment (e.g., construction drills and cranes), differ significantly from hedonic products, like consumer goods (e.g., chocolate bars and beer), which are typically bought for their experiential value.

Given this dynamic, organizations need practical insights into the dimensions they should use when planning the content to be shared on social media channels. While significant work has sought to identify content strategies (Lee et al., 2018; Meire et al., 2019), no work to date has evaluated how different characteristics of the focal product or brand pages influence such strategies. The goal of this research is to further the burgeoning literature on content strategies that promote online engagement (Barcelos et al., 2018; Dennis et al., 2016; Gretry et al., 2017; Lee et al., 2018; Meire et al., 2019; Tellis et al., 2019; Zhao et al., 2021). While the concentration of prior inquiry has focused on an array of page types like community involvement pages (Chiu & Juang, 2013), celebrity pages (Jin, 2018), sports team pages (Meire et al., 2019), and general company pages (Lee et al., 2018), our work focuses specifically on product pages.

Social media product pages are pages dedicated to a product provided by a company.<sup>3</sup> The focus on product pages is essential because we are interested in examining how the nature of the product page affects social media content strategies. To better understand how page characteristics influence engagement, we utilize the dichotomy of users’ hedonic and utilitarian content motivations. Hedonic content motivations are intrinsic and gratify the user with content that provides experiential and emotional enjoyment (Salehan et al., 2017). In contrast, utilitarian content provides rational, goal-oriented messages (Salehan et al., 2017), and its motivations are extrinsic and arise from the need to attain information or accomplish a task (Xu et al., 2012). We utilize work on hedonic and utilitarian products (Batra & Ahtola, 1990) to operationalize key characteristics of firms’ product pages on social media. In this study, for product pages that are dedicated to hedonic products, users are expected to have hedonic content motivations because hedonic products (e.g., beer) are purchased for sensory and affective gratification (Batra & Ahtola, 1990). In contrast, for product pages dedicated to utilitarian products, users are expected to have utilitarian content motivations because utilitarian products (e.g., insurance) are purchased for their functional attributes (Batra & Ahtola, 1990) and customers seek this content when making purchase decisions. In addition to the type of content, the style of the message, or how the message is delivered, is also important, and research has shown that aligning style to match the motivations of the customer is associated with favorable engagement (Barcelos et al., 2018).

In the traditional marketing literature (Batra & Ahtola, 1990; Ratchford, 1987; Ratchford & Vaughn, 1989), marketing content that is congruent (i.e., aligned) with customers’ content motivations is considered the most effective content strategy. In this work, we seek to advance the literature on content strategies by integrating prior information systems literature on content motivation with marketing literature seeking to identify ideal advertising/marketing appeals for products based on their different characteristics.

With this confluence of research streams, we address two research questions in this paper. First, how do content strategies congruent with the expected content motivations of the product page translate to user engagement on Facebook? Second, how does language style, an understudied dimension of business communication, influence user engagement on content shared on Facebook?

We utilized a proprietary dataset from a major social media analytics platform to evaluate these research questions. This dataset included 85 product pages randomly sampled from a list of Fortune 1000 companies. We then used a machine learning methodology to develop predictive models to classify the style (i.e., formal vs. informal) and content (i.e., informational vs. emotional) of company posts. To address our specific research questions, we examined the relationship between the nature of posts made by these Fortune 1000 firms on their product pages and the level of user engagement, measured as the number of positive, negative, and neutral comments on these posts. Though firms desire more engagement, they also want more favorable engagement, i.e., more positive and neutral comments and fewer negative comments (Chau & Xu, 2012; Zhang et al., 2009; Zhao, 2019). Thus, we refer to receiving more positive and neutral comments and/or fewer negative comments as receiving more favorable engagement.

While research on traditional mediums like print, television, and radio advertisements has found that messaging appeals congruent with a customer’s expected content motivations (Johar & Sirgy, 1991; Sirgy et al. 1997) are better received by customers, our findings surprisingly suggest that incongruity regarding user content motivations generates more favorable user engagement. Since users generally expect product attribute information via informational posts for utilitarian product pages, incongruity in content (i.e., providing emotional posts) can potentially cause arousal (i.e., increased consciousness, attention, and information processing), which may thus engender more favorable engagement (Duffy, 1957; Neiss, 1988; Yerkes & Dodson, 1908). Similarly, for hedonic product pages, users generally expect emotional posts; thus, an incongruity in content (i.e., providing informational posts) may result in more favorable engagement. Finally, regarding style, we found that informal posts (as compared to formal posts) generated more favorable engagement for all product types in the dataset. This finding may be due to Facebook’s unwritten protocol of communication, which largely emphasizes informality (Salehan et al., 2017). More broadly, the key contribution of this paper is showing how product page characteristics and the linguistic style of posts influence user engagement. We found that incongruity in the content dimension and convergence with the informal linguistic style used by Facebook users is associated with more favorable engagement than matching the expected content and style to the product page.

## 2 Literature Review and Hypothesis Development

## 2.1 Hedonic and Utilitarian Content Motivations on Social Media

Researchers have investigated why individuals use different forms of media channels. One wellestablished theory developed in communication literature is uses and gratification theory (UGT) (Katz, 1959). This theory proposes that individuals are not passive viewers of content; instead, they take an active and goal-oriented approach to their media use (Katz, 1959; Stafford et al., 2004). Through this lens, technology researchers explain an individual’s adoption of internet technologies arising from content, social, and process gratifications (Stafford et al., 2004). While social gratifications (e.g., from finding social connections online) and process gratifications (e.g., from the act of browsing the internet) are important drivers of adoption, content gratifications (e.g., from the content presented on the medium) are also important drivers of technology use and very relevant to Facebook content strategies.

Information systems scholars have found that users have two types of content motivations: hedonic and utilitarian (Salehan et al., 2017; Wu & Lu, 2013; Xu et al., 2012). Hedonic motivations are satisfied by hedonic content. This form of content can be described as intrinsic and speaks to users’ search for content that provides experiential and emotional enjoyment (Salehan et al., 2017). Hedonic social media content contains messages that elicit emotional arousal (e.g., joy, fear, rage, and jealousy) and provide cognitive consumption value (Xu et al., 2012)—for instance, funny pictures of animals, jokes, or fun games. In contrast, users’ utilitarian motivation is satisfied by utilitarian content. Utilitarian motivations are extrinsic and arise from the need to obtain information or accomplish a task (Xu et al., 2012). This content provides rational, goal-oriented messages (Salehan et al., 2017)—for example, posts outlining product details or information about events. While considerable prior work has investigated content motivations on social media, this work can best be characterized as descriptive, seeking to describe why individuals use social media. This current study seeks to expand on this prior line of inquiry by identifying how content motivations can help shape effective posting strategies for product pages on social media platforms.

The content dichotomies mentioned above have been used by information systems and marketing scholars to better understand content engineering strategies on social media. In this emerging literature, content can be split into two dimensions, emotion-based content or informational content (Lee et al., 2018; Meire et al., 2019; Tellis et al., 2019). These dimensions can be used strategically to increase on-platform engagement, support purchase paths, increase content virality, and influence customer sentiment (Lee et al., 2018; Meire et al., 2019; Tellis et al., 2019). Emotion-based content, which speaks to hedonic content motivations, contains expressive elements like humor, warmth, and joy and facilitates community growth, yielding greater social engagement in terms of Facebook likes and comments (Lee et al., 2018) and sharing (Tellis et al., 2019). In contrast, informational content speaks to utilitarian content motivations, contains items like price and product details, supports the path to conversion (Lee et al., 2018), and can improve user sentiment (Meire et al., 2019).

Although research in this area has highlighted the critical dimensions of content features, a limitation of this prior work is that the investigated social media categories can vary wildly in their goals and purposes. For instance, prior literature has focused on a wide array of different types of pages, like celebrities (Jin, 2018), sports teams (Meire et al., 2019), organizations, and websites (Lee et al., 2018). As users who interact with these pages have different content motivations, it is unclear how page characteristics, which drive these motivations, moderate the results of prior studies.

## 2.2 Hedonic vs. Utilitarian Product Messaging Appeals

The dichotomies of the content motivations of systems were inspired by the dichotomies of utilitarian and hedonic products (Heijden, 2004). In consumer behavior, these distinctions have been used to identify ideal ways to advertise products to consumers. Early on, products were divided into two types, “thinking” versus “feeling,” where thinking products were products best advertised with rational appeals, and “feeling” products best advertised with emotional appeals (Vaughn, 2000). Hedonic products are those most aligned with “feeling” and are purchased for their sensory and affective gratification, whereas utilitarian products are those most aligned with “thinking” and are purchased for their functional attributes (Batra & Ahtola, 1990).

Delving deeper, Johar and Sirgy (1991) and Sirgy et al. (1997) identified the messaging appeals used when advertising hedonic and utilitarian products. For hedonic products, value-expressive appeals, congruent with the hedonic content motivations of the prospective customer, are deemed more persuasive and are more positively perceived by consumers (Johar & Sirgy, 1991). In contrast, for utilitarian products, appeals that are congruent with the utilitarian content motivations of a prospective customer are viewed more positively and are more persuasive (Johar & Sirgy, 1991; Sirgy et al., 1997). Additionally, research on social media has shown that content that is congruent with a brand is shared more with a page follower’s social network than posts that are incongruent with a brand’s image (Montaguti et al., 2022). As congruent advertisements are viewed more favorably by users, it follows that social media content congruent with the focal customer’s content motivations would potentially yield more favorable engagement. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H1: On product pages with hedonic (utilitarian) content motivations, posts with emotional (informational) content will be associated with more favorable engagement.

## 2.3 Formal vs. Informal Style of Communication

Another critical dimension of social media messages is their style. Where the content dimension is “what” the organization is talking about, the stylistic dimension concerns “how” the organization says it. In human communication, these stylistic dimensions can be just as important as what is being said (Giles & St. Clair, 1979; Heylighen & Dewaele, 1999; Holtgraves, 2002;

Ireland et al., 2010). In linguistics and communications research, one of the most informative stylistic dimensions is formality (Heylighen & Dewaele, 1999), as it helps to communicate an interlocutor’s detachment or involvement with a message and can signal attempts of common-ground building (Holtgraves, 2002). In business contexts, this stylistic dimension has proven to be critical in online mediums like online reviews and social media posts, as they can build customer trust (Gretry et al., 2017) and further support the path to conversion (Barcelos et al., 2018; Ludwig et al., 2013).

Although the style of a communicated message is important, research on the strategic use of linguistic style is surprisingly somewhat underdeveloped. One recent study in this area (Barcelos et al., 2018) found that content in an informal “human” style can help increase purchase intentions for customers seeking hedonic value from a social media page. However, for customers seeking utilitarian value, or those who are facing a highly risky purchase decision, these informal posts negatively impact purchase intentions (Barcelos et al., 2018). These findings are intuitive, as formal language, by definition, places an emphasis on form (Heylighen & Dewaele, 1999) and is used in situations that require precise and clear details to ensure that the information conveyed is understood by all parties involved in an equivocal manner (Andrén et al., 2010; Morrow et al., 1994). As formal posts present clear descriptions of factual information written to inform (rather than entertain) the reader, when used on pages with utilitarian characteristics, content motivations would likely be met by higher levels of favorable engagement.

In contrast, informal language can best be characterized as a style of language used among close friends (Heylighen & Dewaele, 1999). On social media, informal posts often include surprises, rhetorical language, contractions (“textisms”), and other types of colloquial narrative. Given that hedonic content motivations seek emotional and sensory gratification (Zhang & Zinkhan, 2006), informal posts would likely be congruent with users’ expectations and would thus be associated with more favorable engagement. Based on the theoretical argument for matching the expected linguistic style of the product page, we hypothesize:

H2: For product pages with hedonic (utilitarian) characteristics, using an informal (formal) style in posts will be associated with more favorable engagement.

## 3 Data and Variables

## 3.1 Sampling Procedure

To collect data for the main analysis of our study, we conducted the following sampling procedure. First, we started with the Fortune 1000 list of companies. Then, we established the population of Facebook business pages of these companies by identifying pages at two levels: the main page<sup>4</sup> (i.e., the main Facebook page of a company) and product pages liked by the identified main page (for example, the page dedicated to a key specific product of a company). Since we were interested in how the relationship between the nature of the firm-generated posts and engagement differs based on product characteristics, our analysis was based on data from company product pages. Company pages that were not in English were excluded from the dataset. Through this process, we identified 482 product pages on Facebook. After identifying these pages, the research team collectively labeled page product characteristics (i.e., hedonic vs. utilitarian).

It is important to note that, at the time of data collection, the Facebook API had several security layers preventing the collection of detailed product page data. Additionally, there were some aspects that we needed to account for, like the number of impressions a post had as well as whether the post had been promoted. To deal with these issues, we partnered with a firm that performs social media analytics and provides services to large organizations; this company tracks these pertinent pieces of information and maintains a repository of posts for a large collection of organizations and their brands. As the company safeguards its data, we were able to request detailed product page information for only 100 pages from our 482 identified pages. To make sure that our dataset of 100 product pages was representative, we randomly selected 100 pages from the 482 pages that we originally identified. To ensure that the data was balanced on observable characteristics (i.e., similar representative portions of utilitarian vs. hedonic product pages), we performed balance checks using linear regression. In performing this analysis, we regressed the “hedonic product type” dummy variable on a dummy indicating whether the page was in the sample; this is akin to randomization checks in controlled experiments. After executing this analysis, we found no significant differences between the 100- page sample and the full population of 482 pages. Data for these 100 pages was collected every week for 12 months between November 2016 and November 2017. Of these original 100 pages, 85 pages had been posted to at least monthly. This set of 85 pages was used in our subsequent analysis. In this revision, further analysis was conducted to confirm that the sample of 85 pages was adequately representative of the broader population (please see Appendix B for details). Our final dataset used for the analyses reported in the paper included 29,367 company posts made on 85 product pages across 49 firms.

## 3.2 Dependent Variables

Users can engage with Facebook business pages in many ways (Brodie et al., 2013). We used the comments on a post as the measure of user engagement with the post. Prior research (Lee et al., 2018) has also used the number of “Likes” and the number of “Shares” as measures of engagement. However, compared to writing a comment, there is little cognitive effort required in clicking the Like or Share button (Burke & Kraut, 2014). In other words, Liking is a one-click action that can be used to provide feedback (Scissors et al., 2016), whereas commenting represents “composed communication” that requires greater cognitive effort (Burke & Kraut, 2014). In addition, comments can express a broad range of reactions, emotions, and attitudes, whereas liking is mainly used to describe simple sentiments such as agreement and awareness (Scissors et al., 2016). There also has been some controversy around the fidelity of Likes and click-related activity on brand pages, with many companies reportedly “buying” Likes via click farms. <sup>5</sup> In addition to offering a better means of understanding engagement, comments generated by a brand’s Facebook users have also been shown to positively influence consumer purchase behavior (Goh et al., 2013). Thus, we used comments on a post as our measure of engagement with the post.<sup>6</sup> The comment data was collected for each firm’s post from each product page in our study. Figure 2 presents an illustrative screenshot of a firm-generated post on the firm’s Facebook page.

A user comment can have a positive, negative, or neutral sentiment. Thus, we labeled each user comment as positive, negative, or neutral and used the number of positive, negative, and neutral comments (Comments\_Pos, Comments\_Neg, Comments\_Neu) on a given post as the key measures of user engagement with the post. Firms naturally prefer positive and neutral comments over negative comments. Therefore, we considered more positive and neutral comments and fewer negative comments to be more favorable engagement.

We used the natural language processing tool Stanford Core NLP to label the sentiment of user posts (Socher et al., 2013). This tool uses machine learning algorithms to classify text into positive, negative, and neutral. Furthermore, it has a proven ability to classify sentiment on single-sentence text with up to 85.4% accuracy (Socher et al., 2013). Due to the brevity of comments on Facebook posts, we determined that this tool was suitable for sentiment classification in our setting. In the dataset posts received more negative than positive comments, on average, which is not an unusual phenomenon for firms’ business pages on Facebook (Yang et al., 2019).

## 3.3 Independent Variables

The central proposition of this paper is that the nature of the company’s posts affects user engagement. As mentioned earlier, we characterized the nature of a post in terms of the style and content. The style of a post was categorized in terms of formal and informal style (Formal). Formal posts (Formal = 1) are written using concise, official language to inform the reader when the author wants to ensure that there is no miscommunication (Heylighen & Dewaele, 1999). Informal posts (Formal = 0) use more “fuzzy” language and rely on shared contextual information (Heylighen & Dewaele, 1999).

In terms of content, each post was categorized as informational or emotional (Informational). Informational content (Informational = 1) discusses company events, products, or services. Emotional posts (Informational = 0) are intended to evoke sensory feelings and enhance a sense of community (Meire et al., 2019). An example can be seen in the clothing retailer Hollister’s post “I can’t get the song out of my head!,” where the firm utilizes figurative language, explicit prompts for user feedback, and contractions to enhance feelings of common ground with their followers.

We used machine learning techniques to label the posts as formal vs. informal (in terms of style) and informational vs. emotional (in terms of content). First, three of the coauthors independently labeled 1,200 randomly chosen posts manually based on the content and style of the post. The interrater reliability was 0.93 for content and 0.87 for style. Machine learning techniques were then used to label the 29,367 posts used in the empirical analysis. We did this in several steps that involved: (1) processing the text to extract descriptive features (i.e., words), (2) choosing a subset of the most informative features, (3) building a predictive model based on the manually coded subset of data using machine-learning-based classification techniques, and (4) deploying the classification model for automated classification of all posts. Each of these steps is briefly summarized below.

Processing the text: We processed the textual post information using the standard “bag-of-words” approach, commonly used in natural language processing and information retrieval, to represent each post as an unordered collection of words (Baeza-Yates & Ribeiro-Neto, 2011). In particular, we used traditional text preprocessing elements, such as tokenizing the text (i.e., splitting the text into a sequence of tokens using nonletter characters), transforming the text into lowercase words (i.e., to remove case sensitivity), removing stop words (i.e., typically uninformative words such as “the,” “to,” “of,” etc.), stemming the words to reduce the words to their root form (for example, both “fishing” and “fishes” would be reduced to their base form of “fish”). Finally, we created n-grams of tokens (i.e., common sequences of n words) in the document. The set of resulting terms (including single words and n-grams) represented a common feature vector for each document, where the feature values for each document were assigned based on the commonly used TF-IDF approach (Baeza-Yates & Ribeiro-Neto, 2011).

Selecting the most informative features: Bag-ofwords text processing can typically generate many features (e.g., many thousands). Therefore, we used the information gain criterion (Mitchell, 1997). The information gain criterion originated from information theory and is used in determining which features are important in predicting a specific class. It determines how well a feature separates the training data from its target classification (Mitchell, 1997). We used this method to select the 1,100 most informative features, chosen empirically as the number of features that gave the best performance during the parameter optimization step.

Building the predictive model: Following the standard predictive modeling procedure in machine learning (Bishop, 2006), we split the manually coded data into training and validation datasets. We used many different machine learning classification techniques to build predictive models on the training dataset and evaluated their performance on the validation dataset. The naive Bayes classification technique (Bishop, 2006) provided the best classification performance in predicting the nature of the posts and was selected for deployment. The naive Bayes classifier is a machine learning technique that uses the Bayes theorem to guide its predictions. Using conditional probabilities, the model predicts a target classification given the occurrence of observed characteristics (Mitchell, 1997). Using this method, we achieved an accuracy of 89% in accurately classifying the content of the posts (i.e., informational vs. emotional) and 86% in classifying the style (i.e., formal vs. informal) of the posts.

Deploying the classification model: The naive Bayes model was then applied to automatically code the complete dataset of the 29,367 Facebook posts. The nature of posts and the users’ engagement with them on a firm’s Facebook page may be influenced by the characteristics of the product page. We classified product pages as having utilitarian or hedonic characteristics (Utilitarian) by using principal component analysis (PCA). Products are often evaluated along three binary dimensions: (1) risk in the purchase process, (2) having sensory characteristics, and (3) having instrumental characteristics (Eisend, 2009; Lee & Mason, 1999; Schmidt & Spreng, 1996; Zhang & Zinkhan, 2006). A product’s risk is considered to be high if the purchase is an important decision that requires a lot of thought and there is a lot to lose if a wrong choice is made, as the product may be expensive (Schmidt & Spreng, 1996). A product is considered to have high sensory characteristics if the purchase decision is based on effects such as appearance, taste, touch, smell or sound, or attitude or holistic feeling toward the product and if the product’s utility is based on the consumer’s assessment of how beautiful, soothing, or pleasing the product will be and how much pleasure one will get from the product (Eisend, 2009; Zhang & Zinkhan, 2006). A product is considered to be high in instrumental characteristics if the purchase decision is based on a logical thought process, if the product’s utility is based on the consumer’s assessment of how safe, ordered, functional, and clean the product is, and if the consumer believes that the practical value of the product is high (Eisend, 2009; Lee & Mason, 1999).

Though products are evaluated along the dimensions of risk in the purchase process, sensory characteristics, and instrumental characteristics, there is often significant overlap among these three dimensions. In particular, products considered hedonic (e.g., beer) are often low in risk, high in sensory characteristics, and low in instrumental characteristics, whereas utilitarian products (e.g., health insurance) tend to be high in risk, low in sensory characteristics, and high in instrumental characteristics. Thus, we used PCA to classify products as being utilitarian or hedonic. Three co-authors independently coded each product as being of high/low risk, high/low in sensory characteristics, and high/low in instrumental characteristics. After the first round of coding, any disagreements were resolved through discussion. We then used principal component analysis to aggregate the three dimensions to classify a product as hedonic or utilitarian. In our assessments, pages were independently assigned separate dummy variables for hedonic and utilitarian characteristics, allowing for pages to have elements of both of these dimensions. In our PCA analysis, the first principal component explained about 78% of the variation in the data, which is highly consistent with the idea that there is significant overlap among the risk, sensory, and instrumental dimensions of products.

## 3.4 Control Variables

We controlled for post-, page-, and firm-level variables. A post with more comments may have more positive, negative, or neutral comments. Thus, we controlled for the total number of comments (Total\_Comments) on a post. Since the complexity of the posts on a page could influence the number of comments, we controlled for the Automated Readability Index (ARI) of posts. The ARI is a readability test designed to assess the understandability of a text. The ARI formula outputs a number that approximates the grade level needed to comprehend the text. For example, if the ARI outputs the number 10, this equates to a high school student, aged 15-16 years old; similarly, the number 3 means that students in 3rd grade (aged 8-9 years old) should be able to comprehend the text. Thus, the ARI assesses the complexity of a post; the higher the ARI, the more complex the post is in terms of comprehension (Kitchenham et al., 2008). The number of comments on a post is also likely to be influenced by the number of users the post was exposed to and whether the post was a promoted post. We controlled for the likelihood that the company promoted the post and the estimated number of views of the post using data from a proprietary social media analytics platform (Promoted\_Probability, Impressions). The number of comments on a post may be influenced by the presence of a photo, link, or video on the post. Thus, we controlled for the type of content on a post using a content type factor variable (Content Type Dummy). Thus, the ARI of the post, post views/impressions, promoted probability, and the content type factor variable represent the post-level control variables.

Since it is likely older and more established product pages on Facebook are likely to have more users and receive more comments, compared to more recently created pages, we controlled for the number of “page likes” on the first day of data collection (Starting\_Likes), which served as a proxy for the longevity and installed user base of the page. Also, since the firm-generated posts on a product page may be influenced by product characteristics, we controlled for whether the product was hedonic/utilitarian (Utilitarian).

We also controlled for the firm size, as larger firms may have bigger audiences and receive more comments on their posts from Facebook users. We used the firm’s annual revenue as a proxy for the firm’s size (Revenue).<sup>7</sup> Firms may have firm-specific ways of posting on their pages. Thus, we controlled for their tendency to post by controlling for the proportion of formal (style) and informational (content) posts on the company’s main page (Main\_Formal\_Prop, Main\_Informational\_Prop). Firms may also have specific ways of posting on their product pages that differ from how they post on their main page. Thus, we also controlled for the proportion of formal and informational posts on their product pages (Product\_Formal\_Prop,

Product\_Informational\_Prop). A strong online brand may receive more comments on its Facebook pages. We thus controlled for the strength of the online presence in terms of the Alexa Rank of the firm’s main website (ALEXA). <sup>8</sup> Thus, the proportion of formal and informational posts on the main page, the proportion of formal and informational posts on the product pages, revenue, and the Alexa Rank represent the firm-level control variables. The day of the week (Day) on which a post was published was also controlled for, as posts on different days may receive a different number of comments. ## 4 Empirical Analysis

Our primary hypotheses posit that the nature (i.e., style and content) of posts influences user engagement. We used the number of positive, negative, and neutral comments on a post as our dependent variables. Given that the dependent variables are counts, we initially considered Poisson and negative binomial regression models. For all key dependent variables, the standard deviation was larger than the mean; thus, there was overdispersion in the data that made the negative binomial models more appropriate. Therefore, we present their results in the paper. We present panel models with the firm as the panel variable, as the errors across posts on different product pages of a company may be correlated.

H1 and H2 suggested that formal and informational posts may be associated with more favorable engagement for firms selling utilitarian products and that informal and emotional posts may be associated with more favorable engagement for pages with hedonic characteristics. Our initial exploratory analysis of firms’ posting behavior provides empirical support for the specific formulation of these hypotheses. Specifically, the majority (58-63%) of the firms’ posts consistently fell into two (out of four possible) content-style categories, i.e., informationalformal and emotional-informal posts, regardless of whether we aggregated data across all product pages (see Table 3) or looked at pages of utilitarian vs. hedonic products separately (see Tables 4-5). However, the relative proportion of these two major post categories (informational-formal vs. emotionalinformal) differed substantially depending on the product characteristics. Firm pages of utilitarian products had more informational-formal posts than any other type of post (see Table 4), while firm pages of hedonic products had more emotional-informal posts (see Table 5) than any other type of post.

To examine whether the relationship between the nature of the post and user engagement is contingent on product characteristics, the regressions include the dummy variables for whether a post is formal (vs. informal) and informational (vs. emotional). We also used an indicator variable to determine whether the post was on a product page with utilitarian (vs. hedonic) characteristics and interaction terms to distinguish between the nature of the post and the product type indicator variable. To evaluate H1 and H2, we examined the interaction terms between the nature of the post (style and content) and the producttype indicator variable. Table 6 presents the analysis of the number of positive, negative, and neutral comments. As is well known, in contrast to linear models, in nonlinear models, moderating effects cannot be evaluated by examining the interaction coefficients (Karaca-Mandic et al., 2012). Thus, to evaluate our hypotheses, we examined the average marginal effects (see Table 7) of formal and informational posts on the number of positive, negative, and neutral comments for utilitarian and hedonic products. In Table 7, each cell represents the change in the number of positive (negative or neutral) comments if a post were to be changed from informal to formal (or from emotional to informational).

In Table 7, the row for product pages with utilitarian content motivations indicates that if a post is changed from an informal to a formal post, the number of positive comments decreases by 10.1%. This is inconsistent with H2. Similarly, if a post is changed from emotional to informational, the number of negative comments increases by 4.2%, and the number of neutral comments decreases by 9.2%. This result is contradictory to H1.

Next, we examine the average marginal effects of formal and informational posts on hedonic product pages. The row for hedonic products indicates that if a post is changed from informal to formal, the number of negative comments increases by 2.5% and the number of neutral comments decreases by 3.1%. This is consistent with H2. However, if a post is changed from emotional to informational, negative comments decrease by 3.0%, and positive and neutral comments increase by 5.9% and 7.6%, respectively. This result is the opposite of H1.

To summarize, regarding post style, the analysis suggests that informal posts generate fewer negative comments and more neutral comments for hedonic product pages. This is consistent with H2. However, inconsistent with H2, we found that formal posts reduce the number of positive comments on utilitarian product pages. This suggests that, in the Facebook environment, informal language is associated with more favorable engagement for hedonic as well as utilitarian product pages.

## 4.1 Robustness Analysis

In our main analyses, we used the number of comments (rather than Likes or Shares) as the measure of engagement, as comments require more cognitive effort from the user and thus arguably represent a more substantive form of engagement (Yang et al., 2019). However, we performed additional analysis with alternative outcome measures, such as total reactions, number of reactions (Likes), number of reactions (Love), number of reactions (Angry), and Shares. The results of the analysis (see Table A1 in the Appendix) are consistent with the results in Tables 6 and 7. Specifically, the number of reactions (Likes) decreased by 4.3% when a post changed from informal to formal on hedonic product pages. The number of reactions (Love) increased by 9.3% when a post changed from emotional to informational on hedonic product pages. The number of reactions (Love) decreased by 10.8% when a post changed from informal to formal on hedonic product pages. The number of reactions (Angry) decreased by 8.2% when a post changed from emotional to informational on hedonic product pages. The number of reactions (Angry) increased by 15.3% when a post changed from informal to formal on hedonic product pages. The number of Shares increased by 4.8% on hedonic product pages when a post changed from informal to formal on hedonic product pages. Overall, these findings are generally consistent with the findings in our main analysis.

## 5 Discussion and Conclusion

Social media platforms like Facebook are quickly becoming the lifeblood of many firms’ customer engagement strategies. This fervent adoption is not surprising, as recent work suggests that even industrial goods brands can benefit from using social media channels (Bill et al., 2020). While emerging information systems and marketing literature is identifying how firms should behave on these platforms (Lee et al., 2018; Meire et al., 2019), there is little research on how product characteristics influence this behavior. The present research seeks to address this gap in the literature by using the concept of content motivations (Heijden, 2004) as a lens to understand how product characteristics drive content strategy.

To address this important empirical question, our work integrates the emerging content strategy in IS and marketing (Lee et al., 2018; Meire et al., 2019; Tellis et al., 2019) with IS literature on content motivations (Salehan et al., 2017; Wu & Lu, 2013; Xu et al., 2012). More specifically, we examine how the relationship between the nature of firms’ posts and user engagement is contingent on the utilitarian or hedonic characteristics of the product page and user content motivations. This line of inquiry is essential, as the post and user engagement relationship likely depends on the product characteristics and users’ content motivations. With this goal in mind, our analysis is based on the data from product pages, as opposed to company posts and user comments on other pages (e.g., main page, careers page, community involvement page, etc.).

Regarding the content of firms’ posts, we hypothesized that on the product pages of utilitarian products, users will have utilitarian content motivations and will thus expect informational posts; therefore, informational posts will generate more favorable engagement. In contrast, on the product pages of hedonic products, users will typically have more hedonic content motivations and expect emotional posts; therefore, we hypothesized that emotional posts will be associated with more favorable engagement. However, our analysis actually revealed the opposite findings. We found that for the product pages of utilitarian products, informational posts generated more negative and fewer neutral comments than emotional posts. In contrast, on the product pages of hedonic products, informational posts were associated with more positive and neutral comments and fewer negative comments.

Regarding the style of firms’ posts, we hypothesized that a style that matches the content motivations of the product page will be associated with more favorable engagement. In this case, users would expect formal posts on product pages with utilitarian content motivations (i.e., on utilitarian product pages), which, in turn, would generate more favorable engagement. Users would expect informal posts on pages with hedonic content motivations (i.e., on hedonic product pages), which would be associated with more favorable engagement. Our results indicate that compared to formal posts, informal posts are indeed associated with fewer negative and more neutral comments on product pages with hedonic characteristics. However, we also found that formal posts on product pages with utilitarian content motivations reduced the number of positive comments. One plausible explanation is that Facebook is a social media channel and the platform’s unwritten protocol of communication largely emphasizes informality (Salehan et al., 2017). This informality may be an unwritten communication rule for the platform. Because the nature of the platform is more informal and conversational, we found that all product page types increased engagement through convergence with the informal style of the platform.

In general, we found that firms’ posting behavior is consistent with our hypothesized expectations: on hedonic product pages, there were more informalemotional posts than posts of any other type, and on utilitarian product pages, there were more formalinformational posts than of any other type. However, user engagement with the posts exhibited specific key differences from the hypothesized expectations, especially in the content dimension. In other words, firms may not be completely justified in their chosen posting behavior. It is important to understand the psychological reasons why user engagement increases in the presence of incongruities.

One possible reason that incongruity is so effective is that the social media environment is unlike traditional customer engagement channels. More specifically, the amount of content a user can interact with on these platforms is immense. Before the advent of social media, consumers’ attention was spread across a finite set of print, radio, and TV mediums. Today, consumer attention is split across a more comprehensive array of platforms, all competing for consumers’ finite amount of attention (Athey et al., 2016). This intense informational environment adheres to many of the principles of the attention economy, initially discussed by Herbert Simon, in which the overabundance of information causes a poverty of attention (Simon, 1971). Organizations must find unique ways to capture consumers’ attention, as the environment is highly competitive. Research focusing on traditional advertising settings like print, television, and radio has shown that one way to accomplish this is to adopt an incongruous advertising strategy to break through message clutter (Torn et al., 2007), as it attracts attention and increases users’ engagement with the message (Heckler & Childers, 1992).

Heckler and Childers, 1992 contend that incongruency attracts attention and increases users’ engagement with a message. When a user is presented with incongruent, unexpected information, the user may be more aroused, i.e., the user may become more alert and attentive. The most effective means of reducing arousal is to explore the source of the incongruity (Berlyne, 1972). Thus, when a follower of a utilitarian product page is presented with an emotional post, or when a user on a hedonic product page is presented with an informational post, the user is aroused and pays more attention to the post, which results in more favorable engagement. Arguably, users follow product pages primarily for their content motivations; thus, incongruities in the content dimension may cause arousal and generate more favorable engagement. Further studies on why incongruency in the content of the post increases engagement would be an interesting direction for future research.

Regarding the results on message style, we hypothesized that matching the post style to product characteristics would be associated with more favorable engagement. However, the results suggest that informal posts are associated with more favorable engagement regardless of the product type. In line with the predictions of communication accommodation theory (CAT), this suggests that convergence with the linguistic style of the platform audience is associated with more favorable engagement than matching the style to the characteristics of the product. Computermediated communication (CMC) studies have noted that social network platforms like Facebook exhibit a phenomenon of context collapse (Gil-Lopez et al., 2018). Context collapse occurs when many smaller networks of diverse individuals are joined together into a larger network, leading to a flattened, more general context (Androutsopoulos, 2014; Brandtzaeg & Lüders, 2018; Gil-Lopez et al., 2018). As these networks collapse, users will adapt their language style to accommodate this more general audience (Brandtzaeg & Lüders, 2018; Gil-Lopez et al., 2018).

Language accommodation is derived from CAT, which describes how individuals use style strategically in their communications to build, maintain, or distance themselves in interpersonal relationships (Giles & St. Clair, 1979). This theory emphasizes that individuals may try to change their communicative style to be similar to or different from the person they are communicating with (Giles & St. Clair, 1979). In the social media context, CAT has two strategies that can be employed: maintenance and convergence (Barcelos et al., 2018; Gretry et al., 2017; Ludwig et al., 2013). Maintenance occurs when an organization already matches the style of the focal user, thus further building common ground (Giles & St. Clair, 1979). Convergence occurs when an organization changes its typical style to match that of the user they are communicating with (Giles & St. Clair, 1979). As informal language has been shown to be the linguistic style of the context-collapsed Facebook platform (Gil-Lopez et al., 2018), matching the style of posts to that of the users on the platform may result in higher levels of favorable engagement (Jakic et al., 2017). This potentially explains why informal posts are associated with more favorable engagement, regardless of content motivation. While this reasoning seems plausible, further experimental work on this topic is needed to better understand why this occurs in online social media contexts.

This study has important implications for practice. As noted above, firms generally appear to be applying old advertising tactics to new digital platforms. Whereas old methods of content motivations would dictate that messages should be congruent with motivations, the highly competitive nature of these new platforms may demand otherwise. Thus, firms that manage product pages with utilitarian content motivations may need to depart from the norm and post more content that is emotion focused. Similarly, firms that manage pages with hedonic characteristics may need to post more informational content. Additionally, at least on Facebook, informal-style posts generate more favorable engagement than formal posts, making the typical formal business linguistic style less effective.

In addition to providing new, alternate strategies for social media managers, our work also highlights an important element that managers should use to drive their posting strategies—content motivations (Salehan et al., 2017; Wu & Lu, 2013; Xu et al., 2012). Although this element has long been a fixture in IS research, these motivations may not be immediately salient to social media practitioners. By identifying the content motivations of the customers following a certain page (e.g., based on the characteristics of a product that the page represents), managers can better plan their content to more effectively elicit on-platform engagement from their followers. Furthermore, practitioners can judge whether the content type of the post that they plan to share on their page is incongruous with the expected content motivations of the page followers. Similarly, regardless of the product characteristics, firms should keep the language of the shared content informal, as it converges with the prevalent style of popular social media platforms.

This study has some limitations that suggest directions for future research. First, the primary analysis in this paper is at the level of individual posts, i.e., how the style and content of a post influence post-level engagement. For a more aggregate analysis of engagement (e.g., total engagement across the firm’s entire presence on social media on different pages and platforms), it is likely that a portfolio-based approach would need to be considered. Research using a portfolio-based approach will need to take into account both the hedonic and utilitarian motivations of users, as well as the affordances of the platform, and evaluate how they can be utilized to best orient the collective mix of posts. As many organizations and researchers are examining video platforms like YouTube (Susarla et al., 2012), Twitch (Zhao et al., 2021), and TikTok (Yang et al., 2021), an integrated approach involving many IS theories will need to be applied to help address this research question.

Secondly, this work also seeks to understand how content characteristics influence engagement in terms of onplatform outcomes, like comments and Likes/reactions (shown in Table A1 in the Appendix). Another type of engagement by users is that of more passive personal engagement (Pagani & Mirabello, 2011), in which users are simply engaging in viewing content. Understanding what content is more viewable can help improve the design of algorithms that recommend content on social media platforms. Also, in this study, we did not account for off-platform outcomes, like purchases or conversion on specific actions. This, too, would be an interesting avenue for future work. As much of the emerging research in content engineering evaluates active interaction on posts (i.e., Lee et al., 2018), understanding how content directly relates to off-platform outcomes (Goh et al., 2013) could directly impact company revenue and other organizational goals.

In summary, this work seeks to integrate fundamental business research with emerging research on digital content strategy. We provide robust evidence that favorable user engagement on social media product pages is related to the content and style of the messages that firms share, depending on the focal product characteristics. Specifically, whereas more traditional work would suggest that organizations should share content that is congruent with the product (Johar and Sirgy, 1991; and Sirgy et al. 1997), we found that posting incongruent content on social media can lead to higher levels of favorable engagement. Similarly, we found that convergence with the linguistic style of the audience is associated with more favorable engagement than matching the style to the characteristics of the product. As these digital channels are omnipresent and represent a critical aspect in the engagement efforts of organizations, it behooves researchers to better understand how the nature of the posts can facilitate online engagement for an array of products. We hope that this study sparks further research in this direction.

## Appendix B: Representativeness and Power of the Sample

We sought to understand better whether our sample provides an adequate representation of firms in terms of their industries. To demonstrate this, we evaluated how the NAICS industry distribution of the 49 firms in our sample compares to the distribution of NAICS code distribution in the most recent Fortune 500 list (from 2022), and we found that there are only very small differences between the two distributions. Some of the 2-digit NAICS categories are not in our sample (i.e., 11, 21, 49, 55, 61, 71). However, these make up only about 6.5% of the total population of companies on the list. All other NAICS categories have a representation in our dataset to a very similar degree except for 51 (information) and 32, 33 (manufacturing). Information is slightly overrepresented, and manufacturing is slightly underrepresented. Overall, the industry distribution of data used in our analysis is highly representative of the Fortune 500 list as of 2022 (see Table B1).

To quantify more formally how well our selected sample matches the most recent distribution of the Fortune 500 companies with Facebook pages, we assessed the Bhattacharyya coefficient (Bhattacharyya, 1946), which explains how close a distribution of categorical values matches another. A value of 1 indicates a perfect match, and 0 indicate no overlap. After conducting this analysis, we found that the Bhattacharyya coefficient was 0.89, suggesting that our sample of companies indeed has a very significant overlap (in terms of their industry representation) with the actual Fortune 500 population that had Facebook pages in 2022.

Next, we assessed whether the sample has enough power to detect the effects that we find in our model. To calculate this, we used the effect size from a similar study (Lee et al., 2018), which found an effect size (measured via R2) of 0.44. Our sample is at the same level of analysis (i.e., post-level); with our sample (containing 29,367 data points with 26 predictors), we found that the power of detecting the same effect is 1. This suggests that our sample is more than adequate to detect the effect we found in the manuscript.

## About the Authors

Scott Schanke is an assistant professor of information technology management at the University of Wisconsin-Milwaukee’s Lubar College of Business. His research seeks to identify and quantify effective approaches to the design and implementation of the entities that represent firms and brands in customer interactions, e.g., AI-enabled conversational agents and digital brand personalities. To causally identify the merits and detriments of alternative design characteristics, he has employed a combination of controlled laboratory experiments, econometrics, as well as field experiments in partnership with several U.S.-based firms. He has published his research in Information Systems Research and Journal of the Association for Information Systems. He received his PhD in information systems from the University of Minnesota’s Carlson School of Management in 2021.

Gautam Ray is a professor at the Carlson School of Management at the University of Minnesota. His research interest are in the areas of the impact of IT on firm scope and structure and how IT creates value. His research has appeared in the Academy of Management Journal, Communications of the ACM, Information Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, Journal of Operations Management, Management Science, Marketing Science, MIS Quarterly, and the Strategic Management Journal. He received his PhD from Ohio State University in 2000. Currently, he serves as the department chair of the Information and Decision Sciences Department at the Carlson School.

Gediminas Adomavicius is a professor of information and decision sciences at the University of Minnesota, where he also holds the Larson Endowed Chair for Excellence in Business Education. He received his PhD in computer science from New York University. His research interests include recommender systems, machine learning, and electronic market mechanisms. His work has been published in leading information systems and computer science journals. He received the NSF CAREER Award for his research on recommender systems, has served as senior editor for Information Systems Research and MIS Quarterly, and is a Distinguished Fellow of the INFORMS Information Systems Society.

Mihir Wagle is currently working as a software developer at MasterClass, an ed-tech startup in San Francisco. Prior to that, he was a part of the M365 group at Microsoft. His work has been published in MIS Quarterly. His research interests question how IT affects the nature of work and the impact of social media on corporate engagement. He received his master’s degree from Purdue University in 2010 and his PhD from the University of Minnesota in 2016.
