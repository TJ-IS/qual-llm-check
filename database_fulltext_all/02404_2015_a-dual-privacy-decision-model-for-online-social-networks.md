## Abstract

Privacy concerns in online social networks (OSN) use include the desire to control both information and interaction leading to the “dual privacy decision” in which individuals choose what information to release and who may view it. OSN privacy controls now offer more choices. We assess motivations that may override a user’s concept of privacy, and present four key motivations for OSN use: ability to (1) seek information, (2) socialize, (3) to express oneself to others, and (4) meet social expectations or please others. We find that the calculus constructs influence the manner in which OSN users handle different information categories.

KEYWORDS: online social networks, privacy calculus, privacy tradeoff, interaction management, information management, scale development

## Introduction

Technology has been shown to shift attitudes and expectations of privacy [29]. The growth of digital communication and the rise of online social networks (OSNs), defined as technologies that support online relationships with others and selfdisclosure behaviors [15], allow for a form of near constant social interaction and information exchange. It has been proposed that “privacy might be a problem for anyone who leads a life mediated in part by digital technologies” [41]. Today’s youth, including the generation currently entering adulthood, are the first to have been exposed to such technologies throughout their lives. As a matter of fact, “young people will be the first to experience the aggregated effect of living a digital mediated life, with the corresponding creating of various identities and digital dossiers over a long period of time” [35]. Although empirical results to this end are lacking, it has been suggested that young people show less concern about their privacy [14, 38, 41]. Another way to look at the phenomenon would be to consider that as technology shifts a greater portion of our socialization and relationship management onto the Internet, those growing up under this paradigm may be developing new views on privacy, which may not be viewed strictly as being less privacy oriented. Moreover, those living a digitally mediated life may benefit from a discussion of alternatives and more granular approaches to managing privacy online. Thus, the way we examine privacy may benefit from new approaches, especially for OSNs. The present study examines information disclosure and interaction management in the digital space mediated by OSNs such as Facebook, Twitter, Instagram, YouTube, etc. [30, 35, 46, 54] by using classical social psychology theory adapted for the OSN environment.

Concepts from a classical exposition on privacy by Laufer and Wolfe [29] have been used widely in many studies throughout subsequent decades [8, 11-13, 19]. Among other contributions, Laufer and Wolfe’s paper suggested two crucial and intertwined principles that we will use in the current study. First, they considered privacy as an “interpersonal concept” and suggested that the concept of privacy is realized and experienced on a daily basis through acts of managing relationships [29]. Second, they suggested that there exist conditions under which an individual’s privacy concept could be supplanted that are situation-specific. In the privacy literature, this is often referred to as the privacy calculus. That is, an individual has a concept of privacy (which we suggest is his or her desire to manage his or her information and interaction) that indicates how he or she will behave in a particular situation. This privacy concept may be superseded in this situation given a reward for violating his or her privacy concept that is more valued. Our study will explore this phenomenon in the OSN context. Hence, our study contributes to the literature in two distinct ways: 1) we explore the privacy concept through managing information and interaction, which is a novel approach in the information systems (IS) literature and 2) we explore the privacy tradeoff using this novel privacy concept and the privacy calculus scales modified for OSNs.

Laufer and Wolfe [29] suggested that a relationship, as an actionable event, consists of controlling or managing two elements: information and interaction. We argue that technology, in the form of OSNs, renders decisions related to the control of these two elements increasingly important and more tangible. Social networking sites are increasingly moving towards a realization of what we refer to as a “dual privacy decision.” The “dual privacy decision” on an OSN is illustrated in Figure 1. In this example, the user is posting a status update (i.e. information – “Let’s go Argentina!”) to an audience (i.e. interaction - “Only Me”).

There are three unique aspects to the “dual privacy decision” on an OSN that warrant more discussion because they distinguish the OSN context from a typical individualto-business information release. Information privacy has been studied broadly in the IS field. For comprehensive reviews, see [5, 47, 48]. Many traditional studies of privacy in the IS literature have focused on the exchange of information between consumers and businesses [11, 12, 34, 48]. This exchange is very important, but quite different in its considerations from the privacy decisions in social computing.

First, let us consider the intricacies of information found on an OSN. The information that a user can place in the “update status” field is virtually unrestricted. For example, the user can post a status update about a new song he or she likes, an opinion on a current event, thoughts about how he or she is feeling at the moment, a picture of a new pair of shoes that he or she is considering purchasing, etc. Thus, the perceived sensitivity and future consequence of the released information may vary. While it is certainly possible that users could release no information at all (i.e. not use the OSN or use the site in a strictly information-seeking capacity), thus avoiding the “dual privacy decision” entirely, an important consideration is that OSNs need individuals to release information to

#

thrive. OSN users are trading in information. That is, users expect to see other peoples’ information or they would not use the site. Research has shown that receiving information from friends, staying connected, and posting personal information create a sense of belonging to a community and generate pleasure, which explain the intent to keep using the OSN [31]. Additionally, for information to be available for consumption, some users must release information (generate content). The OSN would not last long if it could not encourage people to generate content. Therefore, in an OSN environment, it is in everyone’s interest to promote the exchange of information. This suggests that an exploration of granular information/interaction decisions on OSNs is crucial and leads to the following research question:

## RQ1: How can we classify types of information release behaviors on OSN platforms?

Second, let us explore the concept of interaction management on an OSN. On current OSNs (such as the Facebook example in Figure 1), the user may specify the audience permitted to view the post. In an online environment, the immediately reachable audience for any released information is potentially very large. This characteristic makes it more difficult to retract or minimize consequences of information release. Choosing a restricted subset of individuals as an initial audience does not prevent information leakage (i.e. a third party gaining access to the information), but it may at least slow the spread of the information. Thus, opportunities to manage the audience may add another layer to privacy management on an OSN. In fact, many OSNs now allow for more flexible control over the audience with whom a user initially shares information. Therefore, OSN users must now often make very conscious choices about which information to release and to whom. This is a “dual privacy decision” that incorporates the management of both elements of the relationship specified by Laufer and Wolfe and leads us to the following research question:

RQ2: What are the different effects of the perceived need for information and interaction control on how individuals manage their OSN data?

The proceeding discussion provides an illustration of the complexities of privacy decisions on an OSN. Our study will examine OSN privacy behaviors given a piece of information to release and options to manage the audience that has initial access to that information. We believe that by exploring the information–interaction management interplay, we can provide insights into the design of social platforms that could encourage greater levels of interaction, while providing a level of comfort to the users about their privacy in terms of their control over relationships. Furthermore, our study provides the opportunity to examine the influence of a user’s concept of privacy on information/interaction decisions using specific types of information in an environment (OSN) that may provide rewards that supersede the privacy concept. It is to this privacy calculus that we now turn.

#

The third consideration is not unique to OSNs, but the form it takes is unique to each context. Consider that if the audience for a status update is limited, not only are some disadvantages of the OSN possibly reduced (i.e. less likelihood of an unwanted party accessing the information), but some benefits of the OSN may also be constrained (e.g. socialization is reduced because less people have the opportunity to participate in a conversation the status update stimulates). Therefore, it is possible that a user may so desire socialization that he or she will override his or her privacy concept (i.e. desire to manage information or interaction). The concept of “calculus of behavior” put forth by Laufer and Wolfe [29] has been utilized widely in the IS literature. This concept presupposes a rational decision process to determine whether the benefits gained by disclosing certain information to others are sufficient to supersede the user’s need for privacy, under the constraint that the consequences of the information release may be difficult or impossible to determine a priori. The benefits that may be gained through information release and interaction are unique to the context. This leads to our final research question:

RQ3: What relevant sub-dimensions of perceived benefits should be included in a privacy calculus model for OSNs? Theory and Construct Development

## Psychology of the Dual Privacy Decision

Privacy concerns can be viewed as detrimental to the core functioning of OSNs because an OSN’s main function is to promote communication and facilitate relationships. Krasnova et al., Xu et al., and Ku et al. showed that privacy concern hinders self-disclosure [27, 53] and the continued use of OSNs [28]. Private people are less likely to be content generators and, therefore, do not help attract people to the site. It has been demonstrated that privacy concern may be mitigated by the availability of privacy controls [27] and the resulting perceived control [53]. Thus, we argue that it is preferable for OSNs to examine privacy through the lens of relationship management rather than as a concern. This approach allows for greater granularity in the exploration. Additionally, we consider that the degree of control one imposes over their relationships is one way to measure one’s internal privacy concept, otherwise called interpersonal privacy identity (IPI) [24].

Relationships on OSNs can be managed in two primary ways. First, one can control what information is disclosed. That is, an individual can evaluate the information being considered for release, determine if he or she would like to keep it secret, and then choose not to release it at all. Previous research has explored the concept of information control [13]. Second, some OSNs offer the capability to control (the effectiveness of which can be argued) the scope of information release. In other words, an individual can control who gets to see the released information, as well as his or her interaction with other users on the OSN. We will adopt the term “dual privacy decision” to indicate the consideration of (1) whether to release a particular piece of information and (2) the audience to whom it may ultimately be released.

#

Many actions can be taken to enhance information and interaction boundaries on an OSN. For example, an individual may choose not to be online friends with some users and, thus, restrict such users from accessing his or her online information from the source. Less drastically, many social networking environments allow users to choose a group (or individual) that may view some information, whereas people outside that selection are unable to view this content even if they are friends. Facebook and Google+, for example, offer privacy control choices that allow a user to restrict the viewership of most content in this manner. Studies have categorized these options and referred to the various categories as “boundary” options [26, 51]. Owing to the fluid nature of social networks, this taxonomy has been difficult to maintain [51]. In the current study, we do not explore choices involving friend selection (adding/deleting friends or accepting/denying friend requests) or other specific tools that may be provided by various OSNs, which are demonstrably quite fluid. Rather, when examining behaviors, we consider the use of OSN-provided tools that allow a user to simultaneously release information and specify the audience that can access said information. This is a much more granular decision than the overall use of software controls to manage privacy. An appropriate analogy would be to consider each decision an “information transaction.” We argue that methods to manage this “dual privacy decision” will continue to evolve and that by exploring the decision at this level of granularity, we provide a more generalizable view.

Laufer and Wolfe [29] suggest that “privacy as interaction management is actually a form of noninteraction with specified other(s)….” They describe information management as the traditional view of privacy concerning whether to disclose personal information. An individual’s IPI is manifested in his or her perception of the self-desire to control his or her information and interactions. This desire is eventually realized via the actions the user takes on the OSN, as described above, to control his or her information and interaction. Therefore, we define two constructs for representing the manifestations of an individual’s IPI:

Information Management: refers to the desire of an individual to control the disclosure of his or her information.

Interaction Management: refers to the desire of an individual to control his or her interaction with others.

Releasing information online may have negative outcomes. These negative outcomes are many and research has shown they are often hard for people to extrapolate [29, 47]. Therefore, we consciously limit the cost to simply the disclosure of (or relinquishment of control over) information, thereby leaving the ultimate outcomes of the release to the user for consideration (knowledge of which may shape the privacy identity of the user) because such a model is a straightforward representation of the tradeoff and has been employed commonly in past research [11, 12]. In developing the items for the privacy calculus, we followed the example of previous work and specified in the item that the cost to obtain the benefit was the release of information (e.g. I would release information on Facebook in order to socialize with other people.).

## Towards a Privacy Calculus: Defining Benefits of Social Media Participation

There may exist benefits for releasing information to other individuals that may supplant an individual’s privacy concept. In the IS literature, the benefits for releasing personal information that may override privacy concern have been examined in relationship to e-commerce transactions [8, 9, 11, 12]. This tradeoff is often referred to as the “privacy calculus” [8, 9, 11, 12]. The release of information in a social environment is usually not without expectation. Notably, Posey et al. [44] show that some reciprocity is expected (e.g., I tell you something about myself to help establish a friendship).

Social computing technologies promote a digital representation of community, or as Lowry et al. [32] stated, they “create or recreate social conventions and social contexts.” As OSNs have evolved from dyadic exchanges (i.e., email between two people) to publicly viewable community discussions, OSN platforms have been likened to the local town hangout in earlier decades [45], and are widely leveraged for multiple purposes. OSN use is usually motivated by the goals of information seeking, socialization (interpersonal communication or relationship management), and/or self-expression (identity formation). These three goals are often driven by a desire to create or enforce relationships and intimacy, to be entertained, and/or to achieve microcelebrity status [20, 22, 35, 52].

Information seeking (also referred to as “content consumption” and “information acquisition,” e.g. [37, 39]) was the primary use of the Internet when most users were not content generators but rather used the Internet to locate desired content provided by those technologically savvy enough or with the resources to create a webpage. In the OSN sphere, people who log on to sites for nothing other than examining others’ posts, comments, photos, etc., are often referred to as lurkers or voyeurs. This type of activity does not require any exchange of personal information with other people (information flow is one-way toward the lurker), but only minimal interaction with the OSN company in order to log in.

Self-expression and socialization require the release of information about oneself (self-disclosure) to a somewhat public sphere and may result in microcelebrity status, entertainment, or the creation/reinforcement of intimacy and relationships. It has been suggested that self-disclosure is beneficial to the creation of relationships with others [3, 10, 18, 32, 42, 44] because “it reduces stress, builds intimacy, and increases social approval for one’s ideas” [32]. Early forms of social networking sites were seen primarily as a way to advertise oneself, perhaps with the intention of encouraging dyadic communication or simply for voyeurism. This is illustrated by the original form of Facebook, for example, which used a university campus look book as its model (a book of pictures, names, and limited biographical information about students for accelerating a sense of community among Freshmen). As OSNs have become more sophisticated they have grown to encourage group/community socialization, media-rich information exchange,

#

careful presentation and maintenance of an online identity, and frequent selfexpression. Consider, for example, the introduction of Google+ “hang-outs,” the intention of which is to replicate online the congregation places in the physical world.

This discussion suggests that the primary functions of OSNs can be categorized as information seeking, socialization (formation and maintenance of relationships), and self-expression (identity formation). Although we do not expect the information-seeking motivation to influence information release, we develop a privacy calculus that explores the influence of information seeking, socialization, and self-expression as motivations for individuals high on control (information and interaction) to override their IPI and distribute information more broadly. To this end, we develop a set of items for three constructs that focus on the cost (release of information) versus the benefit of the desired activity. Specifically, would an individual release information online in order to be able to seek information, socialize, or express his or herself? To develop the items, we follow frameworks from previous privacy calculus studies [11, 12], but unlike previous studies that focused on e-commerce applications, we substitute the relationship control view of privacy (IPI)[24] for the privacy concern construct.

Therefore, we define the following constructs:

Desire for Information Seeking: refers to the willingness of an individual to release information to seek information online.

Desire for Self-Expression: refers to the willingness of an individual to release information to have an online presence (expression, possibly leading to microcelebrity status).

Desire for Socialization: refers to the willingness of an individual to release information to socialize (establish and maintain relationships) online.

A desire to express oneself to others and/or socialize with others constitutes motivating reasons to use an OSN and manifest a desire for social activity. We argue that a fourth factor motivating OSN use is the desire to please others. Socialization is an activity that an individual may derive individual pleasure from or indulge in because of the expectation of benefits to his or herself from exploitation of social connections. By contrast, a person may feel pressure from family, friends, or other important people to share information that will make said people happy. For example, grandparents may live in another location and desire updates and images of grandchildren that a parent may otherwise not post on an OSN but does anyway to please family. We argue that information release encouraged by others essentially fulfills an individual’s intrinsic desire to please. Thus, we define a fourth calculus construct:

Desire to Please Others: refers to the willingness of an individual to release information for pleasing people important to her or him.

## Dependent Variables: Defining OSN Categories of Information/Interaction Management

Facebook provides the typical set of OSN capabilities and currently counts among its members an overwhelming majority of young people: approximately 94% of teens [33]. Although it has been suggested that Facebook is waning in popularity, it is still the most heavily used social networking site. Moreover, because of its critical mass, some users find it difficult to discontinue. However, recent polling shows an upswing in Facebook’s popularity, suggesting resurgence among teens [2]. While it could be argued that Google+ has more sophisticated options (circles, hangouts), it experienced much difficulty building critical mass and, therefore, has less variety in its user base [36]. Reddit, Tumblr, YouTube, Instagram, and Twitter are all growing in popularity, and all of them offer some variety in the form that information seeking, self-expression, and socialization assume, as well as the controls available. We suggest that the theory is applicable to any OSN that serves as a vehicle for information seeking, socialization, pleasing others, and self-expression, but use Facebook as the specific implementation platform for our study owing to its large and diverse user base and the presence of the basic information and interaction control mechanisms.

Using the Facebook operationalization of relationship control, we examined the development of an integrated representation of information and interaction management for our dependent variables. When confronted with a specific privacy decision, an individual finds his or herself facing a situation that combines elements of the two facets of the privacy concept. That is, rather than being a binary decision of interacting with a person versus avoiding them (or releasing information versus not releasing it), at the operational level, this decision becomes blended [21, 26, 50, 51]. An individual decides the extent of the disclosure from both angles (i.e., what information and to whom – including how many). This is especially true in the OSN environment, where software guided decision processes allow you to type in a piece of information and then to choose from a list of options whether to immediately share this information with one friend, a group of friends, anyone in the world who cares to see it, etc. This technology-mediated functional form of information sharing differs from information sharing in the physical world, especially in terms of ease. For example, photocopying a letter and mailing it to many individuals or physically convening/conference calling a group of people to disclose some information requires significantly more effort than posting the same information on an OSN. The trend in social media sites is to integrate the decisions of what information to share and which group to share it with. Since these two elements are components of the overall privacy decision, we chose to explore the development of behavioral measures of constructs for use (dependent variables) that reflect this decision framework and, therefore, the functional form enforced by the platform.

Because we chose Facebook as the OSN platform to be studied, we aimed to create behavioral constructs that reflect privacy behaviors, both in terms of what information is released and to whom, using Facebook’s functional form. Earlier

#

versions of Facebook limited interaction by the use of privacy controls that required a user to go into the settings to implement. More recent versions of Facebook, arguably in response to Google+’s circle feature, allow a user to choose an “audience” each time they post a status update, photos, videos, etc. This is in addition to, and using similar categories to, the privacy control functions available in the Facebook settings. This function allows easy control over who sees a post, and it is arguably more visible and less cumbersome than limiting the audience using the privacy controls available under Facebook settings. Facebook now also allows for the creation of custom groups, whereas in past iterations of the software, groups were defined as, for example, “global,” “friends-of-friends,” etc. These technologies are evolving constantly, and the aforementioned options are relatively new to the platform. Past studies have been conducted using privacy settings as the dependent variables [1, 6], and it was found that few people use them [1]. The intent of this study is to explore the development of constructs that are not so strictly tied to privacy settings, but rather tailored toward audience (interaction) management. Therefore, we decided to tie the behavioral constructs to Facebook’s functional form by presenting the respondent with a type of information and asking them to choose the audience, using generalized Facebook audience options, to which he or she would release said information. Thus, we could mimic the actual decision behavior that users exhibit on Facebook. For example, in Figure 1, a status update about a sporting event is released to an audience of “Only Me.”

Prior work [23] groups information released on OSNs into two primary categories, which can be defined as follows:

Characteristic data: information that describes an individual; this changes infrequently. This data will form a basic historical record of an individual (i.e., date and place of birth, place of education, places of residence over the course of their life). However, when this characteristic information is current, it provides a current demographic picture of the individual and location information. Examples of characteristic data would include birthday, college attending/attended, past employment, current employment, address, hometown, phone number, and email address. In reference to the functional form of OSN software, some of this information would most often be found as part of a profile.

Observational data: information including thoughts, feelings, and opinions, which an individual produces frequently. This data gives insight into who people were over their lifetime (i.e., their opinions over the course of their lifetime, changes in those opinions, types of situations individuals found themselves in, their way of handling said situations, etc.). An analogy would be that this data would form their biography in book form, rather than a courthouse record, for example, opinions on current events, feelings about other people, successes, worries, opinions on popular culture, etc. In reference to the functional form of the OSN software, this information would most often be found as status updates or comments.

We then created a set of OSN information release behaviors by combining generalized audience (i.e., scope of release) options (Nobody; One Person - Private Message; Some People - List or Custom List; My Network – Friends; Everyone - Public or Friends of Friends) with a set of information items commonly considered as either characteristic (birthday, college attending/attended, past employment, current employment, address, hometown, phone number, email address, etc.) or observational (opinions on current events, feelings about other people, success, worries, opinions on popular culture, etc.) data elements. The dependent variables can then be illustrated in a two-dimensional plane, as in Figure 2. This design allows us to capture the “dual privacy decision” as a behavior. We explain the construction of the dependent variables and their placement in Figure 2 in the methodology section.

Although we test it for the sake of confirmation, we do not expect the desire for information seeking to influence information release on Facebook. We do, however, expect the desire to socialize, desire for self-expression, and desire to please others to motivate individual release and possibly override the privacy concept. Thus, we expect the desires to socialize, express oneself, and please others to influence wider information release, which leads us to the following hypotheses:

Hypotheses 11-15: Individuals who would release information to socialize will release more widely contact information, work history, profile information, introspective data, and extrospective data.

Hypotheses 16-20: Individuals who would release information to express themselves will release more widely contact information, work history, profile information, introspective data, and extrospective data.

Hypotheses 21-25: Individuals who would release information to please others will release more widely contact information, work history, profile information, introspective data, and extrospective data. Methods and Analysis Scale Development and Survey Administration

The original survey items for the information and interaction management constructs were obtained from James et al. [24]. The calculus items were based on those presented in Dinev and Hart [11] but reformulated for the OSN context. The initial dependent variables resulted from prior work by James et al. [23] and were refined further in the current study.

To refine the dependent variables, the items retained by James et al. [23] were supplemented with information/data elements collected from surveys exploring data disclosure1. Additional items were then extrapolated from the combined set of data items. The final list of items and the matrix shown in Figure 2 were provided to a panel of Facebook experts, who were instructed to place the items in the matrix box corresponding to their release of that data element. The experts were seniorlevel students recruited from a technical major, in order to ensure that the panel was technically sophisticated and consisted of frequent, active, relatively long-term Facebook users. The resulting panel information was used to refine several OSN information release behavior dependent variables. We intended to capture at least one factor for each of the four boxes shown in Figure 2, i.e., characteristic data that is released narrowly, characteristic data that is released widely, observational data that is released narrowly, and observational data that is released widely.

The result of our panels and subsequent pilots revealed five distinct factors, shown in Figure 2, which we refer to as contact information, work history, profile information, introspective data, and extrospective data. The first three factors are characteristic data elements, two of which are released more narrowly on average (contact information and work history) and one that is released more widely (profile information). The last two factors are observational data elements, one of which is released more narrowly (introspective) and the other is released more widely (extrospective). We used the scale to capture the scope of release and based the scale on the choices Facebook provides for the scope of release of profile or status elements. Thus, the scale includes the following selections: 1 – Nobody (Do not share this on Facebook), 2 – One Person (Private Message), 3 – Some People

(List or Custom List), 4 – My Network (Friends), and 5 –Everyone (Public or Friends of Friends). The continuum on the top of Figure 2 represents this scale.

The full survey was panel tested and pilot tested in the fall of 2013 and again in the spring of 2014 at two large southeastern 4-year universities in the US. The first step was to subject the entire survey to a review by a panel of content experts and experimental instrumentation experts in the fall of 2013. This process provides multiple perspectives on the validity and clarity of the survey items, and it has been suggested to help mitigate common method bias [43, 49]. The survey was presented to the panelists, along with an explanation of the study. The panelists were instructed to advise on content, appropriateness of items, and language. The resulting feedback was organized and requisite changes were made to the survey items. This resulted in changes in wording, for example, changing “information I release on Facebook” to “information I share on Facebook” in order to make the wording more consistent with Facebook use. Another example would be adding “hometown” as a data field in response to panel feedback. The survey was subsequently pilot tested in the fall of 2013, and the exploratory factor analysis (EFA) for this survey was used to further revise the items. Another panel was convened to obtain additional insight into the dependent variables in the spring of 2014, as described previously. The survey was piloted again in the spring of 2014, and the EFA indicated a robust set of items.

The final data collection was performed in the spring and summer of 2014. The survey for the final data collection was administered using Qualtrics, and the respondents were recruited from two large southeastern US 4-year universities, as well as from Amazon’s Mechanical Turk, a survey panel provider. The use of students is appropriate for the current study, where our target demographic is frequent Facebook users [7, 17]. The addition of data collected from Mechanical Turk broadened certain demographic categories such as age, which provided additional depth to our sample. Participation was voluntary and anonymity was ensured. Ensuring anonymity diminishes the chance that participants will feel pressured to answer the survey in a way they think is expected and, therefore, helps reduce common method bias [43].

Participants, including those recruited through Mechanical Turk, were required to have an active Facebook account to participate. A filter question was presented at the start of the survey asking the participants whether they had an active Facebook account. If the response was no, the participant was thanked for his/her time and was not presented the rest of the survey. If the response was yes, the participant was presented with the rest of the construct measures in a randomized order to reduce common method bias [43]. Additionally, “attention trap” questions were inserted into the survey presentation to identify participants who were not cognitively engaged in the answering process [40]. Any responses that did not accurately answer the “attention trap” questions, in which the respondent was asked to pick a specific scale item, were removed from the final dataset.

From the universities, 682 data points were collected. After cleaning the data (i.e., removing those that did not finish the survey or did not pass the manipulation checks), 546 usable responses remained. From Mechanical Turk, 162 data points were obtained, which after cleaning, resulted in 134 usable responses. The final combined usable sample was n = 680. Reliability, Convergent Validity, and Discriminant Validity

A confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was performed to establish the appropriateness of the final survey items. The CFA was run in JMP® Pro 10.0.2. A principal components factor analysis with varimax rotation was used to analyze the data. Communalities for all items were higher than the recommended 0.4 level. The analysis, summarized in Table 4, revealed 11 distinct factors that correspond to the constructs in our model. It is recommended that factor loadings be greater than 0.7 and that the difference between cross-loadings not be less than 0.2. Most measurement items in the current survey loaded on the primary factor at approximately 0.7. No items had troublesome cross-loadings (difference between loadings < 0.2). For our sample size, factor loadings > 0.3 are acceptable, and all items loaded on their primary factor at greater than or equal to 0.5. Convergent validity requires that all items of a construct be correlated. The CFA indicates that the items for our constructs are correlated, which confirms convergent validity. The construct correlations are provided in Table 5. All construct correlations are lower than 0.5, which, combined with the lack of significant cross-loadings, points to discriminant validity. These results indicate that the constructs (factors) are unique.

## Structural Model

SmartPLS 2.0.M3 (http://www.smartpls.de) was used to test the structural models. The software uses partial least squares regression (PLS) to test the path models. It provides path coefficients and t-values for the relationships in the model. Additionally, SmartPLS gives R-squared values for the endogenous variables in model. The PLS technique allows relationships among multiple independent and dependent constructs to be examined in unison [4, 16]. The path models for the current study are shown in Figures 4 and 5. The sample size for the models is n = 680. In Figures 4 and 5, the t-scores have been converted to p-values. Figure 4 shows the path model corresponding to the original conceptual model. In Figure 5, the Facebook control variables were tested.

All paths present in Figure 4 are significant at the p < 0.05 level. The informationseeking motivation for overriding one’s IPI was tested, and it was found to have no impact, as expected. Therefore, it was dropped from the model. Of the remaining 25 possible relationships in the model, 15 were found to be significant. Out of those 15 relationships, 3 were significant but in the direction opposite to the corresponding hypothesis. The significant relationships are summarized in Table 6. For clarity, only the paths that remained in the model are shown (i.e., those relationships that were significant). The information management—contact information, interaction management—introspective data, socialization— introspective data, and pleasing others—contact information path coefficients are lower than the desired values, but are still significant. The R-square values of the observational data are adequate, but those of the characteristic data are not as desirable (i.e., explaining less than 10% of the variance). However, the phenomena being explored in this study are quite new online privacy behaviors. It could reasonably be expected that the trends seen in this data may continue to strengthen, as OSN communities adapt to new tools. Furthermore, as suggested by Johnson et al. [25], the diversity of communities found on OSNs makes it difficult to find one model that could fit them all. Thus, as will be described in the discussion, valuable insights can be drawn from these path models to pave the way for new approaches for studying privacy within the OSN realm.

Ours is the first empirical analysis of fine-grained information release considerations on a social networking site using the interaction and information management conceptualization of privacy, as well as a privacy calculus developed for this context. This lens offers a number of interesting new insights.

Interestingly, the privacy management scheme that emerges from the structural models (Figures 4& 5) is more complex, nuanced, and thoughtful than we hypothesized. As individuals’ desire to control their information increases, the scope of the release of their contact information (β = -0.094, p < 0.05) and introspective observational data (β = -0.273, p = 0.000) decreases, as hypothesized. However, the scope of the release of their profile information (β = 0.109, p < 0.05)

#

increases. This is an interesting finding because it suggests that the most private people (in this case, those who wish to have tighter control over their information) reduce the exposure of what could be deemed more personal information (i.e., data that would allow others to contact them in multiple ways, as well as their more intimate observational data). However, the scope of the release of basic profile characteristics (e.g., age, birthday, schools, their image) increases to a broader audience, such that there are certain pieces of characteristic information offered for public record, and perhaps doing so is considered to be the price of participating in the online society. The release of this type of information serves as a basic advertisement to attract more friends and (at least superficial) interaction on the OSN. Although individuals with a desire for control over their information release a few basic profile characteristics to a broader audience, the results indicate they select carefully the pieces of information that they expose more widely and those that they do not. Thus, they manage their information by carefully considering the release of different content to different subgroups of friends.

What is even more interesting is the counterbalancing approach to OSN privacy management, which our results indicate is provided by the interaction management component of the dual privacy decision. In complete contrast to information management, individuals who have a high desire to control their interaction decrease the scope of release of their profile information $( \beta = - 0 . 1 4 3 , \mathrm { p } < = 0 . 0 1 )$ This can be interpreted as a way of limiting the number of people that can find an individual’s virtual representation, thereby reducing friend requests, which would make it easier to control the size of his or her circle of friends. There is a significant, but weaker relationship between interaction management and the release of introspective observational data $( \beta = 0 . 0 9 9 , \mathrm { p } < 0 . 0 5 )$ . At first, this may seem counterintuitive. However, consider the fact that those high on interaction management are limiting their profile information, which could be used as a method to constrain their friend requests and/or superficial interaction on the platform (e.g. not receiving birthday wishes). This result may indicate these individuals constrain their number of friends more tightly, which suggests a privacy circle comprising more intimate relations, thus reducing the need to make strategic choices about which friends will see certain observational data.

To further explore this phenomenon, we added the Facebook control variables to the model (length of time the individual had a Facebook account, number of friends on Facebook, and Facebook proficiency). The model, shown in Figure 5, further reinforces the logic described above, through reported practice (constraining Facebook friends is the traditional way to manage interaction on OSNs) in addition to reported desire. The number of Facebook friends fully mediates the relationship between interaction management and introspective data. Additionally, it also partially mediates the relationship between interaction management and profile data. Thus, as individuals’ desire to control their interaction increases, the number of friends they have on Facebook decreases $( \beta = - 0 . 1 6 1 , \rho < = 0 . 0 1 )$ . Furthermore, as the number of friends on Facebook increases, the scope of the release of profile information increases $( \beta = 0 . 1 9 9 , \mathrm { p } = 0 . 0 0 0 )$ and the scope of the release of

#

introspective information decreases $( \beta = - 0 . 2 1 4 , \ p = 0 . 0 0 0 )$ . The interpretation is the same: those who prefer to have large circles of friends offer up basic characteristic information (either to obtain friends or to maintain superficial interaction with many people), but consider carefully the subset of that privacy circle to which they release more intimate thoughts. Moreover, the number of increases, so does the number of friends $( \beta = 0 . 1 8 2 , \rho = 0 . 0 0 0 )$ . However, the relationship between information management and introspective data stays strong and positive $( \beta = - 0 . 2 3 9 , \mathrm { p } = 0 . 0 0 0 )$ , suggesting that those with a high desire for information management may attract friends with profile characteristic data but manage their privacy through finer-grained information release decisions when it comes to more intimate data. In addition, even though they express a high desire for information management (which would indicate “private” on one dimension), they have a lot of friends nonetheless, which means that they manage their privacy in a way other than reducing their privacy circle. Further, as the duration for which respondents had a Facebook account increased, their desire for information management increased. This lends some support to the idea that as users spend more time on Facebook, they learn the value of managing their privacy.

These results suggest that people do indeed approach their privacy management in multiple ways: 1) by making an effort to constrain the size of their privacy circle (number of friends) but, within that constrained privacy circle, releasing even relatively intimate data more broadly and 2) by allowing their privacy circle to grow large but, within that larger privacy circle, releasing curated information to smaller sub-groups. We call these people Interaction Managers and Information Managers, respectively (see Figure 6). Prior to the addition of Google+ circles and more finegrained release options in Facebook, the only way to manage privacy on most OSN’s was to constrain the number of friends one included on the platform or to simply not release any information that is intended for only a small number of those friends. By adding finer-grained interaction controls, OSNs have provided greater flexibility in managing one’s privacy. However, a clear pattern illustrating two very specific management approaches emerges. This suggests that our approach to modeling privacy is very appropriate, especially for online social networks. Further investigation of these two approaches to OSN privacy management would be an interesting topic for future research. This is especially true because the finergrained controls are a relatively new usability feature in OSNs, and as their use becomes more common, understanding how people prefer to manage their privacy will be fundamental to designing future OSNs. Moreover, it would be interesting to explore this phenomenon across multiple OSNs, especially those that are specialized (LinkedIn, ResearchGate, etc.).

## Conclusion

As OSNs become pervasive in most developed countries, the debate over if and how privacy will be managed online intensifies. Our study provides the first fine-grained examination of OSN privacy management schemes based on classical privacy theory. We suggest that exploration of privacy management schemes, that include consideration of both what information is released and to whom, is increasingly important and relevant. This is especially true given the current trends in OSN development.

Our results provide support for multiple privacy management schemes in the OSN environment. We classify the privacy management schemes as information managers and interaction managers. On the one hand, information managers have large circles of friends and possibly leverage common characteristic profile data to obtain the large circle of friends. However, while they widely distribute profile information, they more granularly manage more intimate data amongst subgroups of the larger set of friends. On the other hand, interaction managers manage their privacy by restricting the number individuals they have as online friends. They constrain more tightly their privacy circle (limit their number of friends) by maintaining tighter control over their profile information. However, since their circle of friends is smaller, and likely more intimate, they may be less likely to carefully manage the release of other data to small subgroups.

Furthermore, our results shed light on benefits that may supplant an individual’s privacy concept on an OSN. As expected, information seeking did not influence information and interaction behaviors on Facebook. However, socialization, selfexpression, and pleasing others did influence behavior. Though socialization and pleasing others presented differing influences, self-expression showed a strong positive relationship with all the information and interaction behaviors. Our findings indicate that different benefits influence our information and interaction behaviors in differing ways, which is an important finding. One implication of this is that our privacy behaviors could change on OSNs with different perceived benefits. For example, future work could explore whether self-expression results in the same information/interaction management behaviors on LinkedIn as it did on Facebook. Our initial calculus model for OSNs provided interesting findings, but perceived benefits are contextually dependent, which means adapting the model for other scenarios may provide an interesting avenue for future research. Another

#

important finding is that self-expression led to a wider scope of information release in all cases. The finding that self-expression is driving behavior indicates an overwhelming desire to share and be seen, as well as less thoughtful consideration of what is being released. Laufer and Wolfe suggest that consequences of information release and interaction are often very difficult to foresee. Future work could examine the psychology of expressing oneself on an OSN or explore measures to better educate individuals of the possible consequences. It may also be possible to explore this implication by expanding the privacy model in this paper to include other concepts (e.g. risk, trust, legal influences, etc.). Extending our model may further enhance our understanding of information and interaction behaviors, as well as perceptions of consequences.

It has been argued that young people show less concern about their privacy. However, our results could be interpreted as indicating that privacy (in terms of being able to control one’s information and interaction) may still be important or desired, but that it is being adapted to new technological environments in ways we do not yet fully understand and that are not yet fully realized. There are obvious benefits to belonging to and using an OSN: being able to keep up with goings-on, socializing with others, expressing and making oneself known to others, and pleasing other people. These are very attractive benefits and our results demonstrate that many of them will supersede an individual’s privacy concept, but in varying ways. How much control an individual desires, how much control an individual is willing to cede in order to participate in an online society, and what OSNs can do to support these individual preferences are very arguably currently being sorted out by society. We suggest that our study provides a first look at the initial stages of this process.

To ensure their own longevity, OSN providers may be best advised to give users very fine-grained options to manage their OSN relationships. This may seem counterproductive to their primary business model, but our results indicate finegrained controls may provide users with more flexibility to manage their relationships in the way that corresponds best with their IPI, which may provide happier and longer-term users. If one thinks of privacy as relationship management, there are multiple options for controlling privacy. For example, one could friend a very small group of trusted individuals and then spend less time curating the information one releases (similar to using the OSN as a small email list). One could have a very large group of friends, create sub-groups (circles) of these friends, and treat each circle as a separate network when it came to releasing information. One could have a very large group of friends and not release anything on the OSN (similar to using the OSN as more of an address book or informational site). A different relationship management strategy could be used on each OSN and be dependent on the benefits obtained from using that OSN.

One issue that was outside the scope of the current study, but is important to discuss and address in future work, is third party access to information. It is increasingly becoming public knowledge that the OSNs themselves are selling the

#

information that is collected on their sites. Additionally, releasing information on the Internet raises considerations that may be diminished in offline relationship management. For example, the speed of information leakage and distribution of information between individuals on the Internet are much quicker than in offline social conditions. These considerations may indicate that OSNs may want to consider letting users opt-in or opt-out (or perhaps be paid for) the business use of their information. Additionally, next generation privacy management controls may need to provide the ability for users to mark posts as “unsharable” to reduce the spread of their information by others. Future work may examine in which situations and how often users apply relationship management strategies (e.g. if a user has established specific custom groups or how often a user specifies particular people on a post, etc.) or how users would respond to new relationship management controls such as marking posts as “unsharable.”

Finally, our study explored the creation and testing of items for new dependent variables that would capture both information and interaction management behavior. Our intent was to explore the influence of the individual’s privacy concept (desire to manage information and interaction) and the OSN privacy calculus on information/interaction behaviors on Facebook. Our dependent variables are limited to the exploration of decisions regarding defined information and the user’s determination of the scope to which they would release that information on Facebook (ranging from nobody to everyone). Thus, we do not capture a situation where a user chooses to not use Facebook or has no information to release. Thus, future research could explore the influence of the IPI/calculus on intention to use Facebook, intention to quit Facebook, or various levels of Facebook use. Additionally, previous studies have explored control as an antecedent of perceived privacy [13], which would also be an interesting option to explore in certain contexts. An investigation of alternative dependent variables would be interesting future work.

Our study offers the research community an opportunity to examine the complexities of fine-grained privacy considerations for social networking sites through a lens grounded in classic privacy theory that suggests the exploration of privacy through relationship management and consideration of benefits.
