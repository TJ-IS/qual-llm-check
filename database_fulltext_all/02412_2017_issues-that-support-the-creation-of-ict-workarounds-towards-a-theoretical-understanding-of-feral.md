# Issues that support the creation of ICT workarounds: towards a theoretical understanding of feral information systems Anthony Spierings,\*<sup>,†</sup> Don Kerr<sup>†</sup> & Luke Houghton<sup>‡</sup>

\*Energex, Network Operations Group, Newstead, Queensland Australia, email: anthony. spierings@research.usc.edu.au, <sup>†</sup>Faculty of Arts and Business, University of the Sunshine Coast, Sippy Downs, Queensland Australia, and <sup>‡</sup>International Business and Asian Studies, Grif<sup>fi</sup>th University, Nathan, Queensland Australia

ENTERPRISE SYSTEM (ES) software is often supplemented indepen dently by end users who develop personal solutions that establish connections between the required business practices and the ES. One solution is a type of information and communications technology workaround, a feral information system (FIS) – de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a workaround designed to achieve certain requirements by using any information technology tool that an end user uses in conjunction with, or instead of, the mandated information system. To explore this, we conducted a case study at a large utility company. We conclude that feral information systems are not a behavioural deviance. FISs are by-products of end users seeking operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, namely, to nullify additional transactional costs imposed by the ES. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that end users of ES will fall into one of four modes of operation: mode 1, where end users submit to the ES; mode 2, where they dismiss the ES; mode 3, where they develop an FIS that remains hidden from the ES proponents; and mode 4, where they operate the FIS in open de<sup>fi</sup>ance of the ES proponents. In this research, we deliberately take the practitioner view and, therefore, outline how different pressures help to create an FIS as a response to a poorly mandated ES. We also make a theoretical contribution by exploring issues that lead to workarounds and suggest that future research into these modes of operation can be theorized in future proposed studies.

Keywords: feral information systems, shadow systems, workarounds, structuration theory, Enterprise System

## INTRODUCTION

ENTERPRISE SYSTEM (ES) software is often supplemented independently by end users who de velop personal solutions in order to establish connections between the required business practices and the ES. Consider the following scenarios.

An administration of<sup>fi</sup>cer, low in the organizational hierarchy, gathers project-related data from the paperwork that crosses the of<sup>fi</sup>cer's desk and records it in a desktop spreadsheet. When the business prepares its annual report, it uses some of this spreadsheet data in preference to similar data stored in the multi-million dollar ES. To access the backend database of the ES, an engineer uses a network-monitoring packet sniffer to determine the username and password that the ES desktop client is using. The engineer then reverse-engineers the Struc tured Query Language (SQL) obtained from the packet sniffer to develop a user-friendly web based front end that integrates the ES data with the other project-related information. The web page becomes so popular that over 10% of the business uses it. Each month, an accoun tant does a massive data dump from the ES. Pushing desktop spreadsheet and database tech nology to its very limits, the accountant regroups and reformats the monthly <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports.

These scenarios exemplify the interaction amongst the ES, the ES end users and the ad hoc information technology (IT) tools that end users have developed as workarounds to the ES. Such a workaround is called a feral information system (FIS). It is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a workaround designed to achieve certain requirements by using any IT tool that an end user uses in conjunction with, or instead of, the mandated IS (Kerr & Houghton, 2010).

Alter (2014) argued that there is no real theoretical framework to conceptualize workarounds, and that they usually exist as a means to join some loosely coupled systems or to plug the gaps between elements to complete a process. FISs are a by-product of end users seeking opera tional ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, that is, to nullify additional transactional costs imposed by the ES. These include cost transfers onto end users by both internal ES proponents (e.g. an ES implementation team) and external ES proponents (e.g. vendors and consultants). FISs are thus an inno vative solution developed during a period of crisis for the end user. The crisis occurs when ES proponents corner the end user into resolving goal con<sup>fl</sup>ict, namely, ES processes that are incompatible with mandated business processes. End users can choose to resolve this tension by creating new linkages to patch over de<sup>fi</sup>ciencies in the ES by building an FIS to keep the business processes <sup>fl</sup>owing. Alternatively, they either utilize non-IT mechanisms to work around de<sup>fi</sup>ciencies or, if they have access to suf<sup>fi</sup>cient transformative resources, simply refuse to use the ES.

Businesses seek to maximize the returns on their capital outlays. The use of IT is no different. It can be argued that <sup>fi</sup>rms care little about IT per se; they care about results, which is to deliver ever-increasing pro<sup>fi</sup>ts and productivity. It can be further argued that the presence of an FIS is an indication that there are inadequacies along the boundary between business work<sup>fl</sup>ow and the sanctioned ES that are hindering productivity. In turn, these inadequacies are encouraging end users to resort to other tools and workarounds. Such circumstances are generally a double investment in time and money, often resulting in a ‘make do’ solution. The development of an FIS may therefore be sometimes justi<sup>fi</sup>ed.

The aim of this study, therefore, has been to examine examples of the interaction amongst the ES. the ES end users and the ad hoc IT tools that end users have developed as workarounds to the ES. Our approach has been to introduce an operational point of view to satisfy two of the problems Alter introduced. Firstly, we present a workaround in the form of an FIS in situ in a major utility company from the workplace's point of view. Second, we seek to theorize workarounds further – as outlined in Alter's (2014, p. 1060) section on future research about workarounds – by ‘starting from other underlying theories’. We adopted Alter's suggestion by exploring FIS from the alternative theoretical standpoint of structuration theory and sociomateriality and through the lens of FIS creation in the utility company mentioned in the preced ing texts.

This paper has the following structure: It starts with an overview of FISs, positioning them in relation to shadow systems and workarounds. Next, we touch on underlying theories – including structuration theory and <sup>fi</sup>ve roles of ISs – that informed our research objectives. Following is a short summary of our research methodology, data acquired and analysis. Finally, we discuss the implications of our research.

## WHAT IS A FERAL INFORMATION SYSTEM?

As mentioned earlier, an FIS is any IT tool or workaround that the end user uses instead of the mandated IS. We do not restrict the term FIS to just shrink-wrapped software (like desktop spreadsheets and databases). Although such FISs are in the majority, we have also observed FIS as standalone applications written in C, C++, Java, Python, plus a combination of Active Server Page (server) with JavaScript (desktop client). We regard shadow systems as a subset of FIS. As a general comment, we do not consider workarounds as FIS, unless they involve an IT tool that is used instead of the mandated ES. That said, we consider an FIS as steps that ex tend beyond simple ES rejection.

The literature on this topic is confusing because of the drift in naming conventions that various researchers have applied over the last 30 years. The earliest reference to the term shadow system is from a 1980 investigation into subterfuge methods to achieve cross-border data ex<sup>fi</sup>ltration on mainframe computers (Turn, 1980). Some authors apply the shadow system label to any end user-developed application, often with negative connotations. Other de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of shadow systems include software applications that have not been controlled by the <sup>fi</sup>rm's central IT department (Zimmermann et al., 2014). Some describe shadow systems more in terms of locally developed ‘bolt on’ solutions to overcome inadequacies in the sanctioned ES (Behrens, 2009; Györy et al., 2012; Stein et al., 2013; Lyytinen & Newman, 2014), others as legacy systems including spreadsheets that remained in use despite a newer system being available (Boudreau & Robey, 2005) or to act as an of<sup>fl</sup>ine local data store that was more accessible than the ES and occasionally synchronized back to the ES (Berente & Yoo, 2012). Selander & Henfridsson's (2012) study on cynicism about enterprise resource planning, while mentioning in passing the use of a parallel system, is silent on whether the end users extended themselves to build alternative systems. Confusingly, the label workarounds was occasionally applied not only to cover what others (preceding texts) had described as shadow systems but also to cover changes in access pro<sup>fi</sup>les, re-appropriating data <sup>fi</sup>elds for data classi<sup>fi</sup>cation or abusing administrative privileges (Petrides et al., 2004; Ignatiadis & Nandhakumar, 2009).

Current research into shadow systems and workarounds generally fails to acknowledge the body of research in the 1980s and 1990s into end user computing/development that covered remarkably similar matters. Back then, local managers were expressing dissatisfaction with the IT department's inability to keep pace with rapidly evolving business requirements. In return, the IT department was disturbed about the proliferation of end user development reports and applications. What today we call FISs (and subsets called shadow systems) clearly existed on mainframes back in the 1970s (McLean, 1979; Rockart & Flannery, 1981). The arrival of desktop computing only exacerbated the matter. End users increasingly turned to desktop computers to resolve pressing information needs (Alavi, 1985; Huff et al., 1988; McLean et al., 1993). Recent research suggests that end user-developed IT tools are alive and well, with two thirds of IT managers noting their existence (Chejfec, 2012). It appears then that despite 35 years of development and countless billions of dollars in sunk costs, there are, at times, still considerable gaps between what ES is delivering and what the end users need to complete prescribed tasks.

The conceptual framework with existing literature into shadow systems and end user comput ing is too narrow, insomuch as it assumes that end users developing their own tools are an issue that starts and stops with IT. End users – scientists, engineers, machinists, etc. – have been modifying existing tools and innovating new tools for centuries. Most of this in-house develop ment is conducted initially to satisfy the end user's immediate needs (Von Hippel, 1988, 2005). When dealing with sticky knowledge, end users often modify and develop systems at signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly lower cost than commercial software (Von Hippel, 2007). We agree with Orlikowski (1992), who states that workers appropriate and use technology in diverse ways and there are often heterogeneous outcomes at the organizational and individual levels.

Most large organizations have installed an ES; yet, the ES success rate is highly variable, even for organizations that share similarities and compete in the same environment. The literature provides some detail on how an organization responds to a failed implementation (Scheer & Habermann, 2000; Nah et al., 2001; Gattiker, 2002; Botta-Genoulaz et al., 2005; Gattiker & Goodhue, 2005). However, researchers have paid less attention to what happens inside an organization when some modules of an ES are not realizing their design goals. In particular, there is little research into how some end users manage the loss of productivity by building an alternative IS. While acknowledging the trend towards cloud-based services such as <sup>fi</sup>le hosting, <sup>fi</sup>le synchronization and web-based of<sup>fi</sup>ce suit software, in this study, we concentrated on noncloud-based approaches. We did not collect data on such phenomena, as the site under study was highly active in blocking unapproved access to cloud services to reduce the risk of unauthorized data ex<sup>fi</sup>ltration.

## UNDERLYING THEORIES

We suspected that some of the problems we were observing in the workplace had less to do with technology and more to do with organizational matters. If this were the case, we needed to turn to other theoretical tools to gain insight. Pozzebon & Pinsonneault (2005, page 1354) argue that structuration theory is ‘a powerful alternative not yet fully explored by students of organisations’. Orlikowski & Robey (1991) noted that relationships exist amongst the ‘IT, human action and social structure’. Recent changes to the theory by Orlikowski (2014), towards a socio-material view, argue that these processes are more intertwined than previously thought. We agree that they interact but do not necessarily hold to the view that process entanglement is explainable without understanding the hegemony or social structure. We also agree with the concept that ISs are social systems (Staehr, 2008). The literature suggests that structuration theory can provide insights as ‘investigation of unacknowledged conditions and unintended consequences (that designers or implementers may not have considered in their plans) (Jones, 2008). The sum of these arguments convinced us to utilize structuration theory in ou research.

## STRUCTURATION THEORY

Using Giddens's (1979, 75–77) structuration concepts as a framework, our view is that an ES implementation is more akin to structural transplant than a simple IT project. Before ES implementations, there is usually a rich and complex structure in place at the <sup>fi</sup>rm level buil upon by human actors over many years. Actors have engaged in recursive feedback between themselves via social integration (i.e. the reciprocity between actors) and their system integration (i.e. the reciprocity between groups or collectives). Included in the structure are human constructs, like ISs. This recursiveness continues across time–space until someone comes along with suf<sup>fi</sup>cient resources that can alter the previous arrangements. A dismissive wave from an ES proponent that these matters are simply ‘change management’ problems indicates a lack of appreciation on their part for the organizational stress associated with replacing an existing system (be that ES, desktop applications or even paper based) that is deeply embedded in the structure of the business.

An aspect of structuration theory is the concept of how resources are the media of domination and access to a transformative mechanism. Transformative capacity in turn can feed back into the original mode of domination. If human agents have access, then they may use authoritative and allocative resources in either modes of domination or transformative capacity on the structure of domination.

In our research, we used the concepts of resource, domination and transformative capacity to gain insight into the interactions between ES proponents and ES end users. On one side, there are the ES proponents who are trying to force (dominate) the end user into using the ES. On the other side, there are end users who have productivity issues with the ES and are drawing on their modes of domination to parry away the ES proponents.

## FIVE ROLES OF AN INFORMATION SYSTEM

Askenäs (2000; 2004) used structuration theory to explore how an IS can become an actor inside an organization. Her theory allocated <sup>fi</sup>ve roles that an IS can play. In the context of an ES, we can assume that the ES will represent one of four role types for the end users who use the system, namely consultant, manipulator, bureaucrat or administrative assistant. The <sup>fi</sup>fth role is ‘played by the information system when it is disregarded or ignored by its intended users’. Askenäs created the <sup>fi</sup>fth role when she noted how sales representatives were able to draw on their domination over future sales into the business to dismiss the IS. Utilizing this dominance, the sales representatives were able to parry away the domination of the ES proponents and alter the state of the proponent's transformative capacity.

From Askenäs' research, we determined that end users are divisible into two groups: those who have accepted, or have no option other than to accept, the ES as provided, and those with suf<sup>fi</sup>cient access to transformative capacity that they can choose to ‘dismiss’ the IS if they so wish. With this in mind, we reorganized Askenäs' <sup>fi</sup>ve unique roles into two distinct end user modes of operation. End users either will ‘submit’ (mode 1) to the ES or ‘dismiss’ the ES (mode 2). We put Askenäs' roles of consultant, manipulator, bureaucrat and administrative assistance into the ‘submit’ mode of operation, with the ‘dismiss’ mode standing by itself. Figure 1 concep tualizes our modi<sup>fi</sup>cation of Askenäs' <sup>fi</sup>ve roles into two modes of operation.

Askenäs is silent on whether the end users moved beyond simply dismissing the IS to build ing an alternative system. We continue the conversation by exploring what happens when end users take matters into their own hands to develop an alternative IT system – an FIS.

One aspect of structuration theory is that individuals have some domination over their own resources and to some degree can in<sup>fl</sup>uence their course through time–space. The extent of individuals' ability to develop an alternative IT system depends heavily on the choices they make. This <sup>fi</sup>ts within Weber's concepts of Lebenschancen (life chances) along with Lebensführung (life conduct), as the two basic components of Weber's Lebensstil (lifestyles; Cockerham et al., 1993).

An implicit assumption in our research is that not all end users are equal when it comes to computer skills. At one end of the scale are end users who have little desire or opportunity to acquire skills in computer programming; at the other end are end users/knowledge workers who are adept at learning a programming language and see this skill as complementary to their core profession of being a scientist or engineer (Segal, 2007).

Therefore, our three research objectives in this study were:

RO1 Determine how end users acquire the skills to build an FIS.

RO2 Explore how end users (as knowledgeable agents) appropriate and allocate available resources – be this for an ES or FIS – to ful<sup>fi</sup>l demands for information.

## RESEARCH METHODOLOGY

We used case research as it is <sup>fl</sup>exible and versatile, particularly when the technical issues are secondary to the organization under study. Case studies provide a mechanism for academics and practitioners to keep abreast of the rapid changes taking place in organizations and the IT community (Dubé & paré, 2003). A process of triangulation and corroboration will minimize research bias inherent in single points of view (Yin, 2009, 115–116). Our triangulation tools included interviews of professionals from the IT department to provide their views, our direct observations of the ES and FIS in action and an archive of contemporaneous records from the ES implementation. Further, we adhered to Yin's three principles of data collection: using multiple sources of evidence, storing evidence in a case database and maintaining a chain of evidence (Yin, 2009, 114–124).

The case site was a medium-sized utility based in Australia. We called this site UTILITY1. It had a strong engineering culture. Internal resources performed the majority of the core work as sociated with planning, designing, building and operating the asset. The ES under study was nearly 10 years old when the <sup>fi</sup>eld data were collected. This provided an excellent opportunity to examine a system in its mature phase. For this research, we utilized ‘special access’ arrangements (Yin, 2009, 91) through industry contacts.

For RO1. we profiled end user skills by using three mechanisms. The first mechanism was to catalogue end user computer skills for using desktop applications and the end users' ability to write code. Instead of a formal skill test (Compeau & Higgins, 1995) or assessment by an independent technical expert (Ko & Myers, 2005), we used end user self-assessment (McCourt Larres et al., 2003) moderated by our observations. The nominated skill sets related to our view of how far FIS developers had scaled the ladder of capability. The bottom rung represented a simple spreadsheet; the top rungs represented the ability to code in various languages, including SQL, Java and C. The second mechanism was to determine how end users acquired these skills. We categorized these into structured learning (typical at a tertiary institution), unstructured learning (typically from a workplace colleague) and autodidact (typically off the internet or from hobbyist roots). The third mechanism was to assess the contribution that formal education had made to developing end user skills. For this, we sought information about end users' levels of academic achievement under the Australian Quali<sup>fi</sup>cations Framework (AQF). As the AQF is a national policy for regulated quali<sup>fi</sup>cations in the Australian education and training sector, it provides a suitable benchmark for comparing skills obtained via structured learning.

For RO2, we sought evidence of the demands for information that the business placed on eno users and their <sup>fi</sup>rst choice preferences to ful<sup>fi</sup>l that demand. Next, we collected the views from end users and IT professionals on functional gaps between ES and business processes and the strategies used to resolve functional gaps. In addition, we explored end user selection and appropriation of IT tools to build an FIS.

For RO3, we sought evidence on other resources (i.e. resources from a structuration theory perspective) that FIS developers engage in to boost their ability to interpose into circumstances to parry away attempts by ES proponents to foster the ES.

## DATA AND ANALYSIS

Data collected included 21 semi-structured interviews, 10 FISs and relevant business documents. A senior executive recommended the IS specialists to interview and provided tips on where to <sup>fi</sup>nd potential FIS developers. This follows Yin's suggestion that interview screening may be achieved ‘by querying people knowledgeable about each candidate’ (Yin, 2009, 91). One end user was able to provide a personal archive of their involvement with the ES imple mentation. The data set was 289 MB in size, containing 672 <sup>fi</sup>les loosely categorized in 47 folders. All the <sup>fi</sup>les related to the ES implementation and subsequent embedment. Yin (2009, 113) state that a ‘physical or cultural artefact – a technological device, a tool or instrument, a work of art’ can serve as a source of evidence. As part of this research, we collected 10 FISs for further study. Considering the commercial con<sup>fi</sup>dentially of the data contained in these FIS, they remained secured on a network drive at the host site.

NVivo 10 was used to facilitate the storage and coding of all the data. This comprised interview transcripts, archival documents, the researcher's post-interview re<sup>fl</sup>ective memorandums, the researcher's theoretical memorandums and extant literature most relevant to the data anal ysis. Using NVivo to manage the whole data set also supported coder reliability, allowing all au thors to access the data and follow and comment on the coding process.

End users applied a wide spectrum of skills to build an FIS. These ranged from rudimentary skills used to develop a local spreadsheet through highly advanced skills that combined multi ple ITapproaches (e.g. packet snif<sup>fi</sup>ng plus SQL plus asynchronous JavaScript). The entry barrier to a basic FIS is low. However, more-sophisticated FIS drew on ever more complex IT skills. End users can acquire FIS-building IT skills through all three nominated pathways: autodidact, unstructured learning and structured learning. The simplest FISs we observed in this study were a result of unstructured learning at the workplace. As the level of formal quali<sup>fi</sup>cation rose, so too did the capabilities of the FIS. However, it is hard to separate out cause and effect between formal quali<sup>fi</sup>cations and FIS development capabilities. What is more apparent is that the builders of simple FIS drew on only one mechanism of skill acquisition. As the FIS builders drew across diverse skill acquisition pathways, so too did the resultant FIS grow in capability and sophistication.

## RO2 - END USER ALLOCATION OF RESOURCES

The demand for information was diverse. Common themes noted were the gap between ES functionality and business requirements and that the problem could be broadly categorized as either a reporting matter or a forecasting matter. The business impact of the demands for in formation varied across a broad continuum of needs: from seemingly trivial reporting needs through to resource planning for the whole of business.

FIS developers exhibited a strong preference to use the ES. Typical end user views were:

If it is available from a corporate system; I'd go down that route. Second route that I go down is to obviously follow the corporate process off inquiring with [IT Department] to see what the process would be to obtain that information. [IC1: Lines 43–45]

I'd love the data to come out in a nice, useful, pretty way, and hopefully [names proposed new reporting system] is going to be able to do that. [IC4: Lines 361–362]

We've got some really good tools in there [in the ES] where we use really well. [IC6: Lines 149–150]

All FIS developers and supervisors experienced frustration with not having ES data available in a usable form.

Reports, on the other hand, I've heard people waiting two years, which seem a bit excessive… [IC10: Lines 284–285]

When the error was found – the [business custodian] said, ‘I'll <sup>fi</sup>x it. I'll go and get the other existing report changed’. So this – you would expect this is high priority of the organisation, that was at the end of March and initially we were told 13 April it'll be released and we're now at 30 March, almost a full year later! But the corporate systems, you didn't even bother wasting your time talking to IT. You can jus write ‘NO!’ on your own whiteboard, and save yourself a week's work. [IC9: Lines 341–348]

The IT professionals held similar views on issues associated with ES reporting systems.

Reporting's been a big black hole for IT for a long time. If you looked at the inventory of reports over a period of time that you've run, my experience is you'd be lucky to see 10 per cent of those reports were actually in use and the remaining 90 per cent were legacy. So reporting is very expensive. A lot of IT organisations try to get out of the reporting game and push it back to users. I think vendors were trying to sell it in that way as well. [IT7: Lines 261–268]

End users' continual and simmering frustration with their inability to access the ES data – for legitimate business purposes we might add – can drive some surprising behaviours. One skilful end user resorted to packet snif<sup>fi</sup>ng to obtain access to ES data. IC1 installed Wireshark, a packet sniffer application, on the corporate computer. At that time, the desktop applications communicated with the corporate databases over Transmission Control Protocol/Internet Protocol in plain text. By using Wireshark, IC1 was able to capture usernames and passwords to connect to the various databases. More importantly, IC1 was able to observe the various SQL statements to decode the structure of the tables. When the researcher questioned why IC1 went down the packet-snif<sup>fi</sup>ng route, IC1 commented:

Yeah, so, <sup>fi</sup>rst of all I should just say, my choice, my personal preference would be not to packet sniff, I actually think that, as a corporation it would do us a great deal of good to actually have a little bit more openness behind our corporate reporting. So, not just document the output of the report but also document the methodology by which the report is produced. Such that [it is] open to people checking that it actually produces the numbers that they need, and also, it would enable people to take that query and adapt it for themselves. I personally think that packet snif<sup>fi</sup>ng shouldn't be needed …

So, I guess, that is a fundamental philosophy that I disagree with about how [IT Department] is run. But as far as how packet snif<sup>fi</sup>ng works, I just download an open source product, such as Wireshark, and that enables me to monitor packets being sent back and forth over the network. I then run a query from inside a corporate package. It will often have to send away a query to a database server to be run. And, on its way over the network cable I can – over the network connection – I can intercept it and inspect the SQL that was used. [IC1: Lines 354–356]

It would be a mistake to assume that IC1 did all of this on his own undertaking. IC1 received strong encouragement from his colleagues and supervisors. As IC1 notes:

So, existing in that environment for long enough, people start to accept that they are not going to be able to achieve the things they require. The minute they are exposed to someone with the technical capability to achieve those things, they clearly jump at it. It is clearly like I said before, if all those circumstances do happen, if they can, people will <sup>fi</sup>nd a way to do these things. When it comes to my supervisors, managers, often times they might not have the ca pability themselves. So, it is a question of if someone happens … happens to join their team who does have the capabilities, they will often leverage them because that's the only way they can get what they need to get done, done. [IC1: Lines 421–428]

An ES can generate additional business costs that are well outside the immediate problem at hand. SUP8 commented on the concept of ES being a single source of truth:

So the fact that you have complete opposite views out of what I can only presume to be the same data set – and this is all similar data sets, it's all coming out of the same system. It just goes to show the fallibility of the [concept of a] ‘single source of the truth’ [being the ES] and we've passed this back to the [Reporting Department] and said whatever you're doing we're not sure that you actually got it right… [SUP8: Lines 227–234]

While at <sup>fi</sup>rst glance, a simple reporting error may seem a rather trivial matter, SUP8 detailed the additional workload it created for the department as follows: There is an internal political el ement as one business division generates the report. The reporting department sends it up their chain of command. It then travels across the top at the senior executive level, where a discussion occurs that is unfavourable to one executive. Finally, the report travels down the chain of command in another business division. In this way, SUP8 noted the impact on the business as a whole. In particular, the error in this report was an unnecessary distraction and needless consumption of managerial time.

It gets a whole lot of focus but it's noise. It creates conversations in the management of the or ganisation which costs us a whole lot of time and energy for no purpose. [SUP8: Lines 239–241]

The FIS that SUP8 was discussing is an example of how some FIS builders have accumu lated and can wield considerable resources to defend the existence of the FIS. The accumu lated resources include:

knowledge of how to export data from the Enterprise System;

skill to build a spreadsheet or desktop database to store the ex<sup>fi</sup>ltrated data;

capability to develop queries on the local data set; and importantly,

managerial sponsorship, sometimes at a very high level in the organization, to expend the necessary labour to develop and maintain the FIS.

Vendors often tout the advantage of ES as being a common data repository, the so-called single source of truth. However, this common data repository is also a weakness, especially when a well-intentioned but poorly supervised employee is altering key data. Part of IC2's justi<sup>fi</sup>cation for the local FIS was its use as an auditing and change log tool. IC2 had this to say about the activities of some work colleagues:

… Holy crap. That's the approved amount on that [managerial approval], that's what was ap proved, you don't go in and add 15 per cent to that. You don't go in and change all those pro ject managers without looking at the rest of the information. There's no start date, there's no end date. Project manager's wrong, the workgroup no longer exists…

It is a data cleansing issue. It is a constant data cleansing issue because the people do not take ownership of their own things. People – we're in such a big organisation. Taking ownership of something is actually frowned on, I think. [IC2: Lines 1025–1044]

End users make do with the resources they had available. They appear to stop development when the solution reached either their technical limits or a ‘make do’ limit. The ‘make do’ limit implies that the end user has determined that additional investment in time to further develop the system exceeds the payback in a future reduction in labour.

One theme that we concluded from the data was dyad and triad relationships involving the FIS. The primary dyad is the end user and the FIS. ## DISCUSSION

An Edgeworth box and production possibilities frontier (PPF) can explain the microeconomic behaviours of end users when confronted with an ES that generates additional workload. Pre-ES implementation, end users divided their allotted time between using the existing business process for data management (be that ES, IS or even paper-based systems) and aggregate time on tasks that generate a pro<sup>fi</sup>t for the business. While a new ES can increase the time end users spend on the system, the theory is that the ES simultaneously pushes the organization's PPF outwards, thereby increasing end user productivity. However, if there is a mis<sup>fi</sup>t between the ES and required business practices, then the ES is forcing the end user to allocate more time to interfacing with the ES. In turn, this pushes the end user along the PPF curve, resulting in a reduction in the aggregate time the end user spends working on pro<sup>fi</sup>t generating tasks (Figure 2). While this may seem obvious, the literature has largely ignored microeconomic influences as drivers of end user responses and has concentrated on social theories to rationalize behaviours.

Further, we suggest that end users may respond to a loss of production function by adopting one of four possible modes of operation. Which mode they choose is largely dependent on their access to transformative capacity using interposable resources and their ability to develop their own IT tools. Those end users with low access have no other option than to submit to the ES. End users with suf<sup>fi</sup>cient access to interposable resources have the option to dismiss the ES and thereby throw the problem back into the lap of the ES proponents to solve. Some end users, who have an ability to develop an alternative IT tool but lack suf<sup>fi</sup>cient interposing resources, will build a system that they keep hidden from ES proponents. While the literature has discussed such constructs as shadow systems, it has overlooked the fourth variant that we discuss: the de<sup>fi</sup>ant system. End users who have access to suf<sup>fi</sup>cient interposing resources and skills to build IT tools may elect to construct defiant systems. Given the resources with transformative capacity that are sponsoring de<sup>fi</sup>ant systems, there is little that ES proponents can do about them. It also shows the positioning of modes of operation against existing theories of <sup>fi</sup>ve roles of an IS, shadow systems and workarounds.

## IMPLICATIONS

This study demonstrates that when ES fails, there is an underlying tendency for workers to in tuitively <sup>fi</sup>ll the gaps. One outcome from ES mis<sup>fi</sup>t is a cost transfer onto end users by both internal ES proponents (e.g. ES implementation team) and external ES proponents (e.g. ven dors and consultants).

Feral information system is an innovative solution developed during a period of crisis for the end user. The crisis occurs when ES proponents corner the end user into resolving goal con-<sup>fl</sup>ict, namely, incompatible ES baked-in processes vs. mandated business processes. A facet that was a surprise to us is the interposing role of supervisors (of FIS developers). Supervisors are often an important resource for building and defending the FIS. They can add additional (in structuration terminology) ‘resources’ available to interpose into the problem that FIS devel opers are attempting to solve. Supervisors have access to suf<sup>fi</sup>cient modes of domination to ef fectively parry away the capability of ES proponents to shut down an FIS.

Current research into workarounds identi<sup>fi</sup>es the FIS as a simple patch. We theorize that these are not merely patches, but extensions of the ES that end users construct to improve operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and to keep the business processes <sup>fl</sup>owing. Alternatively (as observed by others), they utilize non-IT mechanisms to work around de<sup>fi</sup>ciencies. Other open options are available to end users with suf<sup>fi</sup>cient transformative resources to refuse to use the ES. Refusal throws the problem back into the lap of the ES proponents. For a business struggling with poor ES <sup>fi</sup>t, the worst-case alternative to an FIS is for end users to follow ES proponents instructions.

Instead of being passive-aggressive, FIS builders take a pragmatic approach to reappropriate available technology to <sup>fi</sup>x a problem. Their IT skills, sometimes deliberately enhanced during the building process, determine the FIS quality and usability. Some FIS builders, particularly those with mathematical and language skills from allied disciplines of science and engineering, have IT skills that can easily exceed those of the average IT profes sional. That said, the skill barrier to building a rudimentary FIS is remarkably low. Further, we see the entry barrier to FIS continuing to fall as IT tools become more readily accessible, user friendly and take on differing forms (e.g. cloud-based solutions succeeding over desktop IT tools).

We suggest that punishing FIS builders is counterproductive, as these employees are amongst the more proactive and committed business problem solvers. FIS is a symptom of an underlying business problem, not the cause. However, we agree that if the number of FIS becomes out of hand, then they will be highly problematic for the host organization. FIS will naturally dissipate if remedial work makes good the <sup>fi</sup>t between the ES and business needs. If <sup>fi</sup>x ing the ES is not achievable, then supervisors, specialists from the IT department and end users should work together to make the FIS provide acceptable levels of quality and reliability.

This study contributes to Alter's (2014) work in that it begins to provide a theoretical structuring and assessment of why someone creates an FIS in the <sup>fi</sup>rst place. We provide a theoretical base for further explanation of workarounds as a process of ‘creative’ behaviour or adaption to disjointed impositions. We argue that workarounds give inadequate theoretical insight into the ‘drivers’ of behaviour or the modes of operation. Thus, researchers cannot practically or effectively work to understand them, and subsequently, such drivers are under-theorized in the literature. This study has revealed the ‘modes’ of operation that lead to FISs and conceptualizes them as part of a lifestyle choice made willingly by participants in order to ‘<sup>fi</sup>x’ broken organiza tional linkages through poor ES implementations.
