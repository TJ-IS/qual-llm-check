# The teaching of qualitative research methods in information systems: an explorative study utilizing learning theory

Robert D. Galliers<sup>1</sup> and Jimmy C. Huang<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Bentley University, Massachusetts, U.S.A.; <sup>2</sup>Warwick Business School, The University of Warwick, U.K.

Correspondence: Robert D. Galliers, Bentley University, 175 Forest Street, Waltham. Massachusetts 02452. U.S.A. Tel: þ 1 781 891 2105; Fax: + 1 781 891 2896 E-mail: rgalliers@bentley.edu

## Abstract

Empirical evidence suggests that the Information Systems field continues to be dominated by the positivist paradigm and the use of quantitative methods. This is so despite the diversity of research methods available, the emergence of an interpretivist tradition, and some isolated examples of methodological pluralism. While there have been significant contributions in guiding qualitative research methods teaching, little research has been undertaken that considers the training actually provided, and the possible impacts this may have on the quality and quantity of qualitative research articles in our journals. This explorative study aims to provide some insight into what training is provided, the issues confronted, and possible impediments to publishing qualitative research. Data were collected from interviews with a panel of international colleagues who have a range of experience in teaching and applying qualitative methods. Grounded Theory is used to identify emergent and interconnected themes. Experiential and social learning lenses are used to reflect on the results, providing some initial understanding of the complexities of teaching and undertaking qualitative research. Theoretical as well as practical contributions are made regarding the means by which qualitative research may be introduced, developed and extended within the IS academy, and further theorisation of social and experiential learning.

## The chequered history of publishing qualitative Information Systems (IS) research

Despite various calls for the field of study, that is, IS, to become more diversified in terms of the topics that are of research interest to the IS academy (e.g., Robey, 1996; Galliers, 2003; Hirschheim & Klein, 1989; Avison & Elliot, 2005; Galliers et al, 2007), and the attendant need for greater plurality and diversity in the manner by which these phenomena are studied (e.g., Mumford et al, 1985; Galliers & Land, 1987; Galliers, 1991; Lee, 1991; Mingers, 2001; Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991), the incidence of published qualitative research in the IS academy’s major journals remains limited (Chen & Hirschheim, 2004). Exceptions do, of course, exist, for example in relation to the use of both quantitative and qualitative approaches, and purely qualitative research (e.g., Hirschheim & Klein, 1989; Trauth & Jessup, 2000; Newell & Edelman, 2008; Remus & Wiener, 2010 and Orlikowski, 1996; Robey & Sahay, 1996; Schultze, 2000, respectively). Indeed, certain journals have excellent reputations for publishing qualitative and interpretivist research, such as the Information Systems Journal<sup>1</sup> and Information & Organization.<sup>2</sup> Other leading journals are methodologically agnostic, for example, the Journal of Information Technology and the Journal of Strategic Information Systems, emphasizing, inter alia, trans-disciplinarity and a range of alternative contributions such as critical reviews, as well as empirical studies of all kinds. Guidance for undertaking qualitative research is also extensive (e.g., Eisenhardt, 1989; Lee, 1989; Walsham, 1995a, b; Trauth, 2001; Myers & Avison, 2002; Myers 2008). This includes, for example, guidelines that deal with specific issues, such as control of action research projects; undertaking longitudinal field work; building theories from case studies; establishing a set of principles for conducting and evaluating interpretive field research, and drawing valid conclusions from a single case (e.g., Eisenhardt, 1989; Barley, 1990; Klein & Myers, 1999; Avison et al, 2001, and Brown, 1997, respectively).

While the extant literature has provided useful guidelines in conducting qualitative research, our understanding of what might lead to the preponderance of quantitative studies, undertaken – most often – in the positivist tradition, remains relatively under-explored. Our purpose in this paper is to investigate the underlying assumptions, and any myths, that may have arisen in the IS academy regarding qualitative research, particularly with respect to how we prepare new generations of IS scholars. We do this, given the growing focus on doctoral training programs in Europe and elsewhere by, for example, the Economic and Social Research Council in the U.K. (ESRC, 2009), and as a result of the Bologna Accords, and the creation of the European Higher Education Area. The following is also illustrative:

Traditional individual study programmes based on a model of a working alliance between the doctoral candidate and the supervisor without a structured coursework phase are increasingly seen as being inappropriate in preparing doctoral researchers for multiple careers in different sectors y about 30 percent of European [Higher Education Institutions] have introduced a new model of doctoral school. (UK HE Europe Unit, 2010, pp. 2–3)

To explore these issues, we formed an expert panel of IS scholars, and invited them to reflect on their experience and to express their personal views. Through articulating and reflecting on these personal views and experiences, we sketch how the qualitative research tradition is perceived by some of the people who are actively engaged in constructing and influencing it. Specifically, we explore individual perceptions related to the publishing of qualitative papers, with a particular interest in understanding whether colleagues’ views of the ways in which qualitative research training is organized and provided have any influence in this regard. To conceptualize these experiences and viewpoints, and to justify our rationale for conducting this research, we draw on theories of social learning and experiential learning (Bandura, 1977; Kolb, 1984). Social learning theory suggests that learning often occurs through observation and imitation of role models, and is triggered by the benefits that learners anticipate will accrue to them. Hence, this theory is relevant in examining, understanding and theorizing the impact of views expressed and actions taken by established figures in the IS academy on future generations of scholars and their approach to research. By contrast, the relevance of experiential learning theory is twofold. First, it is valuable in providing guidelines to help make sense of how tacit experience, such as conducting field research, can be taught and learned. Second, it offers useful insights to help explain how different research approaches, both qualitative and quantitative, can potentially benefit each other in the accumulation, utilization and refinement of experience. Methodologically, we adopted a grounded theory approach (Glaser & Strauss, 1967; Orlikowski, 1993; Adam & Urquhart, 2009), given the explorative nature of the study, and our aim of yielding new theoretical understanding in an area that is relatively under-articulated (Eisenhardt, 1989).

The paper is structured as follows. The next section deals with the theoretical foundations of this research, with an emphasis on illustrating the conceptual compo nents of social and experiential learning theories, as well as their relevance to our research focus. We then elaborate the research methods adopted in more detail, including considerations that informed our choice of research approach, data collection, analysis and presentation. This is followed by a section detailing our research results, following which we reflect on these results and draw some tentative conclusions from this preliminary study. Our discussion is aided by drawing on social and experiential learning theory, as already noted. Compared with prior studies that use social learning mainly to theorize learners’ experiences (for example, Lippert & Forman, 2005; Bercovitz & Feldman, 2008), our focus is on the role models, in this case a group of established scholars in our field, from different parts of the world. By so doing, we hope to contribute not only to means by which qualitative research may be introduced, developed and extended within the IS academy, but also to the further theorization of social and experiential learning.

## Theoretical foundations

Ontological and epistemological differences between the quantitative and qualitative research traditions are well articulated (Easterby-Smith et al, 1991), yet few accounts theorize their differences and impacts on how researchers learn. Kolb’s (1984) account is one of the very few exceptions. In countering the dominance of the quantitative tradition in psychology research, Kolb’s experiential learning theory advocates the need for a more qualitative approach to research. In particular, he argues for the importance of incorporating experience into the theorization of learning. Rather than assuming that experience bears equal weight for both the qualitative and quantitative research traditions, however, we draw on Kolb’s account to differentiate how experience is perceived, accumulated and applied in the process of learning in our context. In particular, two distinctive orientations towards the gaining of experience, namely apprehension and comprehension, appear highly relevant in this regard. According to Kolb (1984, p. 102):

Knowing by apprehension is here-and-now. It exists only in a continuously unfolding present movement of apparently limitless depth wherein events are related via synchronicity – that is, a patterned interrelationship in the moment y . Comprehension, on the other hand, is by its very nature a record of the past that seeks to define the future; the concept of linear time is perhaps its most fundamental foundation, underlying all concept of causality.

While apprehension is closely associated with the knowledge structures of contextualism and organism, Kolb notes that the comprehension mode of knowing is related to formism and mechanism. Due to the different roles that experience plays in the apprehension and comprehension modes of knowing, they require different approaches for their refinement and progression. As Kolb (1984, p. 103, emphases added) put it:

A second difference between knowing by apprehension and knowing by comprehension is that apprehension is a registrative process transformed intentionally and extensionally by appreciation, whereas comprehension is an interpretive process transformed intentionally and extensionally by criticism.

Because of the fluid nature and the ‘felt qualities’ (Feigl, 1958), apprehension is the knowing process through which tacit knowledge (Polanyi, 1961) is created, compared with the more articulated form that is created through the process of comprehension. Kolb (1984) argues that to enhance learning, neither the development of apprehension, nor the reinforcement of comprehension, alone, while necessary, are sufficient. Rather, the two approaches need to work in conjunction with each other. As indicated earlier, apprehension leads to concrete experience, and comprehension results in abstract conceptualization. It is through reflective observation that concrete experience can be fed into abstract conceptualization. Further, it is through active experimentation that abstract conceptualization can be applied to yield concrete experience. Moreover, and as suggested by Kolb, while apprehension mirrors the tradition of qualitative research, comprehension more readily characterizes quantitative research approaches. Such a distinction is useful in explaining fundamental differences in the process of accumulating and utilizing experience in the qualitative and quantitative traditions. In addition, however, the two modes of learning offer a valuable theoretical device to reflect on how research experiences are shared and passed on, in particular through the teaching of research methods and the challenges scholars face in conducting their research.

Even though Kolb’s work has provided some valuable insights, it offers little in the way of how the external environment and contextual settings impact on learning and learners (Crossan et al, 1999). Given that learning is a social (Gherardi et al, 1998), and in some cases, a socializing process (Brown & Duguid, 1991; Lave & Wenger, 1991), putting the social context back into the theorization of learning appears crucial. Social learning theory is one of the few accounts that offer some relevant theoretical elements to achieve this, in particular in advancing our understanding of how role models influence learners. The rationale to learn through modeling is that:

Learning would be exceedingly laborious, not to mention hazardous, if people had to rely solely on the effects of their own actions to inform them what to do. Fortunately, most human behavior is learned observationally through modelling y . Because people can learn from example what to do, at least in approximate form, before performing any behavior, they are spared needless errors. (Bandura, 1977, p. 22)

Bandura notes that behaviors in general, and learning more specifically, can be positively or negatively reinforced by three main sources, namely, external reinforcement, vicarious reinforcement and self-reinforcement. While self-reinforcement comes intrinsically from learners themselves, vicarious reinforcement symbolizes the understanding gained through observing others, such as the rewards gained through the successes of role models. By contrast, external reinforcement represents factors that are normally created by social or institutional norms and values. Even though individually each source can have a certain impact on learners, it is often through the interplay of these three sources of reinforcement that learners decide and take action – a view that is echoed by and exemplified in Crossan and colleagues’ (1999) account. What seems to be less articulated by Bandura, however, is the perspective of the role models themselves; specifically, how they see their involvement in and influence on the learning process.

Summing up, it is clear that experiential learning theory is useful not only in explaining the fundamental differences between the qualitative and quantitative research traditions, but also in offering useful guidelines as to how these differences can be integrated to facilitate a more effective way of knowing. To enrich this perspective, we draw on the theory of social learning to investigate the social context in which knowledge of qualitative research is provided and acted upon, in this case with respect to getting articles arising from qualitative research published in our leading journals. By so doing, we also aim to refine social learning theory by incorporating the perspective of role models into our theorization. This is achieved by analyzing and presenting the experiences and views of some leading scholars in our field. More details related to methodological considerations are outlined below.

## Research methods

## Research considerations

Our first consideration was to define the scope of our coverage. Given the growing incidence of research training noted earlier, we focused our attention on aspects of the manner in which qualitative research methods are taught to graduate (primarily doctoral) students, leaving aside – for now – the mentoring and supervision of research students when actually undertaking qualitative research. We also decided to incorporate the teaching of qualitative research methods in different geographic regions of the world in order to provide a more nuanced approach to the research and to avoid an overly parochial investigation (Galliers & Meadows, 2003), given the different, and changing, educational contexts, traditions and orientations in which the teaching would take place. We also had to consider a suitable format through which meaningful insights might be collected, analyzed and showcased, and as a result, we followed the examples of Callingham (2004) and Gordon (1999) and adopted an expert panel format for our study. Expert panels provide a forum in which leading experts in a given field are invited to share their experiences and thoughts. To overcome the geographical dispersion of our panel members, we adapted the format by interviewing them individually, using semi-structured interviews (see Appendix A), and applying principles and guidelines of grounded theory, to analyze and then synthesize the data we had collected (Glaser & Strauss, 1967; Urquhart et al, 2010). Each member of the panel was given a copy of our analysis and invited to reflect on the findings. The strengths and limitations of grounded theory are well recorded and elaborated, both within and outside the IS community (e.g., Rindova & Kotha, 2001; Levina & Vaast, 2008; Adam & Urquhart, 2009). No claim can be made as to the generalizability of our preliminary findings in terms of a comprehensive view of teaching provision, but they could usefully form the basis for further studies in this under-researched area, contribute to a more balanced portfolio of teaching research methods and extend our understanding of learning theory.

Our third consideration related to the identification of a suitable panel for the purposes of this research. Any such selection is inevitably subjective and judgmental, but we wanted to ensure reasonable international coverage – across each of the three Association for Information Systems (AIS) regions. For example, we believed it important to include Scandinavia in AIS Region 2, given its long-standing tradition of IS research, and to expand the representation of Region 3 to include East and South East Asia, and Australasia, given the emergence of the former in recent years, and the potentially more Western orientation of the latter. To formulate a set of questions that would suit all interviewees is challenging in its own right. Nevertheless, one of our aims was to identify those aspects of the teaching and learning experience that were common across different institutions in each region, and those that were distinctive. We foresaw a need to include institutions that placed a lighter emphasis on qualitative methods training alongside those that give such training greater emphasis. Selection also included such criteria as interviewees’ experiences in teaching and using qualitative methods in their own research, editorial engagements with some of the leading IS journals and contributions to the development of the IS academy more broadly. We aimed at composing a panel that might not only fit the above criteria alone, but would reasonably provide a range of views, expertise and experience, as well as – potentially – some commonality.

## Data collection

Data were collected via three main sources: interviews with panel members, course documentation and additional opinions obtained through discussions with these colleagues and with a small number of other IS academy members who were unable to participate in the actual interviews themselves. In total, the panel comprised 13 members who were interviewed during the period December 2009 – February 2010. From Region 1, interviews were conducted with Marie-Claude Boudreau, M. Lynne Markus, Dan Robey and Eileen Trauth. Region 2 was represented by Ola Henfridsson, Matthew Jones and Mike Newman. Region 3 was represented by Patrick Chau, Kai Lim, Michael Myers, Shan-Ling Pan, Christina Soh and K.K. Wei. Interviews either took place face-to-face or over the telephone. Commonly, interviews lasted from 45 min to an hour or more. In most instances, interviews were recorded, with permission, and transcripts made, but in a small number of cases comprehensive notes were taken instead. In all instances, interviewees were invited to review the transcripts and notes for accuracy and further comment. In addition, further valuable insights were gained on occasion in conversations that took place during lunch, coffee breaks, follow-up telephone conversations and subsequent email communications. Panel members were also invited to comment on early drafts of this paper.

A list of interview questions, as outlined in Appendix A, was developed, piloted and revised. These questions served as a basis for guiding the interviews, but questioning took an open-ended form in order to permit room for further insights to be surfaced. Therefore, for example, while the interview focus related primarily to the provision of qualitative research methods training, panel members were asked to articulate their thoughts on the general impact of research training on the quality of research submissions based on qualitative methods, on comparisons with other fields of study and on any other considerations they believed pertinent. Moreover, we encouraged panel members to provide retrospective insights and reflections on their experience in transferring research know-how to their students, and how such processes were managed and facilitated. While the interviews took place over a short period of time and in the recent past, some of the issues have been of concern to the IS academy for some considerable time. Obtaining retrospective insights not only enabled us to surface underlying assumptions, but also helped us to appreciate and make sense of how past developments and debates (such as in King & Lyytinen, 2007) might impact future agendas.

In addition to these semi-structured interviews, documentary evidence was also obtained. Such documents were either provided directly by the panel members or as a result of their referrals. Most of the documents obtained are not available in the public domain since they comprised syllabi, teaching slides, lecture notes and reading lists. Perusal of this material helped not only to enrich and provide context to the interviews, but also to compensate for the limited interviewing time we had with our panel members. This additional documentary evidence, combined with informal inputs from colleagues outside the panel, provided additional valuable resources, ranging from research articles, alternative panel members and useful insights into on-going concerns.

## Data analysis and presentation

Data collected from various sources were analyzed based on Glaserian coding principles (Glaser & Strauss, 1967). The emphasis was on deriving thematic categories from the data collected. In addition, we took into account advice provided by Urquhart and colleagues (2010) in refining both our research process and this paper. While their guideline of ‘constant comparison’ was used across data from different sources and Regions, their ‘iterative conceptualization’ guideline helped in yielding deeper understanding of the dynamic interplay among the different themes, as illustrated in Figure 1. Their ‘theoretical integration’ guideline contributed in two ways: to the consideration and investigation of (1) linking our initial findings with social and experiential learning theories, and (2) our conceptual linkage of the two theories, which have been more often assumed than empirically elaborated heretofore. Due in part to the amount of background information needed to narrate the storyline, presenting the results of grounded theory research can be problematic, particularly when space is limited. Compromises between the level of detail and article length are evident in prior accounts (e.g., Orlikowski, 1993; Noble & Mokwa, 1999). Our approach was to structure our evidence in a manner similar to the account of Adam & Urquhart (2009). By summarizing the key themes that emerged from the initial coding, a holistic overview of the emerging themes was attempted in the form of a ‘map’ to assist ‘sensemaking’ (Weick, 2001). Rather than highlighting these themes individually, as in the Adam and Urquhart account, we attempted to map their interrelationship. This conceptual map is our attempt to visualize the storyline based on the experiences and viewpoints offered by our panellists.

## Findings: current course provision, experiences and concerns

## Course provision and context

Course provision in relation to the teaching of research methods is extremely varied according to our panellists. For example, the teaching of qualitative and quantitative methods is given relatively equal weight in certain cases (1A; 1B; 2A; 3A; 3E).

Our MPhil and PhD students are required to take two research methods courses. These are introductions to quantitative and qualitative research. Both courses have equal standing (in terms of hours of teaching and weight of assessment). (2A)

In other institutions, the teaching of qualitative methods is more patchy (1D; 2B; 2C; 3B). For example:

y it would be nice if they could take multiple classes that would dig deeper into specific areas, but right now it’s not possible because y the y required classes are quantitative. So [were we to] have y one required quantitative, one required qualitative y then you [could] let them choose the other three y (1D)

The IS research methods course comprises about 12 sessions, of which three or four relate to qualitative methods. (3B)

Even though y using qualitative methods in general is very high, there’s no clear or strong emphasis on teaching qualitative methods over quantitative ones. (2C)

The students taking IS research methods courses also vary from one institution to another, both in terms of educational background and nationality (1B; 2A; 2B; 3B; 3E). This diversity is also seen in the provision of qualitative research training, which relates in part to the location of such courses within the institution: IS department (1D, 3B, 3E), business school-wide (1B; 2A) or ‘iSchool’(1C). The numbers taking the courses also vary appreciably: from 3 to 5 students each year (3B), to a cap of 35 (3A), to as many as 60 (2A).

## Rationale underlying the course

There appears to be a degree of agreement in relation to the purpose of the qualitative methods courses provided by our panellists. In general terms, the rationale would relate to providing students with an introductory understanding of qualitative methods and why such approaches are helpful in understanding the complexities associated with the introduction, utilization and management of information technologies in organizations, and in society more generally (1B; 1C; 2A; 2B; 3E).

I’m y not trying to convert people into the camp of qualitative work. They can make their own choices y . There’s a chance to point out that quantitative analysis is equally subjective but I don’t usually go in that direction except to pose arguments about whether survey instruments have proven validity, and issues associated with the numbers involved in a survey. You can point out that you’re often simplifying something by putting it into quantitative terms y I don’t, I try for logical debate between the quantitative and qualitative. (1B)

I hope the course y can lay some sort of claim that students have some basic understanding of the core methods so that they can make informed choices when planning and conducting their research. I’m under no illusion that the course puts people in a position to be competent practitioners – there’s more to it than that. y I’m not trying to overturn the relative proportion of qualitative as against quantitative research in IS, but to have informed discussion about the suitability of qualitative methods and the situations in which they can make a contribution. I want people to be aware that there’s more than one way to study any research topic. (2A)

## Techniques covered

It was striking the number of times our panel members talked of learning by doing – for example, through exercises that attempted to replicate the experience of conducting fieldwork. Here, the focus might be on data collection methods, including interviewing techniques and observation, or by actually reviewing papers themselves (1B; 1D; 2A; 2B; 3B; 3C; 3E).

Obviously one of the key data sources is the interview. I always tell them that if you’re going to use a technique you should read up on what’s involved – read beyond what’s in the course. I also have them do an exercise where they interview someone on some general topic about technology y . So, they’re told to y create an interview guide, find someone to interview, and conduct the interview – and report back to the class on the experience. I’ve also set up an exercise on observation. (1B)

I try in each session to introduce one or more exercises. For example, in surfacing assumptions, a reading group might be asked to analyse a number of papers and discuss the validity of the authors’ argument; or with data gathering, they may look at interview transcripts. With regard to observation, they may be asked to observe in class the effects of gender, for example, with a view to understanding that what you’re looking for will affect what you notice, and the difficulties of multi-tasking during field work. (2A)

y doing any research is fundamentally about craftsmanship. You need to observe what other people do and learn from them y (3C)

## In an ideal world

When asked how things might be improved, our panellists tended to return to the theme of more opportunities to practice the art of qualitative research, to be more innovative in our teaching methods (1C; 1D; 2A; 2B; 2C; 3B; 3E), for example:

Maybe more role playing y I think the model of the apprentice y it’s a craft they’re learning. They can’t expect to learn it all just from the class y you’ve got to experience it for yourself y (2B)

Ideally, I’d like to do few things to improve the training. The first thing is y to extend the amount of time provided. It would be useful to have one whole year to teach various aspects of qualitative methods y . Second, it would be to teach the methods with a stronger sense of structure y . Third, it would be to emphasise the need to make a case more focused in its orientation and boundary. (3E)

## Difficulties faced

Our panel was also asked whether there were any issues or difficulties encountered in the teaching of qualitative methods. Here, there was considerable uniformity in the views expressed. For example, the diverse nature of the audience was often cited as presenting a challenge (1C; 2A; 3A; 3B; 3E).

Some have Master’s and those that don’t will have working experience. Two-thirds of them are non-native English speakers y . Those without Master’s are not as good at conceptualisation or theory building y in China y the approach to education can be generally described as ‘amassing facts’ y . Those students with Computer Science and Engineering backgrounds tend to see the world in black and white, and this can be fatal when dealing with the complexity of real world situations, as there can be a lack of sensitivity to the context and a lack of richness in the description. (3B)

I get students from all around university y there’re students from Arts who are involved in technology, and people from y Communication and Media, and then our students from Science & Information Technology y . We have a number of students from Asia y . They probably have a degree in Computer Science or Engineering, so their notion of research is very different. They certainly don't know about qualitative y may not even know a lot about theory, and they may not know anything about doing research on human subjects. (1C)

The question of diversity of audience was also raised not only in relation to the teaching of complex concepts, but also to conducting field research in a language other than one’s native tongue (1B; 1C; 1D; 2B; 3F).

y language is the biggest thing that they have a problem with y they have particular problems [with] sentence construction and idioms y . I’ll give you an example, a Saudi Arabian student y his English was very good when he was talking. Lots of ideas and he expressed himself quite well. But his problem was when he came to write, he just couldn’t express himself y so what I said was, ‘Look, this isn’t gonna work yYou’ve got good ideas, you’re quite capable of thinking theoretically, but your problem is you can’t write, so you’ll have to get a copy editor to write with you, to help you with your writing.’ And he did y it transformed [his dissertation] yhe sailed through y (2B)

y I think that students who don’t have English y as their mother tongue are even more anxious about doing qualitative research y this I totally empathise with because y I had the same doubts. When I started y my English was not so good y I remember conducting some interviews for my dissertation. And sometimes y there would be a joke y and I was like, ‘ha ha ha’, but I didn’t get it. And I had to talk to my husband [who’s] American, and y and he would tell me, ‘Well you know, here’s why it’s funny, it’s related to a TV show’ – one that I’d never seen y it really made me come down to earth. (1D)

Concerns were often expressed about the limited amount of time available to instil even quite basic understanding of the issues and concepts associated with qualitative research. Greater coverage was often provided at the expense of going into any depth with respect to any particular methodology or theory (1D; 2A; 3A).

I knew that this was likely going to be their only class on qualitative research, so I tried to cover a lot y at the expense of getting any sort of depth in any specific method or y technique. (1D)

In just one course you can do just so much y all you can do is to try to inspire them so they find it interesting. It’s not easy, but at least they can get excited about it y . While the course provides a good overview, it doesn’t go into any detail. (3A)

## Knowledge transfer and the issue of tacit knowledge

As might be expected from the earlier discussion of the techniques employed on the courses, experiential learning was a recurring theme throughout our interviews. As such, it is a topic to which we will return when discussing our findings. The transfer of tacit knowledge is not so easily done in a classroom setting and that is why our panel members often turned to setting exercises in class, and having the students work on proiects outside of class

The development of the necessary skills over time was also emphasized (1B; 1C; 1D; 2B; 3B; 3D).

y it’s kind of easy to say, ‘Well, these are the dos and don’ts about interviewing’. But until you do it you don’t have a good understanding. You don’t appreciate the difficulty of doing it until you do it. (1D)

Maybe less than 50% of the problems can be y addressed during the course, as the actual skills of conducting qualitative research are not so easily transferred y reading the subtext y the ability to summarise findings interestingly with sensitivity to the context, the signals y the ability to observe actions y (3B)

y this is why I require the students to do a project, and over time, the students get it really. I just want them to have the experience of it so they know what it is I’m talking about y (1C)

y a lot of students y come with [a prescriptive] framework in mind y ‘We’re gonna learn how to do qualitative research, it’s kind of a recipe and we gonna do it’, especially when we get to data analysis when I introduce a couple of software packages y and they’re looking at me like, ‘So, what do you do first, what do you do second, and what functionality will give you the answer?’ and [laughing] I say, ‘Well, you know, this is just a tool to help you, but it’s not gonna give you any answer, there’s more to it than that’ y but there’s still the expectation y . They think that if I can tell them something about the software, they will therefore know how to do qualitative research. (1D)

But essentially they’ve got to do it in the end because you can teach them stuff but y interviewing is not a science, it’s more like craft. Craft is something that apprentices learn in the workshop but in the end they have to go out and earn their living by doing it. And that’s how knowledge is gathered. You can get some knowledge from the classroom but you can get a lot of knowledge, perhaps even more knowledge from practising it. (2B)

## Findings: key emergent themes

The diverse views expressed and experiences shared by our panellists provide a set of rich vignettes to ground our findings. As noted above, the following figure is an attempt to conceptualize the ‘problematique’ (defined as a nexus of inter-related themes), which represents the landscape of teaching and publishing in qualitative methods. Having said that, and given some of the disparate opinions expressed, not all panellists would necessarily subscribe to the inclusion of every theme, the inter-relationships shown, or the influence of each on the quality and quantity of published works. Figure 1 is thus meant to serve as a sensemaking (Weick, 2001) device that aims to provide something of a map of the landscape, for further discussion and reflection. To illustrate the point, we might surmise from the preceding discussion, that the relative or absolute lack of provision of qualitative research methods courses in some of our programs, may be in part due to the concerns being expressed regarding the difficulty in transferring the tacit skills associated with conducting field work. This relationship is shown in the top right hand corner of Figure 1. The very nature of qualitative research – as compared to quantitative research (recall, for example, Kolb’s (1984) discussion concerning the greater emphasis on apprehension in qualitative research compared to comprehension in quantitative research) – may therefore play a role in the lack of provision of such courses. More details concerning the other themes that go to make up Figure 1 – and their inter-relationships – are outlined below.

## Quality and quantity of journal articles – now and in the future

When considering this aspect of our data, let us first focus on the themes included towards the left hand side of Figure 1. These relate to the viewpoints expressed as to potential factors contributing to the quality and quantity of qualitative research published in major IS journals. Our panellists are identified in the following by their AIS region – 1, 2 or 3 – and an alphabetic identifier in order to preserve confidentiality. Some of them see fewer qualitative research articles as arising from issues related to research methods training, while others single out research supervision as a major concern (1A; 3A). Yet others see this as simply a function of the amount of qualitative research that is taking place, or the environment in which they work (1B; 3D; 3E). Indeed, when asked about the numbers of students taking classes who might go on to do qualitative research, the estimates varied depending on the context. In some instances, this was a large proportion (e.g., 2C); in others there was a fairly even spread (e.g., 1C); in yet others the proportion was smaller: a quarter to a third or so (1A; 3B; 3E); and even less (1B; 1D). The very complexity of presenting qualitative research in journal format was also highlighted by some (e.g., 2A), for instance:

Research methods training is part of it, but not the critical issue. It’s more to do with the fit between the journal’s requirements and the mode of writing – the way it’s presented to do justice to the research y . Qualitative research is necessarily more expansive y more complex arguments need to be presented. Things are better and more generally understood with quantitative studies. Similarly, standards are better understood on the quantitative side – they’re more formulaic. They’re much less straightforward for qualitative studies. (2A)

Moreover, there was also the aspect of quality of article submissions (1A; 2C; 3A; 3B). For example:

I’m actually not sure that there is so little qualitative research being done. As an editor/reviewer I come across a lot of it. The problem in my view is that quality is often terrible. (1A)

I wasn’t rejecting more papers – I wasn’t out of line with the other SEs. With ISR, in [my] first year, most papers were very poor y . These were mostly from quantitative researchers without the tacit knowledge necessary for qualitative research who somewhat mechanistically applied qualitative techniques. The standard of submission has improved this year, but I’m nowhere near accepting a paper as yet. (3A)

Reflecting on the papers submitted to MISQ, you get a sense that many people don’t see methods very important and don’t treat it seriously y many people submitting to MISQ only paid lip service to their methods, such as grounded theory. (2C)

Associated with the latter point, the proportion of members of the IS academy actually capable of reviewing qualitative research, and the standards they apply, was also raised (2A). In addition, there were dichotomous opinions as to the future state of play, with some seeing improved opportunities to publish qualitative research, and others providing a more pessimistic view (1C; 1D; 2B; 3E; 3F), such as with respect to the perception that there was in all likelihood a dwindling number of qualitative research champions (3D; 3E; 3F) – a view expressed by colleagues in Region 3 in particular. For example:

We do have a few respectable qualitative researchers who have been actively promoting qualitative research methods y but you also have to worry where the continuity is. Many of them have served their terms [as editors], and some of them will retire soon. How many up-and-coming qualitative researchers are out there, and ready to take up some of these positions? (3F)

Thus, we may surmise that these themes illustrated in Figure 1 may be somewhat related and mutually reinforcing. A lack of supervisory expertise may result, at least in part, from the (perceived) diminishing number of qualitative research champions, which in turn may introduce further difficulties in attempting – and a lowering of motivation to attempt – qualitative research on the part of a new generation of IS researchers. This may result in a lowering of the quality of journal submissions; a relatively small number of submissions, and fewer such articles being published in our leading journals – whether disproportionate or not. The difficulties encountered by novice researchers in undertaking qualitative research, due in part to the lack of course provision, may also have an impact on the number of reviewers able and willing to provide assistance to authors submitting such research, leading in turn to fewer published articles.

## The perception and question of bias

Members of our panel had mixed views as to the extent – or even existence – of bias against, or for, qualitative research in IS and other fields. These themes can be found towards the foot of Figure 1. While some perceived considerable bias against qualitative research in leading IS journals and in their institutions (1C; 1D; 3E), others perceived favourable treatment of qualitative research in some of the major U.S.-based IS journals (1B; 2A; 2C; 3E). For instance, negative bias was expressed by one panellist as follows:

I’ll spend time correcting some of the misperceptions which students have, and in some cases these misperceptions are from my quantitative colleagues y . Explaining differences between qualitative and quantitative methods is not only an academic task, but a political one. As most of the students will collect a lot of intelligence about different supervisors, methods that they used y the selection of research method is often influenced by the rhetoric and stories each faculty puts forward. Being a minority, you need to be extremely careful not to step on others’ toes. (3E)

In certain parts of the world, primarily Europe according to one of our panellists (2C), alternative outlets for qualitative research are sought, given a potential misperception about what might be acceptable to some of the major U.S. journals. Indeed, based on past editorial practices, one such major journal was described as ‘a closed door’ by one of our panellists (3B). By contrast, some of our panel members (1B; 2C; 3E) argued that, in relation to the proportion of qualitative researchers in our academy, for journals such as MISQ, qualitative submissions can stand just as good a chance, if not better, of getting published as compared to quantitative ones. This positive bias towards qualitative research was described as follows:

I totally disagree with the arguments that top journals don’t publish qualitative papers. It might have been that way in the past. Especially at MISQ, you might have a better chance to publish a paper with qualitative methods. y the number of AEs with qualitative profiles is disproportionate to the number of papers submitted y . My advice is that qualitative researchers need to submit. The assumption that top journals don’t publish qualitative papers is just wrong y it seems that in Europe, there’re people who dismiss top journals with y reasons which I think are invalid. (2C)

y I anticipate the assumption that because it’s qualitative it’s not going to be published in a top journal, so all the examples of the articles I use on the course come from ISR, MIS Quarterly, Information & Organization, and similarly the top journals in Marketing, Organizational Science, and so on. y . [Having said that] I think some people are sceptical. They don’t see qualitative methods as a rigorous method analysing data. It’s not convincing to them. (1B)

The misperception is related to the difficulties to get qualitative work published y comparing the total number of submissions and actual acceptance in some of the top journals, qualitative papers seem to have a better chance. However, students will see, and some of my colleagues will argue [the point], based on [fewer] qualitative papers published as a whole, without considering the relatively small size of the qualitative research community. (3E)

Thus, some of our panellists were less convinced about the bias towards qualitative methods (e.g., 1B: 2A; 2C), with one noting that:

I’m well aware of the temptation to represent our lot as being particularly hard: there’s a tendency amongst qualitative researchers to think that our backs are against the wall. (2A)

Others (e.g., 2B, 3D, 3E; 3F) saw the situation as improving, while yet others (e.g., 1C; 1D), whether seeing improvements or not, believed that bias remains present, that it is:

y a culture thing y Information Systems in the United States is heavily quantitative and heavily positivist y . When I interviewed here y I was the first person who came y doing interpretivist work, and I was presenting research on this multiple case study y and while I was giving the presentation, one person cut in and said, ‘But what about bias?’ y I finally looked at him and said, ‘All research is biased, interpretivists just admit it’. (1C, emphasis added)

Indeed, undertaking qualitative research may be seen in certain quarters to be a risky business:

‘Don’t be a punk’ or whatever. ‘Don’t do something too weird. Do that when you’ve got your tenure. Right now, go with the flow and just do what everybody else is doing. Go with the conventional approach.’ (1D)

Questions of bias, therefore, do seem to play some role with respect to what might be seen as a relative lack of qualitative research in IS discourse, as depicted in the themes included towards the bottom of Figure 1. Perceptions of bias against qualitative research may indicate a level of risk that is too great for the novice researcher, or alternatively, the view that senior editors are less interested in this form of research – even though this may not, in fact, be the case. Irrespective, the impact is likely to be fewer published journal articles, which in turn may fuel the view that editors are indeed disinterested or that negative bias may well exist. Notwithstanding, how might questions of bias be dealt with in the teaching of qualitative research methods? Panellist 1B answers this by having students read and discuss exemplar papers that have been published in leading journals, thereby debunking the ‘myth’ that qualitative articles are not welcome in such outlets. We discuss dealing with questions of bias in the classroom in greater detail in the sections that follow.

## Discussion

As indicated, there can be no claim of universal laws arising from such a study as this; we cannot make any claims about the representativeness of our sample, for instance (e.g., Orlikowski, 1996). What we may reasonably attempt, however, is to draw together some of the threads from both the common and disparate experiences and viewpoints of our panel members for consideration and reflection. Doing so through the lens of two distinct learning theories, we argue, can provide further insights, in both theoretical and practical terms.

Recall that our starting point for this research was a thought that raised the question whether the relatively small proportion of articles arising from qualitative research in our major journals had anything to do with the education we provide new generations of IS academics. We found limited or no teaching of qualitative research methods in certain institutions (e.g., 1D; 3B; 3C; 3F). As illustrated by the themes in the upper lefthand side of Figure 1, this lack might lead to extremely limited exposure to and awareness of the methods (3E; 3F), poor quality of journal submissions (3A), or more directly to a limited number of such submissions. The poor quality of some submissions might alternatively or additionally be due to lack of supervisory experience (1A; 3D), inadequate articulation and presentation of methodological thoughts and considerations (2C) – a situation that could worsen given what was perceived by some to be a dwindling number of qualitative research champions (3D; 3E; 3F) and competent reviewers (2A).

These intermingled factors are found to be further complicated by the way that our panellists perceived and debated the existence of bias (the themes included towards the bottom of Figure 1). These mixed viewpoints lead us to sense that, whether bias exists or not, whether it is positive or negative, the perception of bias – how it is spread and projected by colleagues, deliberately or not (2C; 3E) – appears to be what is important here: ‘reality’ – or realities rather – being a social construction (Berger & Luckman, 1966). The perception of bias against qualitative research (1C), or against certain mainstream journals (2C), taken together with the perception of attendant added risks – for example, the length of time involved in conducting qualitative research and mastering the methods (3D; 3F), the difficulties of conducting such studies in a language other than one’s own (1C; 1D; 2B; 3C; 3F), or of coming from a very different culture or educational background (3B), might reasonably be assumed to lead to a smaller proportion of qualitative research being conducted. Our data would seem to indicate that this perception of bias is a rather ingrained issue that can be traced back as far as novice researchers’ entry into the IS community: to the point where they begin their research training (1D; 3E). We return to questions of bias and how they might be confronted in teaching research methods in the concluding section of this article, which considers potential future directions for the IS academy.

What is evident is that there is considerable agreement among our panel members concerning the difficulties in transferring tacit skills in class, and the resultant difficulties faced by novice qualitative researchers, especially when such training is so limited (the themes in the top right of Figure 1). This, combined with alternative research approaches (i.e., mostly quantitative in nature) that are perceived to be acceptable in other fields, such as in Marketing and Strategy (1B), likely has an effect on the number of Ph.D. graduates who go on to undertake qualitative research in their subsequent careers. For example:

y the Marketing students, for example, think this is really neat stuff because they’re exposed to IS articles for the first time: ‘Wow, I didn’t know anything this was going on in the technology group’ y but I also hear things like, ‘This is nice but we’re told in our department not to use these qualitative research techniques y’. The Marketing faculty want them to do a pretty high level of quantitative studies. I think it’s actually the same [for] y strategic management, where there’s very little qualitative research published. So they think, ‘Well, I can’t really use that. I’m not accountable to you; I’m accountable to my department’. (1B)

We heard time and time again about the need to practice field research: ‘learning by doing’ (1B; 1D; 2A; 2C; 3C; 3E); learning from one’s mistakes (1D; 2C); the introduction of exercises in class (1B; 1C; 1D; 2A; 3A; 3B); mini projects (1C; 3C); providing an apprenticeship-style experience (2B; 2C; 3C; 3E). All these were mentioned as being means by which the necessary experiential knowledge might be acquired, transferred and applied. Various approaches that our panellists incorporated into their teaching reflect Feigl’s (1958) and Kolb’s (1984) notions that apprehension is more about grasping understanding through experience rather than by abstract conceptualization.

What seems to be particularly crucial is that ‘here-andnow experience’ (Kolb, 1984) is not only difficult to articulate, but also challenging to transfer and acquire. On the one hand, such challenges echo the difficulties that many of our panel members encounter in passing on their experience. On the other, it is useful to explain situations where many inexperienced researchers may make the mistake of perceiving qualitative methods as simplistic. For instance:

Compared with teaching quantitative methods, which are basically to teach students what’s right and what’s wrong, teaching qualitative methods is teaching students to make judgments and articulate their rationale behind their judgments. Students without proper background, they just can’t make judgments y [and are] not able to appreciate the rationale behind the judgments. For instance y when many new Ph.D. students first listen to Lynne’s course, they may think that qualitative methods are very easy. However, after accumulating six or ten years of research, you start to appreciate how wonderful her research is. (3D)

The point here is that ‘here-and-now experience’ is also a source that learners use to interpret what they encounter. If they have more extensive experience, they appreciate the complexities. By contrast, if they have limited experience they may tend to form a very simplistic view of qualitative research, and lack appreciation of what it can deliver. To appreciate the full complexity of qualitative research requires experience that rests in a different mindset – a freestyle mindset that may not fully developed in many institutions:

It’s very difficult to break students free from their past training to explain to them that case study is not about using one particular method to sort a research question, but an art to look at and theorise one or two aspects of social reality. (3E)

Essentially, then, qualitative research is an experiencedemanding research tradition. Thus, novice researchers who attempt to enter an experience-demanding domain with very limited experience themselves, by definition, will encounter difficulties in initiating the kind of experiential learning cycle suggested by Kolb (1984), unless they do so without considerable assistance. If the IS academy does not take care to articulate what it is that novice researchers might require in preparation to enter the field, we risk placing them in a vicious cycle. For experiential learning to take hold, learners need to be provided with experiences ab initio. More experiences can then be gradually accumulated and nurtured. If insufficient insight is provided initially, however, ‘invalid’ experiences, perceived bias or misperceptions may be fed into students’ future experiences. Even for those who are able initially to apprehend and apply their qualitative research training, their institutional setting may not provide the appropriate supportive environment and mechanisms necessary for them to develop and mature as qualitative researchers (e.g., 1D). It is clear from our findings that variations across regions, and institutions in the same region, can be substantial. The relatively small number of qualitative researchers in some institutions – in our sample, this appears to be particularly the case in AIS Regions 1 and 3 – may well directly impact supervisory capacity – and ongoing, cumulative experiential learning. Potentially more challenging, however, might be the institutional settings and forces that would constrain the nurturing of – or even drive out – qualitative research:

In our program there are five research methods classes that they need to take y four of them are required, and one is elective y the four are [all] quantitative, so our program is very strong on the quantitative side. (1D)

In addition, and in part as a result of their relative lack of experience and being subject to hierarchical pressures, one could ask how novice researchers might observe and react to their role models and peers. Two distinctive yet related implications, as suggested by some of our panellists, include the perceived risks of being in the minority and the reinforcement of certain methods being accepted as more appropriate and legitimate than others. Working outside the mainstream is a risky business apparently. As indicated earlier, one of our panellists (1D) was advised early in their career not to ‘be a punk or ‘do something too weird’, but to ‘go with the conventional approach’.

Perceptions thus appear to play an important role. Novice researchers learn through imitating and interpreting their peers. In other words, there is a social learning (Bandura, 1977) element at play here. Given the harshness of obtaining tenure in some institutions (1D; 3D; 3E; 3F), survival is unsurprisingly viewed in very stark terms. According to Bandura (1977, p. 12):

Because mistakes can produce costly, or even fatal consequences, the prospects for survival would be slim indeed if one could learn only by suffering the consequences of trial and error y . Apart from the question of survival, it is difficult to imagine a social transmission process in which the language, lifestyles, and institutional practices of a culture are taught to each new member by selective reinforcement of fortuitous behaviors, without the benefit of models who exemplify the cultural patterns.

What seems to be particularly crucial from the perspective of social learning is not merely reflected in the increasing homogeneity of modeled behaviors; more important, it is the growing perception of the appropriateness and legitimacy of certain behaviors, and the gradually narrowing of space that disallows other behaviors to take place. Given the instrumental nature of social learning, modeling behavior is essentially a calculative act, which learners anticipate, and from which they project a return on investment based on the success of their role models – the observable patterns behind the success of peers, and the general norms and rewards that an institution or society offers. As indicated by Bandura, an inexperienced learner might not have the capacity to reproduce the exact behaviors of their role models. They do, however, have the abilities to form preferences as to what they might think are more beneficial and rewarding.

Synthesizing these social and experiential learning theories leads us to point to the role that institutional settings and norms play in consciously and subconsciously influencing career paths and preferences. It would seem that, for some institutions at least, these settings and norms leave little room for the reflective observation (Gabriel, 2010), crucial for apprehension and experiential learning (Kolb, 1984). Relating this issue to the novice researcher, their relative lack of experience inhibits still further such reflexivity (Alvesson et al, 2008). Setting this alongside their social learning, it seems that the situation is compounded still further. Social learning may influence the novice learner and lead them to see what they would like to see, and act in ways that they believe might lead to a more promising future with a less risky methodological commitment. As argued by Bandura (1977, p. 132), external reinforcements can sometimes be too difficult to fight against, because ‘the standards by which behavior is judged take other forms in societies organized around a collectivist ethic’. As one of our panellists was moved to say:

Even though [the students] might not know much about qualitative research, many of them have been severely brainwashed and poisoned by their previous quantitative training. Some of them can be very biased against qualitative methods, in particular its relevance to their future careers. (3E)

## Moving forward

Our panellists have shared some valuable insights, which we then considered in light of social and experiential learning theories. While interesting per se, what of the future? Where might this initial study lead? Among all the themes that are highlighted, perceived bias – pro and/or con – is a ‘reality’ to a number of our panellists. The bias might lead us to think that we, as the community of qualitative researchers, have been rather underrepresented and in some cases mistreated (3E). Regardless of whether this is the case (1D) – or not (2C) – what seems to be important here is that some institutions appear to provide a relatively less adequate preparation in qualitative research (1D; 2B; 3B) than one might wish for. Even though many institutions have the mission to provide an all-round experience of methodological training, connecting the individual elements (e.g., abstract conceptualization, active experimentation, concrete experience and reflective observation) to form an effective learning cycle is difficult (Kolb, 1984). When asked for their view of how the teaching of qualitative methods might be improved, one of our panellists in particular talked about having greater balance between the two approaches – qualitative and quantitative (1C) – something that has been implemented in the Bentley University Ph.D. curriculum, for example (www.bentley .edu/phd). This is reflected in Kolb’s writings, as each facilitates a different mode of experience accumulation and knowledge creation (ibid, p. 109):

We are thus led to the conclusion that the proper attitude for the creation of knowledge is neither a dogmatism of apprehension or comprehension nor an utter scepticism, but an attitude of partial scepticism in which the knowledge of comprehension is held provisionally to be tested against apprehensions, and vice versa.

‘All research is biased’ as one of our respondents (1C) so aptly put it. Demonstrating the different conclusions that might be drawn from the same empirical data, utilizing qualitative and quantitative approaches (as in Newell & Edelman, 2008), and as argued more generally by Mingers (2001), might lead to a deeper appreciation of this point and a more balanced perspective concerning what may be perceived to be ‘appropriate’ research methods and approaches. Thus, we would argue that we should apply Kolb’s learning cycle in our teaching of research methods by facilitating apprehension with comprehension, and vice versa. A deeper appreciation of questions of bias, irrespective of method, would emerge as a result.

As we have been at pains to say, this is but a small, tentative step on the path to understanding issues associated with the teaching of qualitative methods in our community. Indeed, Figure 1 incorporates divergent as well as convergent viewpoints on the part of our panel members. There is no universality here. Further, we have yet to investigate the important distinction between positivist and interpretivist approaches (see Galliers, 1991) in the teaching of quantitative and/or qualitative methods. We may claim, though, to have brought together insights and reflections in a manner that may prove potentially insightful, and hopefully seminal. As such, and as our approach has applied social and experiential learning theories, we are hopeful that this research might prove to be a launching pad for further research. Thus, for example, social learning theory might lead us to foresee an on-going underdevelopment of the qualitative research community. Yet, applying Kolb, the weakening of the qualitative tradition will not help the general development of the IS field, given the alternative routes to knowledge creation that appear necessary, and the complexity of the phenomena we study. Here, Ashby’s Law of Requisite Variety comes to mind: ‘only variety can destroy variety’ (1956; p. 207). In other words, rephrasing Shakespeare, ‘variety is the spice of life’. Rather than arguing for the primacy of one or the other paradigms, we (like 1B and 2A for example) see that both have important roles to play. Arising from this study, it would seem that an important line of enquiry might usefully relate to how we as an academy might attempt to overcome the challenges of transferring the required tacit knowledge, through exercises and mini projects, for example (1C; 2B) associated with undertaking qualitative research. Kolb’s call for a mixture of different learning types might prove fruitful in this regard in addition to dealing with the questions of bias discussed above.

Given the explorative nature of this research, it is premature to propose a blueprint for the development of qualitative research training within our academy, but perhaps three agenda items might be proposed, even at this early stage. First, given that our study has surfaced some views and assumptions that may not often be heard and/or spoken in polite circles, it would appear to be important that we attempt to develop, at the community level (as suggested by Bandura), a more positive reinforcement of the qualitative research tradition where negative bias may currently exist (and conversely a more positive reinforcement of quantitative research where this is seen in a negative light). In passing, we might reflect on Argyris & Scho¨n’s (1974, pp. 6–7) distinction between espoused theory and theory-in-use:

When someone is asked how he would behave under certain circumstances, the answer he usually gives is his espoused theory of action for that situation. This is the theory of action to which he gives allegiance, and which, upon request, he communicates to others. However, the theory that actually governs his actions is this theory-in-use.

While we have editorials from various journal editors calling for more qualitative papers over the years, the proportion of such articles published in those journals has remained low (Chen & Hirschheim, 2004; Galliers & Whitley, 2007). As a community, we might seek to explore why this has been so. Major journal editors may need to do more than write editorials on the matter. Demonstrating that sound qualitative research is indeed welcome in our journals is what is required. This may be achieved by such pro-active measures as calling for journal special issues or through developing and leading workshop sessions at major conferences – taking up the opportunity to be active role models (cf., Bandura), in other words. Existing examples include the special issue of JIT on Interpretive Research (Myers, 1998), and of MISQ on Action Research (Baskerville & Myers, 2004). A Professional Development Workshop on publishing qualitative research in major journals focusing on IS, organizations and strategy, was co-organized by one of the authors at the Academy of Management meeting held in San Antonio, Texas in August 2011. Further examples include recent calls for MISQ special issues on the ‘Sociomateriality of Information Systems and Organizing’ (http://www.misq.org/skin/frontend/default/ misq/pdf/CurrentCalls/SI\_Sociomateriality.pdf), and on ‘Critical Realism in Information Systems Research’ (http:// www.misq.org/skin/frontend/default/misq/pdf/Current Calls/CriticalRealism.pdf).

Second, and arising from this, we would hope to see IS research programs becoming more holistic in terms of developing qualitative and quantitative research training jointly (as implied by Kolb). Thus, we would suggest that institutions consider a portfolio of research methods training emphasizing a more significant role for apprehension in qualitative methods courses (with some comprehension) alongside a more significant role for comprehension in quantitative methods courses (with some apprehension). More specifically, within qualitative methods courses, we propose that more effort is spent on apprehension through exercises, and ‘learning by doing’. We see this article as but a start along the road of greater reflection and further research into the research methods training we provide for those joining our academy.

Third, more needs to be done in developing the current discussion to provide greater in-depth investigation of the themes introduced herein in association with the literature on social and experiential learning, and in extending the discussion to incorporate aspects of the paradigmatic debates that have long held sway in the field of IS. For example, delving more deeply into the implications of this study for teaching in relation to positivist, interpretive and critical research, and their underlying philosophical stances, could usefully be contemplated (see, for example, Galliers, 1985, 1991; Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991). Given the perceived difficulties associated with transferring tacit skills in class, associated with the advice to learn through exercises, one might imagine different groups of students analyzing the same data or case using positivist, interpretive or critical stances, with discussion then unfolding as different conclusions are drawn by each group. The bias associated with whichever lens adopted would then become more apparent and, with practice, greater care would be taken in the choice of research method and philosophical stance<sup>3</sup>.

We set out to explore the teaching of qualitative research methods in IS. We have unfurled the curtain; now the play should be enacted: in theory and in practice.
