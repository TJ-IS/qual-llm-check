# Client as designer in collaborative design science research projects: what does social science design theory tell us?

## Abstract

This paper reports a study of a design science research project in which researchers in computer science and earth science collaborated to attack a looming problem in the earth sciences – the need to analyze data of different types (satellite data at various resolutions, text, raster, and vector). The motivations of the two partners appeared complementary – the computer scientists needed a hard problem to solve to focus their research, and the earth scientists had a hard problem in need of solution. This study uses theory and research on design from the social sciences to explore the experience of users who are included as partners in a design project. It finds that it is very difficult for a client partner to enter the ‘design world’ as full collaborators. European Journal of Information Systems (2008) 17, 476–488. doi:10.1057/ejis.2008.46

Keywords: design theory; design practice; scientific collaboration; design science research; designer-client colloboration

## Introduction

Most social science design theory focuses, appropriately enough, on designers. However, in designer-client collaborative projects, the user also participates in design, often at a high level. This paper uses social science theory on designers to explore the collaborative design experience of users. The data are from a 4-year participant observation study of extensive collaboration between computer scientists and earth scientists. Sequoia 2000 was a 3-year, multi-million dollar design science research project funded primarily by Digital Equipment Corporation (DEC). The goal was innovative research in the areas of interactive, multi-terabyte information systems, high-speed networking, and scientific visualization, based on the data-handling needs in global change science.

Data from four critical aspects of the client collaborators’ experience are analyzed: the problem setting embodied in the Sequoia research goal, the incentives brought by the clients to the collaboration, and the benefits and difficulties they experienced. Aspects of the computer scientists’ experience are also discussed when they provide a fuller understanding of the environment within which the earth scientists were working.

The analysis is based on theory and research that takes a social science approach to design. Many of the theorists and researchers are social scientists; others are from design disciplines and have adopted social science methods to explore the work of design. Both will be called, for ease of reference, ‘social science’ research or theory. Social science design theory differs from information system design theory in that it focuses on a phenomenon and actors (Charmaz, 2006); design theory in information systems is prescriptive, articulating knowledge about how to design (Gregor & Jones, 2007). The literature review will highlight concepts that recur frequently in the social science research literature on design.

## Literature review

Design theory and research can be found scattered through many disciplinary literatures – sociotechnical studies, anthropology, software development, architecture, commercial product development, civil engineering, even management. Nigel Cross (2001) has written of ‘designerly ways of knowing,’ an intellectual orientation that crosses disciplinary and professional boundaries. No attempt is made here to summarize and synthesize the diverse literatures that have contributed to our understanding, but rather themes that recur will be identified. This review will first briefly compare social science approaches and information systems approaches to research and theory. It will then highlight the recurring themes in the social science approaches. These themes and concepts will provide the analytical framework for the research reported in this paper.

Most if not all of the social science research on design has been qualitative; methods include participant observation, introspection, ethnography, interviewing, and textual analysis. Research with a social science orientation studies environments, actors, and phenomena. The emphasis may be on any of the three. The aim of positivistic inquiry is to explain, ultimately enabling prediction and control of phenomena; the aim of more recent constructivist inquiry is understanding. Theory, then, consists of the ‘notions used by the researcher to explain the patterning’ of data (Van Maanen, 1979, quoted by Miles & Huberman, 1994, p. 113). One qualitative researcher explains the process as studying what is happening in a setting and making a conceptual rendering of these actions (Charmaz, 2006, p. 22). Positivist criteria for judging research are internal and external validity, reliability, and objectivity; constructivism emphasizes credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability.

A recent paper on information systems design theory by Gregor & Jones (2007) characterizes information systems design theory as articulating design knowledge concerning a specific category of software products or a methodology for designing a category of products. Similarly, Walls et al. (1992, 2004) see information systems design theories as prescriptive. In their 1992 paper, they contrast design theory, which tells ‘how to’ and why, with social science theory, which is explanatory and predictive. A social science theory may be a kernel theory in design, for instance describing user characteristics that design of an artifact should take into account. Gregor and Jones as well include kernel theories from the social and natural sciences in design theory as ‘justificatory’ knowledge. In addition, they list eight components of information systems design theory including requirements (for a class of products, not an individual product), testable propositions, and physical implementation of an artifact.

Although the paragraphs above do not do justice to the rich theory that exists, they are intended to give a sense of fundamental differences between a social science approach to design and an information systems approach. Social science theory seeks to understand how design is done; information systems theory seeks to develop the best possible designs and methods for design. The remainder of the literature review focuses primarily on social science design research and theory.

Herb Simon (1969) is often the starting point for contemporary discussion of design. Simon’s book The Sciences of the Artificial argued that the professions are different from the natural sciences; and that they needed to develop their own science to maintain their standing within universities and avoid being subsumed into the natural sciences. He reflected on the mid-20th century phenomenon of engineering schools gradually becoming schools of physics and mathematics, medical schools becoming schools of biological science, and other similar movements within many professions, and argued that to remain themselves and also to renew themselves, professional schools needed to develop a science of design. Simon argued that there is a common creative activity in which designers in any profession are engaged, and that they should ‘begin to share their experiences of the creative, professional design process’ (p. 137). His 1973 paper ‘The structure of Ill-structured problems’ was a step in that direction; Simon argued that apparently illdefined problems could be broken down into smaller and smaller pieces until a level was reached where each piece was well defined.

Simon’s work toward the creation of a science of the artificial was part of a reaction that began in the early 20th century in many disciplines against an earlier conception of design as containing mysterious, inexact, inspiration-based, often self-indulgent elements. Christopher Alexander’s Notes on a Synthesis of Form, 1964) and A Pattern Language (1977) were similar attempts to decompose architectural problems into smaller problems that could be solved rationally.

There has been, in turn, a pendulum swing from this rationalist approach in design theory to a more constructivist approach that asserts the existence of elements that cannot be broken down into fully logical parts. Donald Scho¨n, a philosopher who developed an epistemology of professional practice, argues that Simon’s science can be applied ‘only to well-formed problems already extracted from situations of practice’ (1983, p. 47). For the working professional, Scho¨n says, goals are confused and conflicting, and there is no clear-cut definition of the problem to be solved. Problem setting must precede problem solving. Problem setting defines ‘the decisions to be made, the ends to be achieved, the means which may be chosen. [Problems] must be constructed from the materials of problematic situations which are puzzling, troubling, and uncertain’ (p. 41).

Scho¨n accused rationalist theories of being radically incomplete. He studied six different professions for his 1983 book The Reflective Practitioner and found phenomena that could not be explained by Simon’s description of the design process. Well-formed problem solving occurred only in later stages, after the design structure had stabilized.

Simon and Scho¨n are among the most-cited authors on the subject of design, often cited in the same sentence with no mention of their profound differences. The insights they did share are that design is fundamental to professional work and that there exists a common experience of design that crosses professional and disciplinary boundaries.

Another theorist reacting against the rationalist assumption that ill-defined problems can be converted into well-defined problems is Horst Rittel. In ‘Dilemmas in a general theory of planning’ (1973), Rittel and Webber argue that design problems are ‘wicked problems.’ Wicked problems are those that are ill defined, messy, circular, and aggressive. ‘Tame’ problems are definable and separable; in addition, it is possible to determine whether or not the problem has been solved. Rittel and Webber list 10 distinguishing properties of wicked problems. Among them are the following: there is no definitive formulation of a wicked problem, wicked problems have no stopping rule, and every wicked problem can be considered to be the result of another problem.

Rittel, like Scho¨n, asserts the importance of problem setting. With wicked problems, ‘one of the most intractable problems is that of defining the problem (of knowing what distinguishes an observed condition from a desired condition) and of locating problems (finding where in the complex causal networks the trouble really lies)’ (p. 159). Or, as Bucciarelli (1994, p. 177) notes, ‘naming is critical to design.

Petroski says about structural engineering that it is ‘designing what has not existed before’ (1982, p. 10). He describes structural engineering design as ‘a series of hypotheses that such and such an arrangement of parts will perform a desired function without fail’ (p. 44). A high tolerance for ambiguity is thus essential to a designer. The ineradicability of uncertainty, the traits inherent in wicked problems, the malleability of the problem itself, all must be accepted if the designer is to proceed rather than freeze in place trying to eliminate the uncertain. As Scho¨n asserts, the designer ‘can never make a move which has only the effects intended.’

Some of the uncertainty results from what Scho¨n calls the ‘reflective conversation with materials’ that takes place as a designer works with the materials in accordance with her understanding of the problem; the materials ‘talk back,’ and she responds to the back talk. The designer is learning as she forms and tests various hypotheses about the solution. Keller & Keller (1993) provide a wonderful description of the interaction between action and knowledge, the way that one move by a designer creates a reaction in the material from which he or she learns something new, thus leading to a new action which creates a reaction resulting in additional new knowledge. It is impossible to know ahead of time how a conversation between designer and materials will end.

The study of conversations with materials contributes to addressing what Orlikowski & Iacono (2001) identify as a need for theorizing the artifact in information technology research. At the most fundamental level of writing code, as well as at other levels of design, there is a great deal of ‘backing and forthing,’ of ‘build and evaluate’ (Hevner & March, 2003), which could provide additional insight into this conversation.

Failure is one form of back talk to the designer. Petroski argues that failure is an essential learning experience for engineers; it shows her where her knowledge is weak and needs to be improved upon. The principal objective of structural design is obviating failure; and colossal failures ‘contribute to the evolution of innovative and inspiring structures y a failed structure provides a counterexample to a hypothesis and shows us incontrovertibly what cannot be done, while a structure that stands without incident often conceals whatever lessons or caveats it might hold for the next generation of engineers’ (p. 163).

Uncertainty is closely linked to creativity. If the best solution could be determined mechanistically, there would be little need for creative thought. Petroski (1982), for instance, views the engineer as both artist and scientist, first synthesizing her experience and knowledge and making a leap of the imagination, then as a scientist analyzing the potential success of the design by posing a series of questions about the behavior of the parts under the imagined conditions of use after construction. Bucciarelli (1994, p. 123) also asserts that ‘design, by its very nature, is an uncertain and creative process. y Uncertainty y allows participants to exercise their creativity.

Uncertainty, then, is a fundamental component of design. ‘If the process lacks uncertainty, then you can be sure it is not designing, but copying’ (Bucciarelli, 1994, p. 177). Design science research (Hevner & March, 2003; Hevner et al., 2004; Vaishnavi & Kuechler, 2007) exemplifies the most uncertain kind of design. Design science research is design that creates artifacts of sufficient novelty and usefulness, ‘solving a heretofore unsolved problem or solving a known problem in a more effective or efficient manner’ (Hevner et al., 2004, p. 82), that it contributes to the growth of knowledge in the discipline. Design science research is contrasted to routine system building, which is important but does not add to design knowledge.

The role of the client in design has been addressed primarily in the literature concerning requirements analysis in software engineering. Brooks in The Mythical Man Month (1995, p. 199) asserts that ‘No other part of the conceptual work is more difficult’ than requirements elicitation. The client is often seen as the major problem in successful analysis of a design situation. It is notoriously difficult to elicit descriptions of current practice and needs that are substantial enough to provide direction in design. Davis (1992, p. 15) asserts that ‘requirements elicited from users are usually not complete’; Newman & Rosenberg (1985, p. 401) write that ‘people have nebulous ideas of what they want. They do not think through the details.’ The fact that they are being asked to think in terms of technical processes makes it more difficult to break out of a narrow frame of reference (Mittermeier, quoted by Zmud et al., 1992). It is difficult to identify those elements of their work practice and needs that will be relevant to designers. Zmud et al. (1992) found that users often perceive their work flow in very concrete ways, and lose specificity when they try to abstract their needs for analysis. When they think of a system that will improve their work, they think in terms of incremental change beyond the system currently in use. Thus the more novel the designers’ ideas, the more difficulty the users will have in identifying what they need from the artifact. The ‘fuzzy not-yet-known (Jevnaker, 2005) is a difficult ground for establishing shared meanings and understandings between designers and clients.

Other researchers have identified imbalances of power between designers and clients who complicate the negotiation of meaning. Analyzing a dominant text of the 1990s, Martin’s Information Engineering, Beath & Orlikowski (1994) found contradictions between the stated ideal of user participation in design and the actual recommendations for practice as they distributed control, responsibility, and accountability between designers and users. Similarly, Star & Ruhleder (1996) identified doublebind situations (originally studied by Bateson in the context of his work on schizophrenia) in which the language of the designer is so unfamiliar to the users that users required to participate in discussions cannot be full participants. More recently, Olsson (2004) describes situations in which users become hostages, with the developers orchestrating decisions, but asserting that the user participants had made all the decisions; she points out that many design decisions are implicit, emerging just as the result of someone doing something.

The time cost of participation for users has been recognized by some researchers. Iivari & Igbaria (1997) draw attention to the considerable cost of participation, pointing out that ‘the primary job of users is not to participate in systems development, but to do their work as well as possible. y Participation [in design] requires time that the participant cannot use in his primary duties, even though participation may increase the user’s qualifications in his primary work in the longer run’ (p. 113). Although she does not address time costs specifically, Rennecker (2002) describes the dure´e, the ongoing flow of activity comprising daily life (based on Giddens, 1984); taking a design approach ‘represents a choice to y suspend one’s self above or just outside the flow, making its patterns and governing rules visible.’ For users collaborating in design, this suspension above the dure´e must be time away from their primary work.

Participatory design is a technique intended to have users not just engaged but actually taking the lead through all the design stages (Hirschheim, 1985; Pilemalm et al., 2007). However, in practice it has been used primarily as a means of gathering needs and requirements information quickly from users. Research in this area relevant to the Sequoia study examines contextual factors that affect user participation. Pilemalm et al. (2007) found that participants did not feel they had time to contribute to stages of design following the requirements elicitation. Carroll & Rosson (2007) in a study of participatory design in a community informatics project found that creating and sustaining users’ motivation depended upon the organization explicitly revising its strategic planning to place importance on technology issues (and therefore on technology work); without organizational commitment to technology work, there was often insufficient incentive for users to invest the time required by the participatory design process. Hirschheim (1985) in his study of user experiences with participative design similarly found that one of the three factors necessary for success was the support of top management, including the allocation of sufficient resources, particularly in terms of manpower.

Scho¨n points out that an important outcome of requirements elicitation is a reification of meaning, negotiation of the definition of the problem and the solution. It is equally clear, however, that this can fail to happen without either side being aware of it until much later; participants often talk across discrepant frames, unaware that they are doing so (Scho¨n, 1990, p. 138). This may be a major obstacle to the work of design, or not. The problem definition may serve as a boundary object (Star & Griesemer, 1989) through which differing understandings can work together toward the same result. However, if the frames are incompatible, then a failed boundary object will be the result and work will not progress, or will progress only with difficulty.

This study draws heavily on Scho¨n’s idea of a ‘design world’ (1992) and Jevnaker’s (2005) concept of different ‘lifeworlds’ inhabited by designers and their clients (in that case, design consultants and the companies employing them). Jevnaker points out that designers and clients operate ‘in the mess,’ that is, in lifeworlds that are in different ways ‘full of awkward complications, fragmentation, and unexpected fluctuation’ (p. 27). When a collaboration begins, living–work relationships must be developed to enable them to work together, resulting in more complexity and change.

Elements of the lifeworld of design, then, as identified by these researchers taking a social science approach, include the existence of a way of knowing that is inherent to design work of any kind, wicked problems that do not decompose into well-formed problems, and a pervasive uncertainty at all stages of design (though more pronounced in early stages than later). The work of design has been described as posing hypotheses about the outcomes of the designer’s actions, and as a reflective conversation with the materials of design. In both descriptions, the outcome is not known in advance, and the actions taken by the designer generate outcomes that contain unexpected elements, resulting in changing hypotheses and experimenting with different actions. Failure is an important element of design, as it allows diagnosis and resolution of problems. These factors require of a designer a high tolerance for ambiguity and uncertainty. The literature also points out differences in the frames through which designers and clients understand technology, and the fact that discrepant frames may go unnoticed and lead to discrepant expectations that the participants are not aware of until problems arise.

## Method

I was a social scientist partially funded by Sequoia 2000 to study collaboration and communication as a participant observer. Both qualitative and quantitative data were gathered over a period of a little more than 3 years. An initial paper about the project, ‘The structure of incentive’ (Weedman, 1998) used research and theory about collaboration and communication in technology projects in analyzing the data.

The qualitative data included field notes on Sequoia activities; I attended the weekly seminars broadcast to the participating campuses from UC Berkeley, attended the project-wide retreats, and made site visits to the campuses. Forty interviews were also conducted, often lasting 2 h, with the entire range of project participants – the coprincipal investigators (co-PIs), the research scientists, the graduate students, and the programmer-analysts, and other staff. The qualitative data were transcribed from notes and tape recordings. The initial coding used concept categories (Miles & Huberman, 1994) that emerged from the data itself and those gathered from the social science research literature on collaboration and communication in technology projects. The combination of emergent concepts with those established by previous researchers allowed an interpretive framework that built on earlier work but was flexible enough to extend it and accommodate new insights. These concepts were then used to answer fundamental social science questions about processes within the project and their meaning for the participants (Charmaz, 2006).

Quantitative data came from three waves of questionnaires administered to all participants at intervals throughout the project; some of the questions were repeated to track change over time, and others were unique to the particular stage of the project. Descriptive statistics were used to obtain an overall sense of participants’ understanding of Sequoia 2000. ‘The structure of incentive’ was based primarily on the quantitative data and field notes. It found an inherent misalignment of incentive in the user–designer relationship. ‘At its simplest, the designers’ incentive to build the best technical system possible is not well aligned with the incentive for users to move their work directly forward’ (p. 337). Surveying the literature of collaboration, participatory design, and requirements analysis, problems that were present but manageable in small-scale projects were found to exist in a project of this size as well, and to be much more difficult to manage.

This study draws on a different body of literature, that of social science research on designers and the practice of design. Its purpose is to add another dimension to understanding of large-scale designer–user collaborations such as those that may be carried out in design science research by adding an analysis of the user participants’ experience as members of a design project. It is based primarily on the interview data, using again a combination of emergent concept categories from the data and concept categories established by previous researchers for coding and analysis. Concepts about the experience and work of designers from the literature reviewed are used as a framework to analyze the experience and work of the client collaborators.

The Literature review, based on the assumption that Cross (2001) is correct in his assertion that there is a designerly way of knowing that transcends discipline, has summarized elements from various social science studies of the designer’s work practice in design projects. These elements characterize what Jevnaker has called the ‘design lifeworld’ – the work of a profession as experienced by its practitioners. The data analysis examines the client role in a collaborative design research project to see if those elements pertain to that experience as well, and if so, what their effect is on the client. Four aspects of the design project were selected: the act of problem setting because of its influence on the rest of the project, the incentive structures that guide how designers and clients deploy their resources, and difficulties and benefits experienced by the client group during the 3-year period. Problem setting and incentive structures were chosen for analysis because they lay the foundation for a design project, difficulties and benefits because they provide insight into the meanings a project has for its participants. Information about the design group is included when it is important as context for the clients.

## Sequoia 2000

The motivations for Sequoia 2000 were multiple and apparently complementary. There was an increased emphasis on applied research by the Federal government; in 1992, the Computer Science and Technology Board of the National Research Council published Computing the Future: A Broader Agenda for Computer Science and Engineering, asserting that computer science theory had become inwardly focused in some areas, and needed a variety of outward-looking interactions, including interactions with problem domains that had sophisticated computing needs. They stressed that it was essential that work be done by investigators from computer science and engineering paired with investigators from other disciplines or professions, and that they regard each other as intellectual equals; to achieve truly collaborative work, there should be two PIs, one from computer science and engineering and one from the domain discipline or profession.

At the same time, computer scientists in the fields of databases and storage at the University of California, Berkeley were looking for a new project. There was a tradition going back to the 1970s of large projects, and as their current projects came to a close they were casting about for the next ones.

The earth sciences at this time were beginning to realize that a major data-handling problem was approaching. Global change researchers inhabit a broad variety of disciplines – hydrology, botany, climatology, atmospheric research, oceanography, and more. In addition, data are taken from different sources and at different resolutions. Sequoia 2000 would focus on satellite imagery.

DEC was also looking for a project. They were finishing Project Athena at MIT (from which X Windows technology came) and, after some deliberation, decided that they would put their research money into a single big project again rather than into several smaller ones. The funding would provide some money and significant equipment grants.

Sequoia 2000 grew out of this mix of motivations. The pieces came together, a proposal for the DEC grant was written, and ultimately the project was selected and funded. The final configuration had 22 investigators, seven from computer science and information science at Berkeley, one from computer science from UC Irvine, two from computer science at UC San Diego, two from the San Diego Supercomputer Center, four earth scientists from UCLA, three earth scientists from UC Santa Barbara, and three earth scientists from the Scripps Institute of Oceanography at UC San Diego. There were also additional partners from Federal and state resource agencies and from other technology companies. The project was headed by two co-PIs, a computer scientist from UC Berkeley and an earth scientist from UC Santa Barbara. Sequoia 2000 was thus a model of the kind of research that the National Research Council recommended.

Four technology components were proposed as the basis for the solution of the data-handling problems: vastly increased storage, much faster networking, visualization techniques for modeling, and a new database management system to replace the existing flat file system.

Secondary and tertiary storage, plus Digital’s new Alpha workstation farms, provided the capacity needed to hold large quantities of data and to run more and larger simulations than had been possible previously. The architecture allowed applications, the database management system, the file system, and the storage devices to be on different machines; a T3 backbone was the network, with a delivery service that could guarantee specific bandwidth and latency. Visualization to test models against data was accomplished with existing offthe-shelf geographic information system tools such as AVS (produced by Advanced Visual Systems) and IDL (produced by ITT Visual Information Systems).

The database research built on Postgres, objectrelational database software developed as the successor to Ingres (1977–1985) by Sequoia’s computer science PI and his graduate students. Neither Postgres nor any of the other existing relational and object-oriented DBMSs met the needs of the Sequoia earth scientists. The Sequoiadesigned software (eventually commercialized as Illustra, then integrated into Informix, which was acquired by IBM in 2001) supported large arrays of spatial data such as points, lines, and polygons. A cross-disciplinary metadata schema was designed that accommodated scale, vector, raster, and text data. A novel chunking strategy was developed that improved retrieval performance; the Postgres interface was also improved.

The frequent claim that Sequoia would change the way that global change researchers did their science seemed appropriate, if a bit dramatic. Vastly increased storage and network speeds would allow more data transfer than had ever been possible. The information system would create change at several levels. First, the way one interacts with the data is different in a DBMS environment than with a flat file. Analyses in a flat file require that the data be written to accommodate the particular analysis to be done. This means that for each different analysis, a new file has to be created. In a database environment, the data are written once into the files, and commands are then written to retrieve the particular data of interest and carry out the procedures; running a different analysis simply requires a different set of commands to be run against the same data file. This also would lead to a second change, greater productivity – less time from idea to code and less processing time (from weeks down to hours). Third, data sharing is much easier with databases than with flat files.

The novelty and importance of the design, and the definition of the project as contributing to the knowledge of computer science in ‘advanced distributed information systems,’ made Sequoia 2000 an example of design science research (Hevner et al., 2004), in which the design itself constitutes research and contributes to disciplinary knowledge growth. More information on the Sequoia hardware and software is provided in a special section in the Digital Technical Journal (Blake, 1995).

## Data analysis

## Problem definition

If the core work of professionals is to turn a problem situation into a solution (Simon, 1996), then logically the first step in a design project is to identify the problem. In The Reflective Practitioner, Scho¨n emphasizes the importance of how a problem is defined: it is in the process of problem setting that ‘we select what we will treat as the ‘‘things’’ of the situation, we set the boundaries of our attention to it’ (1983, p. 40). Rittel & Webber (1973) point out that one of the most intractable problems in a wicked problem such as design is that there is no definitive formulation of it.

However intractable formulating the problem is, projects in search of grant money must indeed formulate a problem and its solution. Official project descriptions, once written and distributed, have the effect of reifying meaning; they set the frame of the collaboration within which the participants position themselves. However, the participants may attribute different meanings to the statement. Scho¨n (1990) observes that participants often talk across discrepant frames, unaware that they are doing so.

The official project description was ‘Sequoia 2000 is a large scale collaboration between global change scientists and computer scientists throughout the University of California system, with funding and participation provided by state and federal government agencies, and industry. It is a research project in interactive, multiterabyte information systems, high speed networking, and scientific visualization in support of global change science. Global change research, in turn, serves as a testbed for the advanced distributed information systems developed by the computer scientists’ (Machefsky, 1991, p. 2). The collaboration was to rest on mutual benefit that was generally phrased as ‘computer science has solutions and needs problems to solve; earth science has datahandling problems in need of a solution.’ The problem and the solution appear well aligned, and the participation of the two groups complementary.

In fact, however, the clients’ understanding of the problem differed from what the official description actually said. The earth scientists saw the problem to be solved as situated in their world of global change research, a need for improved hardware and software for their work. At the end of the project, the earth science participants were asked what their initial understanding and expectations of Sequoia had been. One scientist said that he had been very skeptical about the grandiosity of the goals. Most said that they thought it would be ‘wonderful’ – a cutting edge collaboration – a chance to do lots of new and interesting things – ‘having an ideal situation where, if I get all these data on there and I run a model through it, we visualize it, write up a paper, and then both people’s names go on that paper: the researcher who created the database and the earth science researcher who created the models.’ A programmer working in earth science said his image was ‘here’s a project which is going to be pumping out lots of software for earth science researchers, and what of it can I use?

These expectations do not align well with the official problem description of Sequoia as research into advanced information systems. Although the research will be ‘in support of global change science,’ the clients’ role in the project is to be ‘a testbed y for computer scientists.’ Thus global change research was set within the problem boundaries as an experimental platform existing for the use of the designers as they tried out possible solutions. To the extent that the official project description set the problem, the earth scientists’ participation would take place within the design world of computer science research.

## Incentive structure

Petroski (1982) compares designers to authors who seek professional distinction and therefore avoid the known, tried-and-true formulas, and also to pole vaulters seeking to break records. Design incentives favor the new and innovative, and this is particularly true in a design science research project where contribution to knowledge is an explicit goal of the work.

A questionnaire was administered to all participants in Sequoia 2000 early in the project; some of the questions addressed the initial incentives for joining. Consistent with Petroski’s insight, intellectual challenge was listed by 57% of the designers as their primary initial professional motivation, and the desire to learn by 43% as their initial personal motivation. These goals would be met in the process of carrying out design science research. In earth science, the challenge was a primary incentive for only 16.6%, and learning a motivator for 25%; their primary professional incentive was the presence of computer scientists who would design new hardware and software, and the personal goal was to do better science (42% each). In contrast to the computer scientists, the users’ goals centered on the results of the design research, not on the design research itself.

These incentives are consistent with the incentives inherent in universities and academic disciplines. One of the strongest norms in academic disciplines is the contribution to disciplinary knowledge. Although rewards (rank, salary) come through the university for which faculty and researchers work, it is the discipline that provides the evaluation of journal articles, conference papers, grant proposals, and books on which the rewards are based. Thus the incentives in academia are based on the disciplines, and even university commitments to certain programs of action often do not outweigh those incentives.

The incentive structure along with budget factors interfered with solutions to a major problem that arose midway through the project. To use the emerging technologies in their present work, the earth scientists needed ‘grease and glue’ – small-scale, sometimes temporary, fixes along the way. As the issue was not anticipated, it was not included as a part of the definition of the design problem that Sequoia would solve, and no funds were allocated for such work. The funding came primarily in the form of equipment, so the budget did not allow hiring additional staff members.

When asked in an interview early on about potential problems earth scientists might have with the technology and how they would be handled, the computer science PI had answered that his department was not a service unit, and that these problems were outside the scope of their commitment. As the project went on, however, it became clear that earth science participation could not happen without some ‘service.

Although solutions for the day-to-day difficulties the earth scientists encountered in their Sequoia work were well within the designers’ ability to solve, the incentives supported contribution to knowledge production in computer science, not easy but time-consuming grease and glue solutions. The graduate students needed to build careers on difficult problems with publishable solutions, and therefore were discouraged by their advisors from investing time. The programmers equally were unable to free up time for solving minor problems in another discipline. The designers had been socialized into the commitment to knowledge growth in computer science, and had no time or interest for the small, nearterm problems that arose while the large, long-term problems were being solved.

Neither did solving the grease and glue problems fall within the incentives that motivated the clients in the collaboration. The earth scientists described themselves as driven by grant cycles; most of their students and technical professionals were paid with grant money, and one researcher characterized the students and programmers on his projects as family for whom he had to provide by bringing in the next round of funding. In the earth sciences, grants are given for doing science – not for doing design. Thus the energies of the senior investigators, the technical professionals, and the graduate students had to be directed toward the research for which they had been funded if they were to be awarded funds in the next cycle. Graduate students could not be encouraged to do technology design, because they needed to be building the earth science research skills and reputations that would carry them into their careers after they graduated. Programmers had little time to eke out from their responsibilities in the investigators’ current research. The investigators themselves were motivated by their interest in global change, not by an interest in technology. ‘The structure of incentive’ (Weedman, 1998) explores at greater length the asymmetries in the incentive structure of Sequoia 2000 and concludes that they are inevitable in collaborative design science research projects.

The co-PIs realized that the problems were serious enough that they would lose the earth science participants if they were not solved. They were unable to find a full solution, but did find ways of mitigating the difficulties. Both disciplines freed up some of their programmers’ time to work on the issues. Many of the programmers dedicated personal time to solving problems. The DEC equipment allowed those earth scientists who had included equipment in their climate change grant proposal budgets to take that money and invest it in staff for Sequoia work.

## Difficulties encountered by clients as designers

The PIs expected that there would be major difficulties emerging from the cultural differences between their two disciplines. They were aware that the fields had different languages, different work practices, and different basic assumptions, and they created communication structures and norms of behavior to address those differences (Weedman, 1998).

The problems actually described by the earth science participants, however, did not derive from differences between the disciplines. Rather, they derived from differences in understanding of the design process and of the roles and responsibilities of designers and users. Sequoia 2000 was a design project, creating what Scho¨n calls a ‘design world’ (1992, p. 9), and as the problem statement indicated, the client’s participation was situated within that world, not the world of earth science.

Within a design world, the conversation with materials sometimes results in failure, and the failure results in improvements in the design (Petroski, 1982). One of the first and most dramatic difficulties for the clients arose from the fact that one way of making sure you have created a reliable product is to ‘pound on it till it breaks. This is exactly why the computer scientists wanted unusually large data sets to work with; they wanted to learn how to build systems that would work with terabytes of data. In design science research projects where you are truly learning from the design work, once the system breaks you know what needs to be fixed. Once that is fixed, you stress the system until it breaks again. For computer science, this repeated failure is the means to success – only by pushing the system beyond its limits do you extend its limits. For earth science, a breaking system was a complete barrier to their ability to carry out useful work. In the early days, the network went down repeatedly. After the network stabilized, earth scientists loaded data into the database, and it not only broke, it ate their data. After that problem was fixed, the file system behind the database broke (but at least did not destroy the data).

Earth scientists had not understood their role to be stressors of the system, participants in the ‘conversation with materials’ that moves an artifact from its early to final stages. They needed products that would work and had no time for breaking the system so that computer science could learn something that would make it better. They did not understand the centrality of failure (Petroski, 1982) to the design process; one angry earth science investigator exclaimed bitterly that computer scientist had never planned to build a working system for them in the first place, but rather wanted their participation as guinea pigs.

The computer scientists saw the client as involved in the dure´e of design, providing data for testing the system, using the system, stressing the system, participating in the conversation with materials. The clients themselves, however, saw their role as customers rather than as a testbed; they expected to receive hardware and software that would enable them to do their research in new and better ways. The conversation with materials that is so valuable for designing artifacts is not valuable for the process of using the artifacts to do science. The conversation with materials that the earth scientist wants to have is with the data, determining which analyses and hypotheses can be developed and which are dead ends, and the software is a tool for conducting that conversation. A conversation with the tool is only a costly diversion from the work.

A second problem arose from the instability of the new technologies and from the insertion of more layers between the earth scientist and the data. The addition of remote storage and the network, while it enabled much faster work and greater productivity, also increased the chances of data corruption. Data integrity is essential for doing science – which means that confidence in the integrity of one’s data is essential. Van House (2002, 2003) found concerns of several kinds among environmental scientists about trust in networked data even when the network was mature and stable; her paper title ‘Biodiversity data perversity: Understanding barriers to sharing data across epistemic communities’ nicely captures the issues she studied (Van House, 2003).

A lengthy quotation from an interview with an earth scientist post-doc gives a sense of the dual worlds of design and earth science that they were trying to inhabit.

Just a couple months back we were using the EPIC system, and the data flag hadn’t been turned on to enable error checking. A tiny thing, and easy to fix. But you’d read your data once and read it again, and the two files would be very similar, but maybe a handful of bytes would be different. [Addition of the remote storage and network created a complete change in thinking:] you’re no longer the sole person responsible for your data. You have to trust that this system is going to keep your data uncorrupted – which has been a big problem so far. And will deliver it to you uncorrupted, which was also a problem. At least for us at Scripps. Apparently people outside of Scripps weren’t having the problem because we’re one of the farther links in this chain. If you have to keep trying to send it, and it keeps failing in different places, then you get into an iteration where you’re fighting the system, spending so much time fighting the system that it’s much more productive to go back to the old way of swapping tapes. Because you know that’s going to work and you can trust it. When it comes to where your data sits, trust is very important. And that trust hasn’t been built up yet. It’s through no fault, I think, of the Sequoia people there. The people who are doing this are working hard to solve some pretty hard problems. You really can’t go anywhere and get a system that can handle this quantity of data with the kind of error checking that we have to have and the kind of reliability that we have to have. So this is really groundbreaking stuff.

[But] for the earth scientists, their main priority and their main goal in life is to get their papers done and get their research done. They’re only willing to put up with a certain amount of problems before it’s counter-productive to continue with something like that. And y five years from now it might be fantastic, but if you don’t get the research done and get the papers written in the meantime, you won’t be here five years from now to take advantage of it.

This same post-doc went on to comment that now, at the end of the project, ‘the basic technology is there; it’s just a matter of the details. A smoc (small matter of code). I think that for somebody in computer science doing basic research, a small matter of code really is a small thing. ‘‘Well, this check sum wasn’t computed quite right, but other than that it’s perfect.’’ While to the person who’s trying to use the technology, that little check sum is the most important thing, because if you don’t get your data back in exactly the same way you put it in there, then it doesn’t matter if the thing is 99% right. Getting your data correct and when you expect it is absolutely the most important thing, and if you can’t, the system is totally useless.’ His understanding that computer science had to be doing novel research to be successful, and that ‘small matters of code’ were not novel, was not widely shared among the earth scientists.

A problem inherent with nearly any new technology is the learning curve for those adopting it. Lave & Wenger (1991, p. 103) note that ‘invisibility of mediating technologies is necessary for allowing focus on, and thus supporting the visibility of, the subject matter.’ Unfamiliar technology is not invisible, and time needed to make it so must be taken from the subject matter. Databases are different in their underlying philosophy from flat files, and none of the earth science interviewees had had experience with databases before. In addition to a completely different approach to handling data and running analyses, of course all of the specific commands and routines were new. The DBMS went through three versions before it stabilized, each requiring additional learning. There was also new software for designing GUIs and a new operating system.

Software for which training was provided up front was adopted quickly. But since the database management system was under development, there was no manual, forcing the earth science programmers to call the computer science members regularly. The earth science programmers had to invest a lot of time in learning the software, experimenting with it, finding what worked and what did not, and then, in the case of the DBMS, learning a new version. As with unstable hardware and software, the time required to learn the new software contributed to the ‘socio-economic problem’ of having to do research for the current grant so that the next grant could be applied for, which would fund the programmers and other technical staff, and in some cases the investigators themselves.

Earth science’s understanding of the role of ‘client’ to mean ‘customer’ caused additional difficulties. They had expected, without articulating it, that computer science would be designing specifically to meet their current needs. This is a lovely example of Scho¨n’s (1983) observation that partners in design may be talking across discrepant frames with no awareness that they are doing so. Computer science has the same mandate to contribute to the growth of knowledge that any other academic discipline has. The computer science participants were anticipating the future, trying to push the envelope in the development of specific technologies. Petroski (1982), writing of structural engineers, compares them to pole vaulters going after a new record or creative authors whose work is a unique and daring experiment. ‘While engineers who play it safe and copy designs that have stood the test of time may be well paid (though perhaps not nearly as well paid as the authors of mass market paperbacks who use more formulas in their genre fiction than are available to any engineer), there is no more professional distinction in being such an engineer than there is literary recognition in being such an author’ (Petroski, 1982, p. 79).

Their understanding of the client as customer rather than as participant in the work of design led many earth scientists to see their research goals as central rather than the design goals. One earth science investigator eloquently summed up this lack of understanding at the end of the project, ‘I understand computer scientists a lot better now. They’re scientists just like we are. Egocentric just like we are. They have to do their science just like we do. We thought we were getting a bunch of advanced programmers.’

Requirements analysis, the first step when design is described linearly, actually went on throughout most of the project. The difficulties noted in the literature review arose within Sequoia. It was difficult for the clients to understand what the computer scientists needed to know, and difficult for the computer scientists to find ways of explaining their needs to the clients.

As Zmud et al. (1992) point out, many users conceived of technological advance as incremental change in current practice. The earth scientists would describe something they would like to have and the computer science reply would be ‘that’s easy.’ What neither side may have fully realized at the time, and certainly the earth scientists did not, was that ‘that’s easy’ meant ‘that’s not worth doing.’ For current and near-term global change research, of course, it was worth doing and would benefit their science a great deal; but as partners in design, they were committed to collaboration on hard questions, questions for which the answers were not yet known.

Several earth scientists characterized the design goals as grandiose (‘a Cadillac when I just want a Model T’). One reason for this was the fact that these earth science researchers were not yet actually facing the massive data problem that Sequoia 2000 was designed to solve. Critical mass in the field was still on the side of flat files and Fortran. Although some felt that a change to database technology would be essential in the future to integrate the disparate data that reflect global change, that belief was not held by all, and to most the need to change did not seem imminent. Not only was it not imminent, but since most earth scientists did not use databases, they might actually lose opportunities for collaboration. As one earth scientist said, ‘We’re comfortable in our old shoes, our old climate shoes, and we’re not very comfortable with these new wing-tips that those darn propeller heads use.’ (Early on in the project, in recognition of disciplinary differences in culture, the computer scientists and earth scientists began to refer to themselves as the propeller heads and the dirt bags.)

The corollary was that the earth science collaborators could not identify what it was about their work that the designers needed to know, and did not have the understanding of computer science to explain their work in terms that would provide a foundation for significant innovation in the technology itself. This resulted in attempts at requirements elicitation that continued long into the project.

When discussions at retreats and in person failed to elicit sufficient requirements information, a ‘swat team was sent around to the various earth science departments to watch people at work and gather more information. That also did not produce a deep enough understanding. In a third attempt, the earth scientists were asked to produce scenarios that would describe a project from beginning to end, in detail. The scientists themselves, of course, had no time for that kind of activity, so it was delegated to the programmers. The programmers spent a great deal of time drawing up typical scenarios, and computer science still felt that they needed more. The result was that earth science felt they committed an unacceptably large amount of time to explaining earth science to the computer scientists, only to be told that it was not enough. Here too, their role as co-designers was not understood and was in active conflict with their role as earth scientists.

## Benefits accruing to clients as designers

Perceived benefits of a project provide additional insights into process; they reflect the places where the design collaboration successfully connects the frames of the various participants. As with the problems, the benefits varied among participants.

The value of the computer equipment provided by DEC as part of the grant was mentioned by every earth scientist I interviewed. As one programmer said, ‘The machines are ten times faster. And we’ve switched from 9-track tape to 8 mm tape which holds twenty to thirty times the amount of data, so you’re talking about storage in your office. I’ve run a whole year overnight, and that would have taken a month before.’ One scientist said, ‘The project allowed us to think grander than we otherwise would have done. We were planning to study the primary production of the world’s oceans, but in the context of only the Southern Ocean. But with Sequoia, we took on a grander thing to do because we wanted to push the large scale database interactions that Sequoia made available – and we did the whole world. Typically, you pick off a little piece and then you expand it to the world. By taking on the whole problem I think we see it in a different context than we would otherwise have thought about it. Because small observations don’t always scale. And we learned a lot that we otherwise might not have learned.’ Many reported that they were able to do more complex analyses and do them much more quickly because of the new machines.

In these discussions, it became clear that the primary reason this was such a successful outcome of Sequoia was that the earth scientists were able to use the DEC equipment for the research they had already received grants from their usual funders to do. Thus their normal work practice was not interrupted; they could bring this finished, customer-ready equipment into their earth science world.

Learning about database technology was a benefit mentioned by some programmers and scientists. The programmers saw it as a benefit in part simply because it was new and interesting; they also could see the advantages for data storage and analysis. The third version of the DBMS, which was commercialized and thus provided documentation, upgrades, and user support, provided a stable enough environment to encourage use. Other software was also introduced – the commercial geographic information systems IDL and AVS, which the programmers enjoyed learning and which were very useful for visualization. Some software was designed within Sequoia and used to good effect – TCL/tk for building GUIs was developed in computer science, PGBrowse to use with Postgres was developed by an earth science programmer, and a generic interface was developed by one of the industrial partners. All of these were taken up by some of the earth science participants and put to use to do science.

When software was ‘finished,’ meanings for the computer scientists and earth scientists converged. But at this stage, it had moved out of the design world and became a tool

New collaborations emerged out of the project – between earth scientists, between earth science and one of the industrial partners, and between Scripps and the San Diego Supercomputer Center. All of these were seen as important outcomes, extensions of and compatible with their existing work practice.

Most of the earth science programmers enjoyed the cutting edge aspect of Sequoia, although it was also the source of much frustration and wasted (from their perspective) time. As their education and work involved technology and – as they wrote data analysis programs – elements of design as well, they felt they learned a great deal from the exposure to new technologies, from seeing computer scientists at work, from the new software. One described it as empowering. Others felt that it gave them a more advanced working environment than they would have been able to find elsewhere. The sometimes insupportable delays in their scientific work caused by Sequoia was partially balanced by personal, professional, and intellectual value the programmers experienced.

For the earth scientists able to hire additional people because other grant money was freed up by the DEC equipment, adding people to the research group was a significant benefit, allowing more science to be done as well as more participation in Sequoia.

As of 1995, the metadata schema was implemented at Santa Barbara and UCLA, and the database, network, and tertiary storage were robust and effective in meeting clients’ needs (Stonebraker, 1995).

The PI from earth science had included database technology in what he required his students to learn, and incorporated local databases as a central part of the way his group did science. He asserted that Sequoia ‘has changed the way I work forever.’ They restructured one person’s job to fulltime work on schemas and data organization, because of the understanding that came through the project of the importance of putting in ‘a lot more effort up front on all of our work.’ Another scientist felt that being pushed by the computer scientists to explain in ever more detail what they did as part of the requirements elicitation had forced them to think in a much more rigorous way that would prove beneficial in the future.

The scientist who felt betrayed by the computer scientists left me a bit bemused when he insisted emphatically that ‘I haven’t learned anything at all. But I have changed the way I’m doing things.

## Conclusion

Sequoia 2000 was a design science research project that brought together prestigious scientists from two fields, computer science and earth science. It was intentionally structured to provide intensive collaboration between the disciplines in their roles as designer and client. Most of the goals of the collaboration were achieved: installation of faster networking, tertiary storage, and incorporation of database management into the earth scientists’ work process. Effective visualization was made possible, though only through the provision of already-available commercial software rather than through project research.

This study has found that there were fewer problems caused by differences in disciplinary cultures than by the difference between design worlds and client worlds. Loci of difficulty were the meanings in the original official project description, the need to use tools before they were finished, the role of failure in design, and the learning curve for technologies that were not only new to the earth scientists but often incomplete and therefore lacking documentation. The single biggest problem was a lack of full understanding on both sides of the consequences for clients of collaboration on a design science research project.

In a design world where there is a culture of embracing uncertainty and welcoming challenges – and a reward for doing so – this is a feature of life so ubiquitous as to be hardly worth discussion. (It is, however, discussed a great deal by those who study the design world.) Partly because of this tacit understanding and even embracing of uncertainty (Bucciarelli, 1994), this fact of life may not be made clear to a client upon his or her entrance to the world and may therefore generate surprised resentment. Earth science work practice includes no tolerance for ambiguity about tools or data; the ‘mess’ in earth science lies in the many unknowns about the universe, and the need for tools that provide accurate data is critical to scientific work. This need for maximal certainty was in direct conflict with the design world understanding that ‘you can never get anywhere if you insist on complete confidence in your design before you start cutting metal’ (Bucciarelli, 1994, p. 160). The uncertainty that is desirable as tools are being designed is a highly undesirable feature as tools are being used. Yet design requires that tools be used to test and improve them. It was only when hardware and software was ‘finished’ that meanings for the computers scientists and earth scientists converged. At this stage, the artifact was no longer the subject of design, but was rather a tool.

Orlikowski & Iacono (2001) remind us that artifacts should not be taken for granted in information systems research; and indeed there were significant differences in how the different technologies were experienced by the client participants. Those that provided incremental change – the networking and equipment that allowed programs to be run faster and using larger data sets – were taken up relatively quickly once they were reliable. The database system, by contrast, required a completely different approach to data management and analysis, and most researchers never lost their skepticism about the ‘wing-tips’ that were being designed.

These understandings emerged slowly for the participants during the course of the project and after its end. It took a long time for the earth scientists to understand that the computer scientists were scientists themselves, with the corresponding responsibility to contribute new knowledge to their discipline, and some of them never understood it. Indeed, so great was the frustration and hostility that I discovered later that some of them had thought that I was a spy for the computer science designers. It took equally long for the computer scientists to understand that designing for the next step forward in earth science technology dragged earth scientists out of their normal work practice into tasks that had no benefit for them in the present. Qualitative studies of a single case are not statistically generalizable, but they should be transferable.
