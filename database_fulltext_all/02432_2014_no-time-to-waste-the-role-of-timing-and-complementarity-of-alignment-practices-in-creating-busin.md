# No time to waste: the role of timing and complementarity of alignment practices in creating business value in IT projects

Armin Vermerris<sup>1</sup>, Martin Mocker<sup>2,3</sup> and Eric van Heck<sup>4</sup>

## Abstract

Despite significant research progress, alignment-related issues are among the top concerns of executives. Previous studies mostly focus on a company-wide strategic level of alignment; while this ‘top-down’ view has benefits, it largely ignores the operational practices that help achieve alignment in IT projects as well as the impact that timing and complementarity of practices have on achieving alignment. In our research we apply four alignment practices – communication, shared understanding, management commitment and IT investment evaluation – to individual IT projects rather than at a company level; specifically, we look at the role of timing and complementarity for these alignment practices throughout different project phases. A detailed analysis of six IT projects carried out at three companies in the telecommunications industry reveals that IT projects creating higher business value employ all four alignment practices immediately from the start. No project was able to recover from failing to establish these four alignment practices in the first phase. While supporting the importance of complementarity of alignment practices, our findings also add the importance of the earliness of this complementarity. ## Introduction

Alignment between business and IT has been studied intensively. Different aspects have been the focus of research, such as the strategic, intellectual, structural, social and cultural dimensions (Chan & Reich, 2007); antecedents of alignment or ‘alignment practices’ such as top management commitment, shared understanding between business and IT, and congruence of the business strategy and the IS strategy have been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed (Brown & Magill, 1994; Reich & Benbasat, 2000; Preston & Karahanna, 2009); and the impact of alignment on performance has been studied and at least partially con<sup>fi</sup>rmed in several studies (Chan et al, 1997; Tallon et al, 2000; Sabherwal & Chan, 2001; Cragg et al, 2002; Kearns & Lederer, 2003; Tallon & Kraemer, 2003; Byrd et al, 2006; Kearns & Sabherwal, 2007). However, despite more than 15 years of cumulative research on business-IT alignment, the topic remains one of the top concerns of executives, scoring consistently among the top 10 issues on IT Executives’ agendas for almost 30 years (Luftman & Ben-Zvi, 2010). It suggests that either transitioning the <sup>fi</sup>ndings into practice is hampered or that there are still questions left unanswered with regard to alignment that make it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for practitioners to apply the research <sup>fi</sup>ndings. While probably both factors hold true, we see some indication for the latter.

A project-level focus on alignment should help to connect the day-to-day work of project managers and steering boards to alignment literature. Narrowing the unit of analysis down to individual projects should contribute in that it offers a way to open the black box of alignment. We apply alignment practices (communication, shared understanding, management commitment, IT investment evaluation) identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by previous research to individual IT projects rather than at the company level.

Previous research on alignment has focused on the more abstract, macro levels of strategic alignment, structural alignment, and social alignment. The vast majority of studies looked at a company-wide level when seeking alignment. Up till now, there has been very little research at the level of operational alignment in day-to-day operations of the IT department and the business units. What has been ignored is that alignment ultimately has to result from actions on a micro-level of day-to-day operations. Notable exceptions include the research of Campbell et al (2005) and Cragg et al (2002). Our study addresses these shortcomings by looking at a project level of alignment.

There is an important impact of timing and complementarity of alignment practices within IT projects. Most research studies so far have ignored the impact of timing in alignment. A reason could be that early studies looked at alignment as an end-state (Chan et al, 1997; Sabherwal & Chan, 2001; Cragg et al, 2002; Kearns & Lederer, 2003; Tallon & Kraemer, 2003) rather than a process unfolding over time (for a dynamic view of alignment compare Baets, 1992; Sabherwal et al, 2001). While we did learn from these studies that certain practices and their complementarity are important for alignment (like top management commitment or communication), we did not learn as much about when to apply which practice. To illustrate this point: it is important to know the required ingredients in order to prepare a certain meal. But it is still dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to create the meal unless the timing of when to add which ingredient and when to cook for how long is unknown. In the same sense, it is important and valuable to know that communication and management commitment are important to achieve alignment, but it is unclear when that communication and commitment have to be present. Our study explicitly includes the role of timing and complementarity for IT alignment practices by looking at when they are employed throughout different phases of a project.

The research objective is to identify when alignment practices play an important role in helping to deliver IT business value in IT projects. This paper is organized as follows: the next section discusses the literature and presents the conceptual model, introducing propositions derived from literature. The subsequent section presents the research method we used to validate the propositions. The latter section discusses the empirical results, the <sup>fi</sup>fth section represents the cross-case analysis, and the sixth section re<sup>fl</sup>ects on the propositions by means of the discussion. The penultimate section addresses the limitations of our study and the <sup>fi</sup>nal section presents conclusions and implications of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

## Conceptual model based on four alignment practices

## IT business value and IT business alignment – the importance of alignment practices

Several studies have investigated the path that IT investments take, from making the investments to their impact on performance. The term ‘business value’ has been used to denote a variety of these performance impacts IT can have on an organization (including productivity increases, competitive impacts, and organizational improvements). We follow the broad de<sup>fi</sup>nition (Melville et al, 2004, p. 287), viewing IT business value as ‘organizational performance impacts of information technology at both the intermediate process level and the organization-wide level, and comprising both ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency impacts and competitive impacts’.

The deployment of advanced IT in an organization is an example of value creation. Value creation has been discussed and de<sup>fi</sup>ned by Lepak et al (2007, p. 182) and depends on the relative amount of value that is subjectively realized by a target user (or buyer) who is the focus of value creation – whether individual, organization, or society – and that this subjective value realization must at least translate into the user’s willingness to exchange a monetary amount for the value received.

Business-IT alignment (or just ‘alignment’) has been discussed as an important factor in<sup>fl</sup>uencing whether IT investments can be turned into business value. It is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to <sup>fi</sup>nd a common de<sup>fi</sup>nition of business-IT alignment as the concept has been applied to various dimensions such as strategic/intellectual alignment (Reich & Benbasat, 1996, p. 56; Reich & Benbasat, 2000, p. 82), structural alignment (Brown & Magill, 1994; Chan, 2002), social alignment (Reich & Benbasat, 2000), and cultural alignment (Chan & Reich, 2007). Integrating these diverse perspectives, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne alignment as ‘a continuous co-evolutionary process that reconciles top-down “rational designs” and bottom-up “emergent processes” of consciously and coherently interrelating all components of the Business/IT relationship at three levels of analysis (strategic, operational, and individual) in order to contribute to an organization’s performance over time’ (Benbya & McKelvey, 2006). Operational level alignment is referred to as the alignment between the IS and business departments that looks at components like ‘Communication with each other’. This paper looks at projects as one of the critical instances where the IT unit interacts with business departments on a day-to-day basis.

An important <sup>fi</sup>nding of these and other studies is the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of factors (antecedents) that in<sup>fl</sup>uence alignment. ## The aspect of timing in generating business value from business-IT alignment

The idea that timing plays a critical role throughout projects is well established in the literature on IT projects, especially large IT projects like Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) projects (cf. (Wu, 2008; Chen et al, 2006)). These studies divide projects into phases like pre-implementation, implementation, and post-implementation (Wu, 2008) or design, implementation, stabilization, continuous improvement, and transformation (Ross, 1999; Chen et al, 2006). Requirements engineering literature emphasizes the importance of completing requirements early in the design process, that is, mistakes made in the beginning of the project are most costly to correct (see for example Boehm, 1984). Probably because alignment is often seen as an end-state or as a strategic, company level concern, the aspect of timing has not been widely considered for alignment.

Few studies have looked at the impact of timing alignment practices. There are streams of literature though that will make us aware of the role of timing in creating business value and alignment: studies applying a process theory to explain the generation of business value from IT and studies looking into dynamic alignment. Our study builds on important <sup>fi</sup>ndings from those streams. In those studies, alignment is viewed as a dynamic, continuous process rather than an end-state (Rondinelli et al, 2001). It has been noted that ‘the process view of alignment has been underrepresented in research to date, given the widespread acknowledgement that alignment is an ongoing activity’ (Chan & Reich, 2007). Notable early exceptions to this include Baets (1992), Henderson & Venkatraman (1992), Broadbent & Weill (1993), Luftman & Brier (1999) as well as Soh & Markus (1995). For example, Soh & Markus (1995) proposed a process model in order to conceptualize how IT creates business value throughout different phases they call ‘IT Conversion Process’, ‘IT Use Process’, and ‘Competitive Process’. Their model helps to identify and incorporate the dynamic and ongoing nature of this transformation process. Marshall et al (2005)

adapted the model in order to incorporate a link to the business strategy instead of only focusing on the IT strategy by preceding the ‘IT Conversion Process’ with an ‘IT Alignment Process’.

We re-used and slightly adapted their work by focusing on the <sup>fi</sup>rst three phases (we excluded the ‘Competitive’ phase as it is less relevant to individual projects). As we look at alignment across all of the three phases, we interpreted the essence of Marshall et al’s <sup>fi</sup>rst phase as a planning phase that establishes the (strategic) context and requirements for the project rather than the only phase in which alignment happens. Our adapted model identi<sup>fi</sup>es three phases of transforming IT investments into business value: (I) the IT planning phase starting with a strategic imperative and ending with de<sup>fi</sup>ning IT expenditures; (II) the IT conversion phase ending with the building of IT assets; (III) the IT use phase leading to the required business value of IT.

In the <sup>fi</sup>rst phase of deriving IT expenditures from strategic imperatives, companies should align their business and IT strategies, goals, and needs derived from the strategic context they are operating in (Luftman & Brier, 1999; Marshall et al, 2005). Further on, an IT investment strategy will be de<sup>fi</sup>ned in order to align IT investments with the business strategy in order to capitalize on a company’s strengths (Kohli & Devaraj, 2004). In the second phase, these IT investments (or ‘expenditures’) are converted into IT assets (e.g. information systems including infrastructure and applications). In the third and <sup>fi</sup>nal phase, the created IT assets are brought to use in business processes and work practices to generate business value (Brynjolfsson, 1993; Byrd et al, 2006).

These three phases map roughly to the phases identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by implementation-focused studies (cf. e.g., Wu, 2008) like pre-implementation, implementation, and post-implementation. However, the terms pre- and postimplementation imply that the implementation phase is the main phase – a focus not intended by our study. Arranging the phases around ‘implementation’ as the center also implies a technical focus (stemming from software engineering). We view projects not from a software engineering, but from a more holistic perspective. Furthermore, the terms pre- and post-implementation do not provide an indication of what is done before or after the implementation. Where appropriate we refer to pre-implementation as the planning phase and postimplementation as the use phase.

## Conceptual model

In our study, we seek to combine the phased model of business value creation with the alignment practices identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by previous research. The phased model allows us to look at the timing of actionable alignment practices on an operational level of IT projects by considering different phases in which the alignment practices can be applied. In essence, the phased IT value creation model provides us with a much more <sup>fi</sup>ne grained perspective that enables us to ask how the four alignment practices contribute to value generation in each phase of a project (see Figure 1).

Based on the previous studies, we formulated propositions for each of the alignment practices in IT projects. Four propositions argue for the necessity of employing one alignment practice in each phase of a project; the <sup>fi</sup>fth proposition argues for the complementarity of alignment practices – it is necessary to employ more than one alignment practice in each project phase.

Communication IT projects require specialized but distributed expertise of two types: domain knowledge and technical knowledge. Such specialization is necessary for effective division of labor across functions (e.g., line specialization vs IT specialization) (Becker & Murphy, 1992). The problem is that both of these two types of knowledge are simultaneously needed, and irrespective of whether IT decisions are made by line managers or IT managers, those decisions will always be separated from one type of knowledge (Tiwana, 2009). This is a classic knowledge-decision separation problem in the Jensen-Meckling model (Jensen & Meckling, 1992). The solution then lies in increasing the frequency and ‘early-ness’ of communication (Bolton & Dewatripont, 1994). Therefore, communication facilitates knowledge integration (Grant, 1996) or knowledge transformation (Carlile & Rebentisch, 2003) between the IT and other units of the company. Alignment can be viewed as a collaborative process between units (Niederman et al, 1991; Baets, 1992). Communication has mainly been researched at the executive level (Rockart et al, 1996; Reich & Benbasat, 2000; Tan & Gallupe, 2006), while Sledgianowski & Luftman (2006) argue that communication should be realized throughout the company, on an informal and regular basis.

## Proposition 1: A high level of communication in each phase of the IT project is necessary for a high level of IT business value.

Shared understanding Knowledge integration of domain/business knowledge and IT knowledge in the presence of departmental specialization requires absorptive capacity (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990), which means some shared prior knowledge. Without prior knowledge the two groups of stakeholders (business vs IT) cannot integrate their specialized knowledge in a manner that can be applied to project activities. Such shared knowledge (Armstrong & Sambamurthy, 1999; Luftman & Brier, 1999) provides ‘islands of mutual understanding in a sea of ignorance’ (Postrel, 2002).

## Proposition 2: A high level of shared understanding in each phase of the IT project is necessary for a high level of IT business value.

Management commitment Management commitment has been discussed extensively in IS literature as one of the main enablers of alignment (Luftman et al, 1999; Teo & Ang, 1999). Management needs to be committed to and supportive of IT initiatives in order to understand the strategic potential of IT (Chan, 2002; Kearns & Lederer, 2003; Byrd & Davidson, 2003; Kearns & Sabherwal, 2007). Instead of being just an activity, management commitment is part of the whole alignment process (Baets, 1992; Campbell et al, 2005).

## Proposition 3: A high level of management commitment in each phase of the IT project is necessary for a high level of IT business value.

IT investment evaluation Evaluating IT investments has been argued and empirically proven as an important enabler of strategic alignment (Henderson & Venkatraman, 1993; Tallon et al, 2000; Kohli & Devaraj, 2004; Sledgianowski & Luftman, 2006). Evaluating IT investments at different stages of a project is a mature alignment practice and concerns frequent and formal IT assessments and reviews (Sledgianowski & Luftman,

2006). These mechanisms are important; because without an effective evaluation policy or set of investment guidelines, there is a risk that IT investment will not support the business strategy (Tallon et al, 2000).

Proposition 4: A high level of IT investment evaluation in each phase of the IT project is necessary for a high level of IT business value.

The relevance of complementarity in different phases As stated above, the employment of a phased model enables us to take a more <sup>fi</sup>ne-grained view of the role of alignment in creating business value. The key point here is that we argue that the co-presence of alignment practices is more valuable than the same practices in isolation. Alignment practices complement each other, for example, increasing one practice increases the value of doing more of the other practice (see for the notion of complementarity, Milgrom & Roberts (1995)). In a system of complements, it is important to design IT alignment practices systematically rather than in isolation. For example, the value of communication as alignment practice will be limited (Proposition 1) unless there is suf<sup>fi</sup>cient shared understanding (Proposition 2) to bene<sup>fi</sup>t from that. Therefore, communication and shared understanding are more valuable together, that is, their bene<sup>fi</sup>ts exceed the sum of their parts. Therefore, they are complements. That same logic holds for the other alignment practices. For example, the value of IT investment evaluation will be limited (Proposition 4) unless there is suf<sup>fi</sup>cient shared understanding (Proposition 2) to bene<sup>fi</sup>t from that.

Proposition 5: A high level of complementarity among alignment practices (communication, shared understanding, management commitment, and IT investment evaluation) in all phases of the IT project is necessary for a high level of IT business value.

## Research methodology

The research methodology of this study consists of three parts: research design, data collection and constructs, and data analysis.

## Research design

The research design involved conducting a literature review and de<sup>fi</sup>ning the right strategy for validating existing business-IT alignment theories in order to understand how they in<sup>fl</sup>uence the value creation process of a certain project. In this sense, an explanatory multiple-case study was most appropriate (Yin, 2003). Cases were selected based on replication logic, where projects with a low and projects with a high business value could lead to competing explanations for the same set of events (Yin, 2003; Pare, 2004). Six cases have been sampled (three cases with a low business value, and three cases with a high business value; whether they were considered low or high business value was determined by an assessment described below) on the basis of the theoretical propositions and the criterion that projects had to deal with a large implemented information system, such as an ERP system or a Data Warehouse system.

## Data collection and constructs

Collection Data were primarily gathered through 17 semi-structured face-to-face interviews during the period March – October 2010 using a structured topic list and an IT Business Value questionnaire. Three large Dutch telecom companies $( \mathrm { A } , \ \mathrm { B } ,$ and C) participated in this research project with two IT projects per company. The execution of all projects took place in the year 2009 or 2010 or both, and all incorporated some form of PRINCE2 practices. For each project, the business owner, the IT executive/program manager, and where possible a person responsible for quality assurance were interviewed. Table 2 lists the participants in our study per case company with their respective roles within the company as well as their roles in the respective project.

Triangulation was applied in that documents were collected, which functioned as additional insights into addressed topics as well as validation of interviewers’ statements (Pare, 2004). Interviews were performed according to an interview protocol (see Appendix A) consisting of a topic list, which gave guidance to the overall interview, but not limiting the interviewee in giving his/her opinion. All interviews were recorded and transcribed by one of the authors and then reviewed by one reviewer appointed per company in order to limit interviewer bias.

Constructs Measurement of the different constructs in this study was done by deriving interview questions from the operationalization as described in subsequent sections and in Appendix A.

IT business value The IT business value measurement has been based on the studies of Tallon et al (2000) and Tallon (2008). The IT business value assessment consists of <sup>fi</sup>ve different performance areas, which can be found below.

IT project impact on critical business activities in the value chain:

● Supplier relations: forge closer links with suppliers, monitor quality, monitor delivery times, gain leverage over suppliers and negotiate pricing.

● Production and operations: improve throughput, boost labor productivity. Improve <sup>fl</sup>exibility and equipment utilization, and streamline operations.

● Product and service enhancement: embed IT in products, increase pace of development/R&D, monitor design cost, improve quality and support innovation.

● Marketing and sales: spot market trends, anticipate customer needs, build market share, improve forecast accuracy and evaluate pricing options.

● Customer relations: respond to customer needs, provide after-sales service and support, improve distribution and create customer loyalty.

Communication Communication is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as ‘the level of communication between business and IT executives’ (Reich & Benbasat, 2000). Communication has been measured according to the six most pertinent techniques that are listed below (Reich & Benbasat, 2000).

● Direct communication (e.g., communication between business and IT executives, such as regular or ad hoc meetings, electronic mail or written memos);

● Liaison roles (e.g., a named person as liaison between IT and a line function);

● Temporary task forces (e.g., IT project team, new product development team);

● Permanent teams/committees (e.g., IT steering committees);

● Integrating roles (e.g., IT person leads the business quality team);

● Managerial linking roles (e.g., product management role).

Shared understanding Shared understanding is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as: ‘the ability of IT and business executives, at a deep level, to understand and be able to participate in the others' key processes and to respect each other's unique contribution and challenges’ (Reich & Benbasat, 2000).

Management commitment Management commitment is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as: (1) business executives need to be committed to IS (Chan, 2002; Byrd & Davidson, 2003; Kearns & Sabherwal, 2007); (2) IT executives and managers need to commit themselves to the business (Kearns & Lederer, 2003); and (3) not only top management needs to commit itself, but also business unit managers as well as all other individuals in order to derive bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from IT investments (Campbell et al, 2005).

IT investment evaluation IT investment evaluation consists of several management practices, which are:

● Frequent and formal IT assessments and reviews (Sledgianowski & Luftman, 2006);

● Pre- and post-implementation reviews; especially the learning effect of post-implementations allows an organization to have higher payoffs from IT (Tallon et al, 2000);

Data analysis The analysis of the data was performed in a structured way by using a case study protocol (Yin, 2003; Pare, 2004), which can be found in the appendix section ‘Interview protocol’. During interviews, <sup>fi</sup>eld notes were taken, which have functioned as re<sup>fl</sup>ective remarks. Documentation was used in order to verify data from interviews and to provide additional information. Narratives were developed in accordance with the narrative strategy of Langley, in order to theorize from data in a more accurate manner (Langley, 1999). The analysis in this study could therefore be made on the basis of raw data, and thereby reducing the likelihood on interpretation errors. The application of the narrative strategy in this paper has been realized as follows: (a) Each case in this study represents one project; therefore, for each project data were collected and analyzed according to three phases of the IT value creation model; (b) All interviews have been guided by this phased structure, and transcribed in full, on the basis of voice recordings; (c) These transcriptions have provided the data chronology foundation for the development of narratives; (d) Narratives representing the cases were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed and described, for example, where alignment practices have been presented over time and per project phase. From this the within-case and cross-case analyses have been supported by following how the project process unfolded along the way; and scoring of alignment practices has been supported to assess whether a practice scored a high or low value in the phase of the project. The cross-case analysis was executed by seeking patterns between the levels of alignment practices in the process of value creation (Yin, 2003). Appendix A presents the research approach in more detail.

High/low IT business value scores Interviewees were asked to rate each project in terms of IT business value by assigning a value from 1 to 7 to each of the performance items (e.g., SR1, SR2, PO1, PO2) per performance dimension (e.g., ‘Supplier relations’). Each performance item score has been calculated by taking the average rating of all project interviewees per performance item

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text {e.g.} \frac {\text {SR1 score of interviewee 1 + SR1 score of interviewee 2}}{2} \\ = \text {average score SR1 for Project X}. \end{array}
$$

The total IT business value per project was calculated by taking the average of all performance items per project

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \text {e.g.} & \frac {\mathrm{SR1} + \mathrm{SR2} + \mathrm{SR3} + \mathrm{SR4} + \mathrm{SR5} + \mathrm{PO1} + \cdots}{\text {Number of value items rated}} \\ & = \text {average IT Business value score per project}. \end{array}
$$

Values of 3 and lower represent a low IT business value, values of 5 and higher represent a high IT business value, and the value 4 implies ‘Neutral’ (Table 3).

The IT business value scores have been veri<sup>fi</sup>ed and supported with internal documentation functioning as evidence. The full list of documentation sources is provided below. Project B1 was not facilitated by means of documented evidence (Table 4). ## Empirical findings

## Introduction to cases and investigated IT projects

Three companies in the telecommunication industry were investigated. For each of the companies interviewees rated the projects, allowing us to categorize the projects in those with low vs those with high business value. Following an instrument developed by Tallon et al (2000) and Tallon (2008), interviewees were asked to rate the impact of the projects in multiple areas (supplier relations, production and operations, product and service enhancement, marketing and sales, customer relations). Table 6 provides the characteristics of the six investigated IT projects, with projects A1, B1, and C1 having a low IT business value score, and projects A2, B2, and C2 having a high IT business value score.

## Project A1

Introduction The goal of this project was to consolidate and streamline the current billing system landscape in order to reduce maintenance costs. From the start, the project was seen as a technical project focused on a purely technical integration of the different billing systems. Owing to this technical focus very limited attention was paid to end user functionalities and affected business processes. As the project was heading toward implementation, it was recognized that the integration had business impacts though. Accordingly, business involvement increased by commencing a stronger business engagement mechanism (e.g. workstreams dedicated to business areas) and appointing a new program manager and business owner. The company needed to implement the new system soon since current legacy systems were reaching maximum capacity and the end of their lifecycles. On the basis of this a decision was made to go live with the new system, while a number of functionalities were not yet considered (e.g., exporting of billing reports). After go live, there were some serious issues with the system, customer functionalities were not working properly and dependent systems were no longer fed with accurate data. But as the project team started to drift apart after implementation, a large team of people was set up in order to quickly mitigate problems for end users.

Communication In the beginning the project had not created a mechanism to discuss the business and IT interests in a group setting. The project was primarily driven from a technological perspective aiming to integrate different systems instead of realizing business improvements.

In the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage of the project the basis of the project was concerned around replacing the systems and the project was driven by technical people. Those people said ‘we are going to pick those boxes up, those ones are gone, and we are going to replace it with one new box’. I think that was the reason why the <sup>fi</sup>rst phase was unsuccessful; it was speci<sup>fi</sup>cally so that the project was not an IT project by itself. When you are speaking about boxes then there is more behind it, such as the functionalities, processes and other things. [IT Program manager]

After recognizing the lack of communication with the business was causing problems, an executive and a project managers’ steering group were set up that met on a biweekly basis. The project was divided into different workstreams and communication was stimulated between streams and steering groups by appointing people, among them the company’s CIO, who liaised between business and IT stakeholders. However, due to the technical scope of the project from the start, one workstream (concerned with the exporting of billing reports) was overlooked with respect to its impact.

At that time it was decided, because of the pressure to go live, to consider everything that had to do with workstream X as nice-to-haves. Therefore most people thought like OK, that makes sense because it doesn’t touch the customer, however it turned out that it did touch the customer. [Business owner]

After go live it turned out that the system had some big issues (e.g. reports could not be made for customers) and the collaborative team started to fragment. The steering group and workstreams increased meeting frequency to get involved with business stakeholders in order to solve issues and mitigate their business impact.

Shared understanding In the <sup>fi</sup>rst phase of the project, sessions were targeted at getting a technical understanding of the project’s goal, which was to integrate systems. No attention was paid to achieving an under standing of its business impacts. During development, the project team and steering group took a more business-focused approach by getting an understanding of how the project would affect the business and what value would be delivered to the business.

Design packages were used to see the modular impact of the system on the business; a design package made for every business unit. Within the organization we asked for business and technical people who could review these design packages. We compared the packages to our legacy system and a delta often resulted from that review. We wrote those deltas or differences between the old and new situation down and discussed them with business people. Some things were approved and some things were left the way they were because reverting back to the old situation typically costs more than its value for the business. [IT Program manager]

Business and IT stakeholders were better able to collaborate, assess the impact of changes and mitigate the issues from a business perspective. Furthermore, technical and business resources were added to workstreams to reach a better understanding and commitment for go live.

Management commitment In the initial phase of the project there was commitment from IT management; however, there was very limited business commitment. When the project was running for a while, its impact on business units’ goals was better understood. Managers of these business units increased their involvement and committed themselves by taking part in the steering group and making resources available for critical project activities, for example, testing or reviewing.

I think when it became clearer for the business that they needed to come on board. It was no longer seen as a purely technical party. And they also said that it touches our work and it touches the objectives of our business unit, so we need to come on board. Because when we join this project we can steer based on the outcome. If we don’t join, the project will continue and eventually we will end up with something that we are not happy about. [IT Program manager]

Besides, in review sessions, prioritizations about functionalities could be made more adequately with business and IT management at the table. Heading towards implementation, decisions could be made on what to implement and what not when the pressure to go live increased. Afterwards implementation issues could be resolved more quickly since managers were engaged and able to make fast decisions with all parties involved.

IT investment evaluation During project initiation limited effort was put into evaluating its progress compared with its goal since there was no formal project structure. After the implementation of a stronger business alignment mechanism, progress was measured per workstream and presented in the steering group by means of a dashboard. The dashboard showed a positive picture of the evaluation of the project. However, due to the limited business requirements analysis up front, one speci<sup>fi</sup>c workstream was measured improperly. Therefore the steering group was not able to steer on the right information. Before implementation, a pre-implementation check (limited review), instead of a full review, was performed. This check was incomplete since it assumed a correct de<sup>fi</sup>nition of the goals, which was not the case for the business side.

We had 5000 price plans to test, we tested 200, thus 4700 were not tested. We had carefully selected those 200, the most important ones and also the ones that were the most complex and had a lot of similarities with others. However, sometimes we found details that were different, and we had not tested those. And that was also something we had to explain to Operations colleagues – that we had to make choices, this was the deadline. [IT Program manager]

After implementation, the project was assessed weekly on a formal basis and a post-implementation review was done including a lessons-learned review by all workstreams and the steering group. In addition to internal evaluations, an external auditor kept track of project value, risks and critical factors during the project, which were de<sup>fi</sup>ned in initial business objectives.

## Project A2

Introduction The goal of this project was to improve <sup>fl</sup>exibility and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of customer-facing logistical processes by implementing a new end-to-end logistics platform as part of a company-wide ERP implementation. The CEO personally enforced a strong business involvement from the start of the project. Workshops were held with business and IT management representatives to agree on the scope, prioritize functionalities and specify initial business requirements. These sessions resulted in a revised focus on functionalities that were expected to lead to immediate business improvement. During the project, substantial attention was paid to stimulate collaboration and communication by creating a workspace where people were physically located together. Test sessions were targeted at understanding the customers’ business in terms of potential issues and problems. A lot of time and energy went into preparing the deployment in terms of the technical and organizational readiness of the system. Users were enabled to give input on what capabilities the system needed to have, as well as to assess its impact on business processes and the company’s customers. This understanding led to getting different implementation fallback scenarios in place to make sure that customers could continue day-today business without experiencing any problems.

Communication From the <sup>fi</sup>rst day a steering group was set up for the project with strong business involvement of user and even customer representatives. This group was used to make diverse perspectives explicit and hence acted as a central communication platform.

The steering group started out with the normal departments, such as the principal business department this system was targeted at, another business department, the IT delivery organization, as well as the department responsible for operations affecting customers. Later on different speci<sup>fi</sup>c user groups of business users (second-line) were allocated who represented speci<sup>fi</sup>c channels that had speci<sup>fi</sup>c processes and thus also differing requirements. They also have differing processes and thus also completely different requirements. This allowed for the project to become more de<sup>fi</sup>ned. [Director Finance]

Besides the steering committee meetings, three types of meetings were targeted at stimulating communication among business and IT employees: (1) business meetings with the project team and business representatives to re<sup>fl</sup>ect on planned work, (2) deployment meetings with business units about deployment prerequisites and buy-in, (3) executive meetings where overall progress, mitigation of risks and solutions to issues were discussed.

Shared understanding A strong focus on getting a common understanding of the business requirements was emphasized early on in the project (during the planning phase) since different departments were involved that had to work together.

I think that there was an excellent, really excellent, shared understanding. This project has been, yes I think, it has been over managed and over communicated. But the consequence is that there is absolute alignment or has been absolute alignment, between the business and the technology community on this. All of the suppliers have been totally clear about the importance and the priority of this project and they have committed the resources to deliver it. [CIO]

Sessions in the planning phase resulted in prioritizing those functionalities that led to immediate business improvements. In the business and deployment meetings, users were able to provide their input and knowledge about business processes of end customers. During testing, the involvement of customers and users proved its value since deployment could be prepared based on business process insights and fallback scenarios could be developed so that customers could continue with business as usual without experiencing any problems. In this sense, during and after implementation there was a clear understanding of risks and mitigation actions. In addition, a multidisciplinary taskforce was formed to tackle any bigger issues.

Management commitment Because of negative experiences with a previous project and as the company’s CEO had a personal background in the business area of this project, there was a strong commitment from management from the start.

The CEO forced a heavier business involvement. Different business and customer areas were added to the project, which were not involved before the project. The CEO emphasized that those business customer areas were our internal customers, which have requirements and wants so we need to talk with them and we did that. [Director Finance]

Besides the CEO, business unit managers were involved and engaged with business representatives, project leaders and people responsible for testing and deployment. Through this commitment of various management levels, the steering committee was able to track the project from a strategic to an operational level. Issues and challenges could therefore be discussed and solved immediately.

IT Investment Evaluation The project was formally reviewed every week on all different sub-streams. These streams were driven separately, however were reviewed in one steering group meeting to get a view of the total progress as well as per workstream.

On a weekly basis the progress reporting was done on all different areas, thus on the working of the system, and focused on the status of the product tests since these were lacking in lead time. [Director Finance]

Before implementation, a review was conducted with the involvement of all stakeholders. A risk assessment for implementation was also conducted early on in the project. After implementation, an audit on the quality of the migrated data was performed.

## Project B1

Introduction The goal of this project was to provide a service to the company’s end customers to automatically process their orders via a single database. The business owner established a business case consisting of a simple design speci<sup>fi</sup>cation and requirements gathered by an IT business analyst. The business owner approved the start of the development and appointed a project leader and a developer. During development, both the project leader and the developer left the organization, which made an appropriate handover to IT support dif<sup>fi</sup>cult. Before implementation, the system and its interfaces were tested and most technical issues were resolved immediately. After implementation, the system was directly put to use, and different issues arose. The biggest one was that the internal IT department did not accept the system for support and thus did not resolve customers’ issues. In addition, there was communication back and forth concerning the responsibility for maintenance. The issue was escalated to the highest business and IT executives who decided not to take the system into IT support, resulting in an unof<sup>fi</sup>cial tool for customers with no support.

Communication During the project, there was a strong focus on an informal way of working, instead of formal arrangements in the sense of meeting structures and a project board. The lack of these meeting structures had a negative impact on the structure of the project itself. During the project, effort was put into improving the structure, but the change was never embedded in the project organization.

At a certain moment the project gained structure. However, it could have been done better from the beginning, absolutely. The project team should have been de<sup>fi</sup>ned more clearly. Regular meetings and formal agreements should have been made and followed up. That was the responsibility of the project manager and the IT side, that the project was wellembedded within the organization. That did not happen. [Business owner]

During, and especially after, implementation the system was put to use, however without acceptance of IT in their systems’ landscape for support and maintenance. This resulted in issues logged by internal customers that could not be resolved by the IT department since the system was not handed over to operations. Eventually the business decided to hire an external expert in order to <sup>fi</sup>x issues by themselves.

Shared understanding The project was initiated with a business case including a set of requirements and assumptions for the business case to be successful. There was, however, limited clarity about the business case and on the process of creating the business case, since the project board was not involved to approve the document nor was the business analyst who needed to de<sup>fi</sup>ne initial requirements.

Normally we have a project process and there is a quick scan, which is a kind of impact analysis for the project, which includes the business case, current situation and the new situation. So normally after I make such a quick scan, there is a project board that reads this document and compares the costs with the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and looks at the concept of this project and says ‘Okay, we can spend this time and money’. But there was no project board to approve it; it was actually, I think it was the business owner that decided that we should just do it. Maybe because of the pressure from the business. [Business analyst]

The limited focus on achieving a shared understanding was not only restricted to the early phases of the project, since during implementation, and after, there was limited involvement from the business and no understanding was achieved about handing the system over to operations and IT support. After implementation, the business realized their own understanding by means of <sup>fi</sup>xing the issues on their own initiative.

Management commitment The project manager, who had close contact with the business department, was the person primarily responsible for the business engagement in the project. The engagement was done in an informal way, which meant that progress and status were dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to follow by participants and management. Since management had limited involvement, commitment was low during the project.

The project manager handled project management from an IT perspective and was a former employee of the business department, therefore he knew the people within the business department, which resulted in that the project manager did a lot of business based on relationships. Because you build something informally the commitment is of course also low, you are not able to steer the project manager, let me say it like that. [IT Program manager]

IT Investment Evaluation For the development, the business owner appointed a project leader and a developer. The project leader followed a tollgate project management process in order to develop the system; however, this was not done in a formal way (by means of a steering board), but in an informal way during development (by means of informal conversations). No pre-implementation review was performed; instead it was decided to go through several rounds of testing and it was discovered that certain functionalities were not working according to initial requirements.

The development was done by an external person who had no accurate view on how the development had been carried out. The actual testing was dependent on this view, but did not happen correctly either. And an acceptation took place which was actually not screened properly. [IT Program manager]

## Project B2

Introduction This project was part of a program aimed to implement improvements to current company-wide business support systems (BSS) and operational support systems (OSS). The project had been initiated by a business case concerned with improving organizational performance, lowering operational expenditures, and delivering better service towards external customers. Hence the project was run as a business project with business objectives in mind. Workshops were held in order to gather high-level ideas and requirements, and manageable work plans were created from these requirements in order to get a common view of the impact on customer processes. High-level ideas and requirements were broken down into work plans. This was regarded as a challenging but a crucial assignment, since stakeholders with different interests and priorities were involved. Executives were not only responsible for delivering the high-level ideas and requirements, but were also involved in the project in order to keep their commitment high.

Communication Communication was stimulated in the planning phase by means of workshops and business requirement clari<sup>fi</sup>cation sessions. In these sessions, business and IT stakeholders were involved in order to de<sup>fi</sup>ne clear requirements and scope, and to prioritize the deliverables and understand the functionalities of the system. During the project, a communication triangle was set up consisting of (1) the program manager responsible for the corporate IT department consisting of operations, architects and external parties, (2) the company’s project manager and (3) the business users and business owners.

There was a lot of communication between IT and the business, because within this program there is a lot of information <sup>fl</sup>owing up and down the stream. There are a lot of stakeholders involved: all the departments, like several departments at the corporate level. There is an external party involved, so there are a lot of people involved and you have to make sure that you are on top of it. So basically you need to help each other, getting things done and getting things sorted. [IT Program manager]

Also during and after the implementation, communication was stimulated by means of sessions to prepare deployment and training sessions on how to use the system and its functionalities.

Shared understanding In this project there was a very strong focus on getting a shared understanding on all kinds of different levels in the organization. From the start, sessions were held where business and IT stakeholders had to align on all kinds of different aspects of the project. This was not only restricted to an understanding of the project and its goals, but also the overall performance. Therefore a lot of effort was put into allocating the right people that had an understanding of the technical and business rationales of the project.

Business people have a very good understanding of how it works prior to the program. So they will be able to understand what the differences are. So people have been involved in testing in prior releases. So they are very, very familiar with how a systems works and how it supports their processes. So I think that the knowledge within this organization within different business departments, the system knowledge that is out there, is amazing. You can only achieve that when people work here for multiple years and enable themselves and open themselves up to increase that IT knowledge from the systems. [IT Program manager]

Management commitment In order for the right people to be allocated to the project, management commitment was essential. An ongoing discussion took place between customers and the project in order to get a clear understanding of real business requirements.

It was important to make sure to constantly check what is the perception of that person or that group of people. Are we delivering what they expect us to deliver? That is a question you need to ask constantly, because if your end customer, being operations, is not happy, you have not delivered value to them. That means the project could fail. So management support is there for sure. As a person in the middle of the whole program, you need to make sure that you get that information. It is not coming automatically. You need to get it from people, from all the parties involved. [IT Program manager]

Furthermore, managers were involved in formal review sessions.

There is of course every step that we take, from design requirements, design, to build and deployment. There are some formal steps identi<sup>fi</sup>ed for approvals, tollgates that are very formal. We need approval from the Directors that have requested certain functionalities. [IT Program manager]

Thus, not only business managers were assigned, but also business users, that could help with testing and performing business impact reviews heading towards the end of the project.

IT investment evaluation The project was assessed by a steering committee dedicated to different topics to be discussed. These discussions primarily took place on an informal basis by means of ad hoc meetings or conversations. What did happen in a formal way was the passing of the different tollgates and the approval of requests that were made during development. Before implementation, business impact reviews were performed and after the project two different post-implementation reviews were conducted. Latter reviews were extensive about what went well and what went wrong, but also on communication improvements, way of getting agreements, and how escalations were handled. Furthermore, all project phases were reviewed.

## Project C1

Introduction This project was initiated to have one service portal serving all customers, instead of several. Prior to this project, customers were forced to go to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c portal with the risk of being redirected to another portal. This project was the second in a program; therefore, the project leader and his team were able to bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the lessons learned from the <sup>fi</sup>rst. The project was split into three streams, a project board was set up, risk and work sessions were performed and user representation meetings were set up. The project board, however, was abandoned after a while and it therefore became dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to assess issues and maintain a mutual understanding. Hence, the assessment of the impact on the business was left incomplete. In the implementation phase, a structured approach was followed in order to migrate the system within one weekend. During this weekend it appeared that an appropriate plan of action for the testing procedures was missing. As a result, users were logging a high number of issues during implementation. It appeared that a lot of user groups had not been identi-<sup>fi</sup>ed in advance and were therefore not known by the project team. The underestimation of the system’s impact and the limited involvement of users were the main issues in this project, which caused the implementation to fail. After implementation, six weeks of effort was put into getting the issues (logged by its users) <sup>fi</sup>xed and the system up and running again.

Communication Initially a project board was set up responsible for the overall progress and decision-making. User meetings were set up in order to have a representation of all user groups affected by the project. The project board, however, was abandoned after a while due to the fact that the board made no decisions but only discussed status. Weekly progress meetings and user meetings were held to achieve alignment with the business. These meetings, however, did not always take place due to personal circumstances of the facilitator.

We had user meetings and in those meetings the main users of the ticket management system would sit together and were able to assess the impact on the speci<sup>fi</sup>c business parts within the company because they knew how these users used the system. However, these user meetings ended when the system got developed, because the manager who was responsible for this was not able to steer these meetings due to personal circumstances, and the meetings did not take place anymore during later stages of the project. It got stopped. From that moment in time the different user groups were not involved as much as they were before, so from the beginning of the project. [Business program manager]

During implementation, communication therefore became less and was mainly restricted to the IT department and suppliers. After implementation and deployment, the system was not functioning appropriately and communication mechanisms needed to be re-established in order to get direct feedback from users so that these issues could be resolved as soon as possible.

Shared understanding Since the project board was stopped early in the project (during the planning phase), it became dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to assess priorities on functionalitie and issues related to the different business areas. Besides, the project team struggled to create a sense of understanding about this project in the organization. This lack of business knowledge could not be gained somewhere else and had to come from users; however, with no steering group to make decisions on this, the project continued without. Heading towards implementation, it became clear that the limited user involvement had its impact on the understanding of the development and especially the implementation. The business impact assessment was far from effective, leaving an incomplete risk and impact assessment. This was noticed six weeks before the eventual implementation; therefore, the project manager chose to delay the project in order to get the initial problems <sup>fi</sup>xed.

We did that in that period, and we decided to work really closely together on a speci<sup>fi</sup>c location with the business and IT stakeholders. This was a satisfactory collaboration because you did not have interferences in communication and mails and so on. It was really the situation that we needed to solve the problems and everything was recorded in a quality center where all the issues were logged. Eventually we did solve all the issues. We had set a date for implementation and, due to the hard work of all people, it became clear that when we changed something in the system then somewhere else in the system something else would be negatively affected by this change. [IT Program manager]

After implementation, it became clear that no proper understanding was achieved concerning the testing procedures and that due to limited involvement from users the amount of user groups was underestimated. Therefore a team of business and IT stakeholders was set up to solve all issues as soon as possible, resulting in a focus on getting a shared understanding.

Management commitment Management was keen to achieve the project's bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and was committed to it. However, when the project unfolded and the project board was dismantled, there was no link anymore between management and the project in the form of a steering entity.

I think there was a good management commitment from the beginning, however no project steering. A lot was delegated, and there was a lot of double steering also, people who were looking at each other. [IT Program manager]

The project team therefore had to update managers based on progress they made along the way. During the conversion phase of the system, certain deadlines were not met, resulting in management losing trust and commitment to the project despite their objectives. After implementation, issues arose quickly and the different department managers, whose departments were working with the system, regained their commitment in order to safeguard business as usual while these issues were affecting their ongoing operations.

IT investment evaluation Without proper steering by the project board, there was no emphasis placed on evaluating the project along the way. Progress was measured informally and according to deadlines that had been set initially in the project. Furthermore, towards implementation no formal reviews were executed in order to understand the impact of implementation. On an informal basis the project teams discussed progress and priorities; before implementation, however there was no focus on reviewing. During implementation, surprises presented themselves, which were not taken into account before. After implementation, a postimplementation review was conducted, together with all involved managers, which was the basis for a clear view on work that needed to be done to regain business as usual. Furthermore, an extensive lessons-learned document was created in order to generate insights to prevent similar mistakes in the future.

## Project C2

Introduction This speci<sup>fi</sup>c project was the third release of an overall program consisting of several releases, divided into one release per project. The goal of the program was to develop and implement a system consisting of an OSS and BSS. The <sup>fi</sup>rst two releases focused on developing the basic functionalities of the systems, while the third and fourth releases focused on the quality and delivering fully <sup>fl</sup>edged OSS and BSS systems. The goal of this project was to deliver an OSS for a speci<sup>fi</sup>c business unit within the company. The <sup>fi</sup>rst and second release did not result in a mature OSS system; therefore, the third release got more management attention. In this respect internal customers were invited to attend initial sessions. Management committed themselves to the project through a steering group, a governance board, and several business-IT liaison roles. The reason for this strong commitment was a strong need for the system, which resulted in the project getting a higher priority. The beginning of this project was characterized by discussions that had already taken place in the <sup>fi</sup>rst and second releases. These discussions forced a clear and shared understanding of the scope and goal of the third release among the OSS and BSS IT people, as well as the different business units.

Communication Since the <sup>fi</sup>rst two releases of the OSS system did not result in a mature system, effort was put into getting communication up to speed before the start of this project. The different customer business units were involved early on in initial discussions resulting in clear agreements on collaboration.

Besides, the business units concerned with the third release decided to work more closely with the IT department, as well as the business units concerned with the fourth release. Therefore the different departments could collaborate more effectively and could show each other their planning schedules and were not able to blame each other. [IT Program manager]

Communication was stimulated during the project by a steering group, governance board and other meeting structures that were set up in order to liaise between business and IT. Furthermore, these bodies were also there as decision structures and to steer on a strategic as well as an operational level.

The different business units had differing requirements. However, they also had similar requirements regarding other functionalities and capabilities of the system. We had several meeting institutions where we were able to solve issues regarding the business units on a board level where the alignment between the OSS and BSS systems was discussed and how they should interact. Besides, there were ‘quadrangle’ meetings with four important stakeholders that had weekly deliberate meetings about the content of the systems from an architectural and design perspective. [Head architect]

The meeting structures were effective throughout the project; also during testing and implementation users were involved in order to establish a handover and to create awareness in the user organization for the implementation.

Shared understanding Immediately from the beginning the focus was on aligning the project and its objectives with the business represented by the different business units. For the project stakeholders it was clear that shared understanding needed to be developed from the beginning in order to get agreement and understanding on the content.

When you start a project based on an initial business idea that you need to achieve, when you have to crystallize the start-up phase, then shared understanding has to be developed, which is very important… so alignment was important between the business units and other parties involved in order to get to the same decisions among all stakeholders, and some effort had been put into getting that alignment between decisions. [Business owner]

During the project, the different steering bodies and also the various meeting structures that were set up took care of maintaining alignment between business and IT.

Management commitment Since the <sup>fi</sup>rst and second release did not result in a mature OSS system, this third release got more management attention. Furthermore, there was a strong need for the system, resulting in the project getting a higher priority. Management committed themselves to the project through a steering group, a governance board, and several business-IT liaison roles.

Besides, the commitment from management was very high throughout, so there were no complaints about what we should do. So when you look at the size of the project, which cost a lot of money, and something was built from scratch, then it was such a success because everybody worked closely together and everybody had the same feeling about the priority. [Business owner]

IT investment evaluation From the start, a steering group and governance board governed this project and evaluated the project along the way. Towards implementation, signi<sup>fi</sup>cant efforts were put into testing the system in the eventual value chain.

We assessed the progress on whether the use cases were done already, did we test the system in full, what is the quality, has the test level really been achieved, did we do any rehearsals for implementation and are there already any lessons learned? Those elements were continuously managed like a drum beat. [IT Program manager]

In this respect a proper pre-development review could take place with business users involved to test functionalities in real life. After the project implementation, a postdevelopment phase was performed by means of a lessonslearned report including project aftercare.

## Shared understanding

The investigated projects also exhibit differences in the application of practices for shared understanding throughout the phases. Projects A2, B2, and C2 had a high level of shared understanding in all phases of the project. While projects A1 and C1 increased the level of shared understanding during the ‘IT Use’ phase from low to high, project B1 showed low shared understanding throughout the whole project.

Projects A2, B2, and C2 showed an equal approach to shared understanding from the beginning, whereas in project A1 the project was focused from a technical perspective, limiting the shared understanding of employees. Project B1 started with an unclear business case, also resulting in limited understanding from the business side of the project. Lastly, project C1 showed a very limited stakeholder analysis upfront, leaving the rest of the project to be narrow-focused, thus not enabling a shared understanding during the ‘IT Planning’ or ‘IT Conversion’ phase. Both projects A1 and C1 recovered from this in the sense that a high level of shared understanding was achieved during the ‘IT Use’ phase. Both projects were confronted with potential business issues and risks at the end of the ‘IT Conversion’ phase; therefore, project teams and management had to focus on understanding the business in order to reduce any further impact and/or to mitigate risks.

Projects A2, B2, and C2 were found to have a high level of shared understanding during all phases of the project. The business involvement enabled business and IT stakeholders to spend time on enabling a shared understanding from both sides. Customers were involved in the project to help during development and testing (B2), business was strongly involved to set priorities from the beginning (A2), and business sessions were established to discuss both business and IT interests (C2) during the project.

Looking across cases, projects with a high business value had a high level of shared understanding throughout all phases of the project. The focus from the beginning on achieving a very good understanding shared among business and IT employees appears to be very important. It was found that ensuring a higher level of shared understanding in the ‘IT Use’ phase (A1 and C1) was not suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to generate a high business value from the project.

## Management commitment

The projects differ in the levels of management commitment: projects A1 and B1 exhibit low management commitment in the planning phase, whereas all other projects show high commitment in this phase. In addition, all projects but B1 and C1 have high management commitment in the conversion phase. All projects but B1 demonstrated high commitment in the ‘IT Use’ phase. Thus, only projects A2, B2, and C2 have high commitment during all three phases.

In project A1, business managers regarded the project as a technical project without a lot of business implications and did not realize the importance of the project for their business until the conversion phase (when commitment was rated as high). However, towards the end of the project, the time lost in the beginning contributed to the time pressure and led to a premature go live resulting in numerous issues. In project B1, the issue was that of a ‘<sup>fi</sup>re and forget’ mentality: an IT project manager was charged with the project without formal involvement of higher-level business management in any phase. Problems increased when the project manager left the company leaving the project without any formal management commitment and resulting in the IT unit not supporting the project.

While business management showed commitment through early enthusiasm in the planning phase of project C1, that commitment was not sustained throughout the project as exempli<sup>fi</sup>ed by the abandoning of the project board in the conversion phase (where commitment is rated as low).

The other projects showed frequent management involvement from the CEO (A2) or from a broad set of business executives (B2), using formal mechanisms like steering committees with business involvement (A2, B2, C1 planning phase, C2), approval gates (B2), workshops with business involvement (A2), and business-IT liaison roles (C2).

Looking across the cases, those projects with high business value had high management commitment throughout all phases. Since commitment helped get the right people on board with enough attention to agree on direction and requirements in the planning phases, it proved hard to make up for late commitment (A1) as the time pressure increased in later phases. However, just having high commitment in the beginning (C1) was also not suf<sup>fi</sup>cient. Formal mechanisms helped the high-value projects to sustain high levels of commitment in later stages.

## IT investment evaluation

The projects showed different levels of IT investment evaluation. Projects A2, B2, and C2 exhibit high levels of IT investment evaluation throughout all phases. Project A1 starts showing high levels from the IT Conversion phase onwards, project C1 only in the IT Use phase, and project B1 never shows high levels of IT investment evaluation practices.

In project A1 a pre-implementation check with a limited preview did not produce a clear picture of business objectives and evaluation criteria. The level of IT investment evaluation was low. No formal steering group (B1) and informal progress measurement (C1) also led to very general implementation reviews and low levels of IT investment evaluation. Detailed risk assessment (A2), business impact reviews (B2) and detailed steering and governance board from the start (C2) indicated high levels of IT investment evaluation.

Looking across the cases, projects with high business value had high levels of IT investment evaluations throughout all phases. Detailed IT investment evaluations in the beginning of the project helped to focus the IT investments and to provide clear criteria to evaluate the investments both from a business and an IT perspective.

## Discussion

Based on the in-depth case analysis and the cross-case analysis, the propositions are discussed.

## Re<sup>fl</sup>ection on Proposition 1 – communication between business and IT

Projects with a high IT business value (A2, B2, and C2) all had a high level of communication throughout the lifecycle of the project. In these projects there was a steering committee that provided a platform for communication. In projects A2 and C2, there was a formal steering group consisting of business and IT representatives, while in project B2 there was an informal steering group. Besides, in these projects liaison between business and IT was emphasized. This did not only happen on a strategic or tactical level, but also on a more operational level.

I think it is the whole manner in which we deal with projects like these, that you have cross functional groups working on them. It also forces people to think about the business processes behind the systems, etcetera. And that, at the end of the day, will also bring additional bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. [Business owner – Project B2]

Project A1 showed a lack of communication between business and IT in the beginning. Even though this increased as the project progressed, because the project organization gained a stronger business focus, it seems the lack of communication in the <sup>fi</sup>rst phase could not be recovered in later phases. In project C1, there was a low level of communication during the project; this however changed during the use phase since implementation issues had to be solved. Again, the lack of communication in earlier phases could not be made up for. Finally, in project B1 there was a low level of communication throughout the project. Lower levels of communication in projects indicated less detailed communication between business and IT leading to lower knowledge integration between business and IT.

The results of the case analysis show the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty of integrating business and IT knowledge (e.g., Grant, 1996 and (Carlile & Rebentisch, 2003) and are in line with suggested solutions such as increasing the frequency and ‘early-ness’ of communication (Bolton & Dewatripont, 1994).

Proposition 1 is supported; a high level of communication in each phase of the IT project value creation process is necessary for a high level of IT business value.

## Re<sup>fl</sup>ection on Proposition 2 – shared understanding

In the projects with a high business value (A2, B2, and C2), a lot of attention went to getting shared understanding in place. In these projects, the level of shared understanding was high in all phases of the value creation process. Shared understanding in these projects was mainly concerned with understanding productivity improvement and value creation, prioritizing the scope of the project, and assessing the organizational impact. In project B1 there was no shared understanding at all. Projects A1 and C1 followed a similar pattern with having a low level of shared understanding in the <sup>fi</sup>rst and second phase of the project. In both projects there were large issues during implementation; therefore a lot of effort was put into understanding business issues in order to solve them accordingly.

Proposition 2 is supported by the fact that a high level of shared understanding is needed. If a high level of shared understanding was neither present in the planning phase nor in the conversion phase, none of our projects achieved high business value.

Luftman & Brier (1999) support the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this research by arguing that understanding the linkage between business and IT executives is one of the steps involved in sustaining alignment over time. In several studies it has been acknowledged that shared understanding has a major impact on alignment (Rockart et al, 1996; Reich & Benbasat, 1996; Reich & Benbasat, 2000; Chan, 2002; Tan & Gallupe, 2006; Chan et al, 2006; Preston & Karahanna, 2009). These studies, however, all have a similar de<sup>fi</sup>nition of shared understanding, which is typically concerned with the level of business and IT executives (Reich & Benbasat, 2000; Luftman, 2000). The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of our research suggest that a shared understanding also has to be achieved on an operational project level within the organization. One respondent explained this as ‘…with the right people in the room, who are not necessarily the executives by the way, the people who could really make a difference’ [Business owner – Project A2].

## Re<sup>fl</sup>ection on Proposition 3 – commitment from management

Projects A2, B2, C1, and C2 all started out with a high level of management commitment. In project A2 managers were keen on being involved, even up to the very operational level. Projects B2, C1, and C2 were all part of a business driver or business program; therefore management placed emphasis on these projects from the beginning. All projects realized a high IT business value, except for project C1. Project A1 and C1 have a similar pattern in that both projects had a high level of management commitment at the end of the project. This commitment was essential in order to mitigate, and solve, problems that were found after the implementation. Finally, project B1 had a low commitment because there was limited awareness of management in the project.

Proposition 3 is supported; those projects that did not exhibit a high level of management commitment in each phase of the IT project were also not able to generate a high level of business value.

Management commitment has been found to be the number one enabler of, or critical success factor for, alignment (Luftman et al, 1999; Teo & Ang, 1999). In this study it has been found that management commitment is a necessary practice during the early phases in the project. Latter <sup>fi</sup>nding is supported by the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of Baets (1992), Campbell et al (2005), who found that management commitment is necessary along the way, in different steps and runs from top to bottom within the organization. Furthermore, commitment from management positively stimulated sponsoring of the project, allocation of appropriate and adequate resources during the project, and the set-up of a steering committee consisting of business and IT executives (Luftman & Brier, 1999; Teo & Ang, 1999).

## Re<sup>fl</sup>ection on Proposition 4 – evaluation of the IT investment

In the projects with a high IT business value (A2, B2, and C2) emphasis was placed on evaluating the IT investment during the process of value creation. Project A2 was managed according to a clear business case and project structure with prede<sup>fi</sup>ned accountabilities and responsibilities. The latter was also performed in projects B2 and C2, where business and IT persons took responsibility for evaluating the project during its lifecycle. These projects also did a post-implementation and pre-implementation review. In project A1, the status and progress of the project was assessed frequently throughout the project; even preand post-implementation reviews were conducted. However, in projects A1 and C1 the same thing happened in the sense that due to a limited stakeholder analysis upfront there was no clarity about whom to involve in the project. Progress evaluations could therefore only be made on a limited basis. Finally, in project B1 minimal effort was put into evaluating the IT investment, which was low during all phases of the project.

Proposition 4 is supported in that a high level of investment evaluation is necessary in each phase of the IT project. Projects that did not employ suf<sup>fi</sup>cient investment evaluation from the very start also did not exhibit a high level of business value.

Several authors have stated, and in some studies empirically proven, that IT investment evaluation is an important enabler of business-IT alignment (Henderson & Venkatraman, 1993; Tallon et al, 2000; Kohli & Devaraj, 2004; Sledgianowski & Luftman, 2006). Where several studies view alignment as an end-state (Henderson & Venkatraman, 1993; Kohli & Devaraj, 2004), the <sup>fi</sup>ndings in this study point out that IT investment evaluation has to be utilized during all phases of the process of value creation. This approach is the essence of IT investment evaluation, which is evaluating the investment over time through frequent and formal IT assessments and reviews (Sledgianowski & Luftman, 2006).

## Re<sup>fl</sup>ection on Proposition 5 – complementarity

Some combinations of a high level of communication, shared understanding, management commitment, and IT investment evaluation have been found in cases A1, A2, B2, C1, and C2. In B1 none of the business-IT alignment practices were high during any of the phases of the project. In projects A2, B2, and C2, there was a high level of these business-IT alignment practices in every phase of the project, whereas in projects A1 and C1 this combination was only found at the end of the project. At the end of these projects, business and IT had to collaborate extensively in order to limit the negative impact that the projects could have on the business. Therefore in both projects a lot of effort was put into getting all these practices in place in order to derive as much value as possible.

Projects A2, B2, and C2 had a high level of complementarity of alignment practices during each phase of the project. In these projects the different alignment practices were used together since business and IT management were committed to the project, they fostered a shared understanding by allocating resources, and de<sup>fi</sup>ned a collaborative project structure. The complementarity can also be explained through the observation that certain mechanisms are suitable to support multiple alignment practices. For example, steering committees provide a platform for communication which in turn helps to create shared understanding. Depending on the composition of the committee, having high-ranking executives involved can also demonstrate management commitment. And steering committees can also be the place where investment evaluations are presented and reviewed. These complementarities are in line with the theoretical argumentation by Milgrom & Roberts (1995).

Proposition 5 is supported: complementarity among the alignment practices in the different phases resulted in high levels of business value.

## The importance of the beginning of a project

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings show a pattern that all business-IT alignment practices were high in the <sup>fi</sup>rst phase of projects that had a high IT business value. The same practices were low in the <sup>fi</sup>rst phase of the projects that had a low IT business value, except for project C1, which had a high manage ment commitment at the beginning. Interviewees emphasized the importance of getting alignment in place from the beginning of the project in order to sustain alignment over time and to derive business value from the project. In this respect projects were treated as business projects, instead of solely IT projects, in order to incorporate the collaboration of both IT and business people.

What we are doing at the moment, is pushing Business-IT alignment more to the front of the project, improving the quality of the requirements, and introducing more structure and uniformity in the requirements within the whole chain of the project. We now conduct workshops at the beginning of the project in order to go through change requests in a short time frame, but with a high intensity. We need to make sure that we really understand everything in order to not be surprised by the quality at the end of the project and by forcing it towards the start of the project. [IT Program manager, Project C2]

Several authors have emphasized the importance of the early phase of the value creation process (Kohli & Devaraj, 2004; Marshall et al, 2005). In addition, Luftman & Brier (1999) argue the importance of getting business-IT alignment in place from the beginning of the process in order to sustain alignment over time. The previous studies, however, did not take the project level into account, but focused on the organization as a whole. A pattern of high business value and high complementarity of alignment practices in the early phases was found among projects A2, B2, and C2. The lower valued projects A1 and C1 on the other hand were typically focused on lowering costs and implementing technical changes. Kohli & Devaraj (2004) explain these <sup>fi</sup>ndings by arguing that organizations have to invest time and effort in evaluating their business strategy in order to capitalize on their strengths later on. They, however, found that effort and time is mostly missing since often decisions are based on costs and immediate bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, therefore leaving limited space for alignment (Kohli & Devaraj, 2004).

I think that the value of the project starts with having a good baseline to start from. I think that one of the most important items or areas you should look at is that at the start of the project you have to make sure that it is clear what the goals and the requirements are. [IT Program manager, Project B2]

While we were not able to test all combinations of high and low alignment practices in each phase, the consistency of the observed patterns indicates that high levels of alignment practices and high levels of complementarity from the <sup>fi</sup>rst phase onwards support high levels of IT business value. Or arguing it in reverse: not employing all alignment practices from the very beginning failed to produce high-value business projects in our sample.

## Limitations and implications for future research

This study has several limitations. Firstly, in this study all alignment practices have been treated with the same importance as well as having the same impact on creating business value. Future studies might want to consider whether some practices are more important than others, and which factors have a higher, or lower, impact on generating business value.

Secondly, the IT business value scores have been based on the perception of interviewees about the IT business value of the project. Some of the performance items have been corroborated on the basis of internal documentation provided to the researchers; however, not all scores have been veri<sup>fi</sup>ed with a second data source besides the interviewee's perception. In order to increase the validity of analyzed IT business value scores, subsequent studies could take into account to corroborate each score with evidence-based documentation or data.

Finally, we have looked at six projects. Increasing the number of cases looked at will help to expand the generalization scope of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings and will help to test some of the missing combinations of high and low alignment scores, for example, in the last phase of a project.

Given the importance of the beginning of IT projects, we would like to encourage research on the starting phases of projects. Further detail is needed on the mechanisms and situational factors required for a good project start. An interesting approach might be the transfer of research on agile methodologies from software development to alignment practices that might allow looking at building alignment iteratively. Another opportunity would be to examine the aspects covered in this paper in an explicitly longitudinal study to experience the impact of alignment practices on IT business value over time.

In addition, a replication of this study in another industry would give more insights into differences between industries, for practitioners as well as academics. Our focus on one industry allowed us to control for differences in the environment that come inherently with different industries. In this sense it would be interesting to see whether the <sup>fi</sup>ndings also hold true in other industries.

Besides, a replication of this study that investigates other business-IT alignment practices in the different phases of value creation would generate an even wider view of business-IT alignment. This could include practices that go beyond the immediate in<sup>fl</sup>uence of a project like those external to a company (e.g. environmental volatility) and those beyond the scope of a single project (e.g. historical performance of the IT unit) to explore the importance of creating a set of broader conditions allowing individual projects to be value generating (so to say an environment for project success).

Our study should also encourage research of alignment and business value creation on a more granular level than has been the focus so far. On the one hand, bringing down alignment and value creation to operational levels by looking at individual projects or operational processes; on the other hand, by looking at individual phases of these projects and how they develop over time.

## Conclusions and implications

## Conclusions

Our study looks at business-IT alignment on an operational project level while emphasizing the importance of timing and complementarity of alignment practices. So far, most studies on alignment have focused on alignment at a company level. Our study takes a look at how alignment is achieved in individual projects. Also, only few authors have considered the aspects of timing in alignment. This study investigates the use of business-IT alignment practices in three different phases of business value creation in projects, thus enabling us to include the role of timing.

The empirical results indicate that business value creation in IT projects necessitates a high level of business-IT alignment practices (communication, shared understanding, management commitment, and IT investment evaluation) already during the early phases of the project: the planning and the conversion phase. It is too late to apply alignment practices in the use phase. Although we did not observe all combinations of high and low alignment scores in each phase, we also see ground to argue that it is not suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to employ the alignment practices in just some phases of a project. However, there is a need to apply them in a concerted effort. This adds to the contribution of studies emphasizing the requirement of complementarity of practices (Milgrom & Roberts, 1995). Still, it is not suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to have complementarity of all alignment practices in later phases of IT projects. Ramping up the level of shared understanding, communication, management commitment, and IT investment evaluation in later phases has not been suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to create high business value in those projects. There is indeed no time to waste in a project regarding the employment of all four alignment practices at the immediate start of the project. It takes time to form, for example, common understanding, and early phases are direction setting. Once these directions are set it might be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to ‘merge’ different directions based on different understandings, different ways of communication, or certain perceptions of the project – once people have gotten used to those – in later phases. Despite the above-mentioned limitation that we did not test all combinations of high and low alignment practice scores, we have no reason to assume that alignment does not also matter at the end of a project.

## Implications for practice

Practically speaking and broken down into each alignment practice, this implies the following: in order to achieve a high level of communication, companies need to create opportunities for collaboration between business and IT people from the beginning of a project. Mechanisms like a steering committee can institutionalize and give direction to this communication and collaboration from the start. Furthermore, it is important to have people that liaise between business and IT in order to have an understanding of both environments. Shared understanding can be created through communication, when people work together in cross-functional teams and are forced to discuss project-related issues with each other that they otherwise would not discuss. However, they need to apply these techniques early on in the project, potentially because important directional decisions requiring a common understanding are made in the early phases of a project and that cannot be easily reversed in later phases. When these decisions are made in the absence of a common understanding, even gaining that common understanding later on will not allow for reversing the direction. It is also necessary that management is committed from the beginning in order to align the project with overall objectives and strategies, sponsor the project, allocate (additional) resources when necessary, and create opportunities for collaboration. Finally, a pre- and postimplementation evaluation of the project investment in combination with frequent and formal assessments can challenge the organization especially in early phases to learn from its mistakes and to adopt practices that have been proven successful in the past. In summary, an early adoption of each of the alignment practices can serve to establish a certain culture in a project which might not be easy to reproduce later on in the project, potentially simply because it takes time to establish this common culture.
