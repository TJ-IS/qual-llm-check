# Determinants of successful virtual communities: Contributions from system characteristics and social factors

Hsiu-Fen Lin \*

Department of Shipping and Transportation Management, National Taiwan Ocean University, No. 2 Beining Road, Keelung, Taiwan, ROC

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 6 July 2006 Received in revised form 22 June 2007 Accepted 31 August 2008 Available online 9 October 2008

Keywords: Virtual communities System characteristics Social factors Member satisfaction Member loyalty

## A B S T R A C T

Although there is growing interest in virtual communities, few studies have examined them from an integrated viewpoint including technical and social perspectives. By expanding on DeLone and McLean’s IS success model, the author constructed a model of the impact of system characteristics (e.g., information and system quality) and social factors (e.g., trust and social usefulness) in implementing successful virtual communities. Data collected from 198 community members provided support for the model. Results showed that both member satisfaction and a sense of belonging were determinants of member loyalty in the community. Additionally, information and system quality were found to affect member satisfaction, while trust influenced the members’ sense of belonging to the community. Finally, the findings provided understanding of the factors that measured virtual community success. Implications of my study are discussed

## 1. Introduction

A virtual community is a cyberspace having various Internetbased chat technologies, including discussion forums (discussion or bulletin board), etc. It depends on social interaction among its members, who share interests, build relationships, create fantasies, and conduct transactions. Igbaria et al. [13] suggested that sustaining the virtual community promotes knowledge sharing, with significant impact on businesses.

Preece [22] identified and measured key determinants of sociability (numbers of participants, degree of reciprocity, and trust) and usability (number of errors, productivity, and user satisfaction) of successful virtual communities, while Johnson [14] argued that adequate technical support and online communication techniques were necessary for their success. However, I found that the factors leading to success of virtual communities have not been carefully examined; therefore, I developed and empirically tested a virtual community success model. It was partly derived from DeLone and McLean’s IS success model (the D&M model) [5].

The model developed for early monolithic IS appears to provide a good base for examining the success of virtual communities. In this context, Lin and Lee [18] stated that quality-perception dimensions were the key antecedents of effectiveness. Moreover, social influences, such as leadership, are essential in a successful virtual community while trust is necessary if a member is to be willing to exchange information.

By expanding the D&M model, my study examined the impact of system characteristics (e.g., information and system quality) and social factors (e.g., trust and social usefulness) on success in virtual communities. The research model and hypothesized relationships were empirically tested using SEM, supported by LISREL 8.8 software.

## 2. Theoretical background and hypotheses

## 2.1. Virtual communities

A virtual community-based approach is an effective way of sharing knowledge. It facilitates informal sharing of knowledge available from experienced and skilled people [16]. Furthermore, three Internet-based chat technologies have received attention recently as media for community member communication: discussion forums, weblogs, and wikis. These chat technologies share several common properties; they:

1. create a nonvolatile record of the discussion providing a record of knowledge sharing;

2. are lightweight, with knowledge stored in text form (possibly enhanced by multimedia additions);

3. support collaboration by people in different time zones and places, and

4. support knowledge organization, by threading or hyperlinking, etc.

Despite their similarities, the technologies have differences; discussion forums engage users in a question-and-answer process, whereas weblogs encourage first-person storytelling and commenting while both organize content chronologically. Wiki design and use focuses on incremental knowledge exchange, version management, and multi-user participation, making a wiki into an open-source community providing knowledge content.

From a technical perspective, Internet-based chat technology supports virtual communities, in which individuals access both information and other people. A virtual community is like a social community, giving its members an opportunity to interact.

## 2.2. IS success model

DeLone and McLean proposed a six dimension of IS success model: system quality, information quality, user satisfaction, IS use, individual impact, and organizational impact. In this taxonomy, system quality measures technical success, information quality measures semantic success, and user satisfaction, IS use, individual impact, and organizational impact are measures of effectiveness. The D&M model made a significant contribution by proposing that IS success measures are multi-dimensional and that there are causal relationships among these dimensions.

According to the D&M model, quality has two major dimensions: system quality measures technical success while information quality measures semantic success. User satisfaction and IS use measure user intentions and user opinion of the IS. Organizational impacts are considered to result from expectation of net benefits when using the IS. This implies that a high-quality system will be associated with more user satisfaction and positive net benefits.

## 2.3. Transitioning from IS success to virtual community success

In transforming the D&M model to a virtual community context, some IS-specific meaning of its constructs must be clarified. This involves two important issues, a move from a focus on IS use to one of belonging to a community, and a change from a need for net benefits (e.g. to the organization) to loyalty to the community.

IS use is a key variable in understanding IS success [6]. However, virtual communities are characterized by anonymity, addictive behavior, and voluntary behavior. Sense of belonging is treated as a crucial feature for participation in virtual communities. Sense of belonging is important in a virtual community because no involvement or participation would occur if it were absent. Therefore, I considered sense of belonging to be an appropriate measure for virtual community success. An Internet user can easily become a member of a virtual community, because membership has less requirements than those of traditional communities. It is important for each community to attract more member participation. Loyalty creates a stable pool of members and thus it plays a significant role in expanding the community. Because of the uniqueness of each virtual community, member loyalty was considered to be a more appropriate indicator of virtual community effectiveness than individual or organizational impact. Hence, the variables of interest in my research were information and system quality, member satisfaction, sense of belonging, and member loyalty. Moreover, sense of belonging represented involvement in the virtual community. Finally, member loyalty measures member involvement in a virtual community, i.e., participation in community operations and communication with other members.

In line with the D&M model, I assumed that system characteristics determined member satisfaction, and thus influenced the sense of belonging. These were linked to member loyalty, resulting in five hypotheses:

H1. Information quality positively affects member satisfaction with the virtual community.

H2. System quality positively affects member satisfaction with the virtual community.

H3. Member satisfaction positively affects sense of belonging to the virtual community.

H4. Member satisfaction positively affects member loyalty in the virtual community.

H5. Sense of belonging positively affects member loyalty in the virtual community.

## 2.4. Social factors of a virtual community

From a social perspective, studies on virtual communities emphasized supportive, sociable relations that provided a sense of belonging [12]. Social influences are most relevant to virtual community activities; Preece suggested several characteristics of successful and sustainable virtual communities: the number of participants in the community, trustworthiness, the number of ontopic messages, and the amount of reciprocity. My study examined two social influence variables, trust and social usefulness, which have been considered highly pertinent in the virtual community context.

Trust is the willingness of one party to be vulnerable to the actions of another. Practical online communication needs an element of trust, since virtual community activities lack face-toface contact. Trust provides expectations of successful operation of virtual communities. For instance, trust is crucial when the absence of workable rules requires others to behave in a socially acceptable manner. Lack of trust among participants is a major barrier in establishing virtual communities. I hypothesized that when participants trust in other community members, they will be more inclined to participate and feel a sense of belonging.

H6. Trust positively affects sense of belonging to the virtual community.

Social factors refer to individual’s agreement with the groups culture, and specific interpersonal liaisons with others. In the context of the virtual community, social usefulness refers to perceived support (respect, recognition, approval, etc.) from other community members when using the virtual community. This implies that users seek the approval of other community members when increasing participation. Hence, I expected that social usefulness would have a positive effect on a sense of belonging.

H7. Social usefulness positively affects sense of belonging to the virtual community.

## 3. Research methodology

## 3.1. Subjects and procedure

Data were collected using a paper-based survey from a class of 236 students registered in five business courses at a large university located in the north of Taiwan. Student subjects were selected for several reasons: they were among the first to participate in virtual communities and thus were familiar with several Internet technologies; their participation provided insights for an influential group; and a reasonable body of work has suggested that college students resemble the normal population of Internet users, and thus would be suitable subjects [10,19].

A cover letter explained the purpose of my study and promised to ensure that the responses were kept confidential. A definition and description of virtual communities was included to ensure that all respondents had a consistent view.

Of the 236 questionnaires distributed, 230 completed and usable questionnaires were received. The first question asked respondents whether they had any personal experience in using virtual communities. Those who responded no to this question were removed from further analysis. As a result, 32 questionnaires were removed. Table 1 lists the respondent’s demographic characteristics, including gender, age, Internet experience, involvement with the virtual community, virtual community type, and reasons for using the virtual community.

## 3.2. Measurement development

In my study, all constructs were measured using multiple items, which were gathered in the survey using a five-point Liker-type scale (ranging from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). The constructs were primarily adapted from previous studies but modified to request data on the virtual community. Appendix A lists all of survey items used to measure each construct. These were:

\- Information quality; measured by information accuracy, completeness, currency, customized, and format of information presentation [2,20].

\- System quality; measured using system reliability, convenience of access, response time and system flexibility.

\- Trust; measured by six-items, adapted from Ridings et al. [23], representing the ability, integrity and benevolence of other members of the community.

\- Social usefulness; assessed using items from Gupta and Kim [9].

\- Sense of belonging to the virtual community; measured by Teo et al.’s method [24], in which respondents showed if they strongly felt they were a part of the virtual community having strong morale among members.

\- Member satisfaction with the virtual community; assessed through three questions: ‘I am satisfied with my interaction with the virtual community’, ‘The virtual community’s information content meets my needs’, and ‘Overall, I am satisfied with the virtual community’.

\- Member loyalty; measured using three items taken from Lin [17] and Yoo et al. [25] that determined the extent to which members of the community were involved.

## 4. Data analysis and results

The hypothesized models were empirically tested using SEM, supported by LISREL 8.8 software with maximum likelihood estimation [15]. Data analysis proceeded in two stages. The measurement model was estimated using CFA to test convergent and discriminant validity of the measurement model, and the structural model was analyzed to prove or disprove my hypotheses.

## 4.1. Scale validation

The overall model fit was assessed using five common measures: chi-square/degree of freedom $( \chi ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { d . f . } ) ,$ GFI, NNFI, CFI, and RMSEA. As shown in Table 2, all the indices exceeded their commonly accepted levels, demonstrating that the measurement model exhibited a good fit with the data collected.

The measurement model was then assessed for convergent and discriminant validity. Convergent validity was assessed through three sets of indices: reliability of question items, composite reliability of question items, and average variance extracted by the constructs. Table 3 presents the factor loadings of the measurement items; for all items these exceed the recommended level of 0.5 [11]. Indeed, the values ranged from 0.82 (for member loyalty) to 0.93 (for trust). All latent constructs of composite reliability exceeded the benchmark of 0.7 recommended by Nunnally and Bernstein [21], and latent constructs of average variance extracted were above the threshold of 0.5 [8]. Finally, the correlations of potentially overlapping constructs were used to assess discriminant validity. No pair of measures had correlations exceeding the criterion (0.9 and above), suggested by Hair et al., as shown in Table 4, implying that no multicolinearity existed among the various constructs.

## 4.2. Hypothesis testing

A similar set of fit indices was used to examine the structural model. The ratio of $\chi ^ { 2 }$ to degrees-of-freedom was 2.11 for the structural model, again within the recommended level of 3. Comparison of other fit indices with their corresponding recommended values provided evidence of a good fit (GFI = 0.80, NNFI = 0.96, CFI = 0.96, RMSEA = 0.075). Hence, my study could proceed to examine the path coefficients of the model.

The results of hypotheses testing are presented in Fig. 2, with significant paths depicted by bold lines and insignificant paths by dash lines. All the hypothesized paths, with the exception of that linking social usefulness and sense of belonging (H7), were significant (p < 0.05). The paths from information quality and system quality to member satisfaction were positive and significant, thereby supporting H1 and H2. The path from member satisfaction and sense of belonging was positive and significant, while both member satisfaction and sense of belonging significantly influenced member loyalty, demonstrating support for H3, H4 and H5. The results also showed a positive and significant path between trust and sense of belonging. Consequently, H6 was supported.

## 5. Conclusion

## 5.1. Findings and implications

My study examined the impact of system characteristics and social factors on stimulation of successful virtual communities. I validated the proposed model. Members find that virtual communities meet their needs when they are reliable, convenient, and user-friendly, providing accurate, complete, constantly updated, and customized information. Therefore, online businesses and community providers should put time and effort into ensuring that their customers are satisfied with information and system quality, as these were identified as the key contributors to member satisfaction when using virtual communities.

My findings showed a positive relationship among member satisfaction, sense of belonging, and member loyalty; this is consistent with previous studies [4]; satisfied members with a higher sense of belonging apparently have a high member loyalty to the community. Moreover, online businesses and community providers should offer a high level of interactivity among community members.

Trust has the strongest influence on a sense of belonging to a virtual community. Since online communication lack face-to-face contact, mutual trust plays an essential role in reducing uncertainty and increasing confidence.

Finally, I found that social usefulness did not significantly influence sense of belonging to the community. This was somewhat surprising; however, most respondents in my study were undergraduate students, representing a younger generation engaged in virtual communities to obtain enjoyment, entertainment, amusement and fun. Therefore, sample selection bias may be a reason for the insignificant effect of social usefulness on members’ sense of belonging to the community. This finding might also be because relationship building is a consequence of interaction, rather than the prime motivator for members to participate in the virtual community. I found that most respondents participated primarily to share their common interests, and then to have fun interacting with others. Hence, the insignificant effect of social usefulness on sense of belonging may be due to people’s reasons for joining the virtual community.

## 5.2. Limitations

Although this study provides valuable insights into the virtual community context, it has limitations. First, the data were gathered from a relatively homogenous demographic group: college students. Caution is thus necessary when generalizing its findings to a broader population. Second, there are several benefits of Internet-based chat technologies that are used in virtual community and some may differ in their influence on the success of the community. Third, my study focused on how system characteristics and social factors affected the sustainability of the community but did not consider al determinants of successful communities.

## Appendix A. Questionnaire items

## A.1. Information quality

IQ1: The information provided by the virtual community is accurate.

IQ2: The virtual community provides me with a complete set of information.

IQ3: The information from the virtual community always up to date.

IQ4: The virtual community provides me with all the information I need.

IQ5: The information provided by the virtual community is well formatted.

## A.2. System quality

SQ1: The virtual community operates reliably.

SQ2: The virtual community allows information to be readily accessible to me.

SQ3: It takes too long for the virtual community to respond to my requests (reverse coded).

SQ4: The virtual community can be adapted to meet a variety of needs.

## A.3. Trust

TR1: I feel more confident about discussion skills than the other virtual community members.

TR2: The other virtual community members know a lot about the discussion subject.

TR3: Virtual community members are concerned about what is important to others.

TR4: Virtual community members will do everything within their capacity to help others.

TR5: Virtual community members try hard to be fair in dealing with each other.

TR6: Other virtual community members do not behave consistently (reverse coded)

## A.4. Social usefulness

SU1: Using the virtual community helps me to obtain respect from other virtual community members.

SU2: Using the virtual community improves how I am perceived by the other virtual community members.

SU3: Using the virtual community gives me the opportunity to recommend ideas to other virtual community members.

SU4: Using the virtual community helps me to form warm relationships with other virtual community members.

## A.5. Sense of belonging

SB1: I feel a strong sense of belonging to the virtual community.

SB2: I enjoy being a member of the virtual community.

SB3: I am very committed to the virtual community.

SB4: Overall, the virtual community has a high level of morale.

## A.6. Member satisfaction

US1: I am satisfied with my interaction with the virtual community.

US2: The virtual community’s information content meets my needs.

US3: Overall, I am satisfied with the virtual community.

## A.7. Member loyalty

ML1: I believe it is worthwhile for me to return to use the virtual community.

ML2: I am willing to participate in community operations.

ML3: I am willing to communicate with other community members.
