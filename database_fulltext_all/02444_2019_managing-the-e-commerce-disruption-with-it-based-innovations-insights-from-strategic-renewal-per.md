## Abstract

Transforming brick-and-mortar businesses into integrated online–offline models poses significant challenges for firms operationally and strategically. We conduct a comparative case study to investigate strategic renewals that result in the convergence of offline and online operations and strategies driven by information technology based innovations. We explore two strategic renewal paths used by our case firms: incremental resourcecomplementing renewal and discontinuous resource-substituting transformation. We illuminate the role of resource endowment in strategic renewal and its impact on the renewal path and explore the role of IT as operand or operant resource in strategic renewals and its impact on their success.

Keywords: e-commerce, disruptive innovation, strategic renewal, information technology, resource endowment

## 1. INTRODUCTION

E-commerce as a disruptive business model has turned the retail industry landscape upside down and caused tremendous turmoil in traditional offline retail firms worldwide. The recent success of global e-commerce giants such as Amazon and Alibaba in taking market shares from large and small brick-and-mortar retailers manifests a tectonic shift in future business models for the retail industry [1]. Facing the existential challenge, management in traditional brick-and-mortar firms realizes that they must embrace the e-commerce disruption by transforming their business models while taking advantage of their strengths in offline operations [2].

One strategy that many traditional brick-and-mortar firms have embraced is to integrate their existing offline operations with newly created online operations, the so-called “integrating bricks with clicks” model [3]. However, this transformation has proven to be difficult for firms from both operational and strategic perspectives. While new entrants in e-commerce, such as Amazon, eBay, Alibaba, and JD.com, can quickly build their brands and occupy a segment of online markets, our conversations with many top executives as well as press reports indicate that traditional offline firms often struggle in entering the online markets and surviving in this dynamic market environment.

We submit that the transformation of traditional offline firms into integrated online–offline ones could be viewed as a form of strategic renewal that firms carry out to capture emerging competitive advantage while maintaining existing market positions. The strategic renewal theory argues that firms should refresh or replace organizational attributes that have the potential to substantially affect their long-term prospects [4]. Studies have elaborated on how firms refresh or replace organizational attributes such as product design, organizational learning, structural integration, and dynamic capabilities to accomplish strategic renewal [5–8]. Agarwal and Helfat [4] proposed two basic types of strategic renewal: incremental strategic renewal and discontinuous strategic transformation. Incremental renewal allows a firm to take advantage of path-dependent opportunities, enhance core businesses, and outperform the competition with mature products and excellent services [9]. Firms can reuse existing resources and progressively renew their products/services to adapt to the new market environment, thus reducing the need for a much larger and more difficult transformation later on [4]. By contrast, discontinuous strategic transformation calls for a firm to fundamentally alter one or more aspects of its strategy and organization, including not only operational processes but also business models, technological bases, organizational structures, capabilities, and other resources [5,10,11].

However, few studies have explored the issues in transforming from traditional offline models to integrated online–offline models from a strategic renewal perspective. Digital innovations such as e-commerce compel organizational strategies and operations to be much more dynamic and customer-centric. How do traditional offline firms manage online transactions characterized by high-quantity fluctuations, high customer orientation, high transaction speed, low-profit margins, and wide distribution areas? How do firms overcome organizational challenges in significantly transforming their business strategy and operational processes? And, perhaps more interesting to information systems (IS) scholars, what roles does information technology (IT) play in the strategic renewal processes?

Thus, accomplishing a successful strategic renewal in the dynamic environment brought by disruptive ecommerce innovations has become a theoretical and a practical challenge for scholars and managers alike. To examine the interactive relationships among operation, strategy, and customer in the transformation process of traditional firms, we conducted a comparative case study with two firms in China: JEANSWEST and COMAGIC. Both were traditional offline firms with distinct resource endowment, organizational constraints, and IT capabilities, and both suffered failures in their initial attempts to develop e-commerce operations. However, following different transformational paths, they eventually discovered the key to success and became leading online–offline integrated firms in their own markets. In the following sections, we develop and present a strategic renewal framework that explains the transformation processes of the two case firms and how their IT capabilities and innovations impacted their transformation processes.

## 2. THEORETICAL BACKGROUND

## 2.1 E-commerce Disruption

The disruptive innovation theory [12] offers a salient perspective on why e-commerce is so disruptive to traditional offline firms in the retail industry. The theory argues that technology, product, or service with cheaper, simpler, and more convenient attributes tends to exceed the capabilities of the dominant on a few dimensions that are appealing to a few fringe customers; as the disruptive technology, product, or service improves with time, it gradually outgrows the emerging market where it began and starts capturing the attention of mainstream customers, eventually displacing the dominant in the mainstream market and bringing disruptive shocks [13].

Disruptive innovations such as social media, mobile and cloud computing, and data analytics create tremendous challenges to traditional businesses [14]. More quality products with lower prices are distributed through digital platforms. More customers progressively migrate from offline to online for routine purchases. Furthermore, information sharing online is cheaper, faster, and more abundant. Negative comments and public sentiments of customers diffuse much faster online than offline and have significant impacts on sales, product/service design, production, and consumption [15]. If traditional offline firms could not satisfy customers whose purchasing habits have been changed by e-commerce, it is difficult for these firms to survive the ecommerce disruption and the associated digital innovations.

According to Christensen [12], there are two key concepts in the disruptive innovation theory: disruptive innovation and sustaining innovation. Disruptive innovation not only changes the manner in which a customer behaves but also introduces one or more product dimensions previously unseen in a given market [16], while sustaining innovation represents a continuous (vs. discontinuous) improvement of existing products or technologies. In contrast to disruptive innovation, a sustaining innovation does not create new markets or new value networks but rather only evolves existing ones with better value and efficiency.

Studies show that sustaining innovation has been the most effective way for large firms to update products and maintain market share [17]. Incumbent firms have likely already developed effective routines for handling their customers, thus leading to organizational inertia against disruptive innovation. As such, shifting their efforts onto disruptive innovation would require the incumbents to undertake major changes that do not necessarily build upon their current strengths [16]. The organizational inertia of established firms may impede investment in disruptive innovation projects [18] or constrain their corporate subsidiaries from investing in disruptive innovations, thereby making them to be weaker competitors than freestanding start-ups [19]. Incumbents prefer to invest more in sustaining and incremental innovations that build off their previous products, while new entrants are more likely to invest in disruptive and radical innovations.

In the extant literature, the focus has been on exploring disruptive innovation from a technical perspective in e-commerce environment [20-21] or on acquiring successful disruptive innovations [22–24]. However, some scholars have argued that it is more important for traditional offline firms to figure out how to manage the ecommerce disruption and digital innovations [14,23]. As Christensen and Raynor [25] stated, disruptive technology should not be the unique determinant for successful disruptive innovations. Thus, it is conceivable that developing disruptive technologies should not be the only way for firms to deal with the e-commerce disruption, and effective strategies are needed to help firms manage the disruptive shocks of new innovations [26].

## 2.2 Strategic Renewal

Agarwal and Helfat [4] define strategic renewal as the process, content, and outcome of refreshment or replacement of attributes of an organization that have the potential to substantially affect its long-term prospects. The key aspects of strategic renewal relate to refreshment and replacement, rather to all types of change. There are two basic types of strategic renewal: incremental strategic renewal and discontinuous strategic transformation [4]. Incremental strategic renewal is often preferred by large firms with resource constraints [27,28]. It allows the firms to take advantage of path-dependent opportunities, enhance core businesses, outperform the competition with mature products and excellent services [29], and shape the markets to maximize the advantage of their industry leadership [30]. Discontinuous strategic transformation, on the other hand, is characterized by the “competence-destroying change” [31]. When suffering from radical external changes, a firm’s core business or resources may become useless or significantly devalued. In discontinuous strategic transformations, firms change not only the operational processes but also business models, technological bases, organizational structures, capabilities, and other resources [5,10,11].

For this study, the emergent e-commerce business models and the disruptive e-commerce innovations constitute the context of strategic renewal [32]. In particular, we submit that at least three significant changes brought by the Internet and related innovations have dramatically altered the environment in which firms and customers conduct transactions and develop and maintain relationships. First, the Internet changes customers’ shopping channels and facilitates their migration from offline to online platforms, which results in increasing difficulties for firms to maintain competitiveness by relying only on traditional offline operations. Second, the Internet overturns the traditional power structure between firms and customers [33,34], which has forced the traditional offline firms to transform from firm-dominant culture to customer-centric culture [35]. Last but not least, the Internet changes the connections among firms, which not only promotes information synergy between firms and their partners but also reduces cost of access to information of their competitors [36]. These changes help shorten product life cycle, improve the speed of market innovation, and hence force traditional firms to accelerate innovation [37].

In this context, strategic renewal appears not as an option but as a necessity. Both incremental renewal and discontinuous transformation may help firms adapt to the new environment and manage the challenges. However, it is unclear how strategic renewals can be carried out strategically and operationally. It is particularly of interest to the IS scholars the role and impact of IT in the two different forms of strategic renewal and their success or failure.

## 2.3 Role of IT in Strategic Renewal

IT has been long recognized as one of the most important enablers of organizational transformation [38,39]. Research suggests that IT has a dual role in organizational change [40,41]: as an operand resource that plays a supportive or enabling role in helping firms gain efficiency and effectiveness and as an operant resource that serves as an active agent that triggers or initiates business innovations.

The view of IT as an operand resource argues that IT plays a “supporting” role mainly in cost reduction and efficiency improvement. For example, enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems enable business process integration and efficiency [42], or service-oriented IS support business innovation [43]. As an enabler, regardless of its significant influence on business model innovation, product/service design and delivery, and work team cooperation [39,44,45], IT is not the primary source of innovation.

The view of IT as an operant resource argues that IT plays a “trigger” role for innovation and change [40]. The emergence and significant development of digital technologies and IT-based innovations have caused scholars to rethink the role of IT in organizational change [20,46,47]. As an operant resource, the most important IT itself becomes a source of innovation instead of a “supporter” or “enabler,” and proper use of IT in key organizational decisions and processes can trigger systemic innovations in organizations. For instance, the introduction of e-commerce platform could trigger resource reconfiguration and process reengineering and eventually create innovations in business operations [48].

Despite the academic discussions about the dual role of IT in organizational change and innovation, few studies have examined the roles of IT in strategic renewals. Most critically, it is not clear whether and how the dual role of IT affects the choice of specific strategic renewal path and its subsequent implementation, and how IT can be used to deal with the disruption brought by e-commerce innovation by traditional brick-and-mortar firms in their strategic transformation. In the remainder of this study, we address these critical questions using a comparative case study from the strategic renewal perspective.

## 3. RESEARCH DESIGN AND METHODOLOGY

## 3.1 The Case Study

The research questions and background articulated in the previous sections support a case study methodology for this research, given the nascent nature of the focal phenomenon and the lack of extensive academic studies. While there are many different methods for conducting case studies, we selected a theorydriven exploratory case study approach for the following reasons. First, this study explores how traditional offline firms manage the e-commerce disruption through strategic renewal, and how IT assists strategic renewal in this process. An exploratory case study is preferred when addressing such “how” and “why” questions because of its holistic and descriptive nature [49]. Second, strategic renewal of traditional offline firms occurs in complex and dynamic processes. Case study helps researchers explore such complex and contextual phenomena, explicate key concepts and relationships from intricate processes, and determine the potential significance of those concepts and relationship in the given context [50].

We designed this study with two comparative cases based on our prior knowledge of the firms that were traditional offline firms but undertook strategic renewals toward integrated online–offline business models. By exploring their different IT capabilities and corresponding choice of strategic renewal paths, we hope to explicate and articulate a clear theoretical framework about the relationships among IT capabilities, resources, and strategic renewal. Following the case study principle of Yin [49], each case is conducted as an independent experimentation, and the process of strategic renewal and its important dimensions are analyzed separately. Then, the two cases are put together and compared to develop a theoretical understanding underpinned by contrasting and complementing case evidence.

To select case firms for this comparative study, we set three criteria for inclusion. First, the case firms must be traditional offline firms that successfully adapt to the new e-commerce environment with integrated online– offline business models. Second, because of our focus on strategic renewal paths and the roles of IT in strategic renewals, the case firms should have contrasting strategic renewal paths and contrasting IT resources and capabilities that impact different strategic renewal paths. Third, the case firms must be willing to collaborate and provide extensive access to their managers and employees. The two comparative case firms eventually selected are JEANSWEST, which manufactures and sells casual clothing, and COMAGIC, which manufactures and sells children’s furniture. Both case firms were traditional offline firms and both suffered failures in their initial attempts to develop e-commerce operations. Although they followed different paths for carrying out strategic renewal, both eventually accomplished strategic renewal and became leading online–offline integrated firms in their own markets. ## 3.3 Data Analysis

In the data analysis phase, we followed criteria and stages proposed by Eisenhardt [51] and Yin [49]. First, three researchers coded the transcripts from the two firms and identified core concepts and key patterns related to our research questions using a two-stage coding approach: the initial coding stage and the axial coding stage. The initial coding stage allowed researchers to begin to describe and label key events in the strategic renewal process [49]. The research questions, which guided the coding schemes and codes assignment, focused on how the two case firms transform from traditional offline firms into integrated online–offline firms. The axial coding stage was used to tie together the codes defined in the initial coding stage into organized sets, themes, or constructs [52,53] and was often used to identify the emerging patterns and further develop the theory [51,54,55]. At the end of each stage, the coding results were compared between three researchers, and as disagreements occurred, discussions followed to improve consistency. We followed two criteria to resolve the disagreements. First one is to go back to theoretical definitions. Disagreements often occur when theoretical definitions of codes are not clear. Therefore, researchers would improve or redefine definitions of codes to ensure all coders have a consistent understanding of each code. The second one is to invite the third party to provide neutral judgment. Other researchers who were not influenced by this research or interviewees in case firms were asked for providing suggestions about the disagreements. The discussions to resolve disagreements of coding continued until the disagreements were solved.

Second, based on the initial coding results, three researchers identified emerging associations in key concepts and patterns and formed an integrative theorizing framework. For replication and theoretical saturation [49], we went back and forth between the extant literature and the transcripts. Finally, we identified two unique strategic renewal paths carried out by the two case firms, and we used other internal events to test the emerging theoretical framework. The analysis concluded when theoretical saturation occurred<sup>2</sup>, that is, new sources of data and new rounds of analysis did not change emergent interpretations. If theoretical saturation failed to emerge, the steps above were repeated. Unrelated or isolated events and concepts were dropped from the final evidence set for developing this manuscript but kept in the case database for future analysis. ## 4. MAIN FINDINGS

## 4.1 Initial Attempts and Failures

The e-commerce history of the two case firms helps shed lights on their strategic renewal choices and subsequent paths. Our interviews suggest that, to survive the drastic market changes resulting from shifting toward e-commerce, both firms decided early-on to transform by developing online business, and both suffered significant failures. The two firms shared a commonality in that they both attempted building online business using the experience, resources, and capabilities they had with their offline operations. For example, JEANSWEST assigned staff to establish an e-commerce department who had previously been working on traditional marketing tasks. Then, the firm signed a cooperation agreement with Tmall and began online operations. COMAGIC ventured into e-commerce by putting some professional photos of its offline products on its website and started selling offline products online. However, these simple attempts at e-commerce brought enormous problems.

On the “Singles Day” of 2010, JEANSWEST’s online shopping team of six people received hundreds of thousands of orders from all over the country. This created instant chaos and problems in local warehouses, such as insufficient packing box inventories, order date confusion, inconsistency between orders and local inventory, shipment delays, loss of express items, and damages during transportation. Delayed delivery, mismatched items, and damaged products caused strong dissatisfaction among customers, and a huge number of negative comments were posted online that ruined the firm’s online store ratings on Tmall. Instead of earning profits, JEANSWEST paid a heavy price for its online sales performance that bottomed out as almost 60% of customers demanded refund or returned their goods.

COMAGIC, on the “Singles Day” of 2011, experienced similar problems to JEANSWEST’s experience in the previous year. COMAGIC received online orders amounting to almost 1 million RMB in a day. Because of factory capacity limitations, deliveries were delayed excessively by 60 to 90 days. Packaging difficulties for large pieces of furniture caused damages during transportation, and slow after-sales service responses caused backlogs in unresolved customer complaints. COMAGIC took 2 months to address the various complaints, with a return rate of more than 50%. Additionally, Tmall shut down COMAGIC’s online site because its rating dropped below the minimum required level. This created a direct financial loss of more than 1 million RMB and a plummeting brand reputation.

The setbacks JEANSWEST and COMAGIC suffered in their early e-commerce attempts suggest that it is dangerous for firms to run e-commerce in the same way as they run traditional channels. A painful lesson the management in the two firms learned was that e-commerce was not just another distribution channel. To successfully move into the e-commerce market, a strategic transformation based on the characteristics of ecommerce becomes a necessity rather than an option.

## 4.2 Path to Incremental Renewal: The JEANSWEST Case

## 4.2.1 Problem Analysis

With the initial e-commerce setbacks, JEANSWEST senior management reflected on the failure. By reviewing customer complaints and problems raised by its own staff and partners, JEANSWEST discovered that traditional operations were completely a mismatch with the new characteristics of the e-commerce market. For instance, online orders were characterized by fragmentation, fluctuation, and region diversity, whereas the traditional offline operational system was divided based on regions, which led to frequent out of stock orders where the items could have been allocated from other regional warehouses or stores. This had caused considerable losses in orders and sales. The CIO explained

“For example, when a customer in Beijing places an order for an item that happens to be out of stock but there is sufficient stock in Guangdong Province, warehouses in Guangdong could not simply take the order because it also needs to consider whether it is able to fulfill the demands of its own regional physical stores. It is troublesome to make trans-regional transfers because things always go wrong. But for online customers, what they care about is timely delivery rather than the place of shipment. That’s why we failed to meet the demand.”

JEANSWEST initially had simply offered offline products online, which caused some customers to stop instore purchases or to claim refund because they later found the same products online at lower prices. In addition, many customers stated that they would be unlikely to purchase products from the firm if their online inquiries were not met with timely responses. As stated by the director of online sales

“What’s most important for an online store are service, convenience, and accuracy, followed by the store image. In a physical store, clothes are simply displayed, but for online stores, it is not that simple anymore. Instead, it requires delightful and pleasing pictures of models wearing those clothes, and products must show the brand style and appeal to the masses. A control mechanism that deals with customer response is also critical. We have to respond quickly to customer demands and consider after-sales services.”

## 4.2.2 Resource-Complementing Incremental Strategic Renewal

To adapt to the new market environment, JEANSWEST realized that e-commerce should be considered strategically in conjunction with changes to products, operations, and services. For example, the selection of clothing items should reflect the styles that caters to the needs of online customers, instead of simply posting popular offline items online. In the area of services, they must consider the characteristics of online orders, including order fragmentation, frequent fluctuation, and regional diversity, with redesigning and reinforcing the online–offline integrated system.

After the analysis, JEANSWEST’s senior management concluded that the firm’s core advantage was in its capability to handle its enormous and complicated operational systems (including retail stores, logistics systems, suppliers, and customer services), which highly depended on its powerful IT systems and infrastructure. As one of

#

the top 500 companies in manufacturing with advanced and extensive IT operations in China, JEANSWEST had introduced its point of sales and financial management systems in 1996, with more than 400 system modules developed since then to provide full support for business operations and more than 2,200 physical stores. The IT department of JEANSWEST had approximately 200 employees with experience in systems and application development.

To leverage the advantages of existing IT resources and avoid significant operational financial risks as a result of radical changes, the senior management decided to take an incremental strategy of transformation: maintaining the existing offline operational system, accelerating the development of its online channels and systems by leveraging offline advantages, promoting new and existing resource complementarity using IT, and supporting online–offline collaboration and integration. We call this as an incremental resource-complementing renewal strategy. Resource complementarity reflects that each resource offers unique characteristics, and when combined with the other, the two compensate for each other’s weaknesses and reinforce each other’s strengths. IT is used as an enabler to support the collaboration between existing and new resources and help control the risk of operational conflicts between the two categories of resources. Specifically, resource complementarity is characterized by the following aspects.

## Building Resource-Complementing R&D

Product is one of the important strategic attributes of organization which need to be refreshed or renewed to adapt to e-commerce disruptive innovation. In JEANSWEST’s traditional model, products were designed mainly based on subjective experience of its individual designers. Launching a new product needed a longer period, and thus, only a limited number of new products were offered for the next quarter in offline sales. Existing products clearly could not satisfy e-commerce demand for diverse and personalized styles.

To solve this problem, JEANSWEST used the online channel to collect data generated by customers to compensate for the weakness of offline data and took advantage of the offline channel for face-to-face communication with customers to see what product or service they expect to buy from JEANSWEST. Specifically, for product design, JEANSWEST took full advantage of the convenience of information collection capability through its online platform to collect customer comments and information about rapid developing trends. At the same time, on-going face-to-face sales in the offline channels offered an opportunity for JEANSWEST to probe customers preference in fitting, selecting, and purchasing, and data were collected and uploaded to the central data analytics system. This strategy of “pursuing sales volume online by in-depth studying offline” enabled JEANSWEST to exploit its online and offline resource advantages to collect customer information for R&D. This significantly accelerated new product development cycle, as described by the CIO

“In traditional way, we could collect little information from our distributors. Online channel has its dominant advantage in quickly collecting large volumes of data from online orders. But you may still not understand why customers made certain choice. Therefore, we reached out to our customers in offline stores to have a face-toface conversation. This creative integration helped us quickly and accurately know customer demand.”

The following describes an exemplary case that reflected how complementing offline and online resources helped product R&D in JEANSWEST. Several years ago, the R&D team believed that the colors dark blue, army green, and yellow were not popular among customers because dark blue clothing was reminiscent of the revolutionary period; army green stood for a military uniform; yellow implied a winged insect in northern China; and pinstriped clothes were similar to those worn by patients in hospitals. Hence, none were considered as in alignment with current market demands. Thus, JEANSWEST reduced designing and manufacturing clothing with these colors. This situation changed when JEANSWEST established the data collection and analytics system that enabled the firm to integrate customer data from online and offline. Sales data analysis suggested that younger generations were increasingly comfortable with army green elements. To investigate why this color had once again gained popularity, JEANSWEST reached out and polled customers when they were selecting clothing in offline stores. Based on an analysis of the online and offline data, JEANSWEST perfected its clothing lines by adding army green elements to buttons and edges to cater to the customers’ tastes.

Moreover, differentiating products designed for catering online or offline demand complemented each other and contributed together to JEANSWEST’s service capability. JEANSWEST’s director of online sales explained

“We try to take advantage of both online and offline resources to diversify product categories so that online clothing products meet online customers’ demands for high cost-effectiveness while offline product designs satisfy offline customers’ demands for better service experience. This helps us improve overall service to customers in both channels. ”

## Developing Resource-Complementing Operations

Channel operation is another important strategic attribute of organization in strategic renewal for its immediate impact on how customers experience products and services. Different channels were designed for different customers. JEANSWEST focused on building channel complementarity in which each channel took its advantage to serve differentiating organizational and market needs, and used IT to accomplish dual channel collaboration.

First, JEANSWEST operated the online channel to realize high-volume product sales, which was difficult to accomplish with the offline channel. For instance, JEANSWEST established its own online sales platform and joined a major third-party online sales platform, Tmall. Whenever a new product was launched, the online platforms would offer nationwide promotions, thus making a quick “trial and error” possible because of the short purchaseto-feedback cycle. With the assistance of a backend sales analytics system, JEANSWEST could quickly analyze the “trial and error” activities and predict their popularity or analyze what fashion elements were preferred by customers. JEANSWEST would significantly accelerate offline promotions and operations by better predicting market demand through the trial marketing online. Thus, overstock could be avoided and turnover rate be improved. The manager of industry and trade department stated

“With online–offline collaboration, we have built an efficient sales and distribution network that boasts a high allocation-sale ratio of over 90%, which means at least 90 pieces of 100 allocated items can be sold by sales terminals. This is important for a firm since it not only reduces inventory but helps reduce cost.”

Second, JEANSWEST operated existing offline networks to satisfy regional online orders and after-sales services. For example, in northern China, with clear seasonal variations, offline stores mainly offered seasonal clothing because they could provide quality services, and they could also serve as backend suppliers for online channels. When online customers returned products or claimed a refund due to quality or other issues, the nearest local flagship stores could facilitate the process. As a result, online–offline complementarity offered better aftersales services to all customers. JEANSWEST’s CEO explained

“We transform offline stores, particularly self-supported stores, to let them serve as backend suppliers for online channels. As regional online orders fluctuate, regional offline stores which have adequate inventory can quickly supply to support online sales. Self-supporting stores also serve for customers by offering after-sales services.”

## Designing Resource-Complementing Supply Chain

Supply chain is essential for the provision of products and services for both online and offline markets. JEANSWEST’s initial failure indicated that traditional supply chain could not satisfy the e-commerce characteristics of order fragmentation, frequent fluctuation, and region diversity.

To meet the new demand of online orders, JEANSWEST transformed offline production lines into those suitable for smaller batches and multiple products to better support online demands. To improve production efficiency with the integrated supplier management system, JEANSWEST implemented rapid feedback of online order information to various suppliers.

Second, JEANSWEST transformed traditional supply chains to improve the supply capability of offline stores as the regional warehouses to meet online dynamic demands. Products successful in online presales were formall launched in online stores, and they were also distributed to various offline stores. Once an order was placed by a customer online, JEANSWEST delivered the products to customer from the nearest regional warehouse by a “distributed delivery scheme.” In the case of a stock-out in the nearest regional warehouse, the products would be shipped from a local physical store or another regional warehouse depending on availability. In essence, JEANSWEST had successfully transformed all its offline stores into smaller distributed warehouses to support the highly fluctuating e-commerce channels, as described by the CIO

“Now, every offline stores can be a little warehouse, and if one is out of stock or the distributed warehouse is out of stock to satisfy the online customers, we can fulfill the orders from the nearest offline stores. We have 2,200 offline stores, which means we can do better in responding to online fluctuations. As our information systems have linked almost all offline and online stores, you can exchange your products anywhere.”

Third, the distributed warehouse management system also conducted automatic intelligent analysis and offered replenishment suggestions based on historical demand for timely replenishment of offline store stock. For rapid delivery, JEANSWEST introduced third-party logistics companies and implemented full-route tracking of item delivery by using Global Positioning System (GPS) technology. For after-sales services, a central information system for management and control replaced the organizational structure under which regional branch stores acted independently. A customer-oriented incentive mechanism based on online customers’ demands for services was designed to establish inter-unit cooperation and eliminate the barriers in transregional and transunit collaborations. Offline regional flagship stores served as distributing warehouses supporting the highly fluctuating e-commerce market and also served as after-sales service stations to improve customer satisfaction.

## 4.2.3 IT as Operand Resource

Our interviews with respondents and observations during our visits suggest that JEANSWEST’s IT capability and resources contributed significantly to this incremental resource-complementing renewal as operand resources – supportive or enabling resources in helping JEANSWEST integrate other existing and new resources and make both of the two categories of resources complementary to each other. This is manifested in at least two aspects: IT-enabled collaboration between existing and new resources and IT-assisted control of risk of operational conflicts between the two categories of resources.

First, our case evidence shows that IT enabled better collaboration between online and offline resources. By taking advantage of the existing system modules, JEANSWEST developed an e-commerce management information system that not only supported basic online operations such as ordering, logistics tracking, and aftersales feedback but also coordinated e-commerce and offline business activities. JEANSWEST integrated the system interfaces between the e-commerce management information system and the existing information system supporting offline business by linking multiple regional warehouse management information system to ensure orders would be allocated according to proximity once placed. Meanwhile, a backend supply management system monitored online and offline sales information and devised optimal allocation plans. As the CIO described

“The integrated information system enables better management control and coordination, so we won’t have to worry about product demand, shortage, and mistakes anymore. With the help of the information systems that control all these business procedures, all we need to do is to operate. Compared with manual operations, it is easier for the system to integrate all those processes.”

Equally important, IT assisted JEANSWEST to control risk by quickly identifying risk factors in online and offline channel operations and, with timely feedback, mobilizing countermeasures by corresponding business units using the real-time IT capabilities. For instance, a real-time backend data analytic process allowed JEANSWEST to rapidly identify troubled online sales webpages, provide immediate feedback, and trace the problem back to the responsible unit. That unit could then carry out corrective actions in various systems, for example, identifying whether it was a wrong stock number or an incorrect price, and making a quick correction. Such IT-enabled risk management and error feedback process assisted JEANSWEST’s IT and business teams with rapid risk control actions. JEANSWEST’s senior manager of IT department specifically mentioned the important role IT played in risk control:

“The risks confronting us are not from a single module anymore because of higher level of collaboration; if there is something wrong with one module, the processes that follow will all suffer. So, we need an information system to take control. For instance, excessive time allocation not only leads to the risk of shortages, but it also affects logistics and delivery, and stock-out on the online systems also causes ordering problems. As a result, we pay particular attention to strengthening control of the information system so that once the stock number reaches the threshold, the suppliers are notified immediately for timely allocation. This type of communication of information really matters.”

## 4.2.4 JEANSWEST Case Summary

With IT-enabled resource complementarity, JEANSWEST underwent successful incremental strategic renewal by refreshing important strategic attributes of the organization, including R&D, channel operations and supply chain, and transformed itself into an online-offline integrated e-commerce enterprise by 2012. JEANSWEST reached a sales record of 57.76 million RMB on the “Singles Day” event of 2012 and a record high of 82 million RMB online sales in 2013, retaining its status as the sales champion of all casual wear brands on Tmall for two successive years. JEANSWEST’s successful transformation increased its overall sales by 35% in 2013 compared to 2012.

What JEANSWEST had accomplished is essentially pioneered a path of incremental renewal for traditional firms to successfully develop online business by taking advantage of the rich resources endowment in the offline business, and the key to this success is the complementarity of the resources between the offline and the online businesses. Proposition 1: An incremental resource-complementing strategic renewal approach that takes advantage of existing offline resources to complement new online resources is more appropriate for traditional brick-andmortar firms endowed with rich offline resources and strong IT capabilities to transform into online-offline integrated enterprises.

The case analysis also suggests that IT plays an important supporting role in this incremental resourcecomplementing renewal as an operand resource by enabling collaboration and coordination between new and existing resources and controlling the operational and financial risks resulting from conflicts between the two categories of resources. JEANSWEST’s renewed product R&D, channel operations, and supply chain were all enabled by its strong IT capabilities. These IT capabilities also enabled JEANSWEST to expand online business progressively, compensating for deficiencies in the offline operations while injecting new resources to the online operations by exploiting existing resource advantages. These analyses and discussions lead us to propose:

Proposition 2: An incremental resource-complementing strategic renewal is more likely to succeed when IT serves as an operand resource in enabling resource coordination and controlling operational risks in the strategic renewal process.

## 4.3 Path to Discontinuous Transformation: The COMAGIC Case

## 4.3.1 Problem Analysis

The analysis by the senior management of COMAGIC on the failure of their early ventures in e-commerce revealed that most of the customer complaints were caused by product breakage, delayed delivery, and inadequate after-sales service. COMAGIC works in the durable furniture industry, and their products require a relatively long production cycle. In conventional retail model, the firm would produce a finished product in approximately 2 months after receiving an order, and then arrange for delivery. When COMAGIC put their offline products up for sale online, they discovered that the online customers frequently asked for delivery as soon as in 4 or 5 days after an order was made. Some customers simply could not tolerate the long delivery times, and cancelled their orders within 3 weeks after placing them. Furthermore, as the delivery sites spanned over a wide range of localities, the product delivery changed from full-truckload shipping to less-than-truckload (LTL) shipping. This resulted in many products being damaged during the transportation process because of bumps and jolts, which led to a large number of complaints and bad reviews from customers. The vice president of COMAGIC said

“As our speed of delivery was too slow, and the rate of product damage during transportation was very high, this had led to a very large number of after-sales problems and complaints, as well as return rates that exceeded 50%; we were clearly, completely unmatched with the fast pace of e-commerce. Our Tmall DSR score had fallen to 3.6, which resulted in a warning from Tmall. We thus had no choice but to shut down our online operations.”

For large physical products such as furniture, after-sales service is also extremely important. However, in their early ventures into e-commerce, COMAGIC had not been able to resolve this particular issue. As a result, even when the customers did receive undamaged furniture products, they were still dissatisfied as they frequently encountered difficulties in assembling the products.

## 4.3.2 Discontinuous Resource-Substituting Strategic Transformation

COMAGIC was relatively small in scale and only had a little more than 100 offline stores prior to their venture into e-commerce. Many of their products had also been produced by outsourcing to other manufacturers. Therefore, the offline resources of the firm were relatively light. In their failed venture into e-commerce, COMAGIC also discovered tremendous opportunities alongside the problems they encountered, and recognized from a strategic perspective that e-commerce was the future. Hence, the senior management made a strategic decision to focus their future business development on the e-commerce market.

COMAGIC first performed a comprehensive online customer study and contracted a consulting firm that specialized in online customer analysis to help improve the understanding of customers. The study found that in the traditional market for children’s furniture, the most important aspects for customers were brand, followed by environmental-friendliness, functionality, safety, price, and, finally, the level of service. However, the requirements of the online market were almost the exact opposite, as the most important aspect for customers was the service, followed by environmental-friendliness and safety, then functionality, and finally brand and price. Although COMAGIC had already been endorsed by internationally well-known animation brands, the delays in production, tardiness of delivery, product damage during distribution, and inadequacies in after-sales service resulted in a massive gap between the customer expectations and the customer service experience.

It was clear to the senior management team after the market study and reflection that COMAGIC must refocus its resources and efforts on the online market in its search for new growth opportunities, and drastic strategic and operational transformations were necessary to survive in the new era of e-commerce. In the end, what the management team undertook was a strategic renewal we call resource-substitution discontinuous transformation, in which original offline resources are replaced by or reconstituted toward the new online initiatives and operations in all major areas of the business functions. Resource substitution includes two important processes: the process of identifying new market demands and abandoning existing ineffective resources as well as the process of reconstituting existing resources to adapt to the new market demands. According to our case evidence, the discontinuous resource-substituting strategic transformation happened in COMAGIC can be characterized in three important strategic aspects: product design, customer service, and supply chain.

## Resource-Substitutions in Product Design

To track and identify the changing demand of customers in online purchase, COMAGIC used online instant message technologies such as Aliwangwang on the Tmall platform to directly interact with online customers and collected their reviews of products with the integrated online platform data collection technology. COMAGIC found that online customers were more attracted by products with fast delivery and high quality, and existing product designs that required the long production cycle, professional assembling, and full-truckload shipping could not satisfy these demands. COMAGIC stopped the production of traditional furniture products and tried out a number of innovations in redesigning their products to satisfy the online market.

The first thing COMAGIC did was to transform their furniture products from traditional complex structure and limited variety to new standardized structures and personalized appearances. The structural standardization refers to the fact that all of the products were produced according to the same standardized structure, which greatly increased the rate of production while decreasing the costs of production. The personalization of design refers to the fact that customers could customize the color and other design features of the furniture products based on their preferences. The customers may choose a cartoon-based design online, or send their own pictures to COMAGIC’s customer service through instant messaging services. COMAGIC’s customer service will then pass the order information that includes these requests to the back-end system, which then directly passes this information to specialized industrial printers. This amalgamation between structured production and personalized customization creates significant levels of personalization, rapid production and delivery, and high levels of cost-effectiveness, as stated by the CEO

“We did a lot to redesign of our products. Many traditional furniture designers were moved out and new talents were recruited. Most of them knew e-commerce demand well or have worked in online companies. These new designers were asked to analyze customer data, including product reviews and complaints, and generate new and innovative ideas. ”

Another innovation was to transform the assembly of furniture product from professional jobs to “Do It Yourself” (DIY). One of the more severe issues that COMAGIC encountered during their early ventures into the online retail market was that the customers were unable to assemble the specialized furniture. Delivering the furniture as fully assembled units would have resulted in extremely high costs and high likelihood of product damage. To resolve the twofold difficulties, COMAGIC made a design innovation in the assembly of their products: joints that originally needed to be connected with a specialized glue were instead designed as structures that could be assembled with one or two screws, and the assembly of the products were allowed to transcend their original limitations, such that the furniture panels could be assembled into various configurations. This allowed customers to build various types of furniture in a DIY “building block” fashion and to try out different configurations and styles. This effectively resolved the product assembly issue and also greatly increased the enthusiasm of customers in purchasing these products, as the assembly of these products could be seen as a fun family activity. The director of marketing explained how this innovation was generated

“The idea of redesigning the assembly of children furniture came from our customers. We tracked the feedback of customers who bought our product by using Aliwangwang. Many customers told us the hardware accessories were too complicated for them to assemble themselves. Some of them asked if there was an easier way to DIY. These suggestions inspired our product designers and they found that to redesign the furniture with a ‘building block’ fashion might improve the product. Therefore, we replaced all complicated hardware accessories with newly-designed screws, and changed the structure of the product to make it easier to be assembled with these screws.”

## Resource-Substitutions in Customer Services

Based on data mining and analysis of online orders, COMAGIC found that the main groups of customers were mothers born in the 1980s, who had been rapidly migrating toward online platforms for their routine purchases. They had high demand for high-quality customer service. To meet this demand, COMAGIC replaced their conventional model of service that was mainly based on service in offline stores with the digitalized model in which customers could search, query, experience, and request for after-sales service through online platforms. COMAGIC also added popular online retail platforms such as Tmall, JD.com, and VIPshop.com to allow full access to information from the Internet, so that customers are able to obtain information on COMAGIC’s products from any of these channels and purchase products in a convenient manner, as explained by the CEO

“We discovered that the customer groups of the various third-party e-commerce platforms are different from each other, and also overlapping with each other. For example, some people may prefer to shop on Vipshop.com, while others may frequently visit JD.com. Some customers will base their purchasing on the promotions and discounts being offered on the various platforms, and simply use the platform that is offering a promotion. Therefore, by having a wide web coverage, we are able to expand our brand publicity, and also allow customers to come into contact with our products through multiple channels.”

COMAGIC also developed a new online-to-offline (O2O) service model to improve customer experience. This was inspired by customer data collected from the online platforms. The characteristics of furniture products make it such that even if a customer has browsed the product online, she would still want to see the product in person in a physical store before making a purchase. Therefore, to improve customer satisfaction and the conversion rate, COMAGIC closed their original offline stores and built new offline showrooms to provide comprehensive services that included product exhibition, hands-on experience for customers, product comparisons, and room layout inquiries; these showrooms also took on post-sales services, thus achieving localized service. In addition, this service model also increased the rate of repeat purchases and customer loyalty by providing derivative products and services such as free measurements of the air quality in rooms. The vice president of COMAGIC stated d

“Traditional offline stores were used as the main sales channel, instead of a service stores. To provide better customer service and avoid channel conflicts, we cancelled contracts with all offline franchisers and instead built self-supporting offline showrooms. Online customers are invited to visit the offline showrooms nearby and enjoy a better on-site experience.”

## Resource-Substitution in Supply Chain Operations

To resolve the problems related to delayed delivery and product damage caused by LTL shipping, COMAGIC restructured its supply chain operations mainly in three areas, with the help of an integrated supply chain management (SCM) information system. The first was to redesign the supply chain. Because all aspects of the production cycle were outsourced, COMAGIC selected two strategic suppliers to be exclusive suppliers of its products. New factory managers who had experience in dealing with fluctuating online orders were recruited to manage the production of newly designed products. COMAGIC’s interactions with these strategic suppliers were conducted primarily through its integrated IS. The quantity of stock in the warehouses was shared in a timely manner with the online platform to avoid merchandise shortages. The integrated IS were also used to monitor dynamic market demand. Once order fluctuation occurred, suppliers were informed timely to prepare raw materials and production capacity.

The second was to restructure the production model. To fulfill the twofold requirements of mass production and customization, the suppliers were given standardized designs to allow them to produce parts on a large scale, whereas the panels were printed according to the customized photographs of the customers, to produce products with varying appearances. The integrated SCM and online management system coordinated to support customers’ personalized pictures being directly passed to the specialized industrial printers. It only required 7 minutes to print out personalized designs on standardized furniture panels. The vice president of COMAGIC explained the reason of restructuring production model

“The production cycles of conventional furniture were relatively long, and the fastest manufacturers in the industry would still require a minimum of 20 days, which was awkward for the e-commerce market. As online customers have high expectations in terms of product delivery, the firm basically has to have ready stock. However, the production of ready stock would put a lot of pressure on the suppliers, since no one knows what the customers will order. Therefore, by improving the designs of our products, we have created a new model of production, by producing standardized furniture structural modules on a large scale, while performing customizations on the appearances of these modules based on customer demands. This production model is much more compatible with the characteristics of customers on the Internet.”

In the third aspect, speedy third-party logistics were introduced. COMAGIC switched to home appliance-level packaging for their products to improve resistance to shock and pressure, which alleviated the problem of product damage during LTL shipping. COMAGIC even had specialized tests performed on the packaging, in which products with the new packaging were dropped from a 2-meter high position. The vice president said

“We have made packaging of the highest grade, and performed tests that showed that 2-meter falls essentially caused no damage at all. Therefore, we no longer have to worry about our customers receiving a broken product no matter which logistics firm we use. The word-of-mouth comments on the Internet about our new packaging have been extremely positive, and our boss was really moved when he read the reviews on the Internet.”

## 4.3.3 IT as Operant Resource

COMAGIC transformed itself from a brick-and-mortar business into an e-commerce firm through digitalizing its key business assets and operations and setting e-commerce at the core of its business strategy and operations. This required all business procedures, organizational structures, and resources to be developed around its ecommerce strategy, and offline operational resources that had become ineffective were removed, so that the appropriate digitalized operational resources could be introduced for restructuring the mode of operations. Thus, the decision to digitalize the business essentially affected all of COMAGIC’s resource allocations and capacity building. In this case, IT was no longer a simple enabling resource but instead had induced the strategic change in the organization. In other words, IT acted as an operant resource that played a trigger in helping COMAGIC rapidly replace traditional offline resources with new product designs and reconstituted customer services and supply chain. The triggering role of IT in COMAGIC’s discontinuous resource-substituting strategic transformation was manifested mainly in two aspects: IT embedded business to strengthen market agility, and IT innovated business to accelerate the market-oriented transformation.

First, “IT embedded business” means external and internal information technologies were used to transform traditional business to be digitalized business. When COMAGIC decided to set e-commerce as its primary strategic direction, they first analyzed the technological infrastructure for setting up their business online, and the technological viability of moving toward this direction, as described by the COMAGIC CEO

“We wanted to sell furniture on the Internet, so it was very important to have a clear idea of how we were going to implement all of our (business) procedures.”

During its analysis, COMAGIC found that the most mature third-party platform Tmall had already become the main retail platform relied upon by online firms; just like an offline store, all business processes such as ordering, payment, cash collection, statistics, and post-sales services could be implemented through this platform. However, it was not enough to simply migrate the offline retail processes onto the Internet. For this purpose, COMAGIC constructed front- and back-end IS that complemented the online platform. The front-end IS included the online order management system, the official firm web store linked to Tmall, and the mobile data management system. The back-end system mainly consisted of supporting systems such as ERP, customer relationship management (CRM), and their financial management system. COMAGIC had modified standard IS based on the needs of its ecommerce strategy and had added a few functionalities that were in line with the characteristics of furniture products. For example, the barcodes on furniture parts were added to the system, and this information was shared with the supply chain IS to enable COMAGIC to effectively control the distribution of parts and meet the speedy delivery requirements of online customers. The fully digitalized operations and real-time data sharing made COMAGIC more market agile and triggered further changes in its organizational attributes, as described by the vice president of COMAGIC

“Online orders are always fragmented and fluctuated. At that time, we believed it was very important for us to rapidly collect order information, send the information to business units, and coordinate different units or partners to cope with the information. A fully digitalized operation system could help us realize real-time information transmission. This also helped us recognize market dynamics in time and guided us change or remove inappropriate products or services to better satisfy our customers.”

Second, IT affects discontinuous strategic transformation as an operant resource by triggering innovations in an organization. As mentioned above, disruptive technologies such as e-commerce triggered the radical transformation of COMAGIC from the traditional offline business model to the online–offline integrated business model. In addition, our case evidence shows that, IT had induced systemic innovations that helped COMAGIC rapidly recognize external market changes, accurately understand customer demand, replace ineffective resources, and redesign market-oriented organizational attributes. Just as the examples above have shown, by using its data analytics system, COMAGIC found customers wanted to experience furniture products in a physical store before making actual purchases. This triggered COMAGIC to convert its offline stores into showrooms that provided

product exhibition and experience for customers.

## 4.3.4 COMAGIC Case Summary

COMAGIC successfully implemented the discontinuous strategic renewal with resource substitution by restructuring and reconstituting their resources using IT as a strategic catalyst. After 1 year of learning and adjustments, COMAGIC reentered Tmall in 2012 and generated more than 10 million RMB in sales on the “Singles Day” that year. This was followed by the 28 million RMB in sales on the “Singles Day” in 2013. By focusing their strategy and resources on the online market, COMAGIC has become the leading firm in the children’s furniture industry, with sales exceeding 250 million RMB in 2015.

The success of COMAGIC’s transformation from a traditional manufacturing and retailing business into an online–offline integrated manufacturing and retailing business demonstrates that there are multiple paths to strategic renewal. In circumstances where there are no adequate resources to support both offline and online operations, traditional firms can consider the discontinuous resource-substituting strategic transformation approach to move into the e-commerce marketplace. In COMAGIC’s strategic transformation toward e-commerce, the management used IT as a strategic catalyst to trigger the discontinuous strategic renewal featuring resource substitutions. The strategic effect of IT was manifested by IT acting as a type of operant resource: IT was embedded in new product designs and operations, and IT triggered innovations in operations and services. COMAGIC reconstituted its products, customer services, and supply chain operations on the basis of the IT catalyzed and triggered business innovations. ## 4.4 A Cross-Case Analysis

The two case firms offer an intriguing contrast and deserve a cross-case analysis. Our findings suggest that the primary factor that forced both firms to transform from traditional brick-and-mortar offline businesses to integrated online–offline businesses is the disruptive impact on their respective industry brought by the emergence of e-commerce and associated changes in customer demand for products and services and purchasing behavior. What is interesting to both theory and practice is the fact that the two firms chose different strategic renewal path to manage this disruptive challenge. However, as our case evidence shows, although the resource-complementing and the resource-substituting renewals are significantly different in tactics in terms of how the firms managed and reconstituted existing resources, in the end, both firms accomplished the goals of refreshing or replacing of important strategic attributes of their organizations, thus succeeding in their strategic renewals amid their own unique environment. Our case evidence shows that the key difference between the two firms was the pretransformation resource endowment in each firm. Their initial failures in entering the e-commerce market led the management teams in the two firms to figure out a strategic renewal path based on the strengths and weaknesses of their unique strategic resources. Endowed with a large number of physical stores that are supported by strong IT infrastructure and capabilities but stale product offerings and silos of distribution, JEANSWEST chose resource-complementing renewal and incrementally refreshed its three key strategic attributes: R&D, operations, and supply chain. In contrast, endowed with exclusive intellectual properties and licenses but limited production and distribution capabilities, COMAGIC chose resource-substituting renewal, rapidly replaced ineffective resources, and renewed its three key strategic attributes: product design, customer service, and supply chain.

Although the two strategic renewal strategies eventually led to similar success for the two firms in their respective industry and marketplace, the incremental resource-complementing strategic renewal and the discontinuous resource-substituting transformation have demonstrated key differentiating characteristics in at least two dimensions.

First, two strategic renewal paths are different in the way of managing existing offline resources. Resource-

#

complementing renewal is accomplished by innovatively adapting existing resources to be complementary to new online resources. For example, in the case of JEANSWEST, existing offline supply channel networks were integrated to become the regional warehouses and distribution centers for online customers. However, in the case of COMAGIC, few existing resources owned by the firm were effective to be used for the new online strategy, and if some were, such as the physical stores, they often became distractions for the firm from focusing on developing the new online strategy. COMAGIC eventually abandoned almost all existing offline resources or radically reconstituted them into new resources toward the new online market.

Second, IT resources and capabilities play significantly different roles in the two strategic renewal paths. For the incremental resource-complementing strategic renewal, one key factor is to improve collaboration between the traditional offline and the new online resources, and to control the risk of operational conflicts between the two categories of resources. Therefore, IT mainly plays a supporting role to progressively integrate the two categories of strategic resources. For example, JEANSWEST developed an e-commerce management information system that not only supported online operations such as ordering, logistics tracking, and after-sales feedback but also coordinated and integrated e-commerce operations and offline business activities. For discontinuous resourcesubstituting strategic transformation, one key factor is to digitalize key business assets and operations, and substitute e-commerce as the core of business strategy and operations. Therefore, IT mainly serves as a trigger to radical innovations in strategic organizational attributes. For example, COMAGIC reconstituted its entire business by redesigning its product lines based on the need of e-commerce market, digitalizing all business processes, implementing IT-based production systems that produce standardized furniture with personalized features, and installing front-end IS that linked to Tmall to collect real-time data for rapid product and service changes and improvement.

## 5. DISCUSSION

Based on a comparative case analysis of two Chinese firms, we proposed two distinct strategic renewal paths for traditional brick-and-mortar firms to transform themselves to manage effectively disruptive technological or operational innovations such as e-commerce. The first is an incremental resource-complementing renewal which takes advantage of the abundant offline resources to complement and support the new online business and in which IT primarily plays an operand role that enables the integration of existing and new resources. The second is a discontinuous resource-substituting strategic transformation which replaces traditional ineffective resources and reconstitutes strategic structures and operational processes toward the new online market and in which IT primary plays an operant role that catalyzes and triggers new and innovative products, services, and business processes.

#

The strategic renewal paths selected by our two case firms are not by chance but out of the lessons the management teams learned from their initial failures and analyses of their unique industry characteristics, resource endowment, and IT capabilities. Both firms embarked on a strategic renewal process, but each chose a different path. Our findings from this comparative case study has significant theoretical implications for scholarship in strategic management and IS research and offers practical guidance for brick-and-mortar firms for their strategic transformation amidst the tidal wave of the digital revolution in the global economy.

## 5.1 Theoretical Implications

Our findings from the comparative cases make several theoretical contributions to research on strategic renewal for managing disruptive innovations and to IS research on the essential role of IT in strategic renewal and its impact on the path to strategic renewal.

First, scholars have long argued for providing rich contextual descriptions for strategic renewal processes [4,56,57]. We demonstrate that there are multiple paths for strategic renewal for firms facing disruptive technological or operational innovations. Our detailed description and discussion of how two firms initiated and implemented two different strategic renewals fill at least part of the gap in the strategic renewal literature. Our case firm JEANSWEST chose a resource-complementing incremental strategic renewal path because of its rich offline resources, strong operational skills, entrenched business processes and structures, and strong IT resources and skills. This path is less radical with significantly less risk and offers the firm stability, predictability, and steady profitability while effectively managing disruptive innovations such as e-commerce. By contrast, our case firm COMAGIC elected a resource-substituting discontinuous strategic renewal path because of its relatively small size, more adaptable products and operations, and ability to quickly acquire resources and IT skills and applications. This path would be radical and risky for many established firms, but for emerging and agile firms like COMAGIC, it offers the best chance to capture the unique market opportunity brought by disruptive innovations and take market shares from the more established and less agile firms that have chosen to ignore the disruption or been slow to implement strategic renewal strategies.

In addition, our case evidence shows that the specific successful path for each firm depends on multiple internal and external contextual factors; resource endowment is the main factor among them. Resource endowment significantly impacts a firm’s strategic choice of priority between controlling transformational risk and accomplishing radical transformation, which essentially determines how top management chooses between incremental and discontinuous renewal. This finding extends the discussion of Agarwal and Helfat [4] on the selection of two basic types of strategic renewal. Furthermore, in contrary to the thought regarding existing

#

resources as constraints in developing new business models [27,28], the case of JEANSWEST shows that existing offline resource endowment could be leveraged as enablers to support the online strategy and operations, thus mitigating the high operational risk resulting from radical transformation of business.

Second, we provide a refined view on the theoretical question of “how IT affects strategic renewal.” Extant research has mainly focused on exploring how disruptive technologies such as social media, mobile, and big data analytics impact organizational change [14,58]; few have explored how firms use IT to accomplish a successful strategic renewal to effectively embrace the disruptive impacts. Through the theoretical lens of IT as an operand resource or operant resource [41], our study shows the processes of how IT as an enabler or a trigger affects resource strategy (new resources complement/substitute the existing resources) in accomplishing strategic renewal. We find that when the endowed resources are abundant, management tends to think IT as an operand resource and used IT primary as an enabler to help new resources complement to the existing resources, which hence promotes incremental strategic renewal, as in the case of JEANSWEST. By contrast, in firms that have not been entrenched in the resource-rich offline business models, management tends to realize that IT can be an operant resource and new business strategies and operational models can be created based on the emerging disruptive technological innovations, as in the case of COMAGIC. These findings enrich the exploration of theoretical relationships among IT, resource, and strategic renewal in managing online–offline integrated operations and business models [59] and demonstrate the strategic role and value of IT in achieving strategic renewal [60].

Our study further explains the impact of IT on the path to strategic renewal. When JEANSWEST decided to transform to manage the e-commerce disruption, its large offline operations consisting of more than 2,200 stores could provide competitive advantage to the new online business model, and its strong IT capabilities could minimize the operational and financial risks in the online-offline integration. In comparison, when COMAGIC embarked on its strategic transformation, its offline operations were relatively small and ineffective, its existing products were largely outdated for a fast changing market, and its IT capabilities were weak and bare-bone. COMAGIC was facing an existential threat without a systematic transformation, thus it made strategic and operational sense for its top management team to take a much higher risk in their pursuit for strategic renewal. This finding helps explain why larger and more established firms tend to choose or implement incremental strategic renewal while smaller and emerging firms tend to choose or implement discontinuous transformation [4].

Third, while the concept of IT as an operand resource has been widely studied in the literature [20,47], concrete evidence for how IT as an operant resource works has been rare in published studies, even though Lusch

#

and Nambisan [40] argue that decisions with regard to IT could affect the design of other resources and the development of organizational capabilities. Our case evidence shows that, when IT is treated as an operand resource, it facilitates incremental renewal by improving coordination, controlling risk, and supporting the complementarity between new and existing resources. This finding also indicates that an important enabling condition of IT in facilitating incremental renewal is the compatibility between IT as an operand resource and other organizational resources [60]. As in the case of JEANSWEST, IT supports the coordination of multiple channels services for online orders, allows real-time information sharing that supports timely feedback on operational problems, monitoring, and correcting operational errors, thus minimizing the possibility of greater systemic risk.

Differentiating from the role of IT as an operand resource, IT as an operant resource creates novel opportunities for resource integration to adapt to disruptive impacts, and as mentioned by Lusch and Nambisan [40] and Nambisan [41], such a role of IT was yet to be explicated. Our study provides a detailed description of how IT as an operant resource catalyzes and triggers business innovations in strategy and operations, and therefore helps firm to accomplish a discontinuous resource-substituting strategic transformation. The findings of this study indicate that when IT is treated as an operant resource, it is embedded into business models and operations. Newly-designed digital business models and operations facilitate the replacement of ineffective traditional resources and simultaneously strengthen the introduction of new resources. This resource-substituting process essentially change resource base of firms and gradually trigger a totally new digital business models, which actually makes firms undertake a discontinuous strategic transformation. In the case of COMAGIC, the firm embedded IT into its product design and production processes, used IT to completely redesign its supply chain, integrated online–offline customer experience and after-sales customer services, and eventually became an IT innovation powerhouse in the furniture industry.

## 5.2 Practical Implications

Our findings also have some meaningful practical implications for professional managers who are facing significant challenges in managing major disruptions brought by technological or operational innovations such as e-commerce. In particular, we show that the misconception that e-commerce is just another sales or distribution channel by management teams can be costly, not only in terms of channel conflicts but also in terms of major disruptions in operations, missed opportunities, and even long-term survivability. Management must view the ecommerce disruption innovation, or any other paradigm shifting disruptions from a strategic perspective and consider and evaluate appropriate strategic renewal strategies and paths based on its resource endowment, entrenchment of business processes and structures, and IT resources capabilities. The proposed strategic renewal framework can be used as a road map to understand the major factors in choosing the most appropriate path for strategic renewal.

Our analysis of the critical role of IT as operand or operant resource in the strategic renewal process also provides top management and IT managers a high-level guidance for developing appropriate IT governance structure, building appropriate IT capabilities, and deploying appropriate IT resources to significantly enhance the likelihood of success when undertaking strategic renewals. Firms with similar business models and market positioning to JEANSWEST could find comfort if they decide to leverage their strong IT capabilities and resources in a more traditional enabling and integrating role when pursuing incremental strategic renewal. On the other hand, firms with similar business models and market positioning to COMAGIC could be inspired to take the advantage of new and emerging IT innovations for building new competitive strategies, developing new products and services, and implementing integrated online-offline business models.

## 5.3 Limitations and Future Research

The two cases have provided interesting and significant insights on how to manage the transformation of offline firms to compete in the online e-commerce environment, a dilemma faced by a growing number of traditional brick-and-mortar firms as the economy is becoming increasingly digital resource dependent and digital technology driven. However, as an exploratory case study, it has many limitations. First, we could not present a more detailed account about the two renewal strategies used by the two case firms due to the limited space, leaving some of the discussions less complete. Second, it would have been more interesting if we had a third case in which the transformation from offline operation into online e-commerce was a complete failure. Third, although we illuminated two renewal strategies and described the associated paths, it is entirely possible that there are other strategies and paths that also lead to successful strategic renewal. However, the two cases do present typical challenges that many firms face or have already suffered in their strategic transformation adventures. Finally, it would be even more interesting to contrast the strategic choices and associated successes and failures in the transformation from pure offline to offline/online hybrid models. These limitations create many opportunities and offer directions for future research.

## 6. CONCLUSION

Through a comparative case study, we presented two strategic renewal paths for traditional brick-and-mortar firms that aim to manage the e-commerce disruption, and we highlighted the key factors that impact the choice and success of the two different strategic renewal paths. This research enriches and extends theories of strategic

#

management and strategic renewal by exploring how firms use IT in their strategic renewal to manage disruptive innovations. Our case evidence suggests that traditional firms with abundant offline resources, established strategic and operational structures and processes, and strong IT resources and capabilities are more likely to succeed if an incremental resourcing-complementing strategic renewal is used. By contrast, traditional firms with less entrenched offline infrastructure and weak IT capabilities may consider a discontinuous resource-substituting strategic renewal when moving from pure offline business model into the integrated online–offline model. Finally, our exploration of the relationship between IT resources and strategic renewal for managing disruptive innovations shows that IT can be either an operand or an operant resource in the strategic renewal process, and two comparative cases provide a clear and contrasting illustration. Although this study is limited in many aspects, the findings nonetheless have significant theoretical implications for research on management and IS strategy, and offer valuable insights into managerial practices in organizational strategic renewal and transformation.
