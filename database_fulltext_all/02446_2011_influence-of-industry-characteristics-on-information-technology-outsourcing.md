# Influence of Industry Characteristics on Information Technology Outsourcing

Wen Guang Qu, Alain Pinsoe ault, and Wonse k Oh

Wen Guang Qu is an assistant professor at the School of Management, Zhejiang University, China. He received his Ph.D. in information systems from McGill University. His research interests include IT business value, IT outsourcing, interorganizational systems, and IT issues at the macro level. His work has been published or is forthcoming in the Journal of Strategic Information Systems, Journal of Business Research, Journal of Global Information Management, and Technovation.

Alain Pinsonneault is the James McGill Professor and the Imasco Chair of Information Systems on the Desautels Faculty of Management at McGill University. He received his Ph.D. in information systems from the University of California–Irvine. His current research interests include the organizational and individual effects of IT, user adaptation, enterprise resource planning implementation, e-health, e-integration, group support systems, strategic alignment of IT, and the business value of IT. He has published papers in Management Science, Information Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, MIS Quarterly, Small Group Research, Decision Support Systems, Organization Science, and European Journal of Operational Research.

Wonseok Oh is an associate professor at the School of Business at Yonsei University in Korea. He received his Ph.D. in information systems from the Stern School of Business at New York University. His research interests include network theory, social networks, IT outsourcing, business value of IS, and economic aspects of e-commerce. His research has been published in Information Systems Research, International Journal of Electronic Commerce, Journal of Management Information Systems, Journal of Strategic Information Systems, MIS Quarterly, and Management Science.

Abstract: Despite the extensive research on information technology (IT) outsourcing, our knowledge and understanding of how industry characteristics impact the use of IT outsourcing remain limited. Drawing upon theories from organization behavior and industrial economics, this study identifies four major industry characteristics (i.e., munificence, dynamism, concentration, and capital intensity) and investigates how each of these factors affects the use of IT outsourcing. Specifically, we postulate that the extent of industry munificence is positively related to the utilization of IT outsourcing. Since timely strategic actions are the crucial aspects of leveraging munificent resources, IT outsourcing, which can be implemented in short periods of time, is considered to be a preferred option in such environments. Furthermore, industry dynamism is also positively associated with IT outsourcing, given that firms in dynamically evolving industries tend to look for flexibility and avoid a large amount of fixed investments (e.g., IT development in-house). In contrast to these hypotheses, we predict that industry concentration is negatively related to IT outsourcing. Firm in concentrated industries are likely to develop their own IT infrastructures, as they are not constrained by institutional pressures or cost-driven strategic actions. Finally, because firms in capital-intensive industries tend to conform to long-standing traditional practices, and do not highly value novel and risky practices, they will be less likely to use IT outsourcing than firms in industries with low capital intensity. The data from the U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis along with Compustat empirically validated all of the proposed hypotheses; however, only marginal support was found for the association between industry concentration and IT outsourcing. Our findings offer business executives and IT service providers strategic and managerial insights into the dynamics and complexities involved in the diverse aspects of industry envi ronments and IT outsourcing decisions.

Desp ite the reduction in information technolog y (IT) investment in recent years, IT outsourcing continues to be popular and widely used. A report by Gartner [44] predicted that worldwide spending on IT outsourcing will rapidly increase from \$268 billion in 2009 to \$325 billion by 2013. The benefits of IT outsourcing for firms have been well documented in the literature [25]. Research has suggested that IT outsourcing can provide firms with various economic, technological, and strategic benefits, such as reducing IT operation costs, improving technical competence, and even providing firms with competitive advantages [28, 63].

Many studies have investigated the factors that either facilitate or impede the use of IT outsourcing. Various factors play an important role in determining IT outsourcing utilization, including business and IT cost structures, internal IT competence, transaction costs, knowledge gained from IT vendors, cost advantages of IT vendors, and spatial distance (e.g., [3, 6, 18, 64, 99, 101]). To date, the majority of research on IT outsourcing has emphasized internal antecedents within specific firms [25]. In contrast, limited attention has been paid to the effects of industry characteristics on the use of IT outsourcing, and the underdeveloped literature has revealed mixed results. For instance, research has elucidated that the industrial IT intensity can have a positive, neutral, or negative effect on outsourcing usage [49, 81, 99]. There is still a need for additional research to enhance our understanding of this area, and this is the aim of the present study. This paper draws upon organization theory and industrial organization economics in order to develop a new conceptualization of industry environments based on four key factors (munificence, dynamism, concentration, and capital intensity). Then, we investigate how each factor influences the use of IT outsourcing.

This paper provides important contributions to the research in at least three ways. First, we complement the existing firm-level research by providing a broader industrylevel insight into the antecedents to IT outsourcing use. Second, we identify four critical dimensions of industry environments that can influence the adoption of IT outsourcing.

Finally, our conceptualization of industry characteristics constitutes a framework that can apply to the study of other IT-related phenomena. Our study makes a key practical contribution as well, revealing that industry environments play an important role in either the facilitation or hindrance of IT outsourcing utilization. Further, the present paper suggests that IT vendors can benefit from attending to industry characteristics when developing new markets, as firms in diverse industries may have different propensities and motivations for adopting IT outsourcing.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. First, we survey the studies that investigate the effect of industry environments on the decision to outsource IT. Second, we employ both organization theory and industrial organization economics to identify the four aspects of industry environments that are critical to a firm’s strategic action. We draw upon this information to build a theoretical framework that links industry environments to IT outsourcing use. Following this conceptual development, we empirically test these four industry characteristics based on comprehensive archival data. Finally, we conclude with a discussion of the implications for research and practice and provide recommendations for future research.

## Industry Effects on IT Outsourcing

Althoug h it has been sug ested that industry environments can play an important role in determining a firm’s strategic decision [19, 25], few studies have examined the effect that industry environments have on IT outsourcing utilization (see Table 1). Moreover, our analysis of the literature indicates that the notion of industry environments has been conceptualized in a variety of ways across studies. Some have focused on product characteristics (goods versus services), whereas others have delved into the importance of IT in a given industry [64, 94]. In order to facilitate the comparison across studies, we have organized existing studies in terms of their approaches to industry environment conceptualizations. These studies can be grouped into three fundamental approaches for the conceptualization of industry environments.

The first group of studies divides industries into two classifications—manufacturing and service. The premise behind this classification is that firms in service industries may not rely as heavily on IT outsourcing as firms in manufacturing industries, since IT is often viewed as an integral part of service firms’ core operations [95]. However, this view is not empirically supported. For instance, Loh and Venkatraman [64] found no difference in IT outsourcing levels between firms in manufacturing industries versus service industries. Similarly, Sobol and Apte [95] revealed that the difference between these types of firms is not significant with respect to both domestic and global IT outsourcing levels. However, contrary to the above findings, Han et al. [43] reported that firms in service industries use IT outsourcing significantly more than firms in manufacturing industries.

The second group of studies classifies industries based on their level of IT intensity. However, the expected effects of IT intensity on outsourcing use are inconsistent across studies. For instance, Oh [76] and Slaughter and Ang [94] suggested that because firms in IT-intensive industries consider information systems (IS) skills and resources their core competencies, they may opt to outsource less aggressively than firms in non-IT-intensive industries. However, Han et al. [43] claimed that firms in IT-intensive industries may outsource more aggressively because they have superior capabilities in assessing and using external IT-related resources. Empirically, Slaughter and Ang [94] provided evidence that computing firms are more likely to use an internal IT staff than their noncomputing counterparts. Alternatively, Oh [76] found that the relationship between IT intensity and outsourcing is nonsignificant. In contrast to these findings, Han et al. [43] demonstrated that IT-intensive industries outsource IT more aggressively than non-IT-intensive industries.

The third group of studies uses a diverse array of other industry characteristics. For instance, Lee et al. [61] separated industries into three types: long-linked, mediating, and intensive technologies [100]. They found that comprehensive IT outsourcing and selective IT outsourcing are more effective than minimal IT outsourcing in industries characterized by long-linked technologies. However, in industries with mediating and intensive technologies, no significant differences exist in terms of outsourcing success. Aubert et al. [8] segmented industries according to demand volatility and knowledge intensity and discovered that firms in high demand volatility industries, as well as those in low knowledge-intensive industries, outsource IT functions more frequently.

Overall, prior research has focused on several aspects of industry characteristics, such as product characteristics and IT intensity. However, with respect to the effects of industry characteristics on IT outsourcing, the results are not consistent. One explanation for the observed inconsistencies may be that IS researchers have conceptualized the notion of industry environments in a different manner, and that they may not have captured the fundamental characteristics that differentiate industries. In order to enhance our understanding of this issue, we seek a more comprehensive conceptualization of the notion of industry environments. To accomplish this, we draw upon the literature on organization theory and industrial organization economics [24, 88].

In addition, existing research that investigates the industry effect on IT outsourcing typically takes an economic rationality perspective. For instance, arguments tend to deal with economic aspects of the issue, such as the strategic importance of IT [4, 76] and the cost–benefit aspects of IT outsourcing [8]. A more holistic explanation can be obtained by combining both economic and institutional perspectives [38, 96]. The economic perspective posits that firms pursue economic advantages and are guided by unambiguous preferences and rationality. In contrast, the institutional perspective suggests that firms’ decision-making processes and actions are often driven by pressures to acquire and maintain legitimacy in relation to their stakeholders. IS scholars have employed both perspectives to explain why firms utilize IT outsourcing. For instance, an economic perspective was adopted to account for outsourcing use, based on a desire to reduce production and transaction costs [3]. From an institutional perspective, research has indicated that the highly publicized Kodak outsourcing case legitimized the practice of IT outsourcing and stimulated other firms to replicate these practices [65]. In this paper, we apply both economic and institutional viewpoints to investigate the effect of industry environments on IT outsourcing use.

## Industry Determinants of IT Outsourcing

## Theoretical Foundations

The literature on org anization theory and industrial organization economics takes a broad view of industry environments. Traditionally, environments have long been recognized as being influential forces on organizations [10, 59]. Research in the field of organization theory conceptualizes environments as either sources of information or stocks of resources [90]. Scholars who emphasize the informational aspects of environments focus primarily on the degree of uncertainty faced by organizations. In contrast, researchers who stress the resource aspect of environments focus on the degree to which an organization is dependent upon others for vital resources. The literature has suggested a variety of dimensions to conceptualize environments, including munificence, dynamism, and complexity [15, 83] (see Table 2).

Environmental munificence is the extent to which environments provide sufficient resources to support established organizations as well as new entrants, and enable them to grow and prosper [83]. Theoretical and empirical research clearly suggest that environmental munificence has a pervasive influence on organizational processes, structures, and strategies [37]. In munificent environments, it is relatively easy for firms to survive, and thus they are able to pursue higher goals above and beyond mere survival [14]. As a result, research has indicated that environmental munificence is positively associated with a range of strategies that firms pursue [47]. For example, the growth and prosperity potential in a munificent environment allow the organization to invest in new business initiatives [51] and become more diversified [47]. Furthermore, abundant resources enable firms to generate slack resources [80].

Environmental dynamism refers to the degree of change in environmental activities relevant to an organization’s operations [83]. More precisely, environmental dynamism is concerned with the frequency, degree, and unpredictability of changes occurring in the environment [15]. Since environmental dynamism is the major factor that contrib utes to organizational decision makers’ uncertainty [20], dealing with environmental dynamism becomes the essence of the administrative process [100]. Generally, the more dynamic the environment, the greater the uncertainty faced by the organization, and therefore the greater the amount of information that decision makers must process in order to achieve a given level of performance [33]. It has been suggested that firms can reduce the negative effect of environmental uncertainty or create a more predictable environment by implementing certain organizational strategies and tactics, such as buffering or collusion [24]. The literature also indicates that a highly dynamic environment warrants a flexible organization [59].

Environmental complexity indicates the heterogeneity of an organization’s activities that are relevant to the organization’s operations [20]. All things being equal, managers facing a more complex (i.e., heterogeneous) environment will have greater informationprocessing needs than managers facing an uncomplicated environment [24]. Firms can face different types of environmental complexity depending on several factors, including competitive complexity, market diversity, resource complexity, and process/ facility complexity [13]. Environmental complexity can also significantly influence firms’ behaviors; a high level of environmental complexity may prevent firms from growing, as they are forced to deal with complicated environments [47].

While industry munificence, dynamism, and complexity have been emphasized in organization theory [24, 47], other research fields such as industrial organization economics have introduced different aspects of industrial environments [17] (Table 3). The basic model in industrial organization economics originates from the structureconduct-performance (SCP) paradigm [97]. This framework suggests that industry structure (e.g., industry concentration and barriers to entry) affects a firm’s strategic behavior, such as pricing decisions and investment policies, which in turn influence firm performance. In particular, it has been suggested that concentration and barriers to entry are unique industry characteristics that serve to protect firms from excessive competition while ensuring high profitability [97].

Industry concentration, the extent to which market shares are concentrated within an industry, is one of the most emphasized structural elements in the field of industrial organization economics. Industry concentration often serves as an indicator of the competitiveness of an industry; the less concentrated, the more competitive is the industry [88]. This is because, ceteris paribus, the degree of concentration in an industry increases cooperative behaviors among rivals. In concentrated industries with a small number of established players, firms can monitor one another’s strategic conduct with ease, which in turn fosters implicit collusive behaviors [97]. In contrast, in nonconcentrated industries with a large number of players, it becomes difficult to coordinate firms’ actions; therefore, aggressive competition may result [88]. This competitive situation is also likely to drive down profitability [97]. Another reason that industry concentration can influence profitability is because of the fact that firms with large market shares are able to control prices and obtain monopoly-type rents [36, 75]. Empirical studies have demonstrated that highly concentrated industries have higher prices and likely greater profits [52, 75, 93].

The industrial organization literature has identified barriers to entry as a key factor that determines competition, and its concept has been often defined in terms of the degree of capital intensity, economy of scale, or product differentiation [84]. New firms, for example, are discouraged from entering capital-intensive industries because (1) the higher the capital requirements, the more difficult it is to obtain the required capital [49], and (2) the higher the capital requirements, the riskier it is to enter the industry, as capital investments are often product-specific sunk costs and their return cannot be guaranteed when there are uncertainties in market demand or technologies [45, 67]. A related concept to barriers to entry is barriers to exit, which are concerned with economic, strategic, and emotional factors that motivate firms to continue competing in the market despite the fact that they may be earning low or even negative returns on their investments [81]. Barriers to entry are often closely connected to barriers to exit, because in order to prevent a potential rival from entering the market, an incumbent tends to have some fixed commitments to a market [17]. The fixed commitments in resources then become the source of barriers to exit.

In summary, research on organization theory and industrial organization economics has identified five characteristics of industries: munificence, dynamism, complexity, concentration, and barriers to entry [24, 97]. It should be noted that industry concentration can be regarded as a key component of industry complexity, which is often conceptualized as a multidimensional construct. Environmental complexity has four subdimensions: competitive complexity, market diversity, resource complexity, and process complexity [13]. This paper focuses on competitive complexity (i.e., industry concentration), as it is the key element of complexity [24, 47]. In addition, although several factors have been identified as barriers to entry, this study primarily deals with capital intensity because it not only serves as a barrier to entry but also represents a barrier to exit [17, 81]. Therefore, our paper investigates how the four aspects of industry environments (i.e., munificence, dynamism, concentration, and capital intensity) affect IT outsourcing utilization. This is consistent with strategy research that has also used these four dimensions to conceptualize industries (e.g., [71, 82]).

## Hypotheses

Industry munificence refers to the availability of resources in an industry [83]. When resources are abundant, it is relatively easy for firms to survive. Thus, they are capable of pursuing goals beyond survival, such as growth or diversity [14, 47]. As a result, one strategic priority in munificent industries is to expand in terms of scale and scope [24], as was evident by e-commerce firms during the era of the dot-com boom in the 1990s [79]. In contrast, survival becomes the priority in industries with scarce resources, as the pressures of environmental selection become intensified. In this respect, more growth opportunities exist in munificent industries than in nonmunificent industries. In fact, research typically measures industry munificence based on the extent of industry growth. For instance, growth, in terms of a given industry’s total sales, price–cost margin, total employment, value added, and the number of establishments, has been widely used in the literature as an indicator of industry munificence [24, 47].

IS scholars have suggested that the expectation of growth within a given industry will lead to additional IT investments, particularly in the areas of IT infrastructures [53, 72]. This is because a superior IT infrastructure allows firms in such industries to grow efficiently in terms of scale and scope without a large increase in their operation costs [85]. For example, Amazon.com made significant investments in its distribution and IT infrastructures in anticipation of future growth [92]. Cisco Systems also invested heavily in IT in order to support its business expansion in response to industry growth [53]. In short, firms in munificent industries are likely to face rapid growth and require an effective IT infrastructure to support such growth.

However, building an effective IT infrastructure may require a substantial amount of time [103]. Consequently, firms in munificent industries may be unwilling to wait for their own IT infrastructures to develop. Instead, they can acquire the IT infrastructure services they so urgently need from outside IT vendors. Therefore, when firms expand quickly in munificent industries, they are inclined to purchase IT infrastructure services from the market in order to facilitate their scale expansion. Furthermore, a munificent environment will attract more firms to enter the industry [1]. These firms are either developed from existing firms in related industries or are start-ups that wish to capitalize on the munificent resources. These firms often lack the IT infrastructure resources required for business operations given that they are new to the industry. As a result, firms in munificent industries are more likely to purchase IT infrastructure services from the market than firms in resource-constrained industries. Since industry munificence encourages the growth and entry of new players in the industry, a higher level of IT outsourcing is expected in munificent industries.

Hypothesis 1: Industry munificence will be positively associated with the use of IT outsourcing.

Industry dynamism represents the frequency, degree, and unpredictability of change in relevant industry parameters [15]. Investing in IT resources can be a demanding task for managers in dynamic industries not only because of the important investments involved but also because of the irreversibility of IT investments [103]. This situation becomes worse when the dynamic industry environment prompts firms to modify their business strategies. This is because changes in business strategies often necessitate changes in IT strategies and IT investment decisions. When business strategies become a moving target, it is very difficult, if not impossible, to make effective IT investment decisions. For instance, decisions regarding what and how many IT resources to develop can be difficult in uncertain industries, because overinvestments in IT result in wasted resources. However, underinvestments (or developing the improper IT) could cause delays, insufficient support for business operations, and critical opportunity costs [104].

In the face of uncertainty and irreversibility, “options thinking” may become an appropriate method by which to guide investments, in particular the “option to defer” [32]. An option to defer exists when a decision regarding whether or how to invest can be delayed for a certain period of time without jeopardizing the loss of potential benefits. This option is particularly useful when uncertainty is high, but can be resolved over time. Purchasing goods or services in markets (as opposed to making large investments to produce goods or services in-house) provides firms with a defer option [62]. In-house production could entail greater sunk costs than outsourcing through market contracts and expose firms to the high risk of owning assets that may turn out to be of little value due to the changes in either technology or product demand. In contrast, outsourcing may involve greater short-term transaction costs, but may also provide firms with the flexibility to acquire the proper production technology and capacity [62]. Therefore, option theory suggests that when faced with uncertainty, it may be optimal for firms to defer irreversible in-house investments and utilize market mechanisms that provide greater flexibility.

In summary, when industries are dynamic (e.g., when market demand or IT is changing rapidly), firms may be reluctant to commit large IT investments in order to offer all of their IT services in-house. If there are large demand fluctuations, firms may be swayed to overinvest in IT and other necessary resources in order to satisfy their peak demands. This overinvestment, however, will likely increase costs [104]. In contrast, outsourcing IT services through the market arrangement (e.g., a pay-per-use contract) can provide the firm with increased flexibility, allowing it to meet peak demands. For example, a large U.S. financial services firm has used global IT outsourcing in order to gain strategic agility in the face of uncertain circumstances [105]. During the refinancing boom, the firm was able to outcompete its rivals by quickly meeting the immense surge in market demand for IT services through its outsourcing arrangements. When the refinancing boom ended, the firm was able to immediately downscale its resources and minimize the unproductive slack resources. Similarly, if the IT platform is radically changing (e.g., a shift from a mainframe to a client-server platform), investments in mainframe-based IT assets could result in significant sunk costs. Rational firms will try to avoid such overinvestments arising from the heavy in-house IT development and will turn to IT vendors in order to mitigate the risk associated with industry dynamism. As shown in prior research [8], firms rely on IT outsourcing to a greater extent when they face high demand uncertainty. Accordingly, we posit that there will be a higher level of IT outsourcing in dynamic industries than in stable industries.

Hypothesis 2: Industry dynamism will be positively associated with the use of IT outsourcing.

Industry concentration measures the degree to which the industry is concentrated. We argue that industry concentration will negatively affect the use of IT outsourcing for two reasons. First, low industry concentration may lead firms to duplicate IT outsourcing due to institutional pressures. Consistent with institutional theory [27, 89], research has indicated that IT outsourcing is an organizational practice that firms act upon to imitate other firms [54, 55]. For instance, it was found that the diffusion of IT outsourcing is a process by which the practice of IT outsourcing is revealed to, and imitated by, other firms over time [65]. Firms are motivated to copy other firms IT outsourcing because IT management involves a high level of ambiguity and uncertainty. The issue of how IT management should be evaluated has long been a problem for managers, and firms are usually uncertain about whether, or how, IT generates business value [31, 68], as well as how to manage IT effectively [65]. Given the high uncertainty associated with IT management and value-creation trajectory, firms have a tendency to follow the successful IT management practices of other firms, such as the successful IT outsourcing case of Kodak [54, 65]. Consequently, a bandwagon effect in IT outsourcing appears. DiMaggio and Powell stated, “When organizational technologies are poorly understood, when goals are ambiguous, or when the environment creates symbolic uncertainty, organizations may model themselves on other organizations” [27, p. 151]. This imitative behavior has a ritual aspect; firms adopt IT outsourcing to enhance their legitimacy and to demonstrate that they are at least making an effort to improve IT management.

However, not all firms conform to institutional pressures and follow the behaviors of other firms. A recent development in institutional theory (institutional entrepreneurship in particular) introduces agency, self-interests, and power into institutional analyses of organizations, suggesting that entrepreneurship even exists in highly institutionalized environments [34, 40]. Firms with entrepreneurial cultures can resist institutional forces, or even break old institutional practices and create new ones when they have the power and means to do so [11]. For instance, by using its resources on Java, Sun Microsystems has successfully developed common technology standards despite the dominance of Microsoft’s Windows [35].

Firms in concentrated industries typically have considerable power and substantial resources [81]. They may not simply conform to institutional pressures. Instead, they may have a strategic response because they have the discretion and can proactively shape institutional norms [11, 26]. For example, large banks are not simply governed by institutional pressures and imitate IT outsourcing. Rather, they are more inclined to make strategic responses [2]. On the contrary, the majority of firms in low-concentration (highly competitive) industries may merely adhere to institutional norms due to their lack of power [41, 78], and instead follow the pattern of IT outsourcing in order to gain legitimacy [54, 65]. As a result, more firms will jump on the IT outsourcing bandwagon in low-concentration industries, which will lead to a negative relationship between industry concentration and IT outsourcing.

The second reason industry concentration will be negatively related to IT outsourcing is that firms in concentrated industries can charge higher prices and generate monopoly benefits [52, 75, 93]. As described earlier, high concentration increases the cooperative behaviors among competitors because firms in concentrated industries can more easily monitor one another’s strategic actions [17, 88]. Consequently, firms in such industries are enabled to control the prices and gain monopoly-type rents [36, 75]. In contrast, firms in nonconcentrated industries are prompted to behave more competitively, which will drive down price and profitability [93, 97]. A key motivation for outsourcing IT is to save on costs [55, 64]. Given that firms in concentrated industries, which can charge high prices and generate more profits, may not emphasize cost savings, they should be less motivated to outsource IT. Therefore, we expect a lower level of IT outsourcing in highly concentrated industries.

Hypothesis 3: Industry concentration will be negatively associated with the use of IT outsourcing.

Finally, we propose that the capital intensity of an industry, the extent to which fixed assets are required to do business in a given industry, will be negatively associated with IT outsourcing. The industrial organization literature views capital intensity as both a barrier to entry and a barrier to exit [17]. New firms may be discouraged from entering a capital-intensive industry because capital investments are often product-specific sunk costs and their return cannot be guaranteed when uncertainties exist in market demand or technology [45, 67]. At the same time, these committed investments in the form of fixed assets may have little salvage value if a firm exits from the industry. Consequently, these fixed investments also become a barrier to exit.

As a barrier to entry and, more important, as a barrier to exit, capital intensity tends to constrain firms’ strategic behaviors in several ways [17, 22, 42]. First, firms in capital-intensive industries are generally committed to a course of action due to the high investment in fixed assets. This, in turn, leads firms to favor continuity with traditional practices. Second, efficient management of assets is critical in such industries.

Novel strategies characterized by a higher risk of failure are less likely to be valued. Finally, obtaining the knowledge of what has been effective versus what strategies (or approaches) have failed in the past is crucial to avoiding costly mistakes. These effects of capital intensity make it highly relevant to the use of IT outsourcing.

IT outsourcing is still a relatively new practice in several industries and may involve a high level of risk [5, 77]. For instance, research has identified eight undesirable outcomes of IT outsourcing, some of which include service debasement, cost escalation, and loss of organizational competencies [7]. Moreover, four scenarios in which IT outsourcing can be risky for client firms are recognized: lock-in, contractual amendments, unexpected transition and management, and disputes and litigation [9]. Since firms in capital-intensive industries usually continue to conform to long-standing traditional practices, and as a result may not value novel and risky practices [22], they will be less likely to use IT outsourcing than firms in industries with low capital intensity.

In addition, because the cost of exiting is high in capital-intensive industries, firms in such industries are expected to have a long-term plan for their businesses. IS research demonstrates that investing in in-house IT resources can have long-term positive effects on firm performance [12, 87]. Accordingly, firms with long-term orientations may be willing to invest in in-house IT resources. In contrast, low capital-intensive industries have a lower barrier-to-entry and barrier-to-exit [17, 88]. The ease of entering and exiting the market encourages firms to use “temporary” approaches to acquire IT services in order to allow rapid and flexible entry. Compared with in-house development of IT services, outsourcing (short-term contracts in particular) is often considered a more flexible alternative to obtaining required IT services, particularly when the need is urgent [57, 61]. In fact, one of the main benefits of IT outsourcing is that it enhances flexibility in the provision of technology services [56]. In this light, the use of IT outsourcing is expected to be higher in low capital-intensive industries than in capital-intensive industries. In sum, we suggest that there is a negative relationship between industry capital intensity and the use of IT outsourcing:

Hypothesis 4: Industry capital intensity will be negatively associated with the use of IT outsourcing.

## Research Method

## Data and Variables

Tw o data sources w ere used in this study: the U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) and Compustat.<sup>1</sup> We used BEA’s Use Tables (Input-Output Account) and Fixed Assets Tables to measure IT outsourcing level, industry munificence, dynamism, and capital intensity. BEA’s Use Tables report the amount of goods or services produced by one industry that serve as an input for another industry as well as provide data on industry gross output. There are a total of 61 private industries in the sample, and the data cover the period from 1998 to 2004 (the final sample contains 59 industries because two industries were removed).<sup>2</sup> BEA’s Fixed Assets Tables include investment and stock data on 46 different types of nonresidential capital in 63 industries (these

63 industries were converted into the 61 industries listed in the BEA’s Use Tables). Sales data from Compustat for all the firms in the 61 industries were used to compute industry concentration.

The IT outsourcing use was operationalized as an industry’s outsourcing expenditure controlled by its stock of IT assets, where IT outsourcing expenditure is the amount of IT services used by the industry. The measure is consistent with prior studies that measure IT outsourcing level by the ratio of IT outsourcing budget to total IT investment (e.g., [55, 61]). The data on IT services were taken from BEA’s Use Tables. There are two IT services industries: Information and Data Processing Services industry (NAICS [North American Industry Classification System] 514) and Computer Systems Design and Related Services industry (NAICS 5415).<sup>3</sup> The amount of IT services used by an industry was computed as the sum of the outputs produced by these two IT services industries that serve as inputs for the focal industry. IT assets are an industry’s stock in information and communication technologies (computer hardware, software, and telecommunications equipment). The sum of these three IT assets is a good approximate measure for IT stock level [98]. The data on IT assets (computer hardware, software, and telecommunications equipment) came from BEA’s Fixed Assets Tables.

The choice of industry-level data for a study of IT outsourcing use may require a few words of explanation. IT outsourcing is essentially a firm-level decision and may call for firm-level data. However, this might not be a major concern. While firm-level data are appropriate for research involving interfirm heterogeneity, industry-level data are appropriate for research involving variation due to industry-level characteristics, which is the focus of our study. For instance, numerous studies on industrial organization economics have used industry-level data [88]. In fact, industry-level data enable us to isolate the effects of industry characteristics on IT outsourcing from the effects of firm-specific characteristics [74]. Essentially, by using industry data, we assume that the industry average corresponds to a “representative” firm in the industry. The same approach has been used in previous studies (e.g., [74, 86]).

The data on industry gross output were used to compute industry munificence and industry dynamism. As was done in prior research [47], we regressed log-transformed industry gross output for the previous five years on time. The antilog of the regression slope coefficient was used as the indicator of industry munificence, and the standard error of the regression slope was used as a measure of industry dynamism. As in previous studies (e.g., [88]), we measured industry concentration using the Herfindahl-Hirschman index, or H I: ${ \mathrm { H H I } } = \Sigma _ { i = 1 } ^ { N } S _ { i } ^ { 2 }$ , where $S _ { _ i }$ is the market share of the ith firm. We used sales data from Compustat to compute the H I, as was done in previous research [48]. For each of the 61 industries in the Use Tables, we collected sales data for all the firms in that industry.

Following previous research [45, 71], capital intensity was operationalized as an industry’s capital of fixed assets, controlled by industry gross output.<sup>4</sup> Data for fixed assets were obtained from the Fixed Assets Tables. Recall that the Fixed Assets Tables include investments and stock data on 46 different types of nonresidential capital in 63 industries. Nonresidential capital includes equipment capital (e.g., IT hardware and software, general industrial equipment, and automobiles) and structure capital (e.g., office and manufacturing structure). We computed the sum of all 46 types of nonresidential capital for each industry to obtain each industry’s fixed assets, while controlling it by industry gross output.

In addition, because the IT intensity of an industry may influence the use of IT outsourcing [43, 94], we need to control its effect in our analysis.<sup>5</sup> The IT intensity was computed as the ratio of an industry’s IT assets stock to its gross output. Another factor that needs to be controlled in the analysis is the average firm size in an industry. One key reason that IT vendors can provide less-expensive IT services than in-house IT departments is that IT vendors have the advantage of economies of scale [63]. For large firms, the relative advantage of IT vendors may not be so strong, because the in-house IT department in large firms can also benefit from significant economies of scale [46]. As such, large firms may be less likely to use IT outsourcing, and we need to control the effect of firm size in an industry. Using data from Compustat, the value of the average firm size in an industry was computed by averaging the size of all the firms in the industry, weighted by their market shares. We used the weighted average firm size because a firm with a large market share has more significance to its industry than a firm with a small market share, as suggested in prior research [60].

IS research suggests that business conditions in previous years can have a significant influence on firms’ decisions regarding IT budget [48, 102]. Following this line of reasoning, we used previous years’ industry characteristics for key independent variables. As shown above, the measurements for industry munificence and dynamism came from the regressions of the previous five years of data [47]. For industry concentration and capital intensity, we used data from the previous year, as was done in prior studies [48, 102]. All these key variables are summarized in Table 4.

## Analytical Procedure

Since our final sample contains panel data on 59 industries from 1998 to 2004 (data for industry munificence, dynamism, concentration, and capital intensity are from 1997 to 2003), we used a panel model to analyze the data. Panel models represent a preferred way to efficiently estimate the parameters for panel data [39]. We used the following model for testing the effects of four industry dimensions on the use of IT outsourcing:

$$
\begin{array}{c} \text {Outsourcing} _ {i t} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \text {Munificence} _ {i, t - 1} + \beta_ {2} \text {Dynamism} _ {i, t - 1} + \beta_ {3} \text {Concentration} _ {i, t - 1} \\ + \beta_ {4} \text {Cap\_Int} _ {i, t - 1} + \beta_ {5} \text {IT\_Int} _ {i t} + \beta_ {6} \text {Firm\_Size} _ {i t} + a _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i t}, \end{array}
$$

where Outsourcing is the level of IT outsourcing use for an industry i at year t. Independent variables include the munificence, dynamism, concentration, capital intensity, IT intensity, and average firm size of an industry i at year t – 1 or t. The $a _ { _ i }$ in the model represents unobserved time-invariant fixed factors associated with industry i, such as the level of regulation or industry type (manufacturing versus services); and $\ \varepsilon _ { \scriptscriptstyle i t }$ is the error term associated with each observation. Because of the presence of $a _ { _ i }$ in the composite error term ${ \bf r } _ { i t } ( = a _ { _ i } + \mathfrak { E } _ { _ i t } ) , r _ { _ i t }$ is likely to be serially correlated across time for the same industry. One way to get rid of $a _ { _ i }$ is to use the fixed effects model. The fixed effects approach subtracts the time average of each industry for each variable, which removes $a _ { _ i }$ from the model and then estimates all βs [106].

The fixed effects model, rather than the random effects model, was adopted for data analysis in this study for two reasons: (1) a Hausman test $( \chi ^ { 2 } ( 6 ) = 9 1 . 2 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ rejected the assumption of a random effects model that unit effects would be uncorrelated with other regressors [39], and (2) the industries in our data may not be viewed as a random sample of a population given that they represent all private industries in the United States [106]. We used the “xtreg” command in Stata 9.0 to estimate the fixed effects model, where IT outsourcing level was regressed on industry munificence, dynamism, concentration, and capital intensity, as well as the control variables.

## Results

The descriptive statistics and correlation matrix for all the key variables used in this analysis are presented in Table 5. The water transportation industry had the lowest level of IT outsourcing (0.003), and the oil and gas extraction industry had the highest level (0.828). It should be noted that the measure for H I is based solely on data from public firms, which are usually large, rather than from all the firms in an industry. As a result, the H I values are probably inflated a little. The H I values are often used as a criterion for monopoly in antitrust cases. Usually, a market is considered highly competitive if its H I is less than 0.1, moderately competitive with an H I value between 0.1 and 0.18, and barely competitive if its H I exceeds 0.18, which is roughly approximated by a top four firm share of around 50 percent [73]. The mean of the H I in this study is 0.122, which, according to Mueller [73], would be considered moderately competitive.

Table 6 provides a summary of the results for the main effects. Hypothesis 1 was supported. We found a significant positive relationship between industry munificence and IT outsourcing use $( \beta = 0 . 0 9 2 ; p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . Hypothesis 2 was also supported, as there was a positive relationship between industry dynamism and IT outsourcing use $( \beta = 0 . 1 3 7 ; p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . The coefficient of industry concentration was negative $( \beta = - 0 . 0 8 5 ; p < 0 . 1 0 )$ , which was consistent with Hypothesis 3—the use of IT outsourcing is expected to be negatively associated with industry concentration. Finally, Hypothesis 4 was also supported, as there was a significant negative relationship between industry capital intensity and IT outsourcing use $( \beta = - 0 . 8 5 7 ; p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . For the control variables, we found no significant relationship between IT intensity and outsourcing use $( \beta = - 0 . 1 0 8 ; p > 0 . 1 )$ . The association between the average firm size in an industry and IT outsourcing use was also not significant $( \beta = 0 . 0 0 8 ; p > 0 . 1 )$ suggesting that the average firm size in an industry does not influence the use of IT outsourcing much.

## Discussion

## Main Findings

Althoug h research has investig ated the antecedents of IT outsourcing at the firm level, our knowledge of how industry environments affect the use of IT outsourcing remains limited. Prior research has classified industries based on product characteristics or IT intensity, and their findings have been inconclusive. For instance, while some researchers suggest that firms in IT-intensive industries may outsource less aggressively than those in non-IT-intensive industries, others posit that firms in IT-intensive industries may outsource more vigorously [43, 94]. Similarly, while some scholars postulate that firms in manufacturing industries may outsource IT services more than those in service industries [95], the empirical evidence proves otherwise [43, 64].

We believe that these inconsistencies may arise because of the fact that the conceptualizations of industry environments in previous studies often failed to capture the essence of industry. In order to fill this void, we have conceptualized the industry environment according to four important dimensions that we derived from organization theory and industrial organization economics. Using this as our framework, our approach has a broad conceptualization of industry and offers a comprehensive understanding of industry effects on the use of IT outsourcing.

More specifically, we find, first, that industry munificence is positively associated with IT outsourcing. This result is consistent with prior findings, suggesting that growth is more likely to occur in munificent industries [14, 24] and requires the support of a well-developed IT infrastructure [53, 72]. Because the development of in-house IT infrastructures may be too time-consuming to support the typical rapid expansion in munificent industries, the reliance on IT outsourcing will be high in such industries. Second, the results also demonstrate that industry dynamism is positively associated with IT outsourcing. The option view of governance posits that outsourcing goods or services through a contract-based market arrangement can be regarded as a defer option, which is of great value in dynamic environments [62]. Therefore, compared with in-house IT investments, IT outsourcing is a preferred alternative in dynamic industries because it offers more flexibility when either market demands or IT are in flux.

Third, our results indicate that industry concentration is negatively correlated with the use of IT outsourcing. Prior studies have attributed the use of IT outsourcing to replicating behaviors [54, 65] and cost savings [55, 64]. However, industry concentration may reduce a firm’s motivation to imitate and save on costs through IT outsourcing. More specifically, IT outsourcing utilization is less likely in concentrated industries, as firms in such industries usually have the power and resources to resist the institutional pressures to attempt to imitate IT outsourcing. In addition, firms in concentrated industries are highly profitable and are less likely to adopt outsourcing for cost-saving purposes.

Finally, our findings suggest that industry capital intensity is negatively associated with IT outsourcing. Firms in high capital-intensive industries do not value novel and risky practices as highly as those in low capital-intensive industries [22]. Compared with the in-house provision of IT services, IT outsourcing is a relatively novel practice in many industries and involves a fairly high level of risk [7, 9]. Because firms in capital-intensive industries tend to continue past practices and have less appreciation for novel and risky practices [22], they are less inclined to use IT outsourcing than firms in industries with low capital intensity.

## Research and Practical Implications

Research has shown that industry characteristics have important implications for ITrelated phenomena. For instance, a meta-analysis reported that industry differences contribute to the variation in IT payoffs across studies [50]. Firms in highly technical and institutional environments are thought to have a greater number of incentives to gather data and utilize online services than firms in low-tech, unregulated industries [58]. Likewise, announcements of IT investments were found to produce positive abnormal returns for firms in industries where IT plays a transformational role, but not in industries where IT plays either an automation or informational role [23].

Although the influence of industry on IS performance has been established, recent research has demonstrated that industry-level factors have received scant attention in previous IS studies [19]. For instance, only 4 percent of the IS studies published in major IS journals and conferences have been conducted at the industry level [21], and only 11 percent of papers published in two top-tier IS journals have either discussed the influence of industry environments on their findings or analyzed industry issues [19]. Consequently, scholars (e.g., [19, 21, 68]) have called for IS research to take industry environments into account, suggesting that the field should explicitly consider the role of industry. This provides an integral contextual space to develop a new IS theory and evaluate the boundaries of existing IS theory.

This study builds on prior IT outsourcing research by investigating how industry environments affect the use of IT outsourcing, based on a comprehensive conceptualization of industry. Our study contributes to the literature by improving our knowledge of the industry-level determinants of IT outsourcing. Further, this study complements prior firm-level research by providing a complementary perspective regarding the factors influencing the use of IT outsourcing in practice.

Furthermore, our conceptualization of industry may also provide a framework for the study of the role of industry in other IT-related phenomena. As mentioned above, environmental factors, such as industry and country characteristics, have received limited attention in IT research to date, in spite of the evidence that they can play a crucial role in explaining IT-related phenomena [19, 68]. For example, although a plethora of studies have investigated the business value of IT, little is known about how industry characteristics influence IT business value [68]. Researchers may use our framework in the future to investigate how industry characteristics influence other IT-related phenomena, such as IT productivity and business value.

Overall, this study has three main implications for firms. First, IT outsourcing can produce numerous strategic and operational benefits (e.g., cost savings and IT competence improvement) due to the economies of scale and the complementary set of core competencies developed by IT vendors [28, 63]. In this respect, firms should consider utilizing IT outsourcing to improve performance. However, firms should take the characteristics of their industries into account when making sourcing decisions. For instance, it may be more beneficial for firms in highly dynamic environments to use IT outsourcing, as it can also serve as an option to defer in-house IT investments, thus providing flexibility to deal with environmental dynamism. In contrast, firms in capital-intensive industries may need to reconsider their IT outsourcing decisions, as outsourcing may introduce risks that may threaten the value of large, fixed assets.

Second, firms often imitate other firms’ use of IT outsourcing to avoid the uncertainty and complexity involved in typical IT-related decisions [54, 65]. This current study suggests that managers should be mindful about duplicating other firms’ outsourcing decisions, as firms from diverse industries are motivated to use IT outsourcing for various reasons. While firms in munificent industries may use IT outsourcing to support swift growth, firms in dynamic industries may adopt IT outsourcing to avoid the risk of long-term sunk costs that are typically associated with in-house IT investments. Yet firms in other industries may use IT outsourcing either as a response to institutional pressures (e.g., in low-concentration intensity industries) or to achieve a certain degree of flexibility (e.g., in low capital-intensive industries). Rather than assuming that IT outsourcing is universally beneficial, firms should take the differences among industry characteristics into account when making sourcing decisions.

Finally, this study offers managerial guidance to IT services vendors planning to expand their markets by entering into new industries. When facing the decision of whether to enter a new industry, IT vendors should consider industry characteristics, such as munificence and concentration. For instance, IT vendors may be inclined to target industries with high munificence and high dynamism, as firms in these industries are likely to use IT outsourcing aggressively. Conversely, IT vendors may enter industries with high concentration and high capital intensity with caution, given that such industries usually have a lower level of IT outsourcing adoption.

## Study Limitations and Future Research

This study has three limitations that may be overcome in future research. First, our study is based on industry-level data of IT outsourcing use, which may represent a firm-level phenomenon. Although industry-level data are appropriate for research involving variations due to industry-level factors, and enable us to isolate industry effects on firm behaviors as well [74], future research could retest our model using both firm- and industry-level data. Using a multilevel approach can provide additional insights into both the industry and firm levels, and can enhance our understanding of what drives IT outsourcing.

Second, the IT outsourcing expenditures used in this study are aggregate data, which include a variety of IT services (e.g., data processing, facilities management, and system design services). Research has found that different types of IT services may be influenced in various ways by environmental characteristics [4]. As a result, it may be interesting to investigate how industry characteristics affect a range of IT services. To do this, detailed information on IT services is needed. In addition, we measured IT outsourcing by the ratio of IT outsourcing expenditures to IT stock in this paper. Future research may use additional measures to validate our findings.

Finally, the industry-level variables used in this study were identified based on organization theory and industrial organization economics. Future research should identify other important industry-level variables using different paradigms such as social and institutional theories [19, 21]. Also, although the fixed effects model applied in this study can rule out the influence of any time-invariant factors on the use of IT outsourcing [39], other time-variant factors that would also impact IT outsourcing probably exist. Future research should include additional control variables drawn from different theoretical models.

## Conclusion

This study aimed to exp lore the influence of industry environments on IT outsourcing decisions. Although numerous studies have examined the diverse aspects of IT outsourcing–related issues, the majority of studies have focused solely on the firm-level phenomenon. Consequently, there is a paucity of research available on IT outsourcing at the industry level. Our results suggest that industry characteristics indeed play a crucial role in determining IT outsourcing use; while both industry munificence and dynamism are positively associated with the use of IT outsourcing, industry concentration and capital intensity are negatively related to such IT sourcing mechanisms. To understand IT outsourcing in a holistic manner, researchers should attend to the factors specific to a firm (e.g., IT cost structures and financial situations) as well as the contextual factors that are embedded in industry environments. In fact, a firm’s strategic decisions and outcomes should be understood and interpreted not only by a firm’s internal conditions but also by the industry environments that shape and govern individual firms’ behaviors. We encourage future studies to examine IT-related phenomena at multiple levels, as doing so would allow us to see both the “trees” (e.g., individual firm’s behaviors) and the “forest” (e.g., industry environments).
