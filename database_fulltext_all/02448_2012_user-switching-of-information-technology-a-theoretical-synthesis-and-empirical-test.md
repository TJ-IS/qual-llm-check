# User switching of information technology: A theoretical synthesis and empirical test

## A B S T R A C T

As competing technological choices emerge in the marketplace, an inevitable consequence is user switching from one IT product or service to another. However, we know very little of this phenomenon or the key drivers of user switching. We examined the reasons for IT switching by proposing a model that synthesized and extended current theories of IT acceptance and customer satisfaction. We believed that IT switching was driven by user dissatisfaction with an incumbent product or service and the fact that the customer was aware of the availability of a potentially superior product or service. Further, to explain differential switching patterns across an adopter population, we posited personal innovativeness as an individual difference variable that moderated the effect of user dissatisfaction and relative advantage of a new IT on users’ switching intention. Our model was empirically validated using a longitudinal survey of web browser switching among student subjects. We hope that our study will generate researchers interest in an understudied area of research and that the model will serve as a starting point for furthering our understanding of IT switching.

## 1. Introduction

Today, IT innovations have proliferated and so have the number of incidences of users switching from one IT to that of a competitor. Users can freely switch between different IT products, such as web browsers (e.g., Internet Explorer, Firefox), operating systems (e.g., Windows, Linux), office productivity software (e.g., Microsoft Office, Linux), and virus scanners (e.g., Mcafee, Norton), and IT services, such as broadband Internet access, online banking. New IT choices are only a click away on the Internet, and switching to a competing IT is almost as easy as downloading and installing it, or completing an online registration form to sign up for a different service.

User switching involves a complete or partial replacement of the use of one IT product or service with a substitute that serves similar needs. IT switching is somewhat different from switching of other products and services, in that it allows for partial switching: the user may choose to use both the old and the new IT concurrently for a period of time (while evaluating the new IT) or permanently (e.g., for different sets of tasks). For instance, rather than switch completely from Microsoft Windows to Linux, some users may decide to implement a dual-boot system with separate memory partitions for Windows and Linux, allowing them to use both operating systems and their applications as needed. However, despite the increased incidence and relevance of such switching phenomenon, our understanding of it has remained scant. Much of the previous research has focused on users’ acceptance of IT products and services or their decision to continue using it. Obviously, some users consider alternative choices, or upgrade from one IT vendor’s product to another (e.g., from Microsoft Windows XP to Windows 7) or a different vendor (e.g., from Microsoft Windows to Linux).

The purpose of our paper was to develop and test a model of a user’s IT switching behavior, specifically focusing on why some, who are already using a certain IT product or service, decide to switch to a competing one. From a theoretical perspective, there is clearly a need to build a model that can help explain switching behavior. Our model can serve as a starting point for understanding IT switching behavior by incorporating the role of supply side effects (e.g., the availability of competing products and services) into a model that has previously only focused on the demand side (i.e., user needs and preferences).

## 2. Prior research

Prior research on IT switching [4,5] has been scant, although there has been a long tradition of switching research in the relationship marketing literature.

## 2.1. IT switching research

Chen and Hitt [2] examined switching of online brokerage services using secondary data from Media Metrix and website ratings of these firms from Gomez Advisors, and found that users’ switching behavior can be predicted by their usage patterns and website characteristics such as ease of use, quality, and breadth of service offerings. However, their analysis was conducted at the firm level rather than at the individual level. Ranganathan et al. [8] studied customer switching of mobile digital services using secondary data from a vendor’s customer database and call log files, and inferred that the extent of their usage, length of service, and number of subscribed services were negatively related to the users’ switching behavior.

These studies are indicative of the sparse, fragmented, and noncumulative nature of prior IT switching research which may be due to a lack of an overall theoretical framework to guide the researchers’ choice of constructs and hypotheses relevant for switching research, as well as understand why and how the constructs are related.

## 2.2. Switching research in the marketing literature

Brand and/or service switching research in marketing can be grouped into two categories of studies: those that evaluated mathematical models of product or service switching, and those that evaluated switching predictors. Initial models of brand switching were examined using an innovation diffusion framework based solely on consumers’ awareness and attitude toward the new product or service brand, though later papers suggested that consumers’ future choice behavior was influenced by their prior choices (their satisfaction with them), in addition to their knowledge of alternatives (thus switching may have occurred when a consumer was dissatisfied with his or her current brand or service and was aware of substitutes). The second category of switching attempted to identify factors related to consumers’ switching decisions; they revealed several interesting patterns that may help explain IT switching. This research found that switching occurs when consumers are dissatisfied with their current choice and are aware of substitute choices as potential replacements for their current choice. Furthermore, it seems that individual differences, such as variety seeking propensity, may explain individual-level variations, given comparable experience with an existing product or service and a knowledge of substitutes across the adopter population.

## 3. Theory and hypotheses

Theories of IT acceptance, such as the UTAUT [10], suggest that the prime predictor of users’ IT acceptance behaviors is their intention to accept a new IT. A positive relationship between intention and behavior has been shown in many empirical studies of. This generalized relationship should also hold true in the context of IT switching. This expectation, shown in Fig. 1, leads to our first hypothesis:

H1. Potential switchers’ intention to switch to a new IT is positively related to their actual IT switching behavior.

Switching research in marketing has also suggested that individual difference variables may play a role shaping users’ IT switching behavior.

## 3.1. Relative advantage

UTAUT hypothesized that three perceptions of IT use were the key predictors of users’ IT acceptance intention:

\- performance expectancy (or perceived usefulness – the extent to which potential IT users expect performance improvements due to the use of the new IT);

\- effort expectancy (or ease of use – the extent to which they expect IT usage to be free of effort); and

\- social influence (or subjective norms – pressure they perceive from others expecting them to use the IT).

Of these three, only performance expectancy has been found to be a stable and consistent predictor of IT acceptance over time and across studies. Indeed, the effect of effort expectancy has been found to decay over time as users gain proficiency in IT usage [3], and is unlikely to play a significant role in users IT switching decisions, especially if the new IT is similar to the prior IT and users prior use of the IT have made them familiar with similar technologies. Also, social norms have also been observed to have an inconsistent effect on the use of IT for personal use and may also be considered to be less relevant for switching of personal use IT.

Parallel research on technology adoption based on innovation diffusion theory (IDT) has also found three technology-related perceptions as being related to its adoption:

\- relative advantage (the extent to which a new technology is perceived as being beneficial over the preceding one – thus similar to performance expectancy);

\- complexity (the difficulty in using it – similar to effort expectancy); and

\- compatibility (the extent to which using the target technology is viewed as being compatible with the user’s beliefs, values, and work patterns).

The effect of compatibility on IT acceptance intentions may be assumed to be fully mediated by performance expectancy, because rational IT users could not expect a new IT to be useful or beneficial if it is incompatible with their beliefs, values, or work patterns. Thus, the predominant predictor of new IT acceptance should be performance expectancy or relative advantage, which should also play a key role in users’ decisions to switch to a new IT. While performance expectancy from new IT usage may be judged independent of previous IT during IT acceptance decisions, for IT switching decisions, such an assessment must be made relative to the current IT. Thus, in order to capture this distinction more clearly, we chose to use the term relative advantage in our discussion of the differential benefits that may be expected from switching from an old IT to a new IT. The benefits may include productivity gains, better access to information, and better decision making. In contrast, there would be no reason to switch to a new IT if it did not have some incremental benefit over the old IT. Thus we hypothesized:

H2. Perceptions of the relative advantage of using a new IT is positively related to potential switchers’ intention to switch to the new IT.

## 3.2. Satisfaction with prior IT

Since IT switching requires user acceptance of the new IT and discontinuance of the old IT, constructs that are salient to users discontinuance decisions should also be considered as key factors shaping the IT switching behavior. Although users can switch ‘‘partially’’ by simultaneously using the old and new IT, the extent to which switching has materialized still depends on user willingness to abandon their ‘‘legacy’’ use of the old IT. The Expectation Disconfirmation Model (EDM) suggests that users’ intention to continue or discontinuing IT usage is based on the extent to which they are satisfied with using that technology. Satisfaction is based on the user’s direct, first-hand experience with using the IT [9]. Satisfied users tend to continue using their current IT and are unlikely to switch, even if the new IT is better. However, dissatisfied users are likely to abandon their current IT and switch. Users’ satisfaction should therefore be negatively correlated with their intention to adopt incremental IT innovations. Hence, we hypothesized:

H3. Potential switchers’ satisfaction with using an incumbent IT is negatively related to their intention to switch to a new IT.

## 3.3. Personal innovativeness

Switching research in marketing suggests that a user population faced with common incumbent and substitute technologies may exhibit differential patterns of switching based on individual differences; the one which appears to be most important in the context of IT usage is personal innovativeness (one’s propensity to try out or experiment with new IT). This tends to be distributed unevenly across the adopter population, which explains why some people are ‘‘early innovators’’ while others are ‘‘laggards’’ in adopting new IT.

IT switching is inherently risky behavior, whose outcomes are often unknown [7]. Individuals who are highly innovative and enjoy experimenting with new products or services may be open to switching to a new IT if they see it as better than their current IT, of if they are only slightly dissatisfied with it. Hence, personal innovativeness can be expected to strengthen the positive effect of the relative advantage of new IT and offset the negative effect of user satisfaction with prior IT on IT switching intention. This lead us to hypothesize:

H4. Potential switchers’ personal innovativeness positively moderates the association between relative advantage of a new IT and their intention to switch to that IT.

H5. Potential switchers’ personal innovativeness negatively moderates the association between their satisfaction with an incumbent IT and their intention to switch to a new IT.

## 3.4. Habit

Limayem et al. [6] showed that IT users who have been using a certain technology for some time are predisposed to continue using it in an automatic and unthinking manner. Depending on their nature and the extent of the habit, they are less likely to switch to a new IT. Although there is some debate on whether habit directly or indirectly influences behavior via intention, it is clear in that habit represents automatic and subconscious use, in contrast to intention, which involves reasoned and conscious use. Since it is less reasonable for conscious behavior to mediate subconscious behavior, we believed that habit directly influenced IT switching behavior, independent of the effect of conscious intention. This caused us to posit:

H6. Potential switchers’ habit regarding IT usage is negatively related to their actual IT switching behavior.

In summary, we integrated the IT acceptance and continuance modes and extended them to include the effect of individual difference on IT switching behavior. It should be noted that our model is only preliminary, being based on our limited understanding of the phenomenon.

## 4. Research method

Our model was tested empirically using a longitudinal survey of web browser switching among a population of students at a university in Switzerland.

## 4.1. Data collection

Subject participation was voluntary. Business students could earn class credit by participating in this study or by choosing to do another assignment. We examined subjects’ perceptions and behavior related to switching from Microsoft Internet Explorer or Mozilla Firefox web browser as their incumbent IT to the Opera web browser (http://www.opera.com/) as the new IT.

At the time of the study, Internet Explorer was the dominant web browser in use at this university, and had been used by the subjects for searching the Internet, accessing news and other content, and conducting online transactions. While some of them had used Firefox (an open source browser), very few had heard of Opera, and none had used it. Though most of Opera’s capabilities, such as tabbed browsing, support for scripts, and bookmarks, were similar to those of Internet Explorer and Firefox, Opera provided some additional benefits, such as text to speech conversion, foreign language translation, and a small efficient download. Thus, it we assumed that some subjects might find Opera attractive and switch to it or that those who had unfavorable experiences with Internet Explorer would opt to switch to it. Further, Opera was a free software, and there was no costs in using it.

Data were collected twice in the course of our study. The first round was conducted near the start of an academic semester, following a two-hour training session in which subjects were introduced to the Opera browser, provided a comparison of its features and functionality (comparing it to the Internet Explorer), and were guided though the process of downloading, installing, and using it. At the end of this session, subjects were asked to complete a questionnaire that asked them about their perceptions of the Opera system and Internet Explorer or Firefox browser that they had used. The second round of data collection was conducted four weeks later, when subjects were asked questions about to their perceptions of the Opera web browser based on their work with it in the interim period, any residual use of their previous browser, and their perceived habit of using their prior browser. The last four digits of the subjects’ mobile phone numbers were used to match their answers across the two rounds of data collection. A total of 188 participants answered the first questionnaire, and 97 completed both rounds of data collection.

## 4.2. Operationalization of constructs

The six constructs of interest to this study were relative advantage, satisfaction, personal innovativeness, IT switching intention, habit and IT switching behavior. The first four were measured using the first survey, and habit and IT switching behavior were assessed using the second survey. All constructs were measured using multiple-item perceptual scales, using prevalidated instruments from prior research wherever possible. Item wording was adapted slightly to fit the specific context of Opera usage. The items are shown in Table 1.

Relative advantage was measured using a five-item Likert scale. These items examined the extent to which subjects believed that using Opera would help them accomplish tasks more quickly, improve the quality of their work, make their work easier, enhance their effectiveness on the job, and provide them with greater control over their work, relative to their current browser. Satisfaction was assessed using a four-item semantic differential scale. These items captured subjects’ satisfaction with their incumbent browser along four adjective pairs: dissatisfactory– satisfactory, unpleasant–pleasant, frustrating–enjoyable, and terrible–delightful. Personal innovativeness was measured with three items in a Likert scale, that examined the extent to which subjects liked experimenting with new IT, whether they were one of the earliest among their peers to test new IT, and their eagerness in working with a new IT. Habit was measured using Limayem et al.’s [6] four-item Likert scale.

Since IT switching intention had not been extensively studied in prior literature, we created a new three-item Likert scale for this construct, loosely patterned after existing scales of IT acceptance [10]. Since IT switching can be viewed as a combination of discontinuing a previous IT and starting a new IT, the first two items in this scale examined the extent to which subjects believed that they were likely to use Opera and decrease their use of the previous browser within the next two weeks, while the third item examined subjects’ overall intent to switch from the old to the new browser. The IT switching behavior construct, measured during the second survey, used two similar items that captured the number of times and the number of hours subjects used Opera over the onemonth period after the first survey.

Data analysis proceeded in two stages: (1) measurement modeling, for psychometric assessment of our measurement scales, and (2) structural modeling, for hypotheses testing. A PLS approach was used for both analyses, using PLS-Graph version 3.00 software. This provides more accurate estimates of the paths among latent constructs that have been measured using multiple items; they tend to be biased downward by measurement error when analyzed by multiple regression techniques.

## 5.1. Measurement model

The goal of measurement modeling was to assess the adequacy and validity of our measurement scales. Convergent validity was measured using three criteria: all scale items should have significant and high loadings (greater than 0.70) on their hypothesized scales, the average variance extracted (AVE) for each construct should exceed 0.50, and composite reliability of each construct should exceed 0.80. Results of our analysis are shown in Tables 2 and 3. All scale items had path loadings exceeding 0.70, and were significant at the 0.01 level. Further, all of these scales had AVE exceeding 0.50 and composite reliability exceeding 0.80. Hence, all three conditions of convergent validity were met in our sample.

Discriminant validity is assured when the squared root of the AVE for each construct is higher than the bivariate correlations between that and all other constructs. As shown in Table 3, this condition was met for all six of our reflective constructs. These results provided confidence that our measurement scales were theoretically and empirically adequate.

Our proposed model explained 54% of the variance in IT switching intention, compared to 48% for the baseline model and 52% for the alternative model (see Table 4). Although the differences in variance explained among these models are not large, nevertheless, they are statistically significant with an effect size f of 0.05 (a mediumsized effect). Given that it is extremely difficult to detect significant moderating effects in small samples, the above findings suggest that perhaps individual difference variables such as personal innovativeness do play a role in shaping one’s IT switching intention.

## 6. Discussion and implications

## 6.1. Limitations of the study

The first limitation of our study is our use of student subjects. It can be argued that findings inferred from a student sample are not generalizable to a population of organizational users. However, many prior studies of IT acceptance and continuance (e.g., [1]) have successfully employed student subjects and the inferences were not significantly different from those when employing users in organizational settings.

Second, it is plausible that our model and inferences based on a study of web browser switching may not be generalizable to other personal-use or organizational IT. Web browsers have been studied in prior IT usage research and they are fairly simple and easy to use. Effort expectancy may play a more central role in switching from more complex technologies such as ERP or HRM systems. Further, most current web browsers have fairly comparable features. Thus for IT choices exhibiting a stronger disparity in relative advantage, the effect of relative advantage on switching intention may be expected to be stronger than that reported in our study.

Third, our dependent variable (IT switching) was measured using self-reported data, which may be susceptible to perceptual and recall bias, and may be subject to common method bias if independent variables are also measured perceptually, we had no choice but to rely on such data given the nature of our target IT (web browser) for which subjects’ behavioral data could not be tracked or stored in a central location. Further, the temporal separation (one month) of our measurement of the independent and dependent variables reduced the chance of common method bias, while this separation was short and should not result in recall bias. Fourth, there may be additional predictors of IT switching beyond those examined in our study that may be explored in future studies.

## 6.2. Implications for research

First, this study directed researchers’ attention to this emerging yet ignored problem of IT switching. IT switching offers a fertile and potentially insightful area of research. Further, if switching between similar products is viewed as an instance of adopting an incremental innovation, in contrast to radical innovation adoption or first-time adoption of a brand new IT, understanding IT switching can provide some insight into the little-understood dynamics of incremental innovation adoption.

Second, we demonstrated how our current theories of IT acceptance and continuance can be integrated to explain the related but different phenomenon of IT switching. While further modification may be needed to our model, our study served as a demonstration on how to build new theories by leveraging knowledge of existing theories.

The third contribution of our study was its construction and validation of a model of IT switching which suggested key reasons why IT users switch to competing, and why switching patterns may vary across a population of users.

## 6.3. Implications for practice

IT switching is inevitable as competing technological options emerge in the marketplace. In order to retain customer base and market share, IT vendors must recognize the potential challenge due to IT switching.

Our study suggests that IT users switch when they are dissatisfied with their current IT and/or find a competing IT that seems better than their current IT. Since dissatisfaction is contingent on an existing product or service, IT vendors should try to minimize user dissatisfaction, or try to reduce its impact by resolving any issues at the core of the dissatisfaction, such as by providing free or inexpensive product upgrades or value-added services.
