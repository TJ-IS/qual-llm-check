# Collaborative technologies in knowledge telework: an exploratory study

France Bélanger\* & Christopher D Allport<sup>†</sup>

\*Pamplin College of Business, Virginia Tech, 3007 Pamplin Hall, Blacksburg, VA 24061- 0101, USA, email: belanger@vt.edu, and <sup>†</sup>College of Administrative Science, University of Alabama in Huntsville, 301 Sparkman Drive, ASB 350, Huntsville, AL 35899, USA, email: allporc@uah.edu

Abstract. Knowledge workers in distributed work environments require substantial communication with colleagues and supervisors to perform their work activities, and collaborative technologies, like groupware, continue to improve the potential for such communication. This study investigates how an improvement in collaborative technology among a group of knowledge teleworkers impacts their communication patterns. The study focuses on one communication-based work process: knowledge sharing. The research involves a 6-month exploratory longitudinal case study of a group of teleworkers where a new groupware technology was implemented. The results indicate that while there are fewer instances of communication among group members after the technology is implemented, communication becomes more centralized around the district manager. These results suggest that increased collaborative technologies result in an adjustment between explicit and tacit knowledge sharing, as the availability of a centralized and updateable database reduces workgroup communications. However, consistent with structuration theory, telework group members do use communication technologies to address their most apparent needs and concerns. Other potential explanations and implications of these findings are discussed.

Keywords: telework, telecommuting, collaborative technology, Lotus Notes, communication patterns, knowledge sharing

## INTRODUCTION

There is no such thing as pure technology. To understand technology, one must first understand social relationships. (Contractor & Eisenberg, 1990, p. 143)

Approximately 4.5 million Americans are teleworking everyday (Balaker, 2005), and there were 44.4 million Americans working at least 1 day a week from home in 2004 (Smith, 2004). In the United Kingdom, there were 2.2 million people teleworking in 2003, up 65% during the previous

4 years. Telework involves working away from the regular workplace using communication technologies to maintain a link to the office. It provides workers with more control and flexibility, which can increase job satisfaction, productivity and performance (Bélanger et al., 2001). Historically, the option to telework has been restricted to tasks with clearly defined and measurable outputs; however, knowledge workers, with more complex and conceptual tasks, increasingly choose to telework (Li, 1998). Knowledge workers are involved with the creation, collation, manipulation, transformation and/or dissemination of information (Watson-Fritz et al., 1998). As knowledge workers, teleworkers require considerable communication to perform their jobs because information gathering and knowledge sharing are key activities defining knowledge work (Long, 1987).

Technologies such as cellular phones, wireless networks, portable computers and high bandwidth networks provide an open and global link for virtual workers who need to communicate with each other and their central office. Collaborative technologies, such as groupware, enable distributed colleagues to communicate, structure and share information anytime and anywhere. Given that such technologies play an important role in telework, it is crucial to obtain an in-depth understanding of their impact within this context. Our study investigates how a collaborative technology used within a work group of teleworkers involved in knowledge work might change their communication patterns, and explores potential outcomes of these changes for the organization and the teleworkers.

## BACKGROUND LITERATURE

## Teleworking

Teleworking, also known as telecommuting, involves working away from the traditional office using computers and telecommunication facilities to maintain a link to the office (Bélanger & Collins, 1998). In today’s environments, more and more teleworkers are involved in knowledge work (Li, 1998; Chudoba et al., 2005), which involves collecting, disseminating and sharing information. This work is often non-routine with large amounts of task variety and uncertainty, and with tasks that are often complex and involve ambiguous outputs. Examples of knowledge work include insurance claim processing, sales, university research, technology support, and so on. No matter which kind, knowledge work requires a substantial amount of information gathering and knowledge sharing, and therefore a considerable amount of communication. However, communication is commonly considered a key challenge in the telework domain (Bélanger, 1999). In fact, research suggests that as communication requirements in telework increase, individuals report lower levels of productivity and performance (Bélanger et al., 2001). This is due to the fact that teleworkers access information and communicate with colleagues from a variety of different physical locations, possibly at different times, and work no longer occurs in a shared context as in the traditional office. While communication difficulties impact productivity and performance, it is also probable that they relate to other problematic outcomes like teleworker isolation and organizational monitoring difficulties.

## Knowledge sharing

In a global business environment, communication-based work processes, such as coordination, relationship development and knowledge sharing, are central to organizational success (Watson-Manheim & Belanger, 2002). In this research, we focus on the work process of knowledge sharing. Knowledge sharing has been described as ‘the dissemination of information and knowledge’ throughout a department or organization (Yang, 2004, p. 119). More specifically, knowledge sharing is the process of supplying organizational knowledge to employees to improve effectiveness, and encouraging the sharing of knowledge between employees to spur innovation. Rafaeli & Ravid (2003) note that workers across organizations generally have incomplete knowledge sets, and that information sharing is essential for efficiency, flexibility, learning and understanding.

Knowledge sharing is generally analysed based on two types of organizational knowledge: explicit and tacit (Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995).<sup>1</sup> Explicit knowledge is documented knowledge, generally available in rules, procedures, manuals and databases. Explicit knowledge is first filtered to determine the appropriate knowledge stock, and then it is shared within the organization. Tacit knowledge is not documented, and it is very specific to the individual and situation (Nonaka, 1991). Sharing tacit knowledge is more difficult than explicit knowledge sharing, in that the emphasis should not be on knowledge stock, but rather knowledge flow. Facilitating such knowledge flow can only be accomplished through enhancing all types of communication (Fahey & Prusak, 1998). While knowledge sharing is extremely important (Rafaeli & Ravid, 2003; Yang, 2004), the telework environment presents unique opportunities and challenges to this communication-based work process. Raghuram (1996) notes that while the distributed work environment generally provides opportunities for knowledge stock via more technological capabilities, the inherent problems of communication and socialization present serious difficulties for tacit knowledge flow.

## Communication in groups

In organizations today, communication-based work activities are often supported by information technology (Kock & McQueen, 1998; Furst et al., 1999; Watson-Manheim & Belanger, 2002) and in distributed settings, such technologies are essential tools for supporting group communication and integrating geographically dispersed knowledge workers (Fulk & De-Sanctis, 1995; Warkentin & Beranek, 1999; Chudoba et al., 2005). Because of the importance of communication-based work processes, such as knowledge sharing, both formal and informal communication networks develop from on-going organizational communications. Communication networks are patterns that define which individuals in which positions in an organization send or receive what kind of information or messages (Zey-Ferrell, 1979). Formal networks (typically based on organizational charts) tend to vary in structure much less than informal networks, which arise naturally out of relationships among individuals (Rogers &

Kincaid, 1981). For organizations, communication structures serve as mechanisms to integrate tasks, and poor structure may impact long-run efficiency. In addition, communication structures serve as mechanisms for knowledge sharing. Even for independent-task workers (with low coordination requirements), communication networks are essential as vehicles for sharing jobrelated knowledge, or organizational identity. However, in the telework environment, group members might be kept out of the communication network (Bélanger, 1999). Yet, considering the importance of these networks for tacit knowledge sharing, analysing communication networks in a distributed work environment provides an opportunity to better understand where deficiencies in tacit knowledge sharing may exist.

## Collaborative technologies

The distributed work environment is particularly dependent on communication-based technologies (Chudoba et al., 2005), and one type of communication tool increasingly used in organizations for knowledge sharing is a set of applications known as collaborative technologies. These technologies allow group members to more easily share files, information and ideas. While collaborative technologies encompass many applications (e.g. email, voice mail, etc.), groupware is what most individuals think of when considering collaborative technologies. Groupware is designed to remove communication barriers, enhance group qualities and improve group interaction (DeSanctis & Gallupe, 1987). Groupware has been recognized as a way to facilitate communication, as well as enhance group coordination and communication modes (Coleman, 1997). In addition, Bhatt et al. (2005) argue that groupware technology facilitates information sharing and manipulation by increasing communications within organizations. Based on the opportunities provided by groupware technology, this research seeks to better understand how these technological advancements impact the communication structure of a group of teleworkers.

In this study, we are using an exploratory case study to consider the effects of improved collaborative technologies on the communication patterns between teleworkers who require knowledge sharing for advice, mentoring and feedback. The collaborative technology is a groupware application (Lotus Notes), which integrates email with document-based databases and allows group workflow applications to be developed. It supports group collaboration and asynchronous communication, providing the means for knowledge workers to communicate while at a distance, regardless of time differences, and provides a location (e.g. a document database) for knowledge to be stored and easily searched.

## METHODOLOGY

To develop a better understanding of how the implementation of collaborative technologies affects communication patterns of individuals in a real organizational setting, we use an exploratory case study approach (Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991; Yin, 1994). This research design allows for an initial understanding of complex phenomenon in a natural and realistic setting. It can be used for theory development, or for testing the impact of some factors on variables of interest, as in this study. The current research analyses an information technology implementation and its effects on communications between teleworkers who are members of a workgroup. Analysing the impact of a technological adjustment for multiple individuals embedded in a complex organizational setting would be extraordinarily difficult in an experimental setting. In addition, the case study approach allows for the careful evaluation of communication patterns and technology use over an extended period of time. Based on these advantages, the case study approach was chosen as the most appropriate research design.

The work group included 17 individuals from a large American insurance corporation from two organizational levels: professional and manager/supervisor. Participation was voluntary (the change of technology, however, was mandatory). Following the case study approach, we used multiple methods of data collection (Yin, 1994), including interviews and surveys. The pre-change data were collected 1 month prior to the technology change, and the post-change data were collected 6 months later. Finally, the pre-change and post-change data were matched to better assess the individual and group impacts of the technology change.

## Sample

Group members were involved in the loss prevention sector of the insurance business. Loss prevention specialists (LPSs) advise clients on how to lower risk and thereby avoid or contain losses. They go to customer sites and analyse claims made by insured individuals. The responsibilities of an LPS include contacting clients, scheduling at least three visits per day, and reporting back to underwriting, claims and loss prevention sectors. The job is designed to be handled mostly by ‘on the road’ workers, with the exception of the group leader and consultants.

Loss prevention specialists provide a special opportunity to analyse the effect collaborative technologies have on knowledge sharing because the need for other communication-based processes is limited. The LPSs’ job is very much independent in terms of shared tasks with work group colleagues. As such, there are typically few conflicts to resolve via communication technologies, and low work group coordination (note: in order to validate the low coordination requirements for LPSs, we measured coordination levels in the work group). Finally, while relationship development could be important to these distributed professionals, we designed the communication part of the survey so that respondents focused on work-related communications only. However, there is a need for LPSs to share work-related information. This information may include advice on how to handle a specific situation, or information on new systems, laws and other findings that can help identify new risks or new solutions or methods to reduce those risks. As such, communication requirements for knowledge sharing are much higher than for other communication-based work processes.

## Interviews

Interviews were conducted with the information technology director, the loss prevention sector manager and the group leader, as well as employee group members. The interviews had several purposes: (1) to obtain full access to the work group for their participation in the survey; (2) to obtain a better working knowledge of the expectations of management with respect to telework, the role of the work group and the reasons for the technology change; and (3) to obtain details regarding the technologies used, with both pre- and post-change configurations. In addition, the interviews provided an opportunity to determine the perceived needs of employees before the technology change, as well as their perceived satisfaction and effectiveness after the change.

## Survey

The survey instrument was developed using mostly existing measures. It contained five sections measuring demographics, technology availability, coordination, communication and outcome measures. Demographics were used to classify participants and technology availability was used to compare pre- and post-change configurations. As explained before, coordination was measured to validate the low interdependence of the LSPs’ jobs, and communication measures were used for the network analyses. We also measured outcomes (productivity, performance and satisfaction) to get feedback from respondents on the overall effect the technology change had on them. Coordination was measured using the validated Van de Ven et al.’s (1976) scale of group coordination. Outcome measures were taken from scales validated in a study of impacts of telework on productivity, performance and satisfaction (Bélanger et al., 2001). Coordination and outcomes were measured on 7-point Likert-type scales. Communication measures are described below. Demographics and technology availability sections offered lists of choices.

The instrument was used in a prior and larger scale study that investigated the impacts of technology on telecommuting (Bélanger et al., 2001). The instrument was pre-tested twice with some rewording done to eliminate confusing items and to evaluate the amount of time required to complete it. It was then pilot tested. Because of the small sample size for this case analysis, we report the validity and reliability numbers obtained in the prior study. The instrument’s reliability and validity were evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha and confirmatory factor analysis in SPSS for Windows. Cronbach’s alpha resulted in reliabilities of 0.91, 0.80, 0.82 and 0.72 for performance, productivity, satisfaction and coordination, respectively. These are above the recommended threshold of 0.70 (Nunnally, 1978). To ensure the unidimensionality of the scales, confirmatory factor analysis was used. As a result of cross-loadings, one item each had been dropped from satisfaction, productivity and performance in the larger scale research. We used the reduced version of the scales which shows the high reliability and construct validity for this study.

The communication section of the survey contained a list of work group members (Figure 1). There are several ways to measure communications in a network analysis. The most basic measure is to look at who communicates with whom, without looking at how often they communicate (i.e. yes/no). A more advanced network analysis requires individuals to indicate the frequency of communications they have with every other participant in the network. As done in prior research (Wigand, 1988), we used a seven-point frequency scale ranging from ‘never’ to ‘several times a day or more’, as shown in Figure 1. Responses were entered into matrices, with rows indicating ‘who’ communicates with ‘whom’ (columns). Reconciliation was performed using the replication approach, a standard approach in which symmetry is forced by averaging conflicting values (Bélanger, 1999). From these measures, communication network analyses were conducted and plots were produced.

Network analysis provides a useful way to understand communication patterns in a work group. In a communication network, each individual is an actor or node. Network analysis looks at the patterns of linkages among actors by mapping all the communications of network members with each other. Some popular metrics include density, reachability, size and centrality. Centrality, a measure of ‘the number of direct contacts’ an individual has with others in the network (Monge & Eisenberg, 1987, p. 316), was selected to describe the communication patterns of individuals in their work groups. It indicates the degree to which communications are centred on certain individuals in the network. In highly hierarchical networks centralization usually occurs around the manager. When computing degree of centrality, we also obtain the network centralization measure. It indicates an overall level of centralization in the network by looking at the percentage of communications. The percentage of network centralization can be greater than 100% when value data are used (i.e. using frequency matrices (0–6 from Figure 1) instead of binary matrices where there are either no communications (0) or some (1)). In other words, this indicates that ‘the data contain edge weights (e.g. tie strengths) where centralization is not constrained to 0 to 1 interval. The situation is analogous to computing density. Density can be seen as the average value in a graph’s adjacency matrix. This average is between 0 and 1 when matrix entries are 0s or 1s. When there are other values, the average is not constrained’ (Borgatti et al., 1992). Finally, we also measured network density, a procedure that computes the number of linkages in the network over the total number of possible connections. It provides, on average, a measure of the extent to which individuals are connected to one another.

Analysis of network data can be done via matrix analysis, factor analysis, block modelling techniques, multidimensional techniques, cluster analysis or specific software packages like NEGOPY (Wigand, 1988; Bélanger, 1999) or UCINET (Borgatti et al., 1992). The package UCINET IV was used for network analysis in this research primarily because of its strength at providing metrics for small- to medium-size communication networks. Larger networks would require the use a software package such as NEGOPY (Wigand, 1988). UCINET is menudriven, and contains routines for network analysis, as well as other multivariate tools such as multidimensional scaling and cluster analysis. The data for analyses were first converted from Microsoft Excel spreadsheets to the format required by UCINET.

## Interview results

## Managers

The manager of the loss prevention sector was pressed by higher management, together with colleagues, to increase telework in the company. His group was selected to become the first fully automated group. Better collaborative and communication technologies were to be implemented for the group, with hopes that successful results would lead to more groups being equipped the same way. The two main reasons provided by the various managers for greater implementation of telework were (1) to increase productivity by not requiring employees to come to the office, and (2) to reduce office space requirements by eventually using a hotel setting, or simply not having offices for full-time teleworkers. The manager was aware of success stories of space savings by large corporations. However, he was personally somewhat uncomfortable with the concept, asking the researcher ‘how do I know that my employees are doing their job instead of goofing off when I can’t see them?’ The IT director, on the other hand, was given the mandate of implementing improved wireless technologies so that an increasing number of employees could work from their homes. The IT manager felt this was a great opportunity.

## Employees

Prior to the change, employees had a variety of workstations: some portable, some not; some powerful, some not. The application software installed on the old workstations provided menubased access to a remote mainframe. Some employees were having trouble accessing necessary programmes and information because of limited computing power. Others felt that the information available was limited, and that this adversely affected their productivity. Prior to the technology change, employees were dissatisfied with their computing power and with manager feedback. Work group members complained that the lack of appropriate technology made them less productive in the remote environment; for example:

[I] need faster response time from the computer. Have trouble at times getting into programs needed to obtain required information. (ID2)

The current system for remote users does not allow us to capture the necessary data we need to perform our job as efficiently as it should. (ID3)

We don’t have scheduled hours to use to access the mainframe and there are often times when the line is busy and I cannot get into the system. Printing report is very slow and time consuming. (ID14)

Everyone involved with the project believed that the proposed technology change would be a major improvement. The new system provided work group members with equivalent pre-configured leading edge portable computers with Lotus Notes, as well as wireless communication devices. This was part of a company plan to fully automate every component of the business transactions from the initial marketing call to policy creation and adjustments, and claim reporting and processing. The laptop interface gave access to the new centralized information database. All transaction components became fully accessible and viewable by employees while at customer sites. For example, claims transactions could be submitted from laptops, validated automatically at the company’s headquarters using expert systems algorithms, and audited online by designated company auditors. Finally, teleworkers were trained on how to use Lotus Notes. After the change there were no complaints about technology; in fact, one person mentioned that the technology was helping him accomplish his tasks.

The programs we have available to us make our job much easier . . . (ID3)

Prior to the technology change, communication was the second most mentioned complaint of participants. In particular, participants had some issues with supervisor feedback, and feelings of isolation.

The type of work sometimes makes it difficult to reach supervision for immediate answers or crisis situations. Another negative is the infrequency of knowing if professional developmen and performance is satisfactory. (ID10)

There is little feedback from supervision. This makes it difficult to know how you are doing until your review. (ID3)

After the change, there were no complaints about communication.

While communication structures may change following the implementation of a collaborative technology (which they did), it is important to see whether the change led to changes in effectiveness. There was a problem with evaluating effectiveness, however, because participants did not always relate the effectiveness directly to the technology or the communication structures but often to telework in general. Prior to the change, some telecommuters rated their effectiveness as generally high in the telework environment:

I am far more efficient in my work without the interruptions of co-workers and the latitude to ‘set my own schedule’ to some degree. (ID10)

My work environment allows me to be very efficient, creative, and provide for a high quality job. (ID11)

After the change, however, more respondents mentioned greater personal effectiveness:

Working from home allows you to do a better job, by allowing you to work at your own pace. (ID3)

There are many less distractions working at home. I believe we are more productive than in a corporate office setting. (ID7)

I feel I am productive in this environment because there are less interruptions and I’m able to manage my own time. (ID10)

Telecommuting provides an opportunity to be creative, self-sufficient, efficient, and highly productive. (ID11)

## Survey results

Given the small sample size of this case study, only the communication network data could be statistically analysed. However, findings regarding the measurements of coordination and effectiveness are provided for their descriptive values, which do provide some insights into the effects of the technology change on respondents.

To better understand the nature of the collaborative work in this group, we measured group coordination (Van de Ven et al., 1976). The average rating of worker coordination, based on the Van de Ven et al. (1976) scale, anecdotally supports the independent nature of these teleworkers, as the average is below the midpoint for both the before and after implementation surveys, e.g. the average rating for group coordination was 2.5 (on a 7-point Likert-type scale) before the technology change and 2.7 after the change.

Three outcomes were measured as perceived by the teleworkers before and after the change: performance, productivity and satisfaction. While these averages show some movement, no further analysis is possible because of the small sample sizes as we cannot perform statistical analysis on the differences between the before and after data.

The higher the degree of centrality of an individual, the higher his level of communication exchanges with others in the network. Table 4 presents the normalized degree of centrality for all individuals in the work group before and after the technology change. The data show that the district manager (DM; ID11) became more of a focal individual in the communication network after the change as his degree of centrality increased significantly compared with the other members of the network whose centrality tended to decrease. Two of the participants who had increases in network centrality (IDs 10 and 11) were two of the three individuals (IDs 3, 10 and 11) who complained about communication prior to the change (but did not have communication issues to raise after the change). Overall, the average degree of centrality shows an overall decrease in the number of communication exchanges after the technology change.

The second measure of centrality, NETWORK CENTRALIZATION, is significantly different after the change from before the change. The network was much less centralized before the change than after the change. As discussed above, the degree of centrality shows that this centralization occurs on the DM.

The final metric we computed from the network data was the DENSITY of the network before and after the change. The measure computes the number of linkages between individuals in the communication network over the total number of possible connections. The density measure before the technology change was 1.18, while it was 1.11 after the change. This indicates that the total number of linkages between individuals in the network went down after the implementation of the collaborative technology. Therefore, communication patterns changed in the direction of less connectivity between the individuals in the work group.

## DISCUSSION

In this study, we investigated the effects of a collaborative technology implementation on the communication patterns of teleworkers. The technology implementation included new hardware, software and network components that permitted group members to more easily communicate, and provided access to a current, updateable and accessible centralized database.

![](/api/attachments/SFF7Q6Z4/fulltext/images/e86cfb955626a687169c11d88d9a7a4efdf3a9aca62579aeb38b231ae4ac8302.jpg)  
Figure 3. Communication network after the technology change.

In addition, the study participants received better and more mobile technology to improve their access to information and to allow more synchronous communication with other teleworkers.

The primary focus of the study was the communication patterns of the teleworkers before and after the technology implementation, and findings indicate that communication patterns changed substantially. Before the technology change, communication patterns were more horizontal, with each of the respondents communicating with most of the other group members. In general, there were few high frequencies of communication for the group members, with the exception of strong communication frequencies between the consultant (ID13) and the DM (ID11), and between the consultant and one of the LPSs (ID14). After the technology change, however, overall person-to-person communication reduced considerably, and communication patterns were primarily vertical with communications centring on the DM (ID11). In addition, our interview results suggest that workers were more satisfied with the available technology, communication with superiors, and the efficiency and effectiveness of teleworking. The measured outcome variables anecdotally suggest an increase in productivity and performance, but a decrease in overall satisfaction.

Based on the interview data from both managers and employees along with the other evidence collected, three broad conclusions are drawn. First, workers increased their perceived productivity and performance by making greater use of explicit knowledge. This explicit knowledge was made available through improved hardware accessibility and capability and the centralized knowledge base. The increased focus on explicit knowledge resulted in decreased person-to-person communication and tacit knowledge flow. Second, while overall communication and tacit knowledge sharing decreased (indicated by lower network density), a more focused and defined formal communication network developed which allowed for increased communication between the DM and other work group members (indicated by high network centralization). This allowed employees to receive greater feedback and direction from the DM, and gave the DM better opportunities for monitoring employees and sharing organizational culture. Third, the use of the new technology appears to be consistent with structuration theory (Orlikowski, 1996; 2000), as users utilized technological advances based on their own needs and desires. The exploitation of an up-to-date and accessible database directly answers a complaint observed in the interview data, as does the increasingly formalized nature of the communication network. Further justification for these conclusions is given below.

The interview data before the technology change suggest that work group members were frustrated with technological capability, software accessibility and information availability. Specifically, workers had varying types of technology and levels of processing power, and this caused some employees difficulty in accessing the information available on the mainframe. In addition, workers complained that the lack of relevant information on the mainframe was limiting their productivity. After the change, however, there were no complaints about technology capability, software accessibility or information availability. This improved technology provided greater opportunity to access better information via a centralized and updateable database (i.e. explicit knowledge stores). Members further noted in interviews that autonomy and flexibility are vital for improved performance and productivity in the telework setting. Comments suggested that the lack of interruption resulted in effectiveness and efficiency; however, difficulties with technology and communication would serve to stifle these increases. After the technology change these problems were alleviated, and more workers felt an increased sense of productivity. The interview data for the technological improvement and increased autonomy strongly suggest that workers increased the use of the centralized database, which resulted in a decrease in the overall level of person-to-person communication as measured in the network analysis. The increased access to explicit knowledge reduced the need for individuals to communicate directly with one another to obtain expert information via tacit knowledge flow. We see that the amount of communication across the group decreases dramatically, while perceived effectiveness seems to have increased. The technology served as a liaison between coworkers, and became the means for communication exchanges. Workers accessed as much information as possible from the hardware and software to eliminate unnecessary communications, which limited interruptions. The performance and productivity outcome variables anecdotally support such an increase in effectiveness.

While the overall level of communications decreased, the interview data suggest that there was a need for tacit knowledge via a better formal communication structure. Employees commented that they feared they would not be able to reach superiors in special times of need, such as crises, and further felt that professional expectations and progress evaluations were not being given in a timely and appropriate manner. After the technology change, however, no like complaints were made, and the communication analysis confirms this improvement, as the DM is the only individual in the work group who did not see a decrease in his communications.

Part of this problem’s resolution likely had to do with the DM feeling insecure about his ability to monitor employees and their work ethic. As pointed out in the interview data, the DM was uneasy about his ability to verify the diligent work of employees because of the lack of visual monitoring techniques. The communication results show that while the DM communicates a great deal with the consultant before and after the technology change, two less experienced LPSs are added as strong communication links. The fact that these workers would know less about company expectations and policies suggests that these individuals were receiving valuable tacit knowledge from the DM. Overall, the results of the communications analyses suggest that the change in collaborative technology could have resulted in increased use of explicit knowledge via the centralized database and more focused, though less overall, tacit knowledge flow.

Structuration theory suggests that collaborative technology use will be based on the needs of technology users and the group members’ appropriation or enactment of the available technologies (i.e. regardless of technology capabilities, users will select capabilities they deem necessary or beneficial for the accomplishment of their task) (Poole & DeSanctis, 1990; Orlikowski, 2000). The current research supports structuration theory by showing that users adapt their technology implementation based on their own problems and complaints. First, complaints revolved around technology and information availability and accessibility, and it appears that users utilized the technology improvements related to the centralized database to a sufficient degree to improve their perceived effectiveness. That is, after the technology change the improved technology allowed users to share knowledge and access necessary information in a timely fashion and resulted in them believing that their productivity had improved. Second, to satisfy the need for increased communications revolving around the DM, users appropriated the enhanced communication technologies to obtain additional advice and feedback. In addition, the DM utilized the technology for additional performance monitoring. These communication changes resulted in a more formalized and vertical communication structure. These results are consistent with Orlikowski (1996), and suggest that the enactment or appropriation of technology is based on the needs and desires of the technology users.

## Future research

It appears from this case study that independent knowledge workers who telework utilize collaborative technology in a way that impacts both explicit and tacit knowledge sharing, and in addition, this usage appears to be consistent with structuration theory. However, some of the data are anecdotal, and these findings need to be verified empirically. While it may seem intuitive that independent teleworkers would utilize technology in a way that decreases communications, at least two major concerns should be further addressed. First, isolation has long been analysed as a problem for teleworkers (Baruch, 2000). With decreased communications brought about by explicit knowledge sharing, there exists a risk of greater isolation and ultimately greater dissatisfaction with the work arrangement. Future research may be able to provide additional insight as to whether this increased isolation led to the counter-intuitive finding of decreased overall satisfaction, despite increased productivity and performance and no indication of dissatisfaction from interview data. Second, organizational identification and corporate culture have been recognized as vital components of overall company success, and communication has been identified as a key component for the maintenance of this construct (Bélanger & Collins, 1998; Wiesenfeld et al., 1999). It is as yet undetermined what level of communication is necessary to establish and maintain this all-important connection with corporate strategy and goals. This is a concern not only for less experienced workers who received increased attention in this study, but also for more experienced workers. The possibility of losing or not fully establishing organizational identification because of limited communications should be further analysed in future research. Future research should analyse the ability of organizations to improve communication in independent virtual settings through the use of communication training. Warkentin & Beranek (1999) find that virtual team communication training improves relational links for interdependent groups, and relational links contribute significantly to communication effectiveness (Burke & Chidambaram, 1995; Chidambaram, 1996; Warkentin et al., 1997). Improving the effectiveness of group member communication through stronger relational links could mitigate the dangers of both isolation and limited organizational identity.

The interplay between explicit and tacit knowledge is seen in this exploratory study. Findings suggest that the increased availability of explicit knowledge decreased overall communication, which is the primary catalyst for tacit knowledge flow. Further research needs to be done to confirm this relationship and address the costs and benefits of such an exchange. Researchers should seek to understand the relative advantage of each form of knowledge, and address strategies to maximize the benefits received from explicit and tacit knowledge sharing. In addition to the presence of knowledge sharing, knowledge creation has been identified as a key determinant of long-term organizational success (Evans, 1997; Hibbard, 1997). Nonaka (1991) suggests methods of knowledge creation through knowledge conversions (i.e. converting one form of knowledge to another). These conversions are largely dependent on knowledge sharing, as knowledge sharing is a prerequisite for all conversions, and conversions impact a company’s ability to continue sharing knowledge. Raghuram (1996) related these conversion methods to telework, and suggested enhanced managerial activity and mentoring relationships as possible methods to increase tacit knowledge conversions. The effectiveness of these techniques should be analysed to determine if they could supplement the loss of socialization implicit with telework. In addition, other variables, such as teleworker personality, should be analysed to determine what impact these traits have on communication levels and tacit knowledge flow.

## Implications and contributions

This study analysed actual teleworkers in the face of a collaborative technology change. The analysis was designed to consider how teleworkers communicated, and how those communications changed with the implementation of groupware technology. In addition, an important contribution of our research is that it looked at the effects of the implementation of a new collaborative technology over time, not just as a cross-sectional evaluation of perceptions or recall. Because of this, it can be said that higher perceived effectiveness reported by the individuals was due not necessarily to the technology change, but also to the telework context. A cross-sectional investigation of effectiveness after the change might not have been able to clearly identify this issue. In this research, the technology change resulted in decreased overall communications and more centralized communication. The authors believe that these results indicate major changes in the knowledge sharing process for these teleworkers. First, teleworkers showed increased usage of explicit knowledge via a centralized and updateable database; and second, they showed decreased tacit knowledge flow via overall communication. In addition, the authors believe that the technology change becomes an occasion for structuring by the individuals involved. They adapted their use of the technology and their behaviours based on their perceived needs. These decreases in communications suggest that individuals may be more productive with low levels of task interdependence, but these findings say nothing for highly interdependent groups. While there may be increased efficiency in the short term, this may change in the long term because lower levels of communication can result in lower worker satisfaction and organizational identification. An understanding of the relationship between explicit knowledge, communication, satisfaction and corporate culture will be vital for further progress of knowledge sharing in the telework context.

## Limitations

This study analysed communication patterns, but there was no measurement of the quality of communications, nor was there a measure of the level or use for the centralized database. While these limitations are important, studies in ‘real-world’ settings often require such compromises. Another limitation is that self-reported frequencies of communications were measured instead of observing actual communications. While asking workers to document each time they access the company database or communicate with others, or for how long, is a major distraction from work responsibilities and could hamper worker performance, future studies should take these issues into account and use the weighted graph methodology to analyse the network data for further insights (Nelson, 1989). It should also be noted that since case studies provide limited control over extraneous factors, other environmental variables cannot be ruled out as catalysts for the results of this study.

One limitation is the time frame analysed, which was approximately 6 months. While this is one of few longitudinal evaluations of technology change in telework, this time period is not long enough to test long-term effects. In addition, the research investigated only one particular collaborative technology. Characteristics of this technology should be investigated so that the findings are not linked to one particular technology but to aspects of technologies that can be found in other/future technological developments. Finally, the sample was small because only one work group was studied before and after an actual technology change in an organizational setting, and participation was voluntary. In addition, requiring a matched pre- and post-survey greatly limited the overall responses. This may limit the generalizability of any findings. Future research should attempt to look at such a change with a larger sample size so that statistica analyses could be performed. Also, the work group had low levels of group coordination. Research in groups with high levels of coordination is needed.

## CONCLUSION

Researchers and practitioners need to understand how collaboration and communication technologies can enable or constrain better knowledge sharing and ultimately better outcomes for individuals and organizations in telework. This paper presented an exploratory longitudinal study investigating the effects an enhanced collaborative technology had on communication patterns of teleworkers. Findings suggest that overall communication decreased, likely because of an increase in the usage of explicit knowledge-sharing capabilities of the technology. The results also show a decrease in tacit knowledge sharing. When having a centralized location for information (or at least a user representation of such), knowledge workers will increasingly go to that source. This may reduce the overall person-to-person communication and tacit knowledge flow, but the individual’s perceived personal effectiveness can still be greater if the access to information is facilitated by the technology. Structuration theory applies to these findings and helps explain results of how individuals adapted their use of the new technology to fit their social and organizational contexts.
