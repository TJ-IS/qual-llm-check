# Promoting the System Integration of Renewable Energies: Toward a Decision Support System for Incentivizing Spatially Diversified Deployment

ABSTRACT: The system integration of intermittent renewable energies (RE) poses an important challenge in the transition toward sustainable energy systems. Their intermittency introduces variability into electricity generation, leading to high ancillary service costs and technical issues impairing grid stability and supply reliability. These issues can be mitigated through spatially diversified capacity deployment, as RE intermittency can be geographically smoothed over sufficiently large regions. Following a design science research approach, we develop a model for the quantification of location-based investment incentives in RE support mechanisms to foster spatially diversified capacity deployment. We evaluate the modeling approach in a simulation study with focus on diversifying wind energy deployment in Mexico under an idealized auction mechanism and demonstrate how location-based investment incentives reduce resource-dependent competition among projects. Our research contributes a nascent design theory that combines the kernel theories for identifying favorable spatial distributions of RE capacity with current policy designs to support capacity expansion management

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: design science, energy policy, green information systems, policy decision support, renewable energy, renewable energy auctions, sustainable energy planning, wind energy.

## Motivation

Climate change mitigation through energy transition is one of the most critical challenges to be addressed by individuals, organizations, and societies in the twenty-first century [13, 58]. As information is a prerequisite for making appropriate decisions on sustainability actions [13], information systems (IS) research has the opportunity to make solution-oriented and effective contributions to the mitigation of global warming and issues surrounding the transition toward renewable energies (RE) [13, 36, 52]. The motivation for our research stems from the globally increasing deployment of RE technologies and the need to manage issues resulting from the variability of electricity generation from intermittent RE resources [45, 57].

In recent decades, the deployment of RE technologies has largely relied on financial support mechanisms with low market integration—for example, feed-in priorities, purchase guarantees, and fixed feed-in tariffs—as these technologies were unable to compete with conventional electricity generation [2]. Although these support mechanisms were effective in facilitating the expansion of RE deployment in many countries, they led to significant subsidy costs [20]. To reduce these costs, policymakers currently shift toward market-based support mechanisms [11, 46]. Especially auctions for RE subsidies are increasingly deployed in many countries as they introduce a price discovery element into the establishment of subsidy levels [48]. Under auction mechanisms, RE projects compete for subsidies and only the most cost-efficient projects are realized, leading to a substantial reduction of associated subsidy costs [20, 48].

However, auction mechanisms tend to accumulate RE deployment at the most resource-rich locations, as these locations enable cost-efficient deployment [1, 48]. The resulting spatial concentration of RE capacity amplifies the stochasticity of electricity generation due to highly correlated resource availabilities [30, 50]. Moreover, locations with high RE resources do not necessarily correspond to areas with high electricity demands. Both the amplified stochasticity and the potential mismatch between supply and demand locations result in high ancillary and electricity distribution costs as well as technical issues impairing grid stability and supply reliability [45, 49, 50]. Hence, there is a trade-off between cost-efficient RE electricity generation at the most resource-rich locations and cost-efficient and reliable electricity distribution.

This trade-off needs to be considered when designing financial support mechanisms to actively manage and improve the system integration of RE by fostering an appropriate spatial distribution of new capacity [45, 50]. As the actual realization of this distribution is the result of the entirety of investment decisions by individual investors, incentives need to be carefully designed [15, 45]. To establish appropriate incentives for investors to realize projects outside the most resource-rich locations, the link between competitiveness and in situ resource availability needs to be weakened. Novel auction mechanism designs, as implemented, for example, in Germany [31], Mexico [21], and Spain [20], incorporate investment incentives based on locations’ resource availabilities to alter the decisions of investors and thus the spatial distribution of new RE capacity [31, 48]. However, designing and quantifying these incentives pose significant challenges because inappropriate designs have substantially adverse effects on the efficiency of electricity supply [20, 48].

In this study, we focus on improving the spatial distribution of wind energy deployment, as wind energy has the potential to contribute considerably to global electricity generation [32] and because accumulation of wind energy capacity under auction mechanisms for RE subsidies is a common issue [30]. Consequently, we pose the following research question:

Research Question 1: How can investment incentives be quantified to improve the spatial distribution of wind energy deployment under renewable energy auctions?

We investigate this question by way of a design science research (DSR) approach and propose a technological artifact that integrates three models: (1) a resource model to describe the availability of RE resources in the region under study; (2) an economic viability model to depict the decision making of economic agents; and (3) a spatial distribution model to assess the effects of support mechanism designs on the spatial distribution of new capacity. We focus on the technical design of these models and their integration in our artifact. We instantiate the artifact as a decision support system (DSS) prototype and apply it in a simulation study based on realworld data to permit a proof-of-concept [39, 40]. Our objective is to demonstrate and evaluate the modeling approach based on the current situation of wind energy deployment in Mexico. We contribute a nascent design theory that is based on the kernel theories for identifying favorable spatial distributions of wind energy capacity with current auction mechanism designs in an integrated modeling approach to improve capacity deployment.

## Theoretical and Practical Foundations

We reviewed the scientific literature on support mechanisms for RE to identify approaches for shaping spatial deployment of capacity. For this purpose, we screened the literature streams of energy policy and sustainable energy planning and conducted forward and backward searches based on the identified literature to delineate the state of the art of these approaches.

Financial support mechanisms and investment incentives for RE have been the focus of policymakers as well as significant research efforts to promote the transition toward RE [1, 15, 38]. Energy policy literature places special emphasis on the analysis of support mechanism designs with regard to their effectiveness and cost efficiency [17, 25, 48], their impacts on investment incentives [5, 26], and their risk implications for investors [24, 34]. In a recent review of current RE policies Abdmouleh et al. [1] analyzed past and current policy implementations to ascertain best practices for supporting RE deployment. In particular, they found auction mechanisms to be the most favorable policies as they reduce electricity prices for consumers and subsidy costs for governments through competitive pricing. Their findings can also be recognized in current RE policymaking as the number of countries employing auctions for RE subsidies has more than doubled from 28 to 63 since 2010 [46]. A similar review by Gonzalez and Lacal-Arántegui [15] with a focus on wind energy also confirmed the trend toward promoting competition among investors. They found that policymakers sought the main objectives of tracking technology-cost reductions, preventing overcompensation of investors, and decreasing total subsidy costs by realizing the most cost-efficient projects. However, the cost efficiency of an RE project is strongly dependent on the in situ resource availability [15]. Due to this relationship, auction mechanisms tend to promote spatial concentration of RE projects at resource-rich locations, as these highly competitive projects outperform projects with lower resource availabilities in the bidding process [1, 15, 48].

## Adverse Effects of Spatially Concentrated Renewable Energy Deployment

In contrast to the flexibility of conventional power plants, dispatching of wind and solar energy technologies is dependent on resource availabilities, which introduces variability into electricity generation [37]. As the relative share of electricity generation from RE grows, this intermittency is amplified and must be matched with flexibility of other electricity sources and demand [1, 4]. Consequently, integrating large proportions of wind and solar energy into electricity systems incurs two types of additional costs from a system perspective. On the one hand, the short-term variability and limited predictability of wind velocity and solar irradiation increase the load balancing costs, which refer to the matching of electricity supply and demand [45, 49, 50]. On the other hand, intermittency increases the back-up power costs associated with maintaining grid stability and supply reliability [45, 50].

Both Abdmouleh et al. [1] and Gonzalez and Lacal-Arántegui [15] advised policymakers to consider incentives for an appropriate grid integration of RE when designing support mechanisms. Furthermore, Gonzalez and Lacal-Arántegui [15] emphasized the need to incorporate the impact of policy design elements on the spatial distribution of RE capacity and its effect on grid integration. A concentrated spatial distribution of RE capacity at few resource-rich locations further increases intermittency due to highly correlated resource availabilities [37]. Such a distribution amplifies the short-term variability of electricity generation and thus further increases both load balancing and back-up power costs [45, 49, 50]. Significant investments in the grid infrastructure are necessary to permit load balancing and electricity distribution at times with high RE generation [4], while high back-up power capacities (i.e., combined-cycle gas turbines, reservoir storage, and hydroelectric power plants) must be provided to maintain supply reliability at times with low RE generation [50]. These adverse effects of RE concentration can be substantially reduced by fostering a more appropriate spatial distribution of new capacity by way of specific policy design elements [45, 50].

Prior research on sustainable energy planning has demonstrated that a decentralized deployment of RE over a large geographical region prevents highly correlated resource availabilities and therefore reduces variability by smoothing resourcedependent electricity generation [45, 51, 57]. This effect—also referred to as geographical smoothing—decreases the necessity for load balancing and thus the associated costs, as the effects of RE intermittency partially cancel each other out [45]. Additionally, electricity distribution costs as well as the necessary grid infrastructure investments decrease, because electricity generation from RE is more evenly distributed [4]. Geographical smoothing is an approach for reducing variability before it arises, while in contrast, energy storage and back-up power are approaches for handling variability. Thus, both approaches can be used complementarily to manage variability.

As more spatially distributed projects are not necessarily realized at the most resource-rich locations, decentralization can reduce the electricity yield from RE for a given amount of installed capacity [50]—also referred to as the capacity factor and thus decrease the cost efficiency of electricity generation. This trade-off between yield and variability was addressed by Reichenberg et al. [45]. They proposed that the spatial distribution of wind energy capacity should be treated as a multiobjective optimization problem. Their approach produces Pareto-efficient solutions with respect to variability and average electricity yield. They found that an efficient spatial distribution substantially reduces variability while maintaining high electricity yields. Similar results were recognized by Roques et al. [50], who applied a mean-variance portfolio approach to identify optimal constrained wind energy deployment portfolios that minimize the total variability for given electricity yields. While they acknowledged considerable room for efficiency improvements by way of more appropriate spatial distribution, they found that grid capacity and resource availability constraints substantially explain suboptimality of current portfolios. Extending the approach of Roques et al. [50], Thomaidis et al. [57] included multiple RE resources to achieve technological in addition to geographical diversification. They formulated a bi-objective optimization problem for the capacity allocation process that increases the electricity yield while maintaining control over the variability of electricity generation. Their results demonstrate that an integrated spatial planning approach among multiple RE technologies can yield significant diversification benefits. Building on their respective results, Reichenberg et al. [45], Roques et al. [50], and Thomaidis et al. [57] concluded that policy and support mechanism improvements with respect to the resulting spatial distribution of RE deployment can yield significant benefits from a system perspective. Hence, it seems promising to incorporate the trade-off between the efficiencies of electricity distribution and generation into policy design and the spatial planning of RE capacity to improve the efficiency of electricity supply.

## Improving Spatial Distribution of Capacity Under Renewable Energy Auctions

Although auctions are becoming the most prevalent support mechanism for RE [46, 48], their adverse effects on spatial diversification of new capacity [1, 15] explain that current energy policy research is aimed at extending auction designs to account for a favorable distribution from a system perspective. In a recent study, Huntington et al. [20] analyzed policy design elements with a focus on their effect on the tradeoff between promoting and efficiently integrating RE into electricity systems. They emphasized the need to design incentive payments to affect the entirety of investment decisions to manage an efficient system integration. They proposed a capacitybased policy design complemented by feed-in compensation mechanisms based on a variety of reference benchmark plants. Under this policy design, investors compete in auctions by bidding a discount on the compensation of reference benchmark plants. These plants are classified according to technology, size, construction date, and location. By accounting for a project’s location and thus its resource availability, this policy design uses location-based incentives to achieve a diversified spatial distribution of RE deployment. However, the authors acknowledged the significant administrative burden on regulators to correctly develop and regularly update a sufficient set of reference benchmark plants.

A similar policy design was proposed by Del Rio and Linares [48]. Based on a comprehensive review of former implementations of auction mechanisms, they discussed and presented a revised proposal for RE auctions that incorporates a set of specific design elements to mitigate identified issues and inefficiencies. Their approach uses an auction design in which investors compete by bidding for outputdependent compensation, possibly including feed-in-tariffs, feed-in-premiums or contracts for differences. The price bids are sorted in ascending order and contracts are granted starting with the lowest price until a predefined capacity or a maximum price is reached. Projects awarded in the auction process receive a compensation contract at the level of their bid price—referred to as pay-as-bid pricing. Today, payas-bid is the preferred pricing rule in most RE auctions as it is found to be more robust against strategic bidding behavior than other pricing rules and finds high public acceptance [59]. As this mechanism neglects location-specific characteristics of projects, Del Rio and Linares [48] suggested using location-based incentives to achieve an appropriate regional coordination of RE deployment, but did not specify how to design or quantify these incentives.

Adjusting auction bids for location-based incentives weakens the link between a project’s competitiveness and resource availability. When carefully designed, they incentivize investors to develop projects outside the most resource-rich locations and thus promote a less concentrated spatial distribution of RE deployment. Locationbased incentives are thus a useful design element to improve the spatial distribution of RE capacity because they allow the entirety of investment decisions to be influenced while still preserving competition regarding marginal costs. This approach directly addresses the trade-off between the efficiencies of electricity generation and distribution by way of active expansion planning combined with market-based incentives to maintain cost efficiency of supply.

## Research Gap and Design

Current research provides methods to identify optimal spatial distributions of RE capacity with respect to different evaluation criteria [45, 50, 57]. Furthermore, a significant body of research investigated design elements of RE support mechanisms and their effectiveness and efficiency with regard to RE deployment and system integration [20, 48, 59]. Nevertheless, combining these research streams to investigate investment incentives for an appropriate system integration of RE by estimating their effect on the spatial distribution of new capacity has not been the focus of previous studies.

We address this research gap from an IS perspective by creating a technological artifact following the DSR process of Peffers et al. [41]. The introduction to our research is classified as problem-centered because the research motivation originates from a real-world issue, namely, the system integration of large shares of intermittent RE. To enable defining objectives of a solution to this problem, we analyzed the scientific literature on the research streams on energy policy and sustainable energy planning, from which we draw our kernel theories. From the sustainable energy planning literature, we used the research findings on geographical smoothing of electricity generation through spatially diversified capacity deployment. From the energy policy literature, we used the knowledge on auction design elements to incentivize a desired capacity deployment. Both literature streams consider the implementation of location-based incentives to be promising. However, the design of these incentives is a challenging task [21] and appropriate models for their quantification have yet to emerge.

With our technological artifact, we aim to investigate a possible approach for quantifying location-based incentives, while remaining flexible with regard to different incentive designs. To demonstrate the use of the designed artifact, we instantiated the technical back end of a DSS prototype and conducted a simulation study based on real-world data on the current situation of wind energy deployment in Mexico. We assumed the Mexican auction mechanism to be extended by including location-based incentives and applied our artifact to parametrize and evaluate these incentives with respect to a desired spatial distribution of new capacity. To evaluate our artifact, we compared the results of our simulation study with the actual distribution of wind energy deployment in Mexico and assessed the variability reduction. In a proof-of-concept, we showed how our location-based incentives can be applied in an application example and used the public documentation on the results of the first auction round for RE in Mexico in 2016 for evaluation of our quantified bid prices.

## Artifact Development

A model for the quantification of location-based incentives requires three components: (1) resource assessments of arbitrary locations in the area under investigation; (2) an economic agent to simulate investment decisions of individual investors; and (3) a model to quantify investment incentives to shape the entirety of investment decisions toward a desired distribution of deployment. This threefold problem structure yields to a decomposition into subproblems, which reduces the overall complexity as each subproblem can be addressed by an individual solution. To exploit this specific problem structure, we decided to design a set of interdependent models: (1) a resource model; (2) an economic viability model; and (3) a spatial distribution model. The integration of these models represents our technological artifact, which enables addressing the original problem.

As the resource assessment depends neither on the economic agent nor a specific support mechanism design, it can be extracted into an independent subproblem and then be addressed by the resource model. This allows the resource assessment to be exchanged without influencing the other models. The economic agent and the support mechanism design intricately depend on each other and therefore need to be considered in conjunction. However, we decided to extract the economic agent into its own model in order to separate the modeling of decision making from the support mechanism design. Both could also be implemented in a single model, but this would make it difficult to independently change the economic agent as well as the support mechanism design. In contrast to an integrated optimization model, our approach simulates the interaction of an economic agent with the support mechanism and allows an analysis of its corresponding decision making. We expect this to be a more realistic representation of the problem’s real-world dynamics.

Figure 1 illustrates our developed technological artifact instantiated as a DSS prototype. The explicit descriptions of each model as well as their inputs, outputs, and interactions are deferred to the following sections. We instantiated our artifact using the numerical computing environment matrix laboratory (MATLAB) and built a simple web front end with Ruby on Rails to use the prototype on a compute server. We further used the PostgreSQL-database with the PostGIS extension to permit storage of wind resource data sets for large geographical areas. However, our artifact design does not depend on a specific numerical computing environment or database implementation. It can be implemented in any programming language that is able to perform numerical computations and interact with databases for efficient storage and retrieval of geographical information.

## Resource Model

Our resource model is responsible for providing estimates of the resource availabilities at arbitrary locations necessary to determine the economic viability of RE projects. The model is oriented toward the Virtual Wind Farm (VWF) model by Staffell and Green [54]. Their model uses high-resolution wind resource assessments based on data from the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) to estimate hourly wind speeds at arbitrary locations and heights. The VWF model uses NASA’s Modern-Era Retrospective analysis for Research and Applications (MERRA-2) data set, which constitutes an openly available reanalysis of global atmospheric observations. Unlike forecasting models, MERRA-2 is an ex post simulation model that combines simulated and globally observed data into a spatially complete gridded meteorological data set [47]. MERRA-2 provides wind data with hourly resolution on a $0 . 5 ^ { \circ } \times 0 . 6 2 5 ^ { \circ }$ (latitude × longitude) grid.

In keeping with Staffell and Pfenninger [55], the VWF model is applied according to the following process: (1) acquisition of wind speeds at 2 m, 10 m, and 50 m above ground at each grid point; (2) interpolation of wind speeds to the geographic coordinates of arbitrary locations using LOESS regression; (3) extrapolation of wind speeds to turbines’ hub height at each location using the logarithmic profile law; and (4) conversion of wind speeds to electricity generation using turbines’ power curves. The power curves are smoothed by applying a Gaussian filter to represent the distribution of wind speeds over a geographically dispersed wind farm on an hourly basis.

We parted from the VWF model to enable performing a Monte Carlo simulation (MCS) in the economic viability model. Instead of directly deriving electricity generation after step (3), we used the MERRA-2 wind data to fit Weibull distributions to hourly wind speeds on an annual basis. The resulting distributions can then be used to draw realizations of hourly wind speeds and thus enable generating artificial data. We simulated turbine electricity generation by applying the smoothed power curve from step (4) of the VWF model to this artificial data.

Figure 2 illustrates the modified VWF model process underlying our resource model. To use this model, a potential wind farm needs to be specified with regard to its location and turbine characteristics. The location of the wind farm is given as a pair of longitude and latitude coordinates and the turbines are characterized by their power curves and heights. Our resource model is then applied to the parametrized wind farm and yields the annual Weibull parameters, its aggregated power curve, the estimated average annual capacity factor as well as the corresponding wind time series.

## Economic Viability Model

Our economic viability model is responsible for simulating the investment decisions of RE investors and thereby specifies the behavior of the assumed economic agent. As this agent represents the investment decisions of real-world corporations, we chose to use standard corporate finance and capital budgeting methods to accurately depict these decision processes [3]. Large-scale RE investments—such as wind farms—are usually conducted using project financing. These projects are realized as independent corporations solely founded for the realization of a single project and typically financed by multiple investors [9]. Because lenders of such corporations focus on cash-flow-related lending [9, 33], we used a discounted cash-flow (DCF) calculation for valuation, which is available in the online supplement.

To simulate the cash-flow streams of wind energy projects, the economic viability model uses the annual Weibull parameters and the smoothed power curve provided by the resource model. Moreover, a variety of investment and technical data is fed into the model, including data on capital, operation, and decommissioning expenditures as well as the financial structure, project life cycle, and wind turbine characteristics. As the construction and operation of wind energy projects and thus corresponding cash-flow streams are subject to various risk factors [12], the majority of these data are provided in the form of stochastic parameters. To incorporate the stochastic parameters into the DCF calculation, we quantified these parameters by means of an MCS approach. MCSs are commonly applied to complex problems with a large number of uncertain input parameters, such as investment project analyses [43].

We applied MCS to our DCF calculation according to the following process: (1) specification of a probability distribution for every stochastic parameter; (2) estimation of distribution parameters for every probability distribution; (3) specification of the number of simulation iterations; (4) generation of random numbers by drawing from probability distributions in every iteration; (5) computation of the DCF calculation based on the drawn realizations; and (6) estimation of probability distributions for financial key figures from the simulated data. This process aggregates the various impacts of risk factors in the probability distributions of the key figures, which finally describe the ratio between return and total investment risk and thus serve as the basis for the investment decision making.

Because the actual realization of RE projects depends on balancing the interests of equity investors and lenders, our economic viability model considers the perspectives of both groups of decision makers. Consequently, we incorporated profitability and bankability key figures as well as their trade-offs in the DCF calculation. We used the flow-to-equity valuation approach and thus calculated the net present value to equity (NPVE) to measure a project’s profitability. We chose the flow-to-equity valuation approach, because it is robust regarding the uncertain debt share over the project life cycle. This simplifies the valuation in comparison to the weighted average cost of capital and adjusted present value approaches. However, all three approaches would lead to the same value of equity if consistently applied. Further, we measured the bankability of a project from the lender perspective by way of the debt service cover ratio (DSCR), which calculates the coverage of amortization and interest expenditures relative to the cash-flow available for debt service. The explicit consideration of bankability is required due to the high debt-shares and the independence of project-financed corporations that must provide debt service entirely from their cash flows.

In the context of our modeling approach, the economic viability model is used to calculate the minimum feed-in compensation—the price per unit of generated electricity (\$/MWh)—required to ensure risk-adequate profitability and bankability for a wind energy project. This required feed-in compensation indicates the competitiveness of wind energy projects and their corresponding locations. To implement the economic viability model, appropriate investment decision criteria must be specified. As illustrated in Figure 3, we applied a value-at-risk approach to the simulated probability distributions of NPVE and DSCR to determine investment decisions by specifying critical values for both key figures. We defined a project to be profitable if the NPVE is nonnegative at the 95 percent confidence level (CL) and to be bankable if the DSCR is greater than or equal to one also at the 95 percent CL. These 95 percent CLs are typically used by investors to determine investment decisions and are crucial to lenders providing debt capital [27]. In an auction mechanism, investors need to determine the minimum feed-in compensation for which the project is both profitable and bankable. To determine this compensation, the investor must account for the trade-off between NPVE and DSCR, which is strongly influenced by the project debt share. We thus solve for the required compensation by adjusting both compensation and debt share by means of a numerical optimization procedure. The determined required feed-in compensation corresponds to the optimal nonstrategic bid price in a competitive auction.

## Spatial Distribution Model

The conjunction of our resource and economic viability models enables us to evaluate the competitiveness of projects at arbitrary locations. This is used by our spatial distribution model, which is responsible for simulating the entirety of investment decisions and the influence of policy designs on these decisions. The resulting information can be used to quantify location-based incentives and alter the spatial distribution of RE deployment.

Our spatial distribution model assumes an RE auction mechanism, which could be oriented toward the state-of-the-art implementation proposal of Del Rio and Linares [48]. Building on this specific type of support mechanism is necessary to permit important assumptions concerning the bidding behavior of investors because a competitive pay-as-bid auction for feed-in compensations forces them to bid prices close to their marginal costs. Comparable auction designs were recently implemented in RE support mechanisms of various countries [21, 46], which makes this a reasonable assumption. In addition, Del Rio and Linares [48] suggested using location-based incentives to foster an appropriate regional coordination of RE deployment by adjusting bid prices in accordance with resource availabilities. To quantify these adjustments, we applied our spatial distribution model according to the following process: (1) definition of a set of potential wind farm locations and corresponding projects; (2) application of the resource and economic viability models to each location and project; (3) definition of a resource availability measure and application of the latter to each location; and (4) regression of required feed-in compensations against resource availability measures among all projects, in order to parametrize location-based incentives.

To define potential locations in step (1), we used average annual capacity factors and the hourly wind time series estimated in our resource model and calculated wind speed correlations between locations. As these correlations decline proportionately with the distance between two locations [53], we postulate that a more even spatial distribution smooths electricity generation from RE. Furthermore, we specified a minimum capacity factor to exclude inefficient projects and considered the current grid infrastructure to restrict RE expansion to areas with an available grid connection. As an alternative to our location selection process, the approaches of Reichenberg et al. [45], Roques et al. [50], or Thomaidis et al. [57] could also be applied. The selection of a measure for resource availability in step (3) substantially influences the resulting spatial distribution of capacity and the extent to which the spatial distribution model can alter the parametrization of location-based incentives. Drawing from literature on wind resource assessments [54, 55], we adopted the capacity factor as the measure for resource availabilities. The regression in step (4) defines and quantifies the location-based incentives based on the resource availability measure selected in the previous step. These incentives establish a relationship between resource availabilities and required feed-in compensations of projects and thus weaken the link between resource availability and competitiveness. For this purpose, we incorporated the data on resource availabilities and required feed-in compensations into a regression model and estimated its corresponding coefficients.

## Simulation Study: Diversifying Wind Energy Deployment in Mexico

Mexico has high though heterogeneous wind resource availabilities [7, 18, 22, 23] with an estimated capacity potential of at least 40,000 MW [6]. In particular, the Isthmus of Tehuantepec in the southeastern state of Oaxaca offers very high wind speeds that are otherwise typical at offshore locations and thus permits very competitive wind energy deployment [22]. Today, Mexico’s installed wind energy capacity is highly concentrated in this region, corresponding to 54.07 percent of the total capacity of 4,566 MW at the end of 2016 [42]. However, as shown in our wind resource assessment given in the online supplement, Mexico has other regions with high resource potentials besides the Isthmus of Tehuantepec that could be used for wind energy deployment. Furthermore, most of these regions have only slightly positive or even negative correlations with the wind resource availabilities in the Isthmus of Tehuantepec due to Mexico’s specific topography and the large distance between these locations. Building on the conclusions of Reichenberg et al. [45], Roques et al. [50], and Thomaidis et al. [57], we thus assert that diversifying the spatial distribution of wind energy deployment in Mexico has the potential to substantially decrease the variability of electricity generation, while still maintaining a high average electricity yield.

As Mexico has recently shifted toward auctions for RE subsidies, the spatial concentration of wind energy deployment is likely to increase further because new wind farms at the Isthmus of Tehuantepec outperform competitors in other regions. This issue was anticipated by Mexican policymakers, who implemented locationbased incentives in the auction mechanism to mitigate spatial concentration of deployment [21]. However, designing these incentives proved to be challenging, as illustrated by the implementation of extensive design changes in the Mexican case following highly undesired distribution results in Mexico’s first auction round in 2016 [21]. Consequently, Mexico serves as an ideal subject of study for demonstrating and evaluating our technological artifact.

## Data Collection and Preparation

To assess wind resource availabilities for arbitrary locations in Mexico, we provided our resource model with data from NASA’s MERRA-2 database. The obtained data set consisted of $1 0 9 , 7 2 5 , 0 0 0 \ : \ : ( 2 , 5 0 8 \times 8 , 7 6 0 \times 5 )$ observations corresponding to 2,508 MERRA-2 grid points at which we gathered hourly wind speed data from the years 2011 to 2015. To calculate the electricity generation at each grid point we parametrized a reference wind farm. This wind farm was assumed to be installed at each grid point and assigned the smoothed power curve of a Vestas V112-3.3 wind turbine with a nominal power output of 3.3 MW and a hub height of 117 m, as illustrated in Figure 2. We selected this turbine type because it is deployed at several locations in Mexico and corresponds to the current state of technology.

We provided investment data to our economic viability model by deriving the project characteristics for the reference wind farm using the clean development mechanism’s (CDM) project database, which contains project characteristics and investment analyses for twenty Mexican wind farms either in operation or under construction. Further, we made use of the renewable energy cost database provided by the International Renewable Energy Agency (IRENA) and applied the results of the first Mexican auction for the purpose of comparison and evaluation. We modeled the stochasticity of wind speeds with the location-specific Weibull parameters given by our resource model. Due to limited data on financial risk factors of Mexican wind farms, we estimated the distribution parameters of these inputs using the project evaluation and review technique. This is a three-point estimation technique that requires specification of the mode, minimum, and maximum values of a stochastic parameter [35]. We used expected values as estimators for the modes and derived minimum and maximum values based on percentage deviations from these expected values. The deviations incorporate uncertainty due to lack of data as well as inherent volatility of corresponding risks and were oriented toward data from sensitivity analyses provided by the CDM project database. The project characteristics that we used in our simulation to determine the reference wind farm are given and described in detail in the online supplement. Based on these characteristics, the economic viability model calculated the required feed-in compensations for all locations.

To apply our spatial distribution model, we preselected a set of potential wind farm locations based on wind resource availabilities and the existing electricity grid infrastructure of Mexico. We only included locations within a radius of 50 km around power substations, which usually serve as grid connection points for wind farms. This distance was chosen based on the investment data of Mexican wind farm projects, where the current maximum distance to the grid is 36 km. The locations of power substations were obtained from the most recent grid expansion and modernization plan from the Mexican grid operator [8]. We determined the wind resource availability by calculating the capacity factor of the reference wind farm at every location and only accepted locations with a minimum capacity factor of 0.22. We chose this specific value, because the average capacity factor of all remaining locations of 0.2866 is then approximately equal to the simulated average capacity factor of Mexico’s current wind fleet. Further, a uniform distribution of capacity among all these locations would reduce the standard deviation of the hourly capacity factors by 15.92 percent. Thus, the uniformly distributed capacity has the same average electricity yield as the current wind fleet, but exhibits significantly less variability. The selection process for wind farm locations is further illustrated in the online supplement.

To weaken the link between competitiveness and resource availability, an appropriate model for quantifying the relationship between required feed-in compensation and capacity factor needs to be specified. The resulting model parametrizes the locationbased incentives for implementation in the auction mechanism. We applied ordinary least squares (OLS) to estimate a linear, a second-order, and a third-order polynomial model. Table 1 presents a comparison of our regression results for the three models. We used the Akaike information criterion to identify the model with the best regression fit and thus selected the third-order polynomial model. The formula $\hat { \ b { f } } ( \ b { c } )$ for this model is given in Equation (1) with <sup>c</sup> as the capacity factor and $\alpha _ { 0 } , \alpha _ { 1 } , \alpha _ { 2 }$ , and $\alpha _ { 3 }$ as the regression coefficients. Table 1 shows the estimated coefficients.

$$
\hat {f} (c) = \alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {1} c + \alpha_ {2} c ^ {2} + \alpha_ {3} c ^ {3}.\tag{1}
$$

We used the estimated relationship between required feed-in compensation and capacity factor to adjust the bid prices. The adjustment is oriented toward the approach implemented in the German auction mechanism. This approach aims at reducing resource-based competition by adjusting the bid prices of all projects in relation to a reference location. This adjustment increases the bid prices of projects with higher resource availabilities and reduces the bid prices of projects with lower resource availabilities than the reference location. The adjusted bid prices are then used by investors to compete in the auction process.

$$
b i d _ {a d j} = b i d \frac {\hat {f} (\bar {c})}{\hat {f} (c)}.\tag{2}
$$

Equation (2) represents our proposed formula for the adjustment of bid prices in the Mexican case with <sup>bid</sup> as the original bid price and $b i d _ { a d j }$ as the adjusted bid price. We used the location with the average capacity factor as the reference location and adjusted all bid prices relative to this location. Therefore, the adjustment factor of a project is described as the ratio between the estimated feed-in compensation for the average capacity factor $\hat { \ b { f } } ( \bar { \ b { c } } )$ and the estimated feed-in compensation for the project’s capacity factor $\hat { \ b { f } } ( \ b { c } )$ . After performing this adjustment, differences in resource availabilities no longer affect competition and new capacity is uniformly distributed among all selected locations.

## Application Example of Parametrized Location-Based Incentives

To illustrate how the results of our simulation study could be implemented in the Mexican auction mechanism, we present an example of how to apply the quantified location-based incentives. We explore the effects on the competition between five projects from our selected set. These projects differ with regard to their capacity factors as well as their bidding strategy and marginal costs. The example demonstrates how location-based incentives eliminate resource-based competition while maintaining competition regarding bidding behavior and marginal costs. Table 2 shows the five projects, which are described by their resource availabilities (capacity factors), bid prices, adjustment factors (location-based incentives), and adjusted bid prices. The adjusted bid prices are used to award projects in the auction process. However, in accordance with the pay-as-bid pricing rule, awarded projects receive compensation contracts at the level of their original bid prices [48].

Project I corresponds to the average capacity factor in the selected set. Hence, this project receives an adjustment factor of unity and its adjusted bid price is equal to its initial bid price. Projects II and III correspond to the 25 percent and 75 percent quantiles of the capacity factors in the selected set, respectively. Projects I, II, and III are assumed to bid their marginal costs and only differ with regard to their resource availabilities. The adjusted bid price of Project II is lower than its original bid price, because it has a lower capacity factor than the average project. In contrast, the adjusted bid price of Project III is higher than its original bid price, because it has a higher capacity factor than the average project. After adjusting their bid prices, Projects I, II, and III are equally competitive even though their capacity factors differ. Thus, resource availabilities no longer affect their relative competitiveness.

Projects IV and V both have the same capacity factor as Project III, but differ with regard to their bid prices. While Project IV either has higher marginal costs or deploys strategic bidding behavior, Project V has lower marginal costs than Project III. After taking the adjustment factors into consideration, these projects still differ regarding their competitiveness. Further, even though Project IV has a higher capacity factor and lower bid price than Projects I and II, its adjusted bid price is higher and thus its competitiveness is lower.

## Discussion

## Analysis of Simulation Results

The results of our simulation study indicate a nonlinear relationship between in situ wind resource availabilities, determined as capacity factors, and the competitiveness of wind energy projects. The lower the capacity factor of a project, the higher its marginal required feed-in compensation. Due to this relationship, location-based incentives for wind energy projects cannot be directly derived from wind resource assessments because this would not match the required compensations of projects. This would rather cause over- or undercompensation of investors, lead to inefficient supply, and adversely affect the spatial distribution of deployment. For this reason, it is necessary to perform economic assessments in addition to wind resource assessments for a sufficiently large set of projects in the area under investigation to determine the relative competitiveness of these projects and appropriate locationbased incentives. These economic assessments are facilitated by our economic viability model.

To assess the quality of our modeling approach, we compared our simulated bid prices to the real bid prices of wind energy projects awarded in the first Mexican RE auction. Further evaluations can be performed once more auction results are publicly available. Four out of six wind energy projects awarded in the first auction are located in the coastal region of Yucatan and will receive feed-in compensations ranging between \$59.66/MWh and \$66.86/MWh [44]. Along the coastline of this region, our resource and economic viability models calculate a required feed-in compensation of \$68.26/MWh, which is close to but slightly higher than the observed bid prices. These price differences originate from both models. The MERRA-2 wind data is known to be spatially biased [55], which could explain slightly different prices. Further, we used investment data from 2016 for our economic assessments. However, the projects awarded in the auction process are granted realization periods that span several years. Therefore, investors include expected technological improvements and cost reductions in their bidding strategy, leading to lower bid prices.

The concentration of awarded projects in the coastal region of Yucatan was primarily a result of the regional location-based incentives of the first Mexican auction. The Mexican auction design uses constant, regionally defined incentives that are added or subtracted from the bid prices of all projects in the corresponding region. In the case of Yucatan, these incentives absolutely adjusted the bid prices of all projects by –\$22/MWh [21]. Instead, our spatial distribution model calculates a relative incentive of 0.87 for the closest MERRA-2 grid point, which corresponds to a relative increase of 15 percent of original bid prices. For the second Mexican auction, the regional incentive for Yucatan was significantly modified and now adds an absolute adjustment of \$0.3/MWh to the bid prices of all projects [21], making them less competitive. This change occurred as a consequence of the undesired results of the first auction [21], as the majority of tendered capacity was allocated to Yucatan due to its regional bid adjustment.

The Mexican example illustrates the core issues of designing location-based incentives as inappropriate implementations can lead to market distortions and adversely affect the spatial distribution of new capacity. Correcting inappropriate incentives requires making unexpected changes to the support mechanism, which leads to regulatory uncertainty for investors and is therefore discouraged by scientific literature [48]. This illustrates the need for integrated assessments of a support mechanism’s incentive effects from an economic perspective when implementing location-based incentives.

In contrast to the regionally defined incentives of the Mexican support mechanism, we propose using globally defined incentives depending on location-specific capacity factors. The reason for using globally defined incentives as opposed to regional incentives is that resource availabilities vary throughout a region and it would thus be inappropriate to use a constant adjustment for an entire region. This issue can be mitigated by linking incentives to resource availabilities, as such incentives can be appropriately defined throughout projects and regions and thus prevent over- or undercompensation of projects.

As demonstrated by our application example, location-based incentives depending on capacity factors can be used to eliminate resource-based competition among projects while maintaining competition with regard to bidding behavior and marginal costs. The resulting location-based incentives imply a uniform distribution of new capacity among all locations in the selected set and thus facilitate geographic smoothing of electricity generation from wind energy. We preselected the set of potential locations to achieve an average capacity factor that is approximately equal to the capacity factor of Mexico’s current wind fleet. By uniformly distributing capacity among these locations, the total variability of electricity generation can be reduced by 15.92 percent relative to the current wind fleet. However, location-based incentives lead to higher average feed-in compensations as projects that would have previously been uncompetitive due to resource availabilities can now compete in the auction process.

Although, location-based incentives lead to higher subsidy costs, the reduced variability has the potential to decrease ancillary costs of electricity supply and thus promotes a better system integration of wind energy in Mexico. By varying the selected set of projects as well as the parametrization of location-based incentives, policymakers can influence the distribution of new wind energy capacity in order to use the trade-off between additional subsidies and reduced ancillary costs to achieve a desired balance.

## Theoretical Implications

Our study integrates the existing knowledge on finding desirable distributions of RE deployment from the literature on sustainable energy planning with the knowledge on RE auction design from energy policy literature. Previous research on sustainable energy planning demonstrates that appropriate spatial distributions of RE capacity reduce the variability of electricity generation and thus improve the system integration of large shares of intermittent RE [45, 50, 57]. While the focus of this literature lies on the identification of optimal capacity distributions with respect to different evaluation criteria, it does not address how such distributions could be actually implemented through support mechanism designs. In contrast, a large stream of energy policy research focuses on support mechanism designs to incentivize the efficient expansion of RE capacity. Although these incentives significantly influence the spatial distribution of new capacity, such effects were not explicitly considered in previous literature. However, recent energy policy literature recommends the implementation of location-based incentives in RE support mechanisms to permit shaping the spatial distribution of capacity [20, 48]. Even though these incentives are recommended, they are usually described qualitatively, while their explicit design and quantification are not addressed.

Our research combines both literature streams and contributes an integrated modeling approach that allows for the quantification of investment incentives to foster a desired spatial distribution of new capacity. The quantified incentives can be used to extend auction mechanisms and preserve their favorable attributes but prevent their adverse effects on the spatial distribution. Furthermore, as our model permits estimations of the competitiveness of wind energy projects at arbitrary locations, support mechanism designs for wind energy can be evaluated with regard to the expected spatial distribution of new capacity.

We further contribute to the green IS literature as our research addresses an important issue of the transition toward sustainable energy systems. With our study, we respond to the demand postulated by Gholami et al. [13] for more solution-oriented IS research on environmental sustainability issues. Previous green IS research on the integration of intermittent RE into electricity systems is mainly positioned within the energy informatics framework and strongly focuses on the demand side of energy systems under the research stream of smart grids [14, 56]. In contrast, we deploy an IS solution designed to inform policy decision making with regard to the incentivization of an appropriate system integration of intermittent RE. This has the potential to mitigate commonly observed issues on the system supply side, which is currently underrepresented in green IS research.

When positioning our technological artifact within the DSR knowledge framework of Gregor and Hevner [16], it can be described as a nascent design theory and classified as a model according to the classification of DSR artifacts by Hevner et al. [19]. Based on our technological artifact and design decisions, we developed several design principles that are useful for the development of artifacts that address similar problems.

As the quantification of location-based incentives touches upon several research areas, we divided the original research problem into three subproblems to reduce its complexity and developed solution components to address each subproblem separately. This more explicit problem representation allows for more solution-oriented reasoning, as every solution to the original research problem must also provide appropriate solutions for each subproblem. Such a modular structure permits exchanging individual models and facilitates future extensions toward other RE technologies, economic agents, and support mechanism designs, for example, by using a resource model for solar irradiation or a spatial distribution model for support mechanisms with fixed feed-in compensations. On the basis of our developed solution approach, we assert that a model for the quantification of location-based investment incentives should be composed of three interacting submodels. First, a resource model, which must provide location-specific resource assessments for arbitrary locations in the area under investigation. Second, an economic viability model, which must depict the investment decisions of the assumed economic agent regarding an investment project. These economic considerations are necessary as a project’s competitiveness cannot be directly determined from resource assessments due to the nonlinear dependence of required compensations on resource availabilities. Third, a spatial distribution model, which must incorporate the support mechanisms into investment decision making. This model must apply the resource and economic viability models to a large set of projects in the area under investigation and determine the respective investment decisions. These decisions can then be aggregated to determine the effect of support mechanism design on the resulting spatial distribution of new capacity.

## Practical Implications

By way of our research, we further respond to the need for practical assistance for policymakers and other decision makers with regard to environmental issues and climate change, as outlined by Dedrick [10]. As evidenced by the frequent and major changes of location-based incentives in current support mechanisms (e.g., in Mexico [21]), their quantification and implementation poses a significant practical challenge. While quantified location-based incentives are publicly available in corresponding laws if countries apply them (e.g., in Germany [31], Mexico [21], and Spain [20]), the approaches underlying their quantification are currently specific to individual countries and are neither published nor available for validation. Our research provides guidance for policymakers to quantify and evaluate location-based incentives for RE support mechanisms. In particular, the design elements of our artifact provide prescriptive knowledge that can help policymakers to assess their current approaches for quantifying these incentives and can further serve as a benchmark model for comparisons.

With our simulation study, we further explored an alternative approach for quantifying location-based incentives for wind energy deployment in Mexico. Following Del Rio and Linares [48], our simulation study assumed technology-specific auctions. The current Mexican auctions, however, include multiple RE technologies such that solar and wind energy projects receive the same location-based incentives irrespective of differences in the actual resource availabilities [21]. This approach distorts the effect of location-based incentives on the spatial distribution of new capacity, which may have been a reason for the recent design changes in the Mexican case. As demonstrated in our simulation study and application example, our technology-specific approach is able to diversify the spatial distribution of wind energy capacity and can thus be a viable alternative to the current implementation in Mexico.

## Limitations and Future Research

This study is subject to two main and four minor limitations. First, the existing approaches for the quantification of location-based incentives in RE support mechanisms are neither published nor available for validation. Hence, our modeling approach can only be evaluated against the actual distribution of RE deployment and the few published results of auction mechanisms that implement such incentives. The current lack of data on auctions with location-based incentives significantly limits the extent to which our model can be evaluated by us and other researchers. However, as the implementation of such auctions increases and thus more auction results will be available, this lack of data is likely to decrease in the future. Due to this limitation, this study focused on a proof-ofconcept [39, 40] of the modeling approach in a simulation study based on realworld data.

The second main limitation is the lack of incorporation of stakeholders into the artifact development process and thus the strong technical focus of our artifact. The only component of our artifact that was developed with stakeholders is the economic viability model. This model is an extension of an artifact from previous design cycles [27, 28, 29] that was evaluated with practitioners in the field wind energy investments. Future research will need to place more emphasis on the collection of requirements from the relevant stakeholders of our model and on the adaption of the current design to meet these requirements. In particular, policymakers will need to be incorporated into the design process of the artifact in order to permit providing them with actual decision support. This will require future research to implement the artifact in a complete DSS to permit the evaluation and further development of both the model side and the actual user side of such a system.

The minor limitations result from explicit artifact design decisions. First, the current design of our artifact is limited to applications in the context of wind energy deployment, as the resource and economic viability models focus on wind resource assessments and wind energy investments. Future research can address this limitation by extending both models to permit assessments of other intermittent RE, for example, a solar irradiation assessment and an appropriate revenue module for our existing economic viability model. Second, our resource model is currently based on reanalysis data, which can be substantially biased with regard to different geographic regions and especially mountainous terrain [55]. However, as we assessed the relative competitiveness of projects to develop our location-based incentives, these incentives remain valid. This limitation could be addressed by applying the linear regional bias-correction approach by Staffell and Pfenninger [55] or using different data sets, which could, however, suffer from similar biases. In our study, we were unable to perform the bias corrections due to a lack of data on the real electricity generation of Mexican wind farms, while policymakers could have access to the relevant metrics or could use actually measured resource data. Third, the economic viability model assumes identical project characteristics at each location, which only differ with regard to their financial structure. As the actually selected turbine type and abrasion characteristics are dependent on the resource availabilities at specific locations, future research should consider modifying the economic viability model to also allow resource-specific expenditures of reference wind farms to be taken into account. Fourth, our spatial distribution model currently assumes a pay-as-bid auction mechanism in order to allow appropriate assumptions to be made regarding bidding behavior. Changing the support mechanism design is difficult, as this could invalidate the assumptions made for a quantification of the competitiveness of a project via required feed-in compensation in the economic viability model. However, our model only assumes that the bid prices of investors are close to their marginal costs and thus any support mechanism design that facilitates such behavior could be analyzed by our model. If future research seeks to extend the spatial distribution model to deal with other support mechanism designs, the consequences of this on our assumptions need to be explicitly considered to maintain the validity of the model.

## Conclusion

In this study, we examined how to quantify investment incentives for improving the spatial distribution of wind energy deployment under RE auctions. To answer this question, we developed an integrated modeling approach for the parametrization of these incentives and thereby took an important step toward providing decision support for improving the system integration of intermittent RE. We contribute a nascent design theory that combines the kernel theories for identifying appropriate spatial distributions of RE capacity from sustainable energy planning literature with current policy designs for capacity expansion management from energy policy literature. Thereby, our research provides prescriptive knowledge that assists in shaping the actual spatial distribution of RE capacity toward desired distributions and extends existing approaches in these research areas.
