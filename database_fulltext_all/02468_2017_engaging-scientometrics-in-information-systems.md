## Abstract

Although scientometrics is seeing increasing use in Information Systems (IS) research, in particular for evaluating research efforts and measuring scholarly in<sup>fl</sup>uence; historically, scientometric IS studies are focused primarily on ranking authors, journals, or institutions. Notwithstanding the usefulness of ranking studies for evaluating the productivity of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld’s formal communication channels and its scholars, the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld has yet to exploit the full potential that scientometrics offers, especially towards its progress as a discipline. This study makes a contribution by raising the discourse surrounding the value of scientometric research in IS, and proposes a framework that uncovers the multi-dimensional bases for citation behaviour and its epistemological implications on the creation, transfer, and growth of IS knowledge. Having identi<sup>fi</sup>ed 112 empirical research evaluation studies in IS, we select 44 substantive scientometric IS studies for in-depth content analysis. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings from this review allow us to map an engaging future in scientometric research, especially towards enhancing the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld’s conceptual and theoretical development.

## Introduction

cientometrics is being applied in sophisticated ways in<sub>numerous</sub> <sub>disciplines</sub> <sub>of</sub> <sub>study,</sub> <sub>both</sub> <sub>within</sub> <sub>the</sub> <sub>natural</sub> numerous disciplines of study, both within the natural and social sciences (Schubert, 2014). Historically, studies have shown that scientometrics was effective in verifying Sscientific data (Garfield, 1955), measuring the growth of science (Gilbert, 1978), studying the quality and impact of research (Leydesdorff and Amsterdamska, 1990), analysing the emergence of new scienti<sup>fi</sup>c <sup>fi</sup>elds and subspecialties (Mulkay, 1974), describing the intellectual structures of the sciences (Grif<sup>fi</sup>th et al., 1974), guiding national economic development policies (Moravcsik, 1985), and more recently, providing re<sup>fi</sup>ned measures for the impact of research (Bornmann and Daniel, 2008), and researcher productivity (Ball, 2005). The information systems (IS) <sup>fi</sup>eld has also made signi<sup>fi</sup>cant strides in this genre of research, especially during its early developmental stages (e.g., Hamilton and Ives, 1982; Culnan, 1986; Culnan and Swanson, 1986); however, in terms of the range of applications of scientometrics in the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld it is limited. As Straub (2006: 241) notes in his editorial on ‘The Value of Scientometric Studies’:

(S)uch work is important, and yet is not as highly valued as it should be in some quarters. At one point in the earlier history of MIS Quarterly, in fact, the editors decided not to continue to publish research-on-research articles. What was particularly ironic about this decision was that the editor-inchief himself had a distinguished record of publishing this kind of work!

In describing the value of scientometrics, both Straub (2006) and Chua et al. (2002) elaborate on the potential of these research evaluation-type studies to better understand how IS knowledge is created and transferred, and lament the disproportionately large number of ranking-type studies that make up the majority of scientometric studies in IS. Although ranking studies are useful for evaluating the productivity of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld’s formal communication channels and its scholars, they do not exploit the full potential that scientometrics offers, and ‘rarely go beyond the simplest research question to explore why journals are highly or lightly regarded’ (Straub, 2006: 243). The confusion surrounding the application of scientometrics in IS research is underscored by the often con<sup>fl</sup>icting results from major IS studies that claim to apply apparently similar scientometric techniques in analysing citation data (e.g., see Katerattanakul et al., 2003, 2006; Nerur et al., 2005, 2006, and the debate between Grover et al., 2006a, b; Wade et al., 2006a, b). In a recent editorial in MIS Quarterly (Goes, 2015: v), scientometric studies in IS are viewed as looking backwards instead of looking forward, ‘barely make contributions beyond what is generally known and accepted and merely proliferating ‘ “better” ways to study the same problem’. Hence, in this study we demonstrate that scientometrics does hold the potential and necessary tools for moving the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld forward. In doing so, we also extend Mingers and Leydesdorff’s (2015) review on the important role of scientometrics in shaping the social and political processes of the academic community.

In this context, we offer three major contributions with this work. First, by highlighting the theoretical depth of scientometric studies, we raise the level of discourse surrounding scientometric research in IS and demonstrate the epistemological relevance of scientometrics. Second, we propose a sensitizing framework that highlights the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of scientometric studies. Third, following the importance of such studies, we suggest how scientometrics offers opportunities for the conceptual and theoretical development of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld.

## Foundations of scientometrics

(W)e know very little about who cites whom in science, and why.

(Mulkay, 1974: 111)

Mulkay’s statement stands in contrast to Newton’s statement in the introduction about bene<sup>fi</sup>ting from the scholarship of others. Mulkay’s statement was among the many historical markers that triggered a more concerted effort to understand citation behaviour and its signi<sup>fi</sup>cance to the research process. In his study of the growth of radio astronomy as a new <sup>fi</sup>eld, Mulkay (1974) highlighted the nature of research as not just the gathering and analysis of data, but as a social phenomenon involving various participants, directly or indirectly in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the direction of that research. In other words, social forces have as much impact on the results of the research, and the related <sup>fi</sup>eld of study, as does the research data. And among the many socially de<sup>fi</sup>ned phenomena in research, citing and referencing probably rank among the most signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. As Price (1963: 64–55) notes, ‘footnoting is as old as scholarship itself’. Footnoting practices (e.g., the use of op. cit., ibid., etc.)

traditionally employed in scholarly journals and publications feature prominently in how authors build and extend knowledge from others. Thus, in this section we highlight the evolution of scientometrics historically as an inherent component of a <sup>fi</sup>eld’s intellectual structure. Citation choices made by authors determine which studies become prominent and which do not and thus have a major impact on the nature of the <sup>fi</sup>eld and its growth, and in the growth of knowledge in general.

With scienti<sup>fi</sup>c research competing for limited resources, identifying and evaluating the quality and productivity of research became critical for research institutions, and research evaluation based on scientometrics became a national priority for many developed countries (Gibbons and Georghiou, 1987; Groot and García-Valderrama, 2006). Researchers needed to know which <sup>fi</sup>ndings were relevant to their <sup>fi</sup>elds and worthy of their attention. Policymakers needed to decide how best to allocate limited valuable resources to national research efforts, and publication patterns and citing practices were instrumental in providing insights towards addressing those questions (Price, 1963). Individual disciplines were also applying scientometrics, in particular using notions of ‘research fronts’ and ‘invisible colleges’ to help them understand where their respective disciplines were heading (Price, 1965; Price and Beaver, 1966). Citations represent the citing author’s interpretation of what the research community expects; and through an elaborate process involving claims and counterclaims, act as markers for justifying researchers’ theories, procedures, and data (Gilbert, 1976). Because of their signi<sup>fi</sup>cance, Gar<sup>fi</sup>eld and Malin (1968) used citation counts to predict Nobel Prize winners, and Thomson Reuters has successfully continued with those predictions since 1989 (Thomson Reuters, 2008).

As scientometrics gained prominence, especially after Gar<sup>fi</sup>eld’s (1955, 1963) publication of the ISI Citation Index, studies that were critical of its claims highlighted how citations could be abused and how authors would perfunctorily cite to bask in the glory of signi<sup>fi</sup>cant studies even though those studies are not directly related to their own research (Moravcsik and Murugesan, 1975). As authors have no control over how the ideas in their papers are cited, the reference lists may not even represent the major in<sup>fl</sup>uences of the article itself (MacRoberts and MacRoberts, 1986). Some journals practice coercive self-citing practices (Gray, 2009) further misrepresenting the research process. The number of citations itself does not necessarily re<sup>fl</sup>ect the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the research area and citation counts would differ in different disciplines and cannot be used to compare the amount of research activity and communication across different disciplines (Narin, 1976). All of these concerns and limitations of citations provided the impetus for a thorough study of the ethics of their use and implications in any discipline’s research (Palevitz, 1997). As a result of these efforts, the different approaches in scientometrics became more established and more trusted as a means of evaluating the past and present, and guiding future research.

In identifying the characteristic problems that the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld faces in setting its research directions, Ramiller et al. (2008) highlights the importance of understanding the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld’s larger institutional milieu to allow the <sup>fi</sup>eld to <sup>fi</sup>nd a zone of mutual discursive interaction that balances the need for outside industry and internal publication goals. The substance of the research that can be found within this zone is characterized by the interpenetration of academic and industry discourses, as re<sup>fl</sup>ected by cross-referencing of themes, ideas, and language. The works of Leydesdorff (1987) and Small (1978) show how the analysis of citation behaviour and scientometrics theory in general can uncover such academic and industry discourses, how concepts are formed, how terms are shared, and help evaluate and encourage such interdiscursive activity.

## A framework for understanding and applying scientometrics in IS research

Scientometric research means different things to different people. In order to situate our work and lessen the confusion surrounding what constitutes ‘scientometric’ studies, we follow Price’s (1978: 3–4) de<sup>fi</sup>nition of scientometrics as:

(a) quantitative evaluation and intercomparison of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c activity, productivity and progress. (Or, put differently, the) analysis of the informational parameters of science development, such as the number of papers, patents, journals, laws of ‘aging’ and ‘dissipation’ of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c information, structure of the <sup>fl</sup>ow of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c documents, citation processes, etc.

Following this de<sup>fi</sup>nition, we include (and limit our analysis to) research evaluation-type studies (research-on-research) that employ publication counts, citation counts, or various permutations, and formulaic constructions using such numbers. This means that we include the increasingly popular social network analysis (SNA) studies that apply publication or citation counts because research publications are, by de<sup>fi</sup>nition, a social network. However, we exclude research evaluation studies that apply peer review, content analysis, meta-analysis, and SNA studies that are not publication networks, all of which are research evaluation studies but are, according to Price, not considered scientometric studies. It is important not to misconstrue scientometric studies as mere ‘bean counting’ of publications. As this study will show, scientometric studies, if properly executed, combines the strength of both quantitative as well as the qualitative assessment of research. In the following sections, we develop our framework for understanding and applying scientometric practices in IS research. In the second half of the study, we categorize extant research evaluation studies in IS using the proposed framework in order to identify substantive scientometric studies and derive opportunities for future scientometric research.

On the basis of the goals of our study, we derive the following research questions: (1) What does scientometrics offer for the conceptual and theoretical development of disciplines and how may scientometrics be used to assist the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld with its own conceptual and theoretical development? (2) What is the level of engagement in the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld in scientometrics and what proportion of scientometric research in IS can be said to be signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and theoretically relevant? (3) How could an in-depth understanding of scientometrics guide future IS research? To address these questions, we perform an in-depth study of scientometrics and of its theoretical foundations to derive a sensitizing framework that can be used to inform IS research of the value and potential contributions of scientometrics.

Deriving the scientometric framework: Towards 4 perspectives and 13 dimensions

In order to appreciate the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance and relevance of footnoting and citing practices, and by extension, the <sup>fi</sup>eld of scientometrics itself in moving the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld forward, it will be necessary to situate citation behaviour and how scholars ‘knit the ‘fabric of new scienti<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge’ through citations (Cozzens, 1981: 16). This process can be better understood by reviewing the theory behind citation behaviours. Although the scientometric community is yet to agree on a uni<sup>fi</sup>ed theory on citation behaviour (Leydesdorff, 1987; Nicolaisen, 2007), much progress has been accomplished in scientometrics to help us to construct our own sensitizing theoretical framework. For example, the background justifying a research study is often supported by citing certain exemplary works. In this crucial phase of what is often called the ‘literature review’, researchers have many options depending on how they intend to proceed. Research that is directly relevant to the study are sometimes called ‘organic’ references (Moravcsik and Murugesan, 1975) because they are truly needed for understanding the citing paper. In the absence of such relevant works, researchers may decide to use studies in the general area, which do not necessarily provide strong support, but are nevertheless accepted by journal editors. These decisions have major implications for the growth of knowledge and the direction of the <sup>fi</sup>eld of study.

Scientometric scholars discuss the choices authors make in choosing their references in great detail and propose certain theories as to why authors choose certain references over others. In the absence of strong theories, we analyse the dimensions of citations, until such dimensions satisfy enough criteria to qualify as theories (Mohr, 1982). These dimensions of citations re<sup>fl</sup>ect the explanatory nature of recurring citing behaviour and answer the ‘why’ questions rather than ‘how authors cite. Scientometric scholars categorize these dimensions into higher abstractions of behaviours, or perspectives, which describe how citations impact research processes and knowledge creation. In other words, the dimensions are closer to theory, whereas the perspectives abstract those theories into similar categories of citation behaviour. Cozzens (1981) suggests three major perspectives: the normative, interpretive, and symbolic. Leydesdorff (1998) adds to these views of citations by describing the nature of citations as either explanandum (something that needs to be explained) or explanans (the explanation). He acknowledges that citations have been ‘regularly used as a tool or indicator within an explanans’ (7). But in order to make progress in building a theory for citations, their nature as explanandum needs to be examined more closely. In response to Leydesdorff, Cronin (1998) supports Cozzens’ (1981) explanation that authors cite as part of the norms of publishing (i.e., the normative perspective), and adds that citations can also be interpreted from functionalist and phenomenological perspectives. Following these scientometric scholars, we begin our framework with the <sup>fi</sup>ve perspectives offered: the normative, interpretive, symbolic, functionalist, and phenomenological. Since the phenomenological perspective makes up one of several philosophical positions underpinning interpretive research (Walsham, 2006), we combine the phenomenological perspective into the interpretive perspective and construct our framework using four perspectives: the functionalist, normative, interpretive, and symbolic. We can safely say that these four perspectives cover the possibilities of how footnoting and citations are applied in the research process.

Using these four perspectives for theorizing about citations as both explanans and explanandum, we reviewed Cozzens (1981), Leydesdorff (1998), and Cronin (1998) to extract from each perspective several dimensions of citations. We also reviewed major scientometric works cited by them as well as studies that cite them to arrive at 13 dimensions of citations that provide an in-depth analysis of citation behaviour. The breakdown of theoretical dimensions from each perspective is summarized in Table 1, which provides the major references for each of the dimensions.

## The four perspectives at a glance

The four perspectives of the proposed framework (functionalist, normative, interpretive, and symbolic) can also be explained using Leydesdorff’s (1998) and Leydesdorff and Amsterdamska’s (1990) conceptualization of citations as establishing links between citing authors, cited authors, citing texts, and cited texts. The functionalist perspective addresses the linkages of the citing text with the cited text and how the choice of citations strengthens the citing text. The normative perspective, focusing on the citing authors, explains the broader institutional or disciplinary rules the citing authors are expected to comply with in the citing text. The interpretive perspective highlights the emergent and negotiated values derived from the cited text and how the citing authors leverage those values. Finally, the symbolic perspective brings attention back to the content in the cited texts, their ideas and concepts, and how they create meanings in the citing text. The four perspectives and their associated dimensions are not incommensurable or mutually exclusive, but are thoroughly intertwined (Luukkonen, 1997). A study of one perspective can overlap with another in multiple dimensions of complexity (Leydesdorff and Amsterdamska, 1990). Luukkonen (1997) adds, following Latour (1987), that whatever perspective is taken in understanding citations, all of them ultimately defend the knowledge claims proposed by the citing authors. The citing process is intimately related to the theory of construction of knowledge and the heterogeneous nature of citations as explanans or explanandum. Notwithstanding the complex nature of citation behaviour, and the overlap that exists among the different dimensions, the analytical separation offered by the 13 dimensions provides a theoretically sound sensitizing framework for our study, to address the sociological and scholarly implications of their practice, especially in regard to the progress of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld.

## Research method

We applied McKelvey’s (1982) method of numerical taxonomy to systematically search for scientometric studies in IS, and content analysis (Krippendorff, 2004) to analyse theoreti cally relevant studies. We conducted our analysis of scientometric studies in IS in three phases: (1) identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of empirical IS research evaluation studies, (2) identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of studies that were theoretically relevant, and (3) content analysis of those substantive scientometric IS studies.

## Identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of empirical IS research evaluation studies

We started with the bibliographical reference lists from the debate between Grover et al. (2006b) and Wade et al. (2006a) to <sup>fi</sup>nd research evaluation-type IS studies, and added the results from a general search in the Web of Science (WoS) database that contained indexed papers between 1993–2012. We used the keywords ‘scientometrics’, ‘citation analysis’, ‘bibliometrics’, and ‘research evaluation’ to locate more papers. Because we were only interested in IS studies, we chose papers published in journals based on the average rank compiled from three studies (Peffers and Ya, 2003; Lowry et al., 2004; Rainer and Miller, 2005). As many studies do not use these keywords, we then applied the cycling method (Weinstock, 1971) to catch the papers that the keyword search had missed. While the cycling method is not exhaustive, it provided us with research evalua tion studies that IS authors considered important and signi<sup>fi</sup>- cant. This cycling method of going back and forth through the reference lists (Webster and Watson, 2002) allowed us to <sup>fi</sup>nd relevant articles for our analysis.

Because several IS journals in the pool are not indexed by the WoS, and because the WoS does not index IS conferences in their entirety, we subsequently performed two manual scans: one for IS journals not indexed by WoS<sup>1</sup> and one for papers in three IS conferences (ICIS, AMCIS, and ECIS). Finally, to consider IS studies published in scientometric journals, we scanned Scientometrics, the Journal of the American Society for Information Science and Technology, and the Journal of Informetrics for articles related to the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld.

Following McKelvey (1982), we applied four numerical taxonomy criteria for identifying scientometric IS studies: The studies needed to (1) de<sup>fi</sup>ne clear goals for measuring or performing their evaluations, (2) specify clear criteria for performing their evaluations, (3) describe the procedures, methods, and techniques for their evaluations, and (4) offer an assessment and discussion of the evaluation(s) they performed. We excluded papers missing any one of these four criteria. For example, Benbasat and Zmud (2003) and Vessey et al. (2002) evaluate the progress of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld, but because they did not describe any procedures, method, or techniques related to scientometrics, they were excluded from the sample. In this identi<sup>fi</sup>cation phase, we found 112 research evaluation studies, which include all kinds of ‘research-on-research’ studies including scientometric studies (Supplementary Resource] contains the list of 112 studies along with the qualifying criteria).

## Identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of theoretically relevant scientometric IS studies

We examined the 112 studies while paying close attention to their goals, evaluation criteria, assessment, and especially the method(s) and technique(s) they applied. We excluded 27 studies that exclusively used non-scientometric methods and techniques such as peer reviews, content analysis, meta-analysis (integrating across research studies), and opinion surveys. We retained <sup>fi</sup>ve studies that combined both scientometric and nonscientometric methods. We reviewed the remaining 85 studies and selected any article that ful<sup>fi</sup>lled all four numerical taxonomy criteria. To be selected, each article had to clearly de<sup>fi</sup>ne a goal and scientometric criteria for the evaluation, describe a scientometric method and offer an assessment of the evaluation based on some kind of scientometric theory (e.g., several studies cited Lotka’s Law<sup>2</sup> or Yule-Simon’s distribution).<sup>3</sup> We found 24 papers using mostly raw publication counts for ranking purposes without mentioning any theory. Two of the publication counts-based studies (Cocosila et al., 2009, 2011) applied theory in their discussion so we included them for further analysis. Including these two studies, we are left with 61 mostly citations-based studies that can be analysed using our proposed framework.

## Content analysis of substantive theoretically relevant scientometric IS studies

The remaining 61 mostly citations-based scientometric studies were reviewed to <sup>fi</sup>nd any studies that applied either raw citation counts or some citation formula for ranking purposes. We found 17 such studies and categorized them into the Science Management Tool dimension in our framework. These studies use citations to simply rank authors, journals, or institutions without any mention of scientometric theory and were therefore excluded from further analysis because they lacked theoretical depth. This leaves us with 44 studies that are considered substantive enough for detailed content analysis, even though some of them combined their analysis with ranking. We distinguish ranking studies by their goals and techniques. A comparison of the number of non-scientometric research evaluation studies, ranking studies, and more substantive nonranking scientometric studies is shown in Figure 1. Ranking studies, which fall into the single category of science management tool dimension, make up 49% or nearly half of all IS scientometric studies, whereas, the other theoretically substantive scientometric studies, which could possibly fall into any of the other 12 dimensions, make up the other half.

To further analyse the theoretically substantive scientometric studies, we apply a hermeneutically guided computer-assisted textual analysis (CATA) method. Content analysis (Holsti, 1969; Weber, 1990; Krippendorff, 2004) has been used successfully in IS research for various purposes (Todd et al., 1995; Wasko and Faraj, 2005; Pavlou and Dimoka, 2006; Ghose, 2009). An initial coding scheme for the content analysis stage was derived from empirical research undertaken to study dimensions of citations in other <sup>fi</sup>elds such as information science, economics, medicine, and sociology. A large number of the terms for the initial coding scheme were extracted from the key references in Table 1. Using NVivo (Bazeley, 2013), a qualitative analysis software tool, we generated a ‘word frequency list’ for each dimension in the framework. We selected keywords from the list that represented each dimension and combined them in a keyword-in-context (KWIC) format or n-grams.<sup>4</sup> Instead of using manual coding processes to analyse the studies, we applied CATA (Kabanoff, 1997; Matthes and Kohring, 2008) with the help of NVivo to achieve a higher reliability with lower cost and greater speed (Neuendorf, 2002; Conway, 2006). This semiautomatic process allows us to focus on other aspects of the analysis such as validity, interpretation, and explanation (Weber, 1990; Morris, 1994). Indeed, several studies in organizational research have shown the effectiveness and validity of combining the processing power of the computer and the hermeneutic skills of the human analyst (Gephart, 1997; Short et al., 2010) and CATA is therefore used increasingly for interpretative research and theory-building (Duriau et al., 2007).

To assess and improve the suitability of the codes for categorizing the 44 papers, we measured coding reliability using NVivo’s coding coverage, that is, the ratio of keywords found by the text search divided by the total number of words in the text. Coding coverage <sup>fi</sup>gures are applied in ‘recall’ and ‘precision’ metrics (Stryker et al., 2006) used to improve coding reliability. Recall is the likelihood the paper is selected given that it is relevant. Precision is the likelihood that the paper is relevant given that it is selected. If the coding coverage is extremely low, the paper is not selected for that category; if the coding coverage is high, the paper is selected. The cutoffs (e.g., 0.1) are measured using NVivo’s coding coverage report (Stryker et al., 2006). Studies coded with a high coding coverage (e.g., 1.0 or more) are selected for providing additional codes; papers with a low coverage (e.g., 0.1 or less) are not selected. As the iterative coding process proceeds, a paper’s coding coverage, its recall and precision metrics will change, until eventually the metrics stabilize and the paper no longer adds additional codes to the overall coding scheme. Our <sup>fi</sup>nal set of codes is provided in the Supplementary Reference.<sup>5</sup>

After six iterations – based on stabilized recall and precision <sup>fi</sup>gures – we grouped (1) papers with 1% or more coding coverage of search terms of a particular dimension as strongly categorized, (2) papers with 0.5–0.99% coding coverage as weakly categorized, and, (3) papers with less than 0.5% coding coverage as not categorized in any particular dimension. Adding a ‘weakly categorized’ group helped us reduce the likelihood of excluding a relevant paper. To accommodate for papers of different lengths, we performed several iterations of coding with paper of different lengths to reach suitable cut-off points that result in stable recall and precision measures. Owing to the multi-dimensional nature of citations, an article may be coded in more than one dimension.

## Functionalist perspective

The functionalist perspective, the earliest and most common reason for citing (Weinstock, 1971; Cronin, 1998) involves the citing author choosing speci<sup>fi</sup>c citations to perform a function or role, accomplish a goal such as providing evidence or furnishing historical context, or to support or refute a hypothesis (Cronin, 1998). The citation index enabled authors and researchers to accomplish a variety of goals (Weinstock, 1971) more ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently, including identifying methodology, providing background reading, correcting the work of others, substantiating claims and authenticating data, all of which are important tasks within the research process. These different roles or functions, primarily as explanans (Leydesdorff, 1998), can be categorized into citations as search tool (Gar<sup>fi</sup>eld, 1979b), veri<sup>fi</sup>cation tool (Gar<sup>fi</sup>eld, 1955; Weinstock, 1971), science management tool (Grif<sup>fi</sup>th et al., 1974; Small and Grif<sup>fi</sup>th, 1974; Gar<sup>fi</sup>eld, 1979c), and evidentiary tool (Gar<sup>fi</sup>eld, 1979a; Leydesdorff, 1998).

## Search tool dimension

The search tool dimension of citations is exempli<sup>fi</sup>ed by the Citation Index, which has, since its formulation in the 1950s, revolutionized the research process. The Citation Index became the <sup>fi</sup>rst large scale attempt of applying citations, as opposed to using subject terms, as means of a reducing time and effort in searching for scienti<sup>fi</sup>c literature. When it was <sup>fi</sup>rst published, the Citation Index with its ‘Source Index’ was unfamiliar to researchers, but became instantly popular because it was ef<sup>fi</sup>cient in locating related research, regardless of its author, discipline, or the number of times the item is cited. Searching for scienti<sup>fi</sup>c literature can take the form of searching for authors undertaking similar research, searching for similar techniques or methods, <sup>fi</sup>nding research that follow up speci<sup>fi</sup>c studies, looking for speci<sup>fi</sup>c concepts the researcher is interested in as well as searching across multiple disciplines (Gar<sup>fi</sup>eld, 1979b).

Searching for and retrieving scienti<sup>fi</sup>c literature is something that every researcher does since, research is after all, re-search. As Webster and Watson (2002) note, a systematic search for relevant studies is the foundation for good research. However, many disciplines, including the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld, do not study how their researchers undertake this important task. Most studies on how researchers retrieve scienti<sup>fi</sup>c literature are undertaken in the natural sciences, computer science, and library sciences under the general area of ‘information retrieval’ (Salton and McGill, 1983). Google Scholar is becoming a popular free alternative to undertake this search function (Noruzi, 2005). As the results show, the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld itself has done little to study how its researchers apply citations as a search tool. This is unfortunate especially because as a multidisciplinary <sup>fi</sup>eld, using citations as a search tool is the most effective and ef<sup>fi</sup>cient way of not only searching for studies across disciplines that are relevant to IS (Benbasat and Zmud, 1999), but also as a means of establishing its own relevance amongst other disciplines.

## Verification tool dimension

Closely related to the search tool dimension is the use of citations for veri<sup>fi</sup>cation purposes. Whereas the search tool dimension deals with the task of seeking, the veri<sup>fi</sup>cation tool dimensions deals with con<sup>fi</sup>rmation. Veri<sup>fi</sup>cation becomes important not just for journal editors and reviewers, it is especially important for those evaluating researching to identify discrepancies in publications, locate missing references, and spot fraudulent research or referencing. The Citation Index was inspired by Shepard’s Citations, a legal tool used, in part, for tracking instances of legal precedence. The analogous use of citations for veri<sup>fi</sup>cation works in the same way (Gar<sup>fi</sup>eld, 1955). In 1988, a psychologist and expert in drug treatment for hyperactive mentally retarded children pleaded guilty to charges of making false statements in order to receive US\$160,000 in federal funding. Scientometric studies of this case found how self-citations helped to prop fraudulent research and why such studies remained cited decades after they were retracted (Gar<sup>fi</sup>eld and Welljams-Dorof, 1990; Korpela, 2010). The results found no IS scientometric studies categorized in this dimension although self-citing behaviour are discussed (Gray, 2009). A focus on this dimension of citations can help enhance the integrity of IS research.

## Science management tool dimension

Citations also enable researchers to evaluate and rank research of individual researchers, organizations, communities, and disciplines, and allow them to better manage their scienti<sup>fi</sup>c enterprises. As a science management tool, citations help measure the impact of current research, discover new inter disciplinary relationships, and understand the developments that lead to major scienti<sup>fi</sup>c advances (Gar<sup>fi</sup>eld, 1979c). As a science mapping tool, citations can be used for understanding and mapping the structure of science (Grif<sup>fi</sup>th et al., 1974; Small and Grif<sup>fi</sup>th, 1974), analysing structural relationships, distinguishing mainstream and emerging research, and positioning the <sup>fi</sup>eld within the larger scienti<sup>fi</sup>c community. For example, tools such as the SCImago Journal & Country Rank portal provides journals and country scienti<sup>fi</sup>c indicators used to map the location of disciplines with respect to other disciplines based on the citations in the Scopus database (SCImago, 2007). The search for scientometric IS studies <sup>fi</sup>nd 41 articles that can be categorized as ranking studies that mostly apply raw publication or citation counts and the CATA method categorized 15 studies in this dimension that demonstrate theoretical depth. Out of the 15, 8 papers that are categorized strongly in this dimension (Table 3) provide macro-level measures for tenure and promotion purposes and help authors identify IS-related journals best suited for their research efforts. Instead of just depending on raw or calculated publication – or citation counts, they base their rankings on theory that provide greater insight on how they are ranked. No studies were found that mapped the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld’s intellectual structure in relation to other disciplines.

Within the science management tool dimension, Cooper et al. (1993) use multiple ranking perspectives and the concept of journal cohesion to identify a stable core set of IS journals. Using secondary data from various rankings, Long et al. (2009) compare IS authors’ productivity with Lotka’s Law. Mingers and Harzing (2007) <sup>fi</sup>nd that, based on citation data, stated preferences for journals are consistent with revealed preferences. Bontis and Serenko (2009) incorporate the H-index and the g-index for ranking journals while Serenko et al. (2010) apply Lotka’s and Yule-Simon’s Law to rank countries, institutions and authors. Polites and Watson (2009) apply citations as a science management tool for assessing the progress and the development of IS based on the relationship among core IS journals. Truex et al. (2008), Truex et al. (2009) and Cuellar et al. (2008) introduce the H-index family of measures of scholarly achievement to the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld, which incorporate both publication counts and the number of citations those publications garnered. The results suggest the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld lags behind other disciplines in applying scientometrics for linking and verifying research, as well as for mapping its research activities in comparison with other disciplines.

## Evidentiary tool dimension

The other side of citations as tools is the dimension of citations as indicators or evidence for the progress of research and knowledge capital (Leydesdorff, 1998). Because citations can be used within a legal framework to support or disallow a claim (Gar<sup>fi</sup>eld, 1979a), we call this indicative nature of the citation its evidentiary dimension. It forms the basis for studies in patents and innovation, industry–academic partnerships, and the economics of R&D, which, among others, demonstrate how innovation spreads near patent sources and areas of knowledge spillovers (Jaffe et al., 1993). For instance, citations have been used as evidence to show how intellectual human capital gave birth to the US biotech industry (Zucker et al., 1998; Jaffe and Trajtenberg, 2002). Despite the importance of innovation, diffusion of technology, and growth of knowledge in general to the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld, no such studies were categorized in the evidentiary dimension. Studies in this area could support efforts in narrowing the perennial gap in IS between academic research and industry practice (Moody, 2000).

## Practice dimension

The practice dimension concerns the practice of citing as it relates to speci<sup>fi</sup>c domains (Kaplan, 1965). For example, normative citation practices in the ‘hard sciences’ have been found to be different from those of the ‘soft sciences’ and such practices are re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the ‘immediacy index’ (Price, 1965; Price, 1970). In the hard sciences, the reporting timeframe tends to be shorter and because the reader is assumed to be familiar with the foundations of the research, there is little need to cite foundational works. In the soft sciences, authors often cite more and allocate more space to the research background never assuming that the reader is familiar even with the classics (Merton, 1968b). Therefore, many questions pertaining to practice can be asked: How many references are considered normal in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c <sup>fi</sup>eld? Are different specialties in within a <sup>fi</sup>eld subject to different norms? Do essays require citations? How many self-citations are acceptable? To what extent should foundational classical citations be included as opposed to more recent works? These questions relate to the normative factors of time, the <sup>fi</sup>eld of study, and the practice of speci<sup>fi</sup>c journals (Bornmann and Daniel, 2008). Different practices result in diverging cultures in publishing and communicating the discipline’s knowledge.

The research results <sup>fi</sup>nd six strongly categorized papers (Table 4) in the practice dimension in IS that focus on two major goals: evaluating the pattern of productivity of research in IS and evaluating the pattern of collaboration in the <sup>fi</sup>eld. Nath and Jackson (1991) note how the pattern of productivity in IS compares poorly with Lotka’s Law, which is the expected frequency of publication by authors in any given <sup>fi</sup>eld. Cocosila et al. (2009, 2011) and Serenko et al. (2011) <sup>fi</sup>nd similar results. Peffers and Hui (2003) and Xu and Chau (2006) explore the norms of citation behaviour in IS, <sup>fi</sup>nding an increase in the practice of co-authoring papers and a move away from alpha betical listing of authors.

## Cognitive dimension

The cognitive dimension views citations as payment for intellectual debt (Kaplan, 1965; Leydesdorff, 1987). It concerns the norms in recognizing scholarship and contributing to the common property of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge (Merton, 1957, 1973b). The cognitive dimension alludes to the extent to which citing authors recognize a cited author’s signi<sup>fi</sup>cance presuming that the recognition of previous research is relevant to the work at hand, hence Newton’s famous adage, ‘If I have seen further it is by standing on the shoulders of Giants’ (Merton, 1965). Citations impact the citing author’s cognitive framework including the choice of problems and methods, the interpretation of the results, and the <sup>fi</sup>nal choice of references. The norms in academic <sup>fi</sup>elds expect citing authors to be honest and generous when giving credit to others. Many questions are relevant to the cognitive dimension. How often are works cited without having been read in detail? How often are citations simply lifted from the reference list without reading or giving credit to the one who performed the search and referenced it in the <sup>fi</sup>rst place? How often are citations added as an afterthought to the paper after it is completed (Kaplan, 1965)? All of these questions directly impact the originality of the research and the building of cumulative tradition within the <sup>fi</sup>eld.

Perhaps, it is not surprising that because building cumulative tradition as well as the originality of research in IS has been a perennial question within the <sup>fi</sup>eld (Keen, 1980; Klein and Hirschheim, 2008), the research results show a paucity of IS scientometric studies in the cognitive dimension. Only two studies (Nerur et al., 2005; Hansen et al., 2006) are found to be weakly categorized in this dimension. Nerur et al. (2005) assess the relative in<sup>fl</sup>uence of IS journals within a constellation of other disciplines and discusses how science progresses through the creation and diffusion of knowledge arising from the journal network. Hansen et al. (2006) investigate whether authors give appropriate credit to a classic study in IS (Markus, 1983). Other studies that investigate the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld cumulative tradition (Culnan, 1986; Culnan and Swanson, 1986; Culnan, 1987) generally focus on the ideational and thematic contribution and ignore the cognitive dimension as individual IS researchers work on speci<sup>fi</sup>c ideas and themes. The focus of these studies is the intellectual contributions of the text, whereas the focus of the cognitive dimension is on the intellectual contributions of the cited authors and how they build cumulative tradition.

## Historiographical dimension

The historiographical dimension views citations as historical markers of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c progress. This view assumes that ‘the compilation of bibliographies will be inseparable from writing the history of that <sup>fi</sup>eld’ (Gar<sup>fi</sup>eld, 1970: 196), therefore citation practice approximates scienti<sup>fi</sup>c practice and norms (Price, 1965). By combining the incidence of highly cited works within a network of discoveries in a <sup>fi</sup>eld, it will be possible to explain how original discoveries are made. For example, in biochemistry, many of the classic publications that led to the discovery of insulin did not even mention the term ‘insulin’. However, by applying this historiographical dimension, earlier studies discovered scientists using the phrase ‘internal secretions of the pancreas’ (Banting and Best, 1922), pointing to the original discoveries that gave birth to insulin (Gar<sup>fi</sup>eld et al., 1964).

Closely related to the cognitive dimension, only one study in IS (Hamilton and Ives, 1982) is strongly categorized in the historiographical dimension. Studies in the historiographical dimension mirror studies in the cognitive dimension because the history of the contribution of authors can be traced with the help of citations. Two studies that were weakly categorized as historiographical demonstrate the potential of this dimension for the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld (Lim et al., 2009; Polites and Watson, 2009). More studies in the historiographical dimension might encourage work in the cognitive dimension. Issues surround ing originality in research, multiple discoveries and singletons (single discoveries), and the general trajectory of growth of knowledge in research fall within this dimension (Merton, 1973a). The signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the historiographical dimension can be seen in recent interest in studies surrounding the history of IS (Straub, 2015; Zhang, 2015).

## Interpretive perspective

In the interpretive perspective, the citations become the explanandum that need to be explained. Citing authors choose speci<sup>fi</sup>c studies to cite after interpreting and creating meaning from the citations they include in their works. The nature of the citation, among others, its implicit value, their source, and authority become part of the explanation for the citation. This perspective follows Law and French (1974), Cole and Cole (1973), Gilbert (1976, 1977), and Latour and Woolgar (1979) in viewing scienti<sup>fi</sup>c research as an interpretive process, not the sel<sup>fl</sup>ess and objective ‘storybook’ image commonly portrayed. Instead, the settings, personalities, personal interests, procedures, politics, and other bewildering array of forces impact the citation behaviour of authors. Using interpretive dimensions it may be possible to describe why certain authors cite in such a way at such-and-such a point in the text (Cronin, 1984). For example, some highly cited works are often perfunctorily referenced in order to bask in their glory while other lesser-known studies are cited because of personal connections. Such practices reinforce existing paradigms instead of contributing to the growth of knowledge in the discipline.

## Sociological dimension

The sociological dimension explains citations as representations of strati<sup>fi</sup>cation, in<sup>fl</sup>uence, impact, prestige and quality. Scholars who are acknowledged to have contributed the most to the <sup>fi</sup>eld also receive the most citations (Clark, 1957). The combination of publication counts and citations is found to explain more than half the variance in departmental prestige (Hagstrom, 1971). The sociological dimension also explains why certain researchers produce only a small number of papers, which are judged to be of higher quality, while others produce a long list of publications, but contribute little to the <sup>fi</sup>eld (Cole and Cole, 1967). In this sense, the sociological dimension distinguishes quality over quantity. On the basis of the qualitative assessment that citing authors provide, the cited works are deemed important or signi<sup>fi</sup>cant to their own research. Many measures of in<sup>fl</sup>uence and impact, including the journal impact factor (Gar<sup>fi</sup>eld, 1972) and the authors H-index (Hirsch, 2005), apply this dimension.

## Social network dimension

The social network dimension suggests that scientists are more likely to cite authors from the same group (Grif<sup>fi</sup>th and

Mullins, 1972; Mullins et al., 1977; White et al., 2004). Citations from within a social, collaborative network or ‘invisible college’ are interpreted as having more value than comparable references from outside the network (Price and Beaver, 1966). These ‘invisible colleges’ are more than just groups of people; as a result of the identity and the control embedded in the network (White, 1992), they hold power that would not otherwise exist without the network. A study of their citations reveals insights surrounding their structure and activity. Rooted in social network theory (Wasserman et al., 1994), this dimension elaborates on citations as representing both the formal (intellectual) and informal (social) networks, and deciphering these networks reveals the symbiotic relationships between intellectual and interpersonal ties (White et al., 2004).

Nine papers are strongly categorized in the social network dimension (see Table 6). They all apply social network theories and measures, con<sup>fi</sup>rming the close relationship between scientometrics and social network theory (Marion et al., 2003). Nerur et al. (2005) and Polites and Watson (2009) use the social network concept of directional ties between journals to measure the <sup>fl</sup>ow of knowledge between IS and other <sup>fi</sup>elds. Using standard social network measures of in<sup>fl</sup>uence, Takeda et al. (2011, 2010) investigate the relationship between social in<sup>fl</sup>uence and ideational in<sup>fl</sup>uence. Gallivan (2010) distinguishes supervisory co-authorships between students and their supervisors from true co-authorships, which comprise the social networks (or ‘invisible colleges’). Vidgen et al. (2007) and Xu and Chau (2006) explore the collaborative networks of ECIS and ICIS pointing out that thought leaders among different IS communities hold the IS research community together. Oh et al. (2006) show how different knowledge sub-networks in IS (behavioural, organizational, computer science, and economics subnetworks) expand their knowledge creation activities depending on their networks’ characteristics. Lim et al. (2009) identify core theories in IS sub<sup>fi</sup>elds and their reference <sup>fi</sup>elds using various social network metrics.

## Linguistic/hermeneutical dimension

The linguistic/hermeneutical dimension views citations as ‘a bid for power, an attempt to place the authors’ ideas into a context that makes a difference for the audience. A citation is <sup>fi</sup>rst and foremost a portion of a power-seeking text’ (Cozzens, 1989: 441). According to this dimension, authors cite because the citations are valued as providing persuasive support for the citing papers’ <sup>fi</sup>ndings (Gilbert, 1977) regardless of their relevance. During the process of transforming a claim, the author constructs and deconstructs the text (Latour and Woolgar, 1979), chooses elements that <sup>fi</sup>t into the citing paper’s model, ignores those that do not, and ends with a social construction that reviewers and audience approve. Therefore, citing an authoritative source is more effective than citing one lesser known, even if it leads to distortion (Nicolaisen, 2007). The linguistic/hermeneutical dimension exposes the blurry boundaries between the social and the technical in scienti<sup>fi</sup>c practice and how scientists transform the meaning of their references to suit their needs in the creation of knowledge.

There is a paucity of studies in this dimension with only one study (Hansen et al., 2006) that seriously investigates how authors perfunctorily cite classic articles in order to enhance the credibility of their own work without seriously extending or building on the major concepts of the cited article. Articles that do build on the major concepts do so by stretching or <sup>fl</sup>exibly interpreting the cited article.

## Phenomenological dimension

The phenomenological view of citations goes beyond singledimension causal models of citations (Moravcsik, 1984) and focuses on the citing authors’ consciousness and inter-subjectivity, thereby capturing socio-psychological factors that drive citing authors to choose certain citations (Brooks, 1995). It describes the process of constructing knowledge claims as part of the author’s lived experience, which includes their traditions, prejudice, and horizons (Gadamer, 1976). Contradicting traditional views of citation analysis, which prides itself in its unobtrusiveness, research into this dimension of citations may require ‘(d)irect questioning of authors about why they referenced who they did, and in what fashion’ (Chubin and Moitra, 1975: 426). Such methods (e.g., Brooks, 1986) will capture the theoretical assumptions of the citing authors that they may be partially or wholly unaware (Hjørland, 2000; Nicolaisen, 2007).

There is also a paucity of studies in the phenomenological dimensions. This situation is indicative of the state of affairs in the IS research for these previous two research genre. Despite the numerous highly cited studies recommending the use of hermeneutics or phenomenology in IS (Walsham, 1993, 1995a, b; Butler, 1998; Klein and Myers, 1999; Walsham, 2006; Cole and Avison, 2007), there are very few notable studies actually applying these research approaches.

## Symbolic perspective

The symbolic perspective focuses on the target of citations – the cited authors, their oeuvres, and the individual concepts that these oeuvres represent. Primarily as explanans, citations explain how the citing author associates ideas and chooses a speci<sup>fi</sup>c citation as a symbol for a concept (Small, 1978). As Wouters (1999: 11) notes ‘(S)emiotically, the citing text is the referent of the citation’. Citations are viewed as symbols for certain ideas and concepts, which the author draws from to give meaning to the citing text.

## Ideational dimension

Focusing on the scienti<sup>fi</sup>c content itself (Small, 1978) the ideational dimension investigates the symbolic aspects of citations as ‘ideational in<sup>fl</sup>uence’ or as ‘output of ideas and the subsequent uptake of the researcher’s ideas by the <sup>fi</sup>eld’ (Takeda et al., 2011: 3). Some citations are ‘standard’ symbols that have achieved consensus among community members. Others may be private symbols for certain ideas the author wishes to promote. Typically, authors prefer citations that agree with their paper instead of citing those that oppose. By associating a portion of a text with a cited document, the citing authors impart a meaning to the document. This act of referencing transforms published documents into symbols (Small, 1978). Sometimes the meanings that are imparted to a document are not necessarily the ones that the author intended and as a result, the citation becomes a new sign that is different from the reference on which it builds.

An application of the ideational dimension is co-citation analysis (Small, 1973), which points to the strength of the relationship between two publications based on the number of citations to both of them in the same citing publication. Depending on the type of co-citation analysis (document co-citation or author co-citation), it is possible to identify shared key concepts and map the intellectual structure of a <sup>fi</sup>eld or research (e.g., Culnan, 1986, 1987).

Many scientometric studies in IS, including 14 that are strongly categorized in the ideational dimension (Table 7), investigate the representation of citations as symbols for ideas and research content. They are among the earliest scientometric studies in IS. Keen (1980) exhorted IS scholars to build a cumulative tradition and identify reference <sup>fi</sup>elds. Using the ideational dimension several studies sought out the sources of those IS tradition and concepts. Hamilton and Ives (1982) identify computer science, management, and management science as reference <sup>fi</sup>elds for IS based on the content of references. Culnan and Swanson (1986) <sup>fi</sup>nd an emerging cumulative tradition, whereas Cheon et al. (1992), replicating their study 6 years later, point to moderate progress. Culnan’s co-citation studies from 1986 to 1987 echo these results and at the same time uncover specialty areas and sub<sup>fi</sup>elds in IS and their corresponding ideational foundations and contributing <sup>fi</sup>elds. Using the same co-citation analysis methods, Eom et al. (1993), Eom (1996), and Eom and Farris (1996) investigate the specialty area of decision support systems and contributing <sup>fi</sup>elds.

Using the relationships between journals, Grover et al. (2006b) <sup>fi</sup>nd that IS relies on organizational science, management, and marketing for its conceptual and theoretical development, and decreasingly on technical foundations. They also <sup>fi</sup>nd that IS contributes ideationally to other disciplines. Nerur et al. ## Symbolic interactionist dimension

The symbolic interactionist dimension incorporates the social network dimension from the interpretive perspective with the symbolic perspective. It unpacks the meanings of interactions by analysing their sources and how they evolve throughout the process of knowledge creation. Thereby, it proposes that social interaction triggers ideas embodied in the chosen citations, implying that different authors working in the same area may view the same citations in different ways. It suggests that the meanings of these citations or symbols – derived from authors’ interactions with themselves and their colleagues – evolve as the result of the interactions and their process of sensemaking (Weick et al., 2005). For instance, when interacting with a reviewer, an author ‘selects, checks, suspends, regroups, and transforms the meaning’ (Blumer, 1969: 5) of the research in light of that interaction.

Four papers are strongly categorized in the symbolic interactionist dimension (Table 8). Gallivan (2010) points out that highly cited IS authors undertake most research with their Ph.D. students and they use their supervisory activities to determine their research content and agenda. Oh et al. (2006) <sup>fi</sup>nd that the patterns of interactions among researchers have a direct impact on the nature of the research. Researchers in close groups tend to collaborate with <sup>fi</sup>elds of study outside of their own. Consistent with those <sup>fi</sup>ndings, Vidgen et al. (2007) points out to ‘paper co-authorship networks’ are more fragmented than ‘panels networks’. Somewhat counter-intuitively, network fragmentation seems to have a positive impact on research productivity and content quality. However, Xu and Chau (2006) reach a different conclusion from examining the ICIS community.

## Multi-perspective and multi-dimensional nature of citations

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings demonstrate the multi-perspective and multidimensional nature of citations. For example, the paper by Polites and Watson (2009) was categorized into the sociological dimension because the authors investigate the impact and in<sup>fl</sup>uence of IS on other <sup>fi</sup>elds. At the same time, they apply social network theory to arrive at their conclusions about impact and in<sup>fl</sup>uence, which also places the study into the social network dimension. The study’s conclusions provide implications for managing disciplinary efforts in the <sup>fi</sup>eld, which, in turn, categorize the study into the science management tool dimension. Moody et al. (2010) identify the mostcited sources in IS, that is, publications that most strongly attract IS researchers’ attention, categorizing the study into the sociological dimension. As the authors also identify several theory clusters and their theoretical origins, which form conceptual foundations of IS, their paper is also categorized into the ideational dimension. Overall, scientometric IS research has begun to explore the underlying perspectives and dimensions of citations; however, as our analysis above show, the <sup>fi</sup>eld has not fully engaged the potential of scientometrics. Under the Research Opportunities section, we propose many such opportunities.

## Research limitations

To cover the complex multi-dimensional space of citation behaviour, we developed a framework for understanding and applying scientometrics in IS research, going back and forth between the applications of scientometrics in IS and extant scientometric research. We analysed a fairly comprehensive list of research evaluation studies in IS-speci<sup>fi</sup>c journals and used the cycling method to avoid omitting signi<sup>fi</sup>cant any scientometric IS papers. However, we have neither included papers in management-related journals (e.g., journals of the Academy of Management) nor certain IS conferences (e.g., PACIS and IFIP conference series). Admittedly, a more exhaustive search might have uncovered more scientometric IS works. Also, by limiting our sample to empirical research, we might have missed in-depth theoretical discussions of scientometrics in IS.

The use of CATA comes with its own limitations. The NVivo coding coverage measure is dependent on the match between the coding scheme and the content of a paper. Shorter papers and papers that contain more <sup>fi</sup>gures and tables will produce skewed results. Content analysis also assumes an inherent one-to-one relationship between the words in the text and the meanings, theme, and intent of the author in order to draw conclusions. This internal <sup>fi</sup>t between the text and our constructs may not be apparent in some cases. Obviously, the coding process is not perfect. While the use of variable length n-grams increases the reliability of the coding process; however, subtle differences between the goals of a paper and the dimension of the paper as represented by the n-grams may occur.<sup>7</sup> Also, one might expect papers to be categorized without overlaps. Instead, our approach allows papers to be categorized into more than one dimension. Finally, scientometrics is not the only research method suitable for studying citation behaviour and the progress of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld. Interpretive research methods such as interpretive <sup>fi</sup>eld studies and case studies (Klein and Myers, 1999), discourse analysis (Fairclough, 2003), and semiotic methods (Stamper, 1991) could be used as alternative or complementary methods. In spite of these limitations, we hope that the insights gained from our work encourage readers to follow up along some the derived suggestions for future scientometric research presented in the next section.

## Opportunities for scientometric research in IS

In deriving the opportunities for future scientometric research in IS, we go beyond simplistic gap spotting in existing research to propose several research programs. First, using the framework we developed, we compare scientometric research in IS with the long tradition of scientometric research analysing for incompleteness, inadequacy, and incommensurability (Locke and Golden-Biddle, 1997). We observe dimensions and techniques in scientometrics that IS research has begun work, but has not delved at the depth demonstrated by extant scientometric studies. We also observe certain scientometric practices in IS that are incommensurable with that in scientometrics itself. Next, we problematize (Alvesson and Sandberg, 2013) the practice of scientometrics in IS to uncover interesting and impactful future research that IS researchers could undertake especially to enhance its conceptual and theoretical development. ## Research opportunities from the functionalist perspective

## Search tool dimension

Within the search tool dimension, existing scientometric IS studies have not adequately leveraged the multidisciplinary nature of citations and the citations index as a search tool to <sup>fi</sup>nd topics, methodologies, and concepts in other disciplines that are relevant to research in IS. An example of how this type of search can be performed by incorporating keywords and subject areas (and thus linkages between studies that may not cite each other) with citations, which permits researchers to mine for interrelationships between different bodies of knowledge among disciplines that may appear to be unconnected (e.g., Swanson, 1990; Swanson and Smalheiser, 1997). The use of citations as a search tool also enables researchers to identity studies that have extended or appropriated IS research and concepts, and discover speci<sup>fi</sup>c works of authors or their oeuvres.

## Verification tool dimension

Many disciplines, especially in the natural and health sciences, are using citations to verify their research, and maintain the quality and integrity of their works. Studies have shown that 2% of researchers admit to serious acts of fabricating, falsify ing or manipulating data or results at least once, while 34% admit to other questionable research practices. When asked if they know of colleagues who commit the same behaviours, the percentages are much higher (Fanelli, 2009). As the volume of research in IS increases, and more publishing channels become available, it is more critical for the <sup>fi</sup>eld to safeguard the credibility of IS research, and the veri<sup>fi</sup>cation tool dimension offers an objective, non-intrusive method.

## Science management tool dimension

What the <sup>fi</sup>eld has not researched in this dimension is to use citation data to better understand how the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld stands in relation to other disciplines in terms of its structure and its process of development. The current ranking studies in IS are mostly done in comparison with other IS authors, journals, and institutions. These ranking studies can be extended to compare with other disciplines. For example, Thomson Reuters regularly spot emerging scienti<sup>fi</sup>c advances and their associated Nobel Prize potential winners using citation data (Thomson Reuters, 2013). Through these kinds of research activities, the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld will be able to locate itself within a larger academic community. Using this dimension of citations, the SCImago (2007) portal provides a validated starting place for IS researchers to understand which <sup>fi</sup>elds are our closest neighbours, what research areas are receiving the attention of other researchers, and what major shifts are taking place in the <sup>fi</sup>eld.

## Evidentiary dimension

Scientometric research in patent citations hold the potential for opening the black-box of technology (Orlikowski and Iacono, 2001) by problematizing the technology with the help of patents and citations, and provide valuable insights into the originating sources of innovation and technological progress (Narin et al., 1997). Using scientometric techniques demonstrated in information retrieval (Park, 1993), research in the evidentiary dimension would be useful in informing the <sup>fi</sup>eld on the nature of disciplinary relevance to narrow the gap between theory and practice. These kinds of studies, called literature mining or literature-based discovery (Kostoff et al., 2001), are accelerating the process of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c discoveries by synthesizing evidence from literature in <sup>fi</sup>elds, such as nanotechnology (Kostoff et al., 2006), biomedicine (Cohen and Hersh, 2005), and technology studies (Yoon and Park, 2004).

## Research opportunities from the normative perspective Practice dimension

Existing research in the practice dimension investigate publishing patterns among IS authors. A common assumption (Alvesson and Sandberg, 2013) that is seldom questioned in such publishing practices are standards for referencing and citing in a multidisciplinary <sup>fi</sup>eld like IS. What practices differentiate scholarly and less-scholarly works in IS research? How much self-citing or perfunctory referencing is acceptable? What is an acceptable length for a reference list? Future scientometric research based on the practice dimension could help establish norms of research and practice of IS research, and thus provides a standard for scholarly and acceptable work in a fragmented and multidisciplinary environment. An example of borderline plagiaristic practice is patchwriting, where authors strategically repeat words and phrases from their sources (Pecorari, 2008). Research in this dimension could explore easily veri<sup>fi</sup>able norms and rules that can help editors ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently <sup>fi</sup>lter offending manuscripts.

## Cognitive dimension.

As Hansen et al’s (2006) study demonstrates, research in this dimension uncovers how closely the cited authors work is organically (Moravcsik and Murugesan, 1975) transferred or extended into existing research. The process of creating new concepts in innovative and original research can be gleaned from citation behaviour. In the organizational sciences, Mizruchi and Fein (1999) study how authors appropriated DiMaggio and Powell’s (1983) classic work on the ‘Iron Cage and institutional isomorphism, and how citing authors socially reconstruct their ideas based on prevailing discourse.. How far back should IS researchers cite in order to pay homage to original thought? More critically, to which extent IS research is said to be ‘original’ if it duplicates essentially all elements of cited works? Such studies will have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant implications on the direction of research in IS.

## Historiographical dimension

Studies in the historiographical dimension provide historical evidence for the cognitive dimension and for cumulative tradition. Research in this dimension offers opportunities to (1) reveal relationships between the <sup>fi</sup>eld’s development and the growth of IS/IT innovations over time, (2) uncover intellectual turning points in IS history, and (3) investigate how IS addressed technological changes and the problems these changes brought. Historiographical studies pinpoint how a particular IS research area emerges and identify the bibliographic antecedents and descendants of that area. By tracing the methods and solutions used in historiographical material, researchers could <sup>fi</sup>nd new solutions to new challenges. For example, by tracing the citations before the discovery of the double helix structure of DNA, Gar<sup>fi</sup>eld (2004) found several original works in the citation network that were not acknowledged. In another study of patent applications over time, Igami (2008) was able to describe the interdisciplinary evolution of the nanotechnology <sup>fi</sup>eld.

## Research opportunities from the interpretive perspective Sociological dimension

We identify two areas of opportunities in the sociological dimension. First, existing research in the sociological dimension in IS are often undertaken with assumptions that are not clearly articulated. Consequently, research <sup>fi</sup>ndings produce con<sup>fl</sup>icting results (e.g., see debate between Grover et al., 2006b and Wade et al., 2006b, and their ensuing rejoinders). Future research in the sociological dimension need to clarify these assumptions before a valid comparison can be made (e.g., for an exemplary examination of such assumptions see Costanza et al., 2004 in ecological economics). Second, the sociological dimension affords an effective way of identifying what are considered signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, valuable, important, and therefore relevant contributions in IS.

Loebbecke and Leidner (2012) points to low average number of citations per paper in the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld highlighting the need to look closely at what is considered ‘important’ and ‘quality’ research. Therefore, following up on Walstrom and Leonard (2000), as well as Whitley and Galliers (2007), IS researchers would be able to organize a generally accepted body of IS knowledge based on the most signi<sup>fi</sup>cant IS research in any particular area, which would be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial for further legitimizing the <sup>fi</sup>eld and enhancing its professional stature in the eyes of its stakeholders and industry. Studies in this dimension could reveal the extent of social strati<sup>fi</sup>cation among researchers (see Cole and Cole, 1973, 1967), the manner in which IS knowledge grows by examining the impact and in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the most-proli<sup>fi</sup>c researchers (Matthew Effect) compared with those of the larger majority of researchers (Ortega Hypothesis), and how universalistic the reward system is in the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld (Ortega y Gasset, 1957; Merton, 1968a; Oromaner, 1985).

## Social network dimension

Because research in IS, like other <sup>fi</sup>elds, are undertaken within social networks (Marion et al., 2003), identifying and uncovering these often opaque ‘invisible colleges’ informs the IS community of its centres of activity. Besides knowing who’s doing what, visualizations of these social networks will show the interaction and <sup>fl</sup>ow of knowledge (Chen and Hicks, 2004) within the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld. In the management <sup>fi</sup>eld, a study in this dimension was used to identify the intellectual neighbourhood surrounding stellar performers (Duncan et al., 2002). The relationship between social in<sup>fl</sup>uence and ideational in<sup>fl</sup>uence that is mapped into the social network dimension may provide answers to resolving the fragmentation and the lack of collaboration among sub-specialties in IS. Because the identity of any <sup>fi</sup>eld of study is intricately linked to the structure of its networks (White, 1992), research into the social network dimension is also especially suited for addressing how identity is socially constructed in the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld.

## Hermeneutical dimension

Future research in the linguistic/hermeneutical dimension requires a major shift in the underlying positivist thinking prevalent in IS (Orlikowski and Baroudi, 1991). Instead of separating social practices from the content of science, the linguistic dimension does not separate the two, and citations, as an example of inscriptions (Latour, 1987), play a major role in creating and controlling the environment of the research (Callon et al., 1986). The extent to which linguistic and rhetorical devices are used to win over peers in the review process speaks volumes of the quality of scholarly work. Thorne (1977) lists 19 such citing behaviours including serial publishing (division of a single research project into many parts, each reported separately), self-serving citations, and evidentiary validity (citations selected to support any point of view) in order to persuade the reviewing audience. Gilbert (1976, 1977) explains how these rhetorical devices transform relatively weak research <sup>fi</sup>ndings into factual knowledge claims. Future research in this area would illuminate the negotiation processes taking place among IS scholars and researchers.<sup>8</sup> In this dimension of citations, co-word analysis (Callon et al., 1991) can be a useful technique to peel away the use of forceful words and terms in legitimizing research.

## Phenomenological dimension

The phenomenological dimension questions the traditional Mertonian view of science as a ‘sel<sup>fl</sup>ess’ search for the objective and veri<sup>fi</sup>able. Future research in the phenomenological dimension could take various approaches. Gadamer's (1976) phenomenological hermeneutics focuses on the history, tradition, and horizon of the experience of the authors that impacts citation behaviour and scholarly activity. An example of this genre of studies is Hassan’s (2014) investigation of the impact of the authors’ lived experience on the classic Banville and Landry's (1989) ‘Can the Field of MIS Be Disciplined?’ Husserlian (Husserl, [1911] 1965) phenomenology focuses on the meaning of citing as a subjective social action between two or more people (Schutz, 1973). The motivation behind the citing can be interpreted by examining the stream of consciousness emanating from the citing (perhaps by direct questioning) and possibly interlocking with the citing author’s interpretation of the cited author’s stream of consciousness. Both of these approaches to studying citations emphasize the citations’ subjective meanings.

## Research opportunities from the symbolic perspective Ideational dimension

The ideational dimension is closely related to the cognitive dimension. Whereas the focus of the cognitive dimension is on the contribution of the author, the focus of the ideational dimension is the contribution of the concepts and ideas in the text. The majority of studies in IS within the ideational dimension is in co-citation analysis. These studies can be extended by going beyond factor analysing co-citation counts to identifying the conceptual axes in which the co-cited authors or documents lie, identifying schools of thought, paradigm shifts, and intellectual origins as was performed by McCain (1983) on microeconomics, and Small (2003) on the natural sciences. The issue of ‘original intellectual contributions’ in IS research can be investigated using insights from sophisticated examples of co-citation analyses as well as context co-citation analyses (Grif<sup>fi</sup>th et al., 1974; Small and Grif<sup>fi</sup>th, 1974; Cozzens, 1985; Anderson, 2006). These exemplars shed light on authors’ philosophical leanings, their shifting associations over time, the agreement among the <sup>fi</sup>eld’s members, and the evolution of concepts. An example using this approach in software engineering <sup>fi</sup>nds certain themes remaining stable while others emerge or fade away through time (Coulter et al., 1998). General keyword analysis is useful in studying the phenomenon of ‘jargon proliferation’ and thereby contribute to a better understanding of how terms are used in IS (see Marshakova-Shaikevich, 2001 in chemistry or Roche et al., 2010 in molecular biology). The study of semiotics in scientometrics (Cronin, 2000) describes how citing authors position the symbol (the citation) in a host of networks: the <sup>fi</sup>eld-speci<sup>fi</sup>c semantic network, a journal network, or an institutional network, explain ing why certain citations are picked (Wouters, 1998, 1999).

## Symbolic interactionist dimension

This dimension focuses on the sensemaking process of the citing author (Weick, 2001) and how meanings interpreted from the citations are created and changed as a result of social interaction. At the level of organizing the citations in the research, various insights can be extracted by studying the creation and enactment of identity, the retrospective process of reviewing a research paper, the social construction of the authors’ reality in the research, and the process of removing equivocality. Who the citing authors think they are in their research contexts shapes what they enact and how they interpret the content they are working with. The process of retrospection, especially after a review, reshapes and the transforms the meaning of the earlier experience. And once the authors observe the consequences of their choices, they work at removing equivocality, and in the process enact their own reality. Each phase of this process – which is worthy of future study – involves the enrolment of various resources and citations that support the work of organizing the research.

## Summary and outlook

With this work, we contribute to revitalizing the understanding and practice of scientometrics in IS, enhance its level of discourse, and describe how the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld can enhance its conceptual and theoretical development by engaging in scientometric studies. We outline the foundations of scientometrics and derive its underlying perspectives and dimensions. As a result, we offer a framework for understanding and a more engaging application of scientometrics in IS. We provide a fairly comprehensive review of scientometrics in IS by reviewing 85 scientometric IS studies and content analysing 44 substantive and theoretically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant studies. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld has not yet taken full advantage of the rich spectrum of scientometric theory and we proposed a variety of research opportunities to <sup>fi</sup>ll that gap. At the immediate and practical level, scientometrics studies in IS should extend beyond journal, author, and institutional rankings, and take advantage of the linkages between citation behaviour and the research process. Using the proposed framework, we uncover insights that inform policymaking, contribute to resource allocation, support hiring and tenure decisions, and help with evaluating journal submissions. With this in-depth analysis of scientometric studies in IS we offer a better understanding how IS researchers could discover, formulate, and communicate their <sup>fi</sup>eld’s body of knowledge, support a deeper understanding of the <sup>fi</sup>eld’s conceptual development, and progress forward in their efforts towards knowledge creation in IS.

## About the authors

Nik R. Hassan is Associate Professor of MIS at the Labovitz School of Business and Economics (LSBE), University of Minnesota Duluth. His area of research includes the philosophical foundations of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld, scientometrics, social network approaches in IS research, and IS development. He is published in the Journal of Information Technology, European Journal of Information Systems, Information Systems Management Journal, Communications of the AIS, Journal of IS Education, Informing Science Journal, and Review of Accounting and Finance.

Claudia Loebbecke is Chair of Business, Media and Technology Management at the University of Cologne. In 2005–2006 she was president of the Association for Information Systems (AIS), and in 2012 named AIS Fellow. She serves as Senior Editor of the Journal of Strategic Information Systems (JSIS), as Advisory Board Member of Information Systems Research (ISR), and the Journal of Information Technology (JIT). Her research focuses on business models and management aspects of digital and creative goods and on the innovative use of new media and IT.
