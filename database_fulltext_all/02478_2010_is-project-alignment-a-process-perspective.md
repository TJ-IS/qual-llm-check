## Abstract

Information system (IS) project alignment has been defined as the degree to which an IS’s project deliverables are consistent with the project’s objectives, which are shaped by the organization’s IS strategy. These objectives may change as the project unfolds, reflecting emergent opportunities, limitations, and strategy (Jenkin and Chan, 2005). The IS project alignment process refers to the sequence and flow of events that lead to project alignment and, thus, strategy execution. To develop an understanding of project alignment processes – how IS projects become aligned or misaligned – this study examines nine projects across two organizational settings to analyze the key events and processes of project alignment. By analyzing three different project patterns using different organizational metaphors, this study explains evidence contradicting widely held beliefs about project management. Rather than planning and process maturity, the results of this study highlight the importance of executing processes, change, learning, knowing in practice, and heedful interrelating between project team members to align final project deliverables. This research, based on an empirical study, contributes to both the IS alignment and project management literatures by further developing the emerging concept of project alignment to include a process view of how to achieve alignment of final project deliverables with IS project objectives and the IS strategy. 0.1057/iit 2009.10

## Introduction

S <sup>trategic</sup> <sup>alignment,</sup> <sup>the</sup> <sup>degree</sup> <sup>to</sup> <sup>which</sup> <sup>an</sup> <sup>organiza-</sup>tion’s IS strategy is consistent with its business tion's IS strategy is consistent with its business strategy, is an important and challenging issue faced by organizations. Business and IS executives have listed strategic alignment as one of their top concerns (Broadbent and Weill, 1993; Luftman et al., 1993; Chan et al., 1997; Papp, 1999; Van Der Zee and De Jong, 1999; Reich and Benbasat, 2000; Hirschheim and Sabherwal, 2001; Sabherwal and Chan, 2001; Chan and Reich, 2007; Luftman and Kempaiah, 2008). Related to strategic alignment is the emerging concept of IS project alignment (Jenkin and Chan, 2005) – the degree to which information system (IS) project deliverables are consistent with the project’s objectives, which are shaped by the organization’s IS strategy. This definition also explicitly recognizes that these objectives may change as the project unfolds, reflecting emergent opportunities, limitations, and strategy. Projects, defined as ‘temporary endeavor[s] undertaken to create a unique product, service or result’ (PMI, 2004: 5), and particularly IS projects, are difficult to complete successfully and often fail (Nelson, 2007). Part of this difficulty stems from the uncertainty and change that unfold over the course of projects. However, projects are ‘critical to the realization of the performing organization’s business strategy because projects are a means by which strategy is implemented’ (PMI, 2000: 4). Given the importance of strategic alignment, yet the difficulty of successfully implementing projects, organizations are left with the question – ‘How can strategy be successfully executed?’ The answer lies in IS project alignment – the IT<sup>1</sup>-enabled enactment of strategic alignment. Successfully aligning IS projects,<sup>2</sup> specifically project deliverables, to project objectives and IS strategy, is required for the realization of strategic alignment.

The domain of project management has matured over time and the principles of ‘good’ project management are understood. Project performance is traditionally understood to include product (i.e., effectiveness of the final project product) and process (i.e., how well the project process was executed, e.g., schedule and budget metrics)

performance dimensions (Nidumolu, 1995, 1996b). Wellentrenched assumptions underlying project management principles include the perspective that planning, measurement, structure, replication, predictability, and maturity are ‘helpful’ and lead to positive project performance, whereas change and uncertainty are ‘unhelpful’ for project performance. Given these generally accepted principles, we asked how and what can the concept of IS project alignment contribute to our understanding of strategic alignment and project management? We address this question below.

Three patterns of project processes and alignment were detected in the exploratory case studies described in this research. First, there were projects that followed the tenets of good project management with project deliverables that were highly aligned – Pattern Green. In contrast, there were projects that violated project management guidelines (e.g., these projects failed to complete adequate planning), and whose project deliverables had low alignment – Pattern Red. Finally, a third pattern emerged in which projects failed to follow good project management techniques and, as a result, had a chaotic project process, yet were able to create highly aligned deliverables – Pattern Yellow.<sup>3</sup> Alternatively, these three project patterns can be assessed using the process and product dimensions outlined above (Pattern Green: high, high, Pattern Red: low, low, and Pattern Yellow: low, high process, and product performance, respectively). Project performance and project alignment operate on a continuum, resulting in different shades of green, yellow, and red projects. However, in this study we describe three distinct project patterns for the sake of parsimony. These project patterns, especially those providing evidence contrary to our understanding of the relationship between project management practices and performance outcomes, warrant further examination and explanation. The concept of aligning projects – the IS project alignment process – is proposed to explain these three project patterns and provide new perspectives on how to manage projects so as to successfully execute organizational strategies.

The concept of IS project alignment, the focus of this paper, is somewhat novel (Jenkin and Chan, 2005). Rather than focusing on the traditional elements that are important for successful project management, as described above, IS project alignment focuses on achieving alignment among project deliverables, objectives, and the organization’s IS strategy. In other words, IS project alignment focuses on creating a set of final project deliverables that will execute the organization’s IS strategy – implement the strategic systems, products and processes – and lead to positive business effects. This exploratory study, by using a process perspective, extends the concept of project alignment (Jenkin and Chan, 2005) originally developed using a variance perspective – how variance in antecedent variables relates to variance in project alignment, the dependent variable. In contrast, a process perspective examines the sequential order and flow of events to develop an understanding of a phenomenon (Sabherwal and Robey, 1995). Although not as common as variance models (Markus and Robey, 1988), process models have a number of advantages including providing rich explanations of outcomes, predicting patterns and outcomes over time, and improved empirical fidelity (Markus and Robey, 1988; Newman and

Robey, 1992; Mackenzie, 2000). In particular, this study seeks to understand how the IS project alignment process unfolds over time in order to understand how IS projects become aligned or misaligned. In other words, it reframes the activities and events in a project to understand how to achieve IS project alignment. We believe this is an important contribution to the Management Information Systems (MIS) literature.

The focus of this study is on assessing the project processes involved in aligning projects from the beginning through implementation such that an organization’s aligned strategy is successfully executed. Previous literature has examined the transition from strategy to projects – identification and prioritization of key projects during the strategic planning phase – as well as the breaking of projects into discrete, implementable sections to deliver business value quickly (Parker et al., 1988; Benko and McFarlan, 2003a, b). Although arguments have been made regarding the link between IS projects and strategic alignment, there has been a dearth of empirical studies examining this relationship. Recently, Kearns and Sabherwal (2006) conducted an empirical study to examine whether IS projects mediate the relationship between strategic alignment and the business effects of alignment. Specifically, they linked strategic alignment to IS projects in terms of how strategic alignment positively affects the quality of IS project planning and reduces implementation problems. Further, they found that these two aspects of IS projects mediated the relationship between strategic alignment and the business effects of alignment. Thus, their research highlighted the importance of IS projects in realizing the benefits of strategic alignment.

As noted in the MIS literature (e.g., Behrens, 2007) and in the broader management literature (e.g., Morgan, 1997), using different organizational conceptualizations – or metaphors – to examine a phenomenon can provide new insights into that phenomenon and highlight new and competing perspectives, which together provide a richer understanding than any single conceptualization. The process lenses we adopt and contrast in this study are the organization-as-machine and organization-as-organism metaphors. The organization-as-machine metaphor views the organization and the project process as mechanistic, where the events, states and outcomes can be identified and evaluated. In contrast, the organization-as-organism metaphor views the organization and project process as more fluid, social, and evolutionary. Although a political metaphor could also be used to examine the project alignment process, we limit the scope of our discussion to the machine and organism metaphors so that we may examine and contrast these two conceptualizations in greater detail. However, using the political metaphor represents an important area of future research on project alignment.

The paper is organized as follows. The first section provides definitions of project alignment and the project alignment process. Next, the research question is specified, followed by a description of the case study methodology used to examine this research question and explore the project alignment process. The results of our study are then presented, including analyses based on each metaphor. The discussion summarizes key findings and lessons learned. Finally, the limitations of the study and important implications for researchers and practitioners are outlined.

## Project alignment defined

Project alignment is composed of two components: the process of aligning and the outcome (Jenkin and Chan, 2005). The project alignment process represents the means – the sequence and flow of events – to achieve the outcome of project alignment. The project alignment outcome represents the end state of the project and is defined as the degree to which an IS project deliverables are consistent with the project’s objectives, which are shaped by the organization’s IS strategy. In this research, we assume that there is IS strategic alignment, that is, the business and IS strategies are consistent. The issues surrounding strategic alignment have had considerable attention in the MIS literature and, therefore, we do not specifically discuss strategic misalignment. The tactics that flow from these strategies, on the other hand, have had less attention. Projects are the instantiation of these tactics. Our focus is on ensuring that tactical projects to implement business applications and IS infrastructure are also aligned.

The project alignment construct is composed of three dimensions (Jenkin and Chan, 2005). The first dimension – strategy-to-project-objectives congruence – refers to the degree to which the project objectives reflect the current IS strategy, which may change over the course of the project. The second dimension – project-objective-to-deliverables congruence – refers to the degree to which the project deliverables reflect the current project objectives, which also may change over the course of the project either due to changes in strategy or due to a more refined understanding of the objectives. The third dimension – project social congruence – describes the social interactions that occur throughout the project, which affect the communication, coordination and shared understanding among team members (see Jenkin and Chan, 2005).

Project alignment represents the end result and ‘doing the right thing’, whereas the alignment process represents the ways to get there. As proposed in Jenkin and Chan (2005), project alignment influences project performance, and ultimately influences firm performance. However, it is the project alignment process, we propose, that mediates the relationship between aligned strategies and firm performance. This process enables strategy execution so that the business benefits of strategic alignment can be reaped. Significant issues in the project alignment process can potentially derail a project from achieving final project alignment and successfully executing the firm’s aligned strategy. The uncertainty and dynamic nature of requirements, for example, can be problematic for achieving final project alignment. These issues and others will be highlighted in the cases analyzed below.

## Research question

The practical problem we consider in this research addresses the challenges associated with successfully aligning IS projects so that final project deliverables are consistent with project objectives and IS strategy, which may be dynamic themselves. The focus is on understanding how projects become aligned or misaligned, using two contrasting process lenses: organization-as-machine and organization-as-organism. Thus, this paper addresses the research question: What are the key events and processes that lead to IS project alignment?

## Process approach

A process approach to theory involves identifying events, linkages between events and the outcomes of these events (Mackenzie, 2000). Outcomes, which are explained by the sequence of events (Poole et al., 2000), are represented as discrete states or ‘changes in state’, as opposed to a range of values that depend on the values of the independent variables (Markus and Robey, 1988). The temporal sequence of events in a process model is critical. A different order of events can result in a different outcome. ‘Each causal event imparts a particular direction and pushes the developing subject toward a certain outcome’ (Poole et al., 2000: 41). However, it is important to recognize that events in a process model are necessary, but not sufficient for the outcome to occur (Markus and Robey, 1988; Poole et al., 2000).

Mackenzie (2000) identifies the key components of a process model: (1) elements used to describe events, states, or steps; (2) relationships or transitions between these elements; (3) links to other processes; and (4) outcomes. The process model, referred to as a process framework in Mackenzie (2000), may contain several paths to a particular outcome (Mackenzie, 2000). In developing a process model, counter examples are useful for identifying alternative paths and paths that do not lead to the desired outcome (Mackenzie, 2000), such as lack of final project alignment. Further, using different, yet competing process lenses can provide a richer understanding of the process under examination.

## Method

To investigate the research question, the case study method was selected. Case research is most appropriate for exploratory studies<sup>4</sup> and is well suited for understanding the complexities of processes in natural settings (Benbasat et al., 1987; Yin, 1994; Dube and Pare, 2003) and the rich dynamics of a single setting (Eisenhardt, 1989). Given the lack of previous literature investigating IS project alignment processes, an exploratory approach and, therefore, case research was justified. With its focus on theory building, this study relied primarily on analyses of case data, rather than an extensive review of the literature (Eisenhardt, 1989). The research question and a review of the literature, which focused on the identification of preliminary concepts for each metaphor, guided the case design and data collection (Eisenhardt, 1989).

The case research proceeded in two phases. The first phase of the study involved studying multiple projects within the same organization. Each project within the organization was analyzed as an individual case. Projects with varying levels of project alignment and success were selected and process differences analyzed.

The second phase of this case research involved studying multiple projects within a second organizational setting. In the first setting, the organization studied was a packaged software product development firm. To provide a richer and more generalizable understanding of project alignment processes, the second setting chosen was a global high-tech manufacturing organization with a large corporate IT department. Thus, software development projects were the focus of the first phase of this research and internal business system development and IT infrastructure projects were the focus of the second phase. Comparing and contrasting the results between these two settings provided a rich understanding of the IS project alignment process. The second phase of the study served to replicate, with extension (Berthon et al., 2002), the results of the first phase.

The quality of the case study design was assessed according to the criteria provided by Yin (1994), which evaluates construct validity, internal validity, external validity, and reliability (see Table 1).<sup>5</sup>

An initial interviewing instrument was developed using the preliminary constructs extracted from a brief review of the literature. The initial interviewing guide was assessed for face validity using an iterative pre-test. After validating the interviewing instrument, interviews were conducted and project, process, and methodology documentation were collected (see Table 2). For each project, the following organizational actors were interviewed: at least one project manager and one developer, and the primary product designer. During the interviews, project participants were asked a series of predefined questions about the project. These include questions regarding the following: success of the project, alignment of the project, justification and initial planning of the project, changes, roadblocks and escalations during the project, evaluation and review processes, implementation of the project, final review of the project and lessons learned. To help validate and triangulate the opinions of the project participants, the following organizational actors were interviewed: vice president, department manager, product manager, IT manager, and business functional manager. These organizational actors were asked questions about strategic alignment, strategy formulation processes, planning processes, change management processes, and evaluation processes, using examples from the projects examined to describe these processes. Across the two organizations, data on nine projects or cases was collected. Fifteen individuals were interviewed, many of whom provided details on multiple projects.

Interviews were recorded and transcribed. All transcripts were verified by the appropriate interviewees. Although the majority of the data collected were qualitative, some quantitative data were also captured (e.g., frequency of themes) to corroborate the qualitative findings (Eisenhardt, 1989). During the first few interviews, data were collected, transcribed and analyzed, providing initial insights and theoretical sensitivity based on the findings in the field. As the interviews progressed throughout the study, some concepts and questions were removed from the interviewing instrument because they were less relevant, while others were added.

Three different types of coding, similar to those applied in grounded theory, were used throughout the qualitative data analysis: open coding, axial coding, and selective coding (Strauss and Corbin, 1990). Coding was iterative and moved between levels as needed. There were two major coding phases: (1) in the first phase, the organization-asmachine metaphor and associated codes were used; (2) in the second phase, the organization-as-organism metaphor and associated codes were used. Examples of the codes used in each phase can be found in Table 3. The coding was conducted by a single coder. One transcript’s coding, which was relatively complex, was reviewed in detail by an independent coder to assess validity and inter-rater reliability. The intent was to compare and contrast coding of a complex transcript to ensure the researchers were of like mind and shared the same understanding of the coding process. Once the researchers reached consensus on this transcript, the remaining transcripts were coded in-depth by one researcher and reviewed in summary by the second researcher.

Another type of inter-rater reliability was also examined during the analysis of the transcripts: the agreement between respondents on each project (Samaddar and Kadiyala, 2006). During the within-case (project) analysis, the authors made note of the extent of agreement between the respondents for that project. To analyze the alignment process in this study, a process sequence of events was outlined for each project in each organizational setting using each metaphorical process lens – machine and organism. The process sequences and resulting alignment outcomes were (1) compared across projects within an organizational setting; and (2) compared across organizational settings to assess generalizability. Similarities and differences in process were analyzed to develop an understanding of the alignment process using each process lens. Cases that differed from the predominant process pattern – counter examples – helped identify important variations, contributing to the theoretical model and understanding of the project alignment process. Comparing and contrasting the results between process lenses also helped developed a richer understanding of the project alignment process. The end result of this qualitative data analysis was a set of events, transitions, and outcomes to explain and describe the project alignment process.

## Case study characteristics

Software Development Company (SDC)<sup>6</sup> is a global software product development company with 1500 employees worldwide and over \$190 million (US) in annual revenue. SDC develops several different packaged software products. A North American office of SDC was chosen as the site for the first phase of this research study. The Software Development (SD) department at SDC, which focuses on software product development, was the primary focal point for this study. The SD department is made up of several areas such as development, quality assurance (QA),<sup>7</sup> and product design. Projects cross all functional areas and include one or more product component areas.

Five projects, each focusing on the enhancement and further development of Product A, one of SDC’s software products, were chosen for this study. The projects were selected by three managers of development projects at SDC, and ranged from being moderately to highly successful. They had all been completed within the last 4 years. These five projects provided sufficient variance in processes and outcomes for the purposes of this study. Project alignment was assessed by asking each of the interviewees the degree to which the project deliverables aligned with the project objectives and the software product development strategy. Other process related questions, such as those related to changes in priorities, requirements, objectives, resources, and schedules, as well as questions related to roadblocks, issues and evaluations, helped the researchers assess changes to the dynamic state of project alignment throughout the project. Multiple respondents on projects increased the validity of the final project alignment assessment.

## Results

Based on the analysis of case data from both SDC and HTM, three broad patterns of project processes and outcomes were found. First, there were those projects that followed the project management processes traditionally seen as ‘good’ or ‘helpful’ in the literature (e.g., sufficient upfront planning, frequent evaluations, risk management, change, and scope management) and were highly aligned (Pattern Green). In contrast, there were those projects that did not follow all project management guidelines (e.g., these projects failed to complete adequate planning or risk management or evaluations), and had low alignment (Pattern Red). However, another pattern emerged in which projects failed to follow good project management techniques, such as planning, and as a result had a chaotic project process, yet were able to successfully execute the IS strategy and, thus, were highly aligned (Pattern Yellow). Our analysis of these three project patterns using each metaphor is described in the sections that follow.

## Machine metaphor defined

Metaphors, often used in the MIS (e.g., Gallupe, 2000; Behrens, 2007) and management literature (e.g., Morgan, 1997) as an analysis device, are similar to mental models – frameworks to help simplify and organize information (Hedberg, 1981; Kim, 1993; Crossan et al., 1999; Day, 2002) – in that both shape how we think and how we behave. The machine metaphor, the one most frequently used (Behrens, 2007), conceptualizes organizations as rational, purposedriven, predictable, and composed of a number of interconnected parts with well-defined roles (Morgan, 1997; Behrens, 2007). One of the underlying assumptions of this metaphor is that everything can be measured and the process and product assessed in terms of efficiency and effectiveness, respectively. Processes, mechanisms that transform inputs into outputs (the product), can be made efficient through specialization, structure, standard procedures, and routines (Behrens, 2007). Following Taylor’s (1911) principles of scientific management, planning is separated from execution and represents the most important component of the process. Execution involves adherence – sometimes heedlessly (i.e., habitually, without question and attentive thought (Weick and Roberts, 1993)) – to the plan and standard procedures (Morgan, 1997).

Projects and project management are often conceptualized as ‘machines’ – using accepted project management standards (e.g., the PM Body of Knowledge (PMI, 2004)), separating management from the staff, frequently measuring efficiency and effectiveness, emphasizing the importance of rigorous planning (e.g., Barki et al., 2001) and control techniques (e.g., Nidumolu, 1996b) and developing repeatable processes (e.g., capability maturity model (CMM) (Herbsleb et al., 1997)). The mechanistic and bureaucratic conceptualization of projects is deeply embedded in how we describe and manage projects. Thus, it is important to use the machine metaphor as a lens to analyze the project alignment process.

Thus, it was difficult to execute poorly defined plans.

Although all projects at HTM conducted evaluations at the end of each phase, both Project F and G failed to identify and discuss major issues during these reviews. These reviews were viewed as ‘gates’ that needed to be passed in order to finish the project. Thus, reviews on these projects were treated as bureaucratic and unnecessary procedures that should be circumvented. In other words, project team members, either the IT project manager or developers, hid the truth about the state of the key deliverables and any existing issues or risks so that they could ‘pass’ the evaluation and transition to the next phase event, giving the appearance of following the formal processes. Thus, any issues or defects could not be addressed because they were hidden from the appropriate project stakeholders. As a result, major project issues were identified too late in the project and could not be resolved in time. Similar to Project C at SDC, these two projects at HTM failed to execute the strategy and, thus, had low alignment.

Lastly, there was one project that followed Pattern Yellow – Project I. This project, in accordance with HTM’s methodology, conducted rigorous planning. However, the processes involved in executing the plan were chaotic. Project I involved implementing new business processes and new technology, which were unfamiliar to the organization. As a result, there was both a high frequency and high degree of change during execution, creating a very inefficient and chaotic process. However, in the end, the project was able to successfully execute the strategy and the deliverables were highly aligned.

The machine metaphor provides us with one perspective on the project alignment process. Although using this lens to analyze the project alignment process suggests that projects employing processes that adhere to good project management principles should be aligned, there were a number of inconsistencies with this relationship, which warrant further exploration. For example, how can projects following Pattern Yellow – projects that have a chaotic process and do not follow standard project management practices – be highly aligned? Further, in the between-case analysis the projects in Pattern Yellow appear to have some fundamental differences according to the machine metaphor lens. Project A conducted very limited planning, whereas Project I completed its planning deliverables. On the other hand, Project I had a high degree and high frequency of change during execution, whereas Project A did not. Further, Projects F and G both adhered to methodologies that were much more standardized and structured than those at SDC, yet this standardization and routinization of tasks did not help with project alignment for these two projects. Thus, the machine metaphor provides us with only a limited perspective on the project alignment processes.

In understanding organization as a rational, technical process, mechanical imagery tends to underplay the human aspects of organization and to overlook the fact that the tasks facing organizations are much more complex, uncertain, and difficult than those that can be performed by most machines. (Morgan, 1997: 27)

To develop a richer understanding of the project alignment process we now examine the process using the organism metaphor.

## Organism metaphor defined

In contrast to the machine metaphor, the organism metaphor views the organization from a more organic and social perspective. The organism metaphor views the organization as a living system composed of interrelated subsystems (e.g., the social subsystem and the technical subsystem), which exist and interact with the environment (Morgan, 1997; Behrens, 2007). This interaction with the environment is bi-directional – the environment influences the organization and the organization influences the environment (Morgan, 1997). The organization, driven by needs – such as survival, competition for resources, and growth – progresses through a lifecycle in which evolution, learning, adaptation to change, and maturation are key processes (Morgan, 1997; Behrens, 2007). Thus, the processes are more organic than those within the machine metaphor.

While often a distinct metaphor itself, we include aspects of the culture metaphor in our conceptualization of the organism. Organisms, as noted above, include social subsystems. These subsystems are linked to the organism’s needs and survival. Thus, in our analysis of the organization-as-organism, we include aspects traditionally included in the culture metaphor, such as subcultures and the distinction between in-group and out-group, as well as rituals and norms (Schultz, 1994; Morgan, 1997; Behrens, 2007), to further examine the social subsystem and the human side of organizations (Schultz, 1994).

## Organism metaphor analysis

The following sections provide a detailed analysis and description of the processes within each project pattern using the organism metaphor (see Table 3). SDC projects are described first, followed by the HTM projects.

Pattern Green – SDC: examining the projects following Pattern Green using the organism metaphor, we found evidence of both evolution and maturation processes. Over the time period covered by the projects examined, SDC began implementing more formal project and development pro cesses. Specifically, SDC implemented a methodology called Feature Driven Development (FDD) (Palmer and Fetsing, 2002). This methodology prescribes an agile, iterative, and adaptable software development process. The FDD methodology suggests breaking down projects into multiple 2-week iterations and milestones, which helps improve the quality of estimates and flexibility to change. Project B was the first project at SDC to use FDD. SDC developer 3 commented:

This was one of the first projects we did feature driven development on and what it did is it made us break all the features down and plan everything by feature. And as far as estimates go it really changes how we estimate. Before we used to look at a feature and give a really rough estimate. It’s really hard to guess what it will take. What feature driven development forced us to do is break everything down into two-week intervals. So even if you did get it wrong, you are only that feature wrong. It really helped to focus and get a more accurate estimate on two weeks rather than on a two month feature.

Further, as described above Project B interacted with its environment using customer reviews at a ‘big user group event’. This collection of processes underscore the evolutionary aspect of projects and depict a maturity in project management processes – moving away from ad hoc processes to defined and repeatable processes (Herbsleb et al., 1997).

However, Projects D and E were not as mature as Project B. These projects both occurred prior to Project B and, thus, before the FDD methodology had been implemented. From an evolutionary perspective, Project D was implemented after Project C in response to the issues identified in that project. Thus, Project D was the next phase in the lifecycle and evolved out of Project C. Project D managers learned from the mistakes of Project C and did things differently, including using a different technology and a more rigorous evaluation process. Project E’s process was less mature in comparison. The developer on Project E commented:

It was like two kids in a candy store before. I completed the prototype and boom I checked it in no questions asked. People completed the windows they checked it in. You couldn’t get away with that now. Now you have to do a code review. For your feature you have two people reviewing the design note. After coding the feature, those same two people [who reviewed the design note] review the code. The process is now much more enriched.

However, the process on Project E was evolutionary and involved learning. As the team lead on Project E noted:

So the team dynamics just to clarify that – I had 4 people at the time, of which I would say maybe one was knowledgeable with [software name] but not an expert. And we went with 4 people and myself, which I didn’t know much about [software name] either, and we jumped into the process. So we learned something on the way too. We learned a lot on the way.

The learning that occurred during the project emphasized the importance of project execution processes and ‘knowing in practice’ – knowledge enacted through the ongoing and situated daily actions of the project (Orlikowski, 2002). The team on Project E, by creating a prototype and immersing themselves in the design and code both reflectively and heedfully (i.e., attentively, critically and purposefully) (Weick and Roberts, 1993), successfully executed the strategy despite not following formal procedures and protocols. As the team lead commented on the team’s ability to adapt:

I have a great team. I have 3 guys with lots of experience so if we run into a problem with the code we can figure it out between us.

It is interesting to note that the FDD methodology adopted by Project B, an agile method, promoted knowing in practice and heedful interrelating during each 2-week cycle. Rather than process maturity, it appeared that heedful interrelating among the team during execution and, thus, knowing in practice were critical processes leading to project alignment for the projects following Pattern Green. Thus, the process of project alignment appeared more organic, interactive and learning-oriented than the machine metaphor led us to believe.

Pattern Red – SDC: Project C, in contrast to the projects following Pattern Green, did not evolve and adapt over the course of the project and did not engage and align with its environment. As described above, it was the end customer who found many of the major issues with the software after it was delivered. By not interacting with and learning about the environment in which the product would ‘live’ and adapting to that environment, the final product did not ‘survive’ long. Rather, the project team followed the ‘rituals’ of the project heedlessly rather than heedfully (Weick and Roberts, 1993). The team gathered the high-level requirements and developed and tested the product within their own insular environment, according to a limited set of user scenarios. As a result, the project team missed picking up on both the issues that other organizations were having with this same technology and the variety of environments within which the product would live.

Thus, Pattern Red highlighted the importance of interacting and aligning with the environment and adapting accordingly. This pattern also supported the importance of heedful interrelating during the execution rather than habitual adherence to rituals.

Pattern Yellow – SDC: Project A appeared to have ‘wandered down the wrong path’ at the beginning of the project. Individuals underestimated the impact and scope of this project and how long it would take to complete. As noted above, Project A threw the standard project processes aside. What was most interesting about this case was that Project A started immediately after Project B. Thus, one would have expected the project to adopt the new FDD methodology, which had been used successfully on Project B. However, this was not the case. The root of this project’s success appeared to lie within the execution phase where, despite poor and ill-defined plans, individuals on the project worked together heedfully to clarify the requirements and the deliverables as they were being developed. For example, one of the developers noted ‘it was actually a process of discovery as to what exactly had to be done’. This discovery process was very active and mindful throughout the execution phase. This developer went on to describe the discovery and learning process:

There were quite a few back and forth discussions of what exactly was going to be on the dialog. That actually was ongoing for quite a while. So at some point I generated a matrix of combinations of what not and had a meeting on it and ended up finalizing exactly what they wanted and even after that was done, they still made changes later on. So in reality the UE changes went well beyond code complete. We did huge changes to that dialog as bug fixes well into the QC cycle.

Rather than avoid change, Project A actively adapted to its situation and environment. One of the project managers admitted to the early failings:

We just didn’t pay it the attention that it needed. So it wasn’t brought up and started as soon as it possibly could because it was ‘We will deal with that later’. We did attempt to drop it from the project because once we got looking at it and said this is going to take longer and tried to drop it but then from a more senior level once they figured out what it was going to look like without it, they said no you need to have it.

The project was not eliminated because it was deemed to be an important part of the organization’s strategy. Thus the project, driven by the needs of the organization and its customers, proceeded so that this strategy could be executed. However, despite the realization by the project team and senior management that the scope had been underestimated, the schedule was not changed. Although developers were asked whether they could complete the project on time, both the developer subculture and organizational culture influenced the answer:

Well yeah I know that developers don’t want to admit it is going to take longer. They will say ‘Yeah I can try to get it done’. (Developer on Project A)

Senior management recognized when they asked the developers whether the expanded scope could be completed within the current timeline the answer would always be yes and, as a result of bypassing the standard development processes, the results were ‘predictable’.

The project did adapt to the environment eventually – the timeline was extended and changes to requirements incorporated into the product. Further, based on the needs of the customer (environment) the project was implemented in two separate releases: (1) an initial release with some minor yet fixable issues to the customers who were in most need of the software; and (2) a second release to all other customers. Thus, similar to the other patterns, Pattern Yellow highlighted the importance of interacting and aligning with the environment as well as heedful interrelating and, thus, knowing in practice during execution. This pattern also identified the importance of adaptation and change and the influence that culture had on the process.

Replication – HTM: Project H, HTM’s Pattern Green project, showed maturity in the project processes that it was following. This project followed Project I and was viewed as the second phase of the organization’s new strategy. Thus, Project H, which included some of the same project team members, learned from Project I. In addition to the evolution and maturation that occurred during this project, there were also examples of heedful interrelating, which were important for aligning. Due to the size and complexity of the project, some areas of the project received more attention than others. Attention was based on perceived need and risk, and depended on the interactions between the project team and business area representatives. In several cases, business area representatives interacted with the project team in a heedless manner until they ‘woke up’ and realized that there were some critical aspects they needed included in the project for ‘survival’. At this point, critical issues were raised and heedful interactions for the area in question began. Thus, heedful interrelating on this project fluctuated by areas and over time, yet was an important part of the project process contributing to alignment.

Projects following Pattern Red had similar issues to the Pattern Red project at SDC. Projects F and G both went down the wrong path from a technical perspective. Developers on project F ‘satisficed’ by choosing to use a technology based on what they felt most comfortable with, rather than a technology that was well suited to the needs of the business. Project G, on the other hand, did not realize the complexity of their technical design until it was too late in the project. As the project manager noted, ‘This was a late ‘‘oops we can’t do everything you want with the tech design’’.’ As a result, a large portion of the project’s scope had to be dropped at the last minute. Both of these projects failed to adapt their technical design and execution to the needs of the business. Further, Project F failed to align with the environment in terms of recognizing the different technological choices available.

As described above, Projects F and G did not disclose major issues during their end-of-phase reviews. In fact, they purposefully misrepresented the details of their phase deliverables. A project manager from Project F commented, ‘We were compliant with MSF in form, but not in function. There was a lot of spin going on. IT couldn’t speak to the business truthfully.’ A project manager from Project G noted that the developers hid the truth about major technical deficiencies, effectively hiding the project misalignment. Once the truth was exposed, it was too late to resolve the issues. As a result, a new project was added to the IT plan for later development. This type of deception and image management did not appear in the projects at SDC, but may be due to differences between the culture and dynamics of a large corporate IT department and those of a software development firm.

A project manager on Project F described project managers as ‘lemmings’, which alludes to the heedless adherence to rituals seen. Interestingly, Pattern Red projects at SDC appeared to heedlessly follow rituals, whereas Pattern Red projects at HTM actively hid their knowledge of issues and appeared to follow the ritual.

Finally, the Pattern Yellow project – Project I – had many similarities to the Pattern Yellow project at SDC. This project was driven by the needs of the organization. In fact, the organization was depending on this project for survival. It was well accepted among the project team that failure to successfully complete the project, which was tightly linked to the rollout of a new product, would likely result in the dissolution of the company.

Due to the new business processes and new technology involved in Project I, the project team and affected business units had a very steep learning curve to overcome. There was significant discovery going on throughout the project, which resulted in frequent changes to requirements and systems during the execution phase. A project manager commented on Project I:

Given the tremendous unknowns and learning curves associated with the various aspects of large strategic initiatives, businesses must remain flexible around delivery timelines, budget and short-term success metrics. With large cross-functional projects like [Project I], on-the-fly change cannot be avoided. As these changes impact the project, company leadership (both project and operational) must manage this change by closely analyzing and keeping the project’s long term goals in mind.

The number and frequency of changes were overwhelming for the project team. Existing change control procedures became useless and interfered more than they helped.

Similar to Project A, timelines were not extended in response to changes in requirements. However, during this period of rapid change, the team was actively engaged with the changes and heedfully interacted across subteams to ensure that each change was properly incorporated. As each change was implemented and tested within the broader functionality of the system, more changes were identified. Thus, it was not ritualistic change control procedures that facilitated alignment, but the constant and active adaptation and learning that took place across the team through heedful interaction and knowing in practice. Although there were stability issues post-implementation, the project deliverables were aligned and the strategy successfully executed.

## Discussion

Strategic alignment, although often represented as an endstate, has been described as a process (Broadbent and Weill, 1993; Henderson and Venkatraman, 1993; Luftman et al., 1993; Luftman and Brier, 1999; Hirschheim and Sabherwal, 2001; Sabherwal et al., 2001; Hussin et al., 2002). Specifically, strategic alignment has been described as ‘a process of continuous adaptation and change’ (Henderson and Venkatraman, 1993: 5). Following the sequence of events and transitions in the process of strategic alignment will lead to higher levels of alignment (Broadbent and Weill, 1993). Although project alignment has not generally been explored as part of the strategic alignment process, it is an important phase in the strategic alignment journey. The goal of this study was to explore project alignment processes.

In this paper, we extend the current understanding of strategic alignment processes so that we may understand how to achieve IS final project alignment. The project alignment process described above using the machine and organism metaphors to analyze data from nine projects across two organizational settings help illuminate the path from strategy to the completion of projects that realize the

IT-enabled aspects of this strategy and, thus, strategy execution.

Our exploration of the project alignment process calls into question some of our long-held beliefs about project management. In our analysis of the case data, we closely examined three patterns of projects in each organization. Through this analysis we found patterns of projects in which our assumptions about good project management and the relationship to project performance did not hold. To understand this contradictory evidence, we examined the concept of project alignment and, more specifically the alignment process using both a rational ‘machine-like’ process lens and a social and evolutionary ‘organism-like process lens. Each metaphor provided valuable insights into the project alignment process (see Tables 6 and 7). We summarize key insights below.

The importance of planning processes and deliverables early in the project lifecycle, such as defining requirements, to reduce project risk and uncertainty is supported in the literature (Zmud, 1980; Boehm, 1989; Turner, 1992; Barki et al., 1993, 2001; Keil, 1995; Nidumolu, 1995, 1996a; Wallace et al., 2004) and one of the most widely held assumptions about project management. However, our findings suggest that the key alignment processes are found in executing rather than planning processes. Executing processes involved knowing in practice and heedful interrelating in order to identify and resolve issues as well as adapt the final deliverables so that they aligned with the current objectives.

The mechanistic and bureaucratic conceptualization of projects, which is deeply embedded in how we actually discuss and manage projects, may be doing project management and, as a result, strategy execution a disservice. While having established guidelines and standards to follow is helpful, there is value in taking a more organic approach and treating projects as learning mechanisms. Learning can result from interactions internally – within the team or organization – or externally – with customers and other external groups. This also helps the team adapt each project to the characteristics and dynamics of the environment within which it resides.

In the projects examined in this research, evolution and learning throughout the projects created the need for change and adaptation. While change is traditionally perceived negatively (e.g., change was viewed as ‘defects’ at SDC), using the organism metaphor, we found that change from the perspective of aligning and executing strategy is positive. Change is an inevitable and a critical part of the path towards project alignment. Although changes may be disruptive and delay a project, they can help bring final project deliverables into alignment. The need for change and adaptation to create aligned deliverables was particularly evident in the Pattern Yellow projects. Incorporating change and learning were facilitated by heedful interrelating across subteams and interacting with the environment. Thus, learning and adaptability are important aspects of the project alignment process.

Our results also provide a different view of process maturity. Both SDC and HTM adopted formal development methodologies and were working on improving and maturing their project processes. In fact, HTM was working towards certification at CMM level three. Standardized output controls, such as those in use at HTM, have been found to help reduce project risk and improve project outcomes (Nidumolu, 1996b). However, while the projects at HTM were more mature than those at SDC, this fact did not result in higher levels of project alignment or performance. Furthermore, on Project E, a project with low maturity, alignment was achieved. It is possible that process maturity promotes heedless adherence to routines and the creation of rituals. In some projects, process maturity led to shirking and hiding behaviors with the intention of ‘appearing’ to follow the rituals (e.g., Projects F and G). This suggests that project management process maturity can be overly mechanistic and bureaucratic.

These findings can be explained in part by links between IS development methodology adherence and ritualistic behavior (Wastell and Newman, 1993; Wastell, 1996, 1999). On the one hand, these methodologies may support the repeatable and measurable processes required for process maturity. However, methodologies may also be followed blindly as an organizational ritual to reduce the anxiety and stress associated with IS development (Wastell and Newman, 1993; Wastell, 1996,1999). These rituals provide developers and the project team ‘with a feeling of security and efficiency at the expense of real engagement with the task at hand’ (Wastell, 1996: 25). Overdependence is more likely when the organization is learning to use a methodology. This may, however, turn into an unhealthy over-dependence as experience grows and the methodology is used to relinquish responsibility, avoid challenges, and reduce anxiety (Wastell, 1996, 1999). Thus, as process maturity increases, there is the potential for developing an over-dependence on methodology and ritualistic behaviors. As a result, project participants may fail to critically engage with one another on the task at hand (Wastell, 1996).

Our results also suggest that organizations should be aware that project team members might provide the image of adhering to routine evaluations while not actually conducting the evaluations in a truthful and faithful manner. A political process lens may help shed further light on these findings by examining conflicting individual and collective goals, as well as power and control issues.

The project patterns examined in this research were discussed as three distinct categories. However these patterns, as well as project alignment itself, reside along a continuum. Project alignment can be measured along its three dimensions to demonstrate the degree (e.g., low, medium, high) to which the project is aligned (Jenkin and Chan, 2005). Similarly, there are also degrees of ‘green’, ‘yellow’, and ‘red’ projects. For example, Project I had a number stability issues post-implementation that affected the degree to which its deliverables were aligned. Combined with the frequent changes to requirements during the project, Project I may be approaching a yellow-orange shade (i.e., closer to red than green). Thus, projects can be measured in terms of degrees of project alignment and process shade.

## Conclusions

IS projects are challenging and frequently fail. However, projects are critical for executing strategy. Thus, how can organizations manage projects to ensure their strategy is executed successfully? Project alignment and, more speci fically, the project alignment process is proposed as a mechanism for strategy execution.

In this study, we examined projects in two different organizational settings: one a software development firm, the other a large high-tech manufacturing organization with a corporate IT department. Using two different metaphors to compare and contrast the projects within and between these two settings, we were able to develop an understanding of the project alignment process and, thus, the process of executing strategy. In this analysis, three patterns of projects were discovered and compared using machine and organism metaphors. Pattern Green projects followed the project management processes traditionally seen as ‘good’ and were highly aligned. Pattern Red projects did not follow all project management guidelines and had low alignment. Pattern Yellow projects, the most interesting pattern, failed to follow good project management techniques and had a chaotic project process, yet were able to successfully execute the IS strategy and, thus, were highly aligned. Using the machine and organism metaphors to compare these project patterns, we found evidence contradicting some widely held assumptions about project management – for example, the value of planning, process maturation, and controlling change. The organism metaphor was particularly helpful in understanding these contradictions and identifying critical processes for project alignment.

Rather than taking a mechanistic and bureaucratic approach to project management, our results suggest that in order to align project deliverables to dynamic project objectives and strategy, conceptualizing projects in a more organic and evolutionary light is valuable. Instead of the frequently touted planning processes, we found that executing processes, in which team members heedfully interacted, knew ‘in practice’, learned and adapted project deliverables, were critical for facilitating project alignment, as demonstrated in Pattern Green and Yellow projects. Further, we observed in Pattern Red projects that process maturation might impede project alignment if it results in heedless adherence to rituals or hiding behaviors giving the image of adherence. However, as shown in Pattern Green projects, processes such as those advocated in agile approaches promote heedful interrelating and executing processes and, thus, promote project alignment.

Our findings suggest that evolutionary processes, learning, and adaptation to change are critical for project alignment. In this research, we provide an expanded view of change processes and a new conceptualization of change management – not opposing change, but channeling it towards successfully executing strategy. As found in the Pattern Yellow projects, change and adaptation were also important for aligning the deliverables. Although the level and frequency of change were disruptive and in some cases delayed the project, change was critical for aligning the deliverables with the objectives and strategy. Ritualistic change control procedures did not facilitate alignment. Instead, heedful interrelating across teams in order to identify issues and learn how to adapt deliverables facilitated alignment with the objectives and strategy. This is not to say that all change was good – changes that were not linked to meeting the project objectives were distracting and needed to be properly controlled.

## Implications for researchers

This research contributes to the strategic alignment and project management literatures and, more broadly, to the MIS literature. This study is focused on exploring and developing an understanding of project alignment using a process approach as opposed to a variance approach as developed in Jenkin and Chan (2005), thus extending the literature on IS strategic alignment. Strategic alignment process research has traditionally focused on the strategic formulation and planning processes as well as the evolution of strategic alignment over time (e.g. Sabherwal et al., 2001). Our analysis focuses on the IT-enabled end results of strategic alignment and the processes to get there. Simply achieving strategic alignment at an intellectual level is not sufficient for achieving final project alignment and thus realizing the aligned strategy. Extending studies of the strategic alignment process into the actual project implementation processes, or tactics, is a novel contribution. Further, using a process approach, a rarity compared to variance approaches, is a contribution to the MIS literature.

Few IS strategic alignment studies focus on projects. Other literatures, such as information economics (Parker et al., 1988) and project portfolio management (Benko and McFarlan, 2003a, b) discuss projects in the context of strategic alignment. However, they are focused on the initial transition from strategy to projects – the process of planning, choosing and prioritizing projects based on strategic alignment among other factors. A recent study linked strategic alignment to IS projects in terms of how strategic alignment positively affects the quality of IS project planning and reduces implementation problems (Kearns and Sabherwal, 2006). These two aspects of IT projects mediated the relationship between strategic alignment and the business effects of alignment.

These studies did not address IS project alignment specifically, however. In contrast, the project alignment process in this study is focused on the process of executing the aligned strategy and implementing the project to ensure the final deliverables are aligned with the IS strategy and project objectives, which may change over the course of the project. In this study, we provide a rich description and explanation of how events unfold on a project, using both a machine metaphor and organism metaphor, such that the final deliverables are aligned. Thus, our study of the process of project alignment is an important extension of the existing literature on projects and strategic alignment. In addition, by adopting multiple metaphors, our work provides a contribution the MIS literature, which predominantly uses the machine metaphor (Behrens, 2007).

This research also contributes to the project management literature by providing insight into evidence that contradicts widely held beliefs about project management processes. Rather than planning and process maturity, we found that executing, change, and learning through heedful interrelating and knowledge in practice were critical for project alignment. In fact, process maturity was seen as hampering project alignment in some instances.

## Implications for practitioners

This research also provides implications for practitioners regarding the process of aligning project deliverables with project objectives and IS strategy. First, this research highlights the importance of learning, adaptation, and change processes. Since change is endemic to projects, it is important to understand how it influences project alignment. Rather than discouraging change on projects, change should be viewed as something that is necessary and valuable for project alignment. Given this conceptualization of change and change processes, this research suggests that new processes and tools for managing change be incorporated in project management standards such as PMI’s PMBOK (PMI, 2004). In fact, the importance of learning and change suggests that learning and change be included as a new project management knowledge area in the PMBOK.

Often the predominant goals in project management are focused on managing scope, time, and costs. Project alignment, as a distinct concept, may not be a conscious goal for organizations. Although project managers are trying to achieve the initial objectives as set forth in the project charter and other planning documents (PMI, 2004), the focus on initial objectives and timelines may be too constraining. Reframing the goals of the project towards project alignment, measuring this alignment throughout the project, and enabling changes that help achieve alignment may help project managers and stakeholders achieve project alignment and the benefits of strategic alignment. Thus, organizations should consider reevaluating the metrics used to assess the performance of project managers and projects so that project alignment, not ‘on-time’ and ‘on-budget’, is emphasized. Further, more emphasis should be placed on execution rather than planning.

This research also sheds light on the importance of honest and faithful evaluations. With the increasing adoption of formal methodologies and processes such as CMM and other agile methods such as MSF and FDD, organizations may think they are adhering to ‘best practice’ project processes. However, process maturation may lead to the creation of rituals and heedless adherence to those rituals. Further, the adoption of these processes may be an image only. Thus, managers must be aware of the pitfalls of these processes and create incentives, or remove penalties, for raising issues and problems.

## Suggestions for future research

This study has increased our understanding of the IS project alignment process. We have extended the alignment process from strategic alignment to project alignment, which in this study refers to the process of achieving alignment at the project implementation level. We examined two very different organizations in this research. Although we developed a rich understanding of alignment in these two organizational settings and projects, additional replications across different types of organizations and different types of projects will further validate the results and contribute to a richer understanding of the project alignment process. Future research into the project alignment process could also examine international and cross-cultural projects. Further examples of projects within each pattern, as well as cases in which major changes in strategy occur may also provide further insights into the project alignment process.

Comparing and contrasting the data using two different metaphors – machine and organism – enabled us to develop a richer understanding of project alignment processes and allowed us to explain evidence contradicting our traditional understanding of project management processes and outcomes. Future research, using a political metaphor, could investigate how power and control issues as well as opposing individual and collective goals (Cule and Robey, 2004) shape the unfolding of events in the project alignment process.

Integrating our newly developed understanding of the project alignment process and the recent research regarding the mediating role of IS projects in the relationship between IS strategic alignment and firm performance would be another fruitful area for future research.

Other interesting extensions of this research include examining how the project alignment process influences strategic alignment and alignment maturity (Luftman and Kempaiah, 2007) as well as how IT architecture and architecture maturity (Ross, 2003) influence project alignment.

## Concluding remarks

In summary, this research contributes to the MIS literature by extending the concept of project alignment, earlier developed using a variance perspective (Jenkin and Chan, 2005), to include a process view of how to achieve alignment of the final project deliverables with project objectives and the IS strategy. By doing so, this research helps to integrate and link the strategic and tactical processes related to strategic and project alignment. These two forms of alignment are tightly interconnected: project alignment requires strategic alignment as a starting point, and strategic alignment requires project alignment to successfully implement the organization’s aligned strategy. The processes identified in this study have provided important insights into the nature of IS project alignment beyond choosing, prioritizing, and structuring projects. We hope this study will be useful to both IS researchers and practitioners.
