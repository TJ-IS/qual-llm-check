# Contractual Provisions to Mitigate Holdup: Evidence from Information Technology Outsourcing

Anjana Susarla

Foster School of Business, University of Washington, Seattle, Washington 98195, asusarla@u.washington.edu

Ramanath Subramanyam

University of Illinois Urbana–Champaign, Champaign, Illinois 61820, rsubrama@uiuc.edu

Prasanna Karhade

Department of Information Systems, Business Statistics and Operations Management, The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, Clear Water Bay, Kowloon, Hong Kong, karhade@ust.hk

he complexity and scope of outsourced information technology (IT) demands relationship-specific invest ments from vendors, which, when combined with contract incompleteness, may result in underinvestmen and inefficient bargaining, referred to as the holdup problem. Using a unique data set of over 100 IT outsourcing contracts, we examine whether contract extensiveness, i.e., the extent to which firms and vendors can fore see contingencies when designing contracts for outsourced IT services, can alleviate holdup. While extensively detailed contracts are likely to include a greater breadth of activities outsourced to a vendor, task complexity makes it difficult to draft extensive contracts. Furthermore, extensive contracts may still be incomplete with respect to enforcement. We then examine the role of nonprice contractual provisions, contract duration, and extendibility terms, which give firms an option to extend the contract to limit the likelihood of holdup. We also validate the ex post efficiency of contract design choices by examining renewals of contracting agreements.

Key words: contract duration; extendibility clauses; holdup; underinvestment; information technology outsourcing; incomplete contracts

## 1. Introduction

Fueled by their desire to improve the quality of services, to lower the costs of information technology (IT) spending (DiRomualdo and Gurbaxani 1998), and to obtain access to scarce resources, firms have increased their reliance on IT outsourcing (Forrester 2006). The growth of this phenomenon is evident from recent industry statistics. The total revenue generated by the global IT services industry (which includes the outsourcing market) was estimated to be over \$716 billion with an anticipated compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 7% until the year 2011 (DataMonitor 2007).

However, practitioners and academics alike have cautioned that IT outsourcing deals are fraught with difficulties and high rates of failure (Young 2004, Gurbaxani 2005). IT systems are inextricably interlinked with business processes, and outsourced IT initiatives involve business process transformation that requires relationship-specific investments by the vendor. Consider the example of the vendor Electronic Data Systems (EDS) that designed IT systems to monitor the supply-chain operations of General Motors (GM). These systems ultimately proved critical to GM’s success (Anderson 2006). The first motive for contracting in such initiatives is to provide incentives to the vendor to undertake specific investments in learning about the industry context and business environment and operations of the firm.<sup>1</sup> In the absence of appropriate incentives, vendors will underinvest in noncontractible specific investments, which lowers the value from outsourcing. The second motive is to minimize the threat of ex post bargaining or rent seeking where parties seek to appropriate surplus. Termination penalties and the potential for business disruption create switching costs for firms, making them wary of changing vendors midway through an outsourcing initiative. Vendors may seek to appropriate surplus through excessive bargaining, with the result that the firm is locked into higher rates and diminished service (Forrester 2006). Conversely, vendors are concerned that their sunk investments could increase the bargaining power of a firm, whereby a vendor is pressured into lowering rates, resulting in a reluctance to invest in relationship-specific capital.

Using a unique data set compiled from public disclosure filings with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), we examine contracts for large scale and complex IT transactions. We consider the need for contract design for outsourced IT services to address two ex post contracting hazards: the twin threats of underinvestment and inefficient bargaining. Contracts for outsourced IT are necessarily incomplete (Banerjee and Duflo 2000) and characterized by substantial post-contract opportunism (Ang and Beath 1993). The fundamental challenge in contracting when specific investments are important is that one party can take advantage of contractual incompleteness to extract rents from the other (Klein et al. 1978, Klein 1996), leading to the potential for underinvestment or inefficient bargaining, collectively referred to as the holdup problem. One stream of prior literature argues that parties to a contract should foresee the contractual hazards that may arise ex post and design the contract ex ante in a manner that limits future potential for holdup (Klein et al. 1978). However, another stream of prior literature argues that contracts are essentially incomplete and that parties cannot credibly commit not to engage in inefficient bargaining ex post. This lowers incentives to undertake specific investments (Edlin and Hermalin 2000, Noldeke and Schmidt 1995).

Measuring and assessing holdup is difficult because its occurrence and consequences are often unobservable to parties external to a transaction.<sup>2</sup> We therefore consider two key facets of outsourced tasks: the complexity of the task and its scope. These facets increase the need for noncontractible specific investments, which in turn raises the threat of holdup. The central research issue investigated in this paper is the role of three key contractual provisions, contract extensiveness, contract duration and extendibility terms, that can serve as a remedy against holdup. The analysis proceeds in three stages. First, we investigate the role of contract extensiveness, i.e., the extent to which firms and vendors can foresee contingencies and specify detailed obligations and processes as part of a formal contract, to deal with the complexity and scope of outsourced tasks. This is in line with prior literature that suggests that detailed contracts can force a vendor to make specific investments (Williamson 1983) and reduce the potential for rent appropriation through bargaining (Klein et al. 1978). However, we recognize that very detailed contracts may still be incomplete with respect to enforcement (e.g., Rogerson 1992). Therefore, the problems of underinvestment and rent seeking may persist in the presence of detailed contract terms (e.g., Klein et al. 1978), necessitating mechanisms to protect against post contracting hazards.

Second, we posit that nonprice provisions such as contract duration and extendibility clauses, which confer to firms the right to extend the contract, can overcome the problem of contract incompleteness. Contract duration can address the problem of underinvestment through the promise of longer term interaction. Extendibility terms balance the potential for opportunistic bargaining with the promise of future payoffs that accrue as a result of specific investments, thus mitigating the potential for underinvestment as well as rent seeking. Third, we validate the arguments in the paper by analyzing contract renewals. We find that contracts with extendibility clauses are significantly more likely to be renewed, suggesting that extendibility provisions can provide appropriate incentives to vendors and protect firms against vendor nonperformance.

This paper can make the following contributions to the literature. Prior research has examined the role of pricing or compensation, primarily at the level of the IT project (e.g., Barry et al. 2002, Gopal et al. 2003, Koh et al. 2004). By contrast, we highlight that contracts are fundamentally incomplete and that nonprice provisions play a strategic role in contracts structuring. Drawing on literature that describes contractual solutions to the holdup problem (e.g., Noldeke and Schmidt 1995, Edlin and Hermalin 2000), we argue that parties are motivated by payoffs from repeated interaction to undertake specific investment and to reduce the likelihood of inefficient bargaining. This paper can complement analytical work on the design of contracts for IT outsourcing (e.g., Whang 1992) with evidence from actual contracts to understand how parties anticipate and design contingencies ex ante that are important to manage potential problems ex post.

The balance of the paper is organized as follows: In §2, we conceptualize the role of contractual hazards and determinants of contract duration and extendibility and present the hypotheses and research model. In §3, we discuss operationalization of measures and the data collection process. Section 4 presents a discussion of the results and §5 provides concluding remarks.

## 2. Theory and Hypotheses

## 2.1. Contract Design and the Holdup Problem

Bounded rationality of economic actors poses limits on their ability to craft complete contracts (Williamson 1975). Contract incompleteness gives rise to a potential for ex post opportunism, whereby one party seeks to extract appropriable quasi-rents (AQRs) (Klein et al. 1978) from the other. Anticipating that the potential gains from (noncontractible) specific investments are dissipated by ex post bargaining, parties may underinvest in relationship-specific assets (e.g., Hart and Moore 1988). Collectively these are referred to as the holdup problem.

Vendors of outsourced IT initiatives need to make substantial investments in defining system specifications (e.g., Gopal et al. 2003) and incur human capital investments such as learning to contract with the firm. Because specific investments generate the greatest value inside the relationship (Williamson 1983), there is a cost to the client in switching to another vendor when the contract is terminated or not renewed, thus increasing the rents to be captured by the vendor through bargaining. At the same time, specific investments that cannot be redeployed outside the relationship create quasi-rents for the client. The complexity and measurement difficulties of characterizing IT outsourcing may pose challenges to crafting complete contracts. Thus, there is potential for holdup in IT outsourcing contracts if there are specific investments that create AQRs and one of the parties can take advantage of contract incompleteness to appropriate the quasi rents (Klein 1996). When vendors try to appropriate the surplus, firms will face an erosion of the value from outsourcing. Alternatively, when firms try to appropriate surplus, a vendor who may anticipate bargaining by a firm will deliver perfunctory performance, as opposed to consummate (e.g., Williamson 1975), by underinvesting in client specific deliverables. ## 2.2. Factors that Increase Holdup in IT Outsourcing

We posit two dimensions of a task that increase the likelihood of holdup in an outsourcing context, task complexity that calls for “new knowledge” (e.g., von Hippel 1994), rather than recycling of existing knowledge, to fulfill the responsibilities of the transaction and the scope of the outsourced transaction (e.g., Anderson and Dekker 2005, Oxley and Sampson 2004). Following March and Simon (1958), we define task complexity as the unknown or uncertain alternatives in task execution (March and Simon 1958), resulting in inexact connection between means and ends transformations (March and Simon 1958). The task scope occurs from a broad spectrum of activities covered under a contract (Whang 1992, Anderson and Dekker 2005). When tasks have multiple performance dimensions and outcomes, some of which may be qualitative (Latham and Yuki 1975), it is more difficult to specify guidelines for task execution and to evaluate outcomes.

IT systems are highly specific to each company (e.g., Whang 1992). Vendors need to undertake an exhaustive process of evaluating solutions and outcomes, a costly problem search that involves “local” knowledge of the problem space (von Hippel 1994), i.e., knowledge of the client organization. The impact of both task dimensions is that the vendor (and to some extent the firm) need to make considerable noncontractible specific investments to conceptualize the potential space of solutions, making it difficult for the transacting parties to foresee contingencies, specify desired outcomes and vendor behavior, and increasing the difficulty of outcome measurement. The result is an increased likelihood of underinvestment by a vendor (e.g., Rogerson 1992). The importance of specific investments also increases the extent of quasirents that can be appropriated ex post, and therefore the likelihood of inefficient bargaining (e.g., Klein et al. 1978). Both task dimensions therefore increase the likelihood of holdup, necessitating diverse contractual mechanisms to protect against holdup. We discuss three such mechanisms next. ## 2.3. Determinants of Contract Extensiveness

The extensiveness of a contract, i.e., the extent to which obligations and processes are specified in the contract (Anderson and Dekker 2005), prevents holdup. When parties can anticipate potential contracting hazards and write detailed contracts, there is less potential to misinterpret contract terms (Klein et al. 1978). This acts as a safeguard against postcontract opportunism by the vendor (e.g., Crocker and Masten 1988). Detailed contractual provisions also reduce the likelihood of underinvestment by enhancing the ability of the firm to monitor the vendor (Brickley 1999, Jensen and Meckling 1992)<sup>3</sup> and by forcing the vendor to make credible commitments in the form of specific investments (e.g., Williamson 1983).

A broader scope of activities exchanged among parties increases the likelihood of outcome measurement and underinvestment, thus requiring more detailed contingencies in contracts (e.g., Klein 1996, Oxley and Sampson 2004). We therefore expect that a relationship that includes a greater breadth of activities (e.g., planning, development, user training, and delivery of IT support) is more likely to be associated with an extensively detailed contract than relationships that involve a single kind of activity.

Hypothesis 1A (H1A). Task scope is positively associated with extensive contracts.

Task complexity increases the nonstandard problem solving required in executing a task (e.g., von Hippel 1994) and the uncertainty in specifying means-ends connections (e.g., March and Simon 1958), limiting firms’ ability to foresee contractual contingencies (Segal 1999) and specifying measurable outcomes and exact transformation processes (Rogerson 1992). Because the means-ends connections are difficult to grasp, the form of optimal transaction cannot be specified with certainty ex ante. This increases the difficulty in delineating contract terms, leading to contract incompleteness.<sup>4</sup>

Hypothesis 1B (H1B). Task complexity is negatively associated with extensive contracts.

We control for the transaction size, which captures the difficulties of communication and overhead in project management and other responsibilities (e.g., Boehm 1981). Vendor personnel often use structured methodologies, modularization and software development practices that reduce some of the cognitive load due to size; Transaction size may not directly increase the complexity of the outsourced task, but may affect the contingencies to be covered in contract design. We also control for task programmability, i.e., the extent to which managers understand the inputs of a process (Ouchi 1979) and thus the extent to which contingencies should be codified in a contract. A firm can implement effective monitoring and behavioral controls (Jensen and Meckling 1992) by imposing strict guidelines on which activities should be performed and how they should be performed (Heide et al. 2007).

## 2.4. Nonprice Provisions in Contracts

There is a greater need for relationship-specific investment when tasks are characterized by greater complexity or greater scope. The outcomes of a contract often depend on the resolution of uncertain parameters, which are difficult to objectively measure and contract for as well as difficult to enforce (Rogerson 1992). Thus extensive contracts that delineate the rights and obligations of parties in addition to detailing contingencies in the exchange may specify actions that could be considered “observable, but not verifiable” (e.g., Hart and Moore 1988). This implies that the holdup problem may persist despite extensively detailed contract terms. We suggest that contract duration and extendibility terms can mitigate such problems.

2.4.1. Determinants of Contract Duration. By offering the vendor a stake in future outcomes, longterm contracts align interests of the vendor with the firm, reducing the risk of underinvestment by the vendor (Joskow 1987, Guriev and Kvassov 2005). A longer duration contract also enables vendors and clients to invest in relationship quality, and over time, this better relationship may help mitigate holdup problems by giving each party an incentive to not underinvest in specific investments (e.g., Poppo and Zenger 2002). Next, we examine how contract duration can mitigate holdup problems resulting from task scope and complexity.

A broader scope of activities exchanged among parties increases the hazards of contracting and thus requires more detailed contingencies encoded in contracts (e.g., Klein 1996, Oxley and Sampson 2004). An increase in task scope increases the extent to which the vendor would need to undertake investments in learning about the client organization (for instance, learning about how different functional areas in the client organization need to work together). A longer term contract gives incentives to the vendor to invest in learning about the client. This is especially useful in outsourcing initiatives with a broader scope of activities.

Hypothesis 2A (H2A). Task scope is positively associated with contract duration.

Task complexity increases the extent of contract incompleteness as well as increases the need for specific investments by the vendor. The difficulty in specifying and enforcing desired outcomes coupled with the need for specific investments offers a greater potential for appropriation of quasi rents (e.g., Crocker and Masten 1988, Joskow 1987). A longer-term contract may then exacerbate a vendor’s propensity for rent seeking, increasing the likelihood of inefficient bargaining between parties. Therefore longer-term contracts will be less desirable when faced with tasks with greater complexity.

Hypothesis 2B (H2B). Task complexity is negatively associated with contract duration.

We control for the presence of exclusivity clauses in contracts, which essentially place restrictions on vendor actions (e.g., Jensen and Meckling 1992) and thereby reduce underinvestment in that vendors need to exert effort that they would not have otherwise (e.g., Brickley 1999).<sup>5</sup> The promise of a steady stream of revenues in a longer term contract could motivate vendors to accept exclusivity restrictions.

2.4.2. Determinants of Contract Extendibility. Building on the literature on incomplete contracts, we consider the connection between the problem of underinvestment and inefficient bargaining. Expectations of ex post bargaining influences the incentives to undertake ex ante specific investments (Edlin and Reichelstein 1996, Noldeke and Schmidt 1995). Thus there is underinvestment by the vendor under two possible scenarios: (1) when the vendor does not have sufficient incentives or (2) when the vendor anticipates inefficient bargaining over AQRs by the firm. In Figure 1(b) we present a hypothetical extensive form decision space for firms and vendors.

The presence of extendibility clauses reduces the likelihood of underinvestment as well as inefficient bargaining in two ways. First, specific investments made by the vendor that increase the value from outsourcing also create switching costs for the firm during contract extensions, strengthening the bargaining position of the vendor and limiting the extent of AQRs by the firm (termed a threat point by Edlin and Hermalin 2000). The vendor then has less reason to believe there is potential for opportunistic renegotiation by the firm. This strengthens investment incentives for the vendor. Effectively, extendibility terms balance the underinvestment problem with a greater threat-point for the vendor during bargaining (Edlin and Hermalin 2000, Lyon and Rasmusen 2004).<sup>6</sup> Second, a firm faced with underinvestment or inefficient bargaining by the vendor has the option of not extending the contract. Thus extendibility terms guard against potential surplus exploitation by the vendor. In tasks characterized by greater task complexity and scope, extendibility provisions can limit the potential for contentious bargaining by both the vendor and the firm, while also strengthening investment incentives for the vendor.

Hypothesis 3A (H3A). Task scope is positively associated with presence of extendibility clauses in contracts.

Hypothesis 3B (H3B). Task complexity is positively associated with presence of extendibility terms.

We control for vendor ownership of residual assets generated during the contractual agreement. This increases a vendor’s incentives for client-specific investment (e.g., Elfenbein and Lerner 2003). However, ownership of such assets may also create conflicts of interest when the vendor redeploys assets in alternative settings, thereby increasing the threat of inefficient bargaining during contract extensions.<sup>7</sup>

2.4.3. Relationship Between Duration, Extendibility, and Contract Extensiveness. We now consider the possibility that the three remedies (duration, extendibility, and extensive contractual detail) may be related. Because of the imprecision in detailing obligations, contracts that are extensive may still be incomplete (e.g., Kaplan and Strömberg 2003), in which case the problem of underinvestment can persist despite the presence of detailed contract terms (e.g., Klein et al. 1978).<sup>8</sup> With a long-term contract, the vendor is motivated by the promise of continuing payoffs and thus refrains from underinvestment. Outsourcing firms can also economize on the high costs of writing extensively detailed contracts by choosing a longer contract horizon (e.g., Joskow 1987).

Hypothesis 4A (H4A). Extensive contracts are associated with longer contract duration.

With an extendibility clause, the promise of continued rents in a future relationship mitigates the threat of underinvestment by the vendor. Additionally, when the firm has the right to extend the contract, the vendor is motivated by the option of future rents to undertake client-specific investments (e.g., Noldeke and Schmidt 1995) and has reduced propensity to undertake inefficient bargaining (Edlin and Hermalin 2000, Lyon and Rasmusen 2004).

Hypothesis 4B (H4B). Extensive contracts are associated with the presence of extendibility terms.

2.4.4. Relationship Between Contract Duration and Extendibility. An extendibility clause may align incentives of the vendor with that of the firm and thus assuage concerns about potential opportunism in a long-term horizon. This raises the possibility that the firms may choose extendibility clauses and duration simultaneously. It is possible for a vendor and a firm to agree on a short-term contract, with the promise that it can then be extended. Indeed, theoretical models posit that renegotiable short-term contracts may implement outcomes achievable in a long term contracting horizon (e.g., Guriev and Kvassov 2005). Alternately, to economize on contracting and renegotiation costs, firms and vendors may write a long-term contract without an extendibility clause. While we do not hypothesize a relationship between these two choices, in §4 we test for the possibility of simultaneity in these choices.

2.4.5. Controls for Contract Design Elements. We control for vendor reputation that acts as a substitute for contractual provisions as well as stringent damage measures for nonperformance (e.g., Joskow 1987, Banerjee and Duflo 2000), we control for vendor reputation. We also control for prior relationships between the parties that may affect the design of contract terms. The size of the firm as well as the vendor is likely to have a bearing on contract terms (e.g., Banerjee and Duflo 2000). Hence we control for firm size and vendor size. When a firm is a highly important customer of the vendor, it may affect the contract duration (e.g., Crocker and Masten 1988) and decision rights (e.g., Kaplan and Strömberg 2003). We therefore control for whether a particular client is a dominant customer of a vendor.

## 3. Data Collection and Measure Development

## 3.1. Data Collection

Prior literature on IT contracts and management control offers normative guidelines on contracting for IT (e.g., Whang 1992, Anderson and Dekker 2005). We also interviewed vendor organization managers and personnel with expertise in managing outsourcing relationships. The measures developed reflect discussions with practitioners, themes emphasized in prior literature (e.g., Lacity and Willcocks 1998, Gurbaxani 2005) and trade and business press (e.g., Greaver 1999).

Our empirical analysis is based on the examination of contract documents obtained from public disclosure filings with the SEC’s EDGAR Database. Contract data were augmented with information from the repository of contracts maintained at the University of Missouri Contracting and Organization Research Institute (CORI). Contracts in our sample were written from 1992 to 2005. A sample of 103 contracts was extracted based on the following criteria: (1) All contracts were classified in the SIC category 73, which denotes Business Services (the categorization pertains to the service type and not the vendor or the firm) (2) Transactions governed by all contracts were explicitly related to some IT task or project, (3) Contracts were governed by the law in some state within the United States, (4) All contracts described a market based transaction, and our sample did not include any strategic alliances, joint ventures, mergers or acquisitions transactions, (5) The business relationship involved only two business units, (6) The identity of the firm and the vendor were specified. Each contract was examined in detail and coded by at least two well informed coders in about eight hours per contract. The variable descriptions, interrater reliability ratings and sample contractual clauses are presented in the online appendix.<sup>9</sup> Firm and vendor information for each contract was matched with data from the One Source Online Business Information database and Hoovers database. We also coded variables from several publicly available databases such as ABI Informs Business and Industry, Dow Jones Interactive, Reuters and Lexis-Nexis that aggregate news and press releases. Firm and vendor size were measured in terms of the number of employees.

## 3.2. Measure Development

## 3.2.1. Dependent Variable Measures.

Contract Renewal This is a binary variable indicating whether a contract was renewed beyond the initial contract term. We coded this measure by examining multiple databases such as ABI Informs Trade and Industry, Lexis-Nexis, Reuters, Dow Jones that aggregate news and press releases. Wherever possible, we confirmed the validity of the coding of this variable from press releases from websites of both the outsourcing firms and vendors.

Contract Extensiveness Extensiveness denotes the extent to which contract designers anticipate contingencies that need to be addressed in a contract. We considered 21 of the 24 potential clauses from Anderson and Dekker (2005) to measure the extent to which a given agreement incorporates protection mechanisms in the contract, in particular, the financial, legal and operational issues that need to be addressed in outsourcing IT. We excluded dimensions such as contractual provisions for flexibility (e.g., Goldberg and Erickson 1987) and joint management (e.g., Dyer and Singh 1998) that could be important in a longer term horizon and therefore could be influenced by contract duration and extendibility.<sup>10</sup> We computed a summation variable for each contract that aggregates these 21 indicators.

Contract Duration This measure refers to the contract length (specified in months).<sup>11</sup> Masten and Saussier (2002, p. 277) posit that the “decreased precision with which contracting parties are likely to be able to assess the tradeoffs from altering contract duration at more distant dates.” Therefore contract duration tends to be fine-tuned for short-term agreements, which vary by intervals of days or months, while the duration of long-term contracts tends to cluster at discrete intervals of three to ten years, making it appropriate to code long-term contract as a binary variable when a contract was longer than two years. Evidence suggests that significant disagreements can occur between vendors and clients when the contracting horizon is greater than two years (Deloitte Consulting 2005). The median duration is 29 months and about 28% are longer than four years.

Extendibility Extendibility terms in contracts grant one party (typically the outsourcing firm) the ability to extend the contract at the terms agreed upon at the outset, providing an option to the firm to continue the contracting relationship. We coded a binary variable as 1 if a contract contained an explicit extendibility clause.

## 3.2.2. Independent Variables.

Task Complexity We operationalized this measure in terms of the uncertainty in the interconnection between means and ends transformation (e.g., March and Simon 1958). We define the contract as involving business transformation when the objectives of the contract are defined in terms of strategic goals or improvements in effectiveness (e.g., Goo et al. 2004, Xia and Lee 2004), where outsourcing is transformational in nature, leveraging strategic objectives as opposed to cost considerations (e.g., Linder 2004). Such agreements often involve reorganization of the client organization and nontrivial changes to the business process of the client, requiring client-specific investments. We define the contract to involve new systems development when the contract calls for custom development of systems specific to the company (Anderson and Dekker 2005, Gopal et al. 2003). We coded a measure access to expertise when the contract specifically mentions the business domain expertise of the vendor essential for performing the task (Goo et al. 2004, DiRomuldo and Gurbaxani 1991) or the specialized consulting services provided by the vendor (e.g., Anderson and Dekker 2005), which draws on a vendor’s expertise. Based on these three objectives, we compute the task complexity measure using a three-point scale as described in Table 3.

## 4. Results

Our study concentrates on large scale and complex IT transactions. The mean transaction size in our sample is \$13 M; transactions cover a breadth of activities including business transformation and new system development. Focusing on complex IT outsourcing is especially relevant because we investigate the role of specific investment, which is likely to be considerably weaker in procurement of standard components of information technology. Furthermore, because we analyze the role of ex ante contract design, our analysis is based on data gleaned from the text of 103 contracts, and we can avoid the problem of hindsight bias from using a survey instrument. For instance, emergence of contractual problems might cause respondents to recall the early stages of contracting differently than they might have otherwise. In contrast, the text of the contract provides objective evidence of the parties’ ex ante understanding of the transaction. Table 2 details the coding procedure for the task complexity measure. A summary of the information systems (IS) activities per contract, which are used to generate the task scope measure, is shown in Table 3. The frequency of occurrence of the 21 clauses constituting contract extensiveness is presented in Table 4. We conducted several sensitivity tests to check the appropriateness of treating duration as a binary variable and the measure for task scope.<sup>12</sup> Descriptive statistics and Pearson correlation coefficients are presented in Table 5.

One possibility we need to consider is that firms could exhibit considerable heterogeneity in designing extensive contracts. Likewise, it is possible that contracts for complex tasks could be considerably different from those for less complex tasks. Accordingly, we tested the model for the existence of heteroskedasticity in the contract extensiveness model using the Breusch Pagan test (Greene 2000). We could not reject the null hypothesis of constant variance $( \chi ^ { 2 } ( 1 ) = 0 . 6 1 $ $p = 0 . 4 3 6 )$ , indicating that heteroskedasticity was not a problem. Correspondingly, we tested for the presence of heteroskedasticity in the duration and extendibility models, and we did not find evidence of the same $( \chi ^ { 2 } ( 1 ) = 0 . 0 3 ; p = 0 . 8 6 4$ in the duration model; $\chi ^ { 2 } ( 1 ) = 2 . 3 6 ; p = 0 . 1 2 5$ in the extendibility model). We also tested for the presence of multicollinearity by computing the variance inflation factors. The maximum variance inflation factor was 1.74 in the extensiveness model, 2.08 in the duration model, and

2.18 in the extendibility model, indicating that multicollinearity was also not a problem with our data.

The results of our estimations are presented in Table 6. In the first stage of the model, we test the hypotheses relating task complexity dimensions and the extensiveness of the contract, shown in Column A. We use a logarithmic transformation of the transaction size measure. For the second stage, we performed a bivariate probit regression shown in Column B to analyze the drivers of contract duration and extendibility including the predicted values of the first-stage model (contract extensiveness measure).<sup>13</sup> Logarithmic transformations are adopted for firm size and vendor size. To identify the direct effects of the task complexity dimensions on duration and extendibility, instead of the indirect effect captured through the predicted values of extensiveness, we performed a seemingly unrelated bivariate probit regression using exclusivity as an exogenous co-variate for the duration equation shown in Column C. Using exclusivity as an exogenous covariate acts as an exclusion restriction for identification (e.g., Evans and Schwab 1995). Finally, we verified the ex post performance of contracts by examining subsequent contract renewal with extendibility and contract duration as explanatory factors shown in Column D. We control for the vendor size, firm size, the reputation of the vendor, and whether the customer is a dominant customer of the vendor. We include the presence of contractually mandated coordination clauses to deal with interdependence across clients and firms (e.g., Dyer and Singh 1998) as an exogenous covariate for consistent identification. In the online appendix we present the tests examining simultaneity between the key dependent variables, duration, and extendibility. We also conduced tests for mediation, i.e., whether the relationship between task complexity measures and outcomes is mediated by the contract extensiveness measure (e.g., Baron and Kenny 1986) and do not find support for the full mediation model. Results are presented in the online appendix.

## 4.1. Contract Extensiveness

Task scope is positively and significantly associated with extensive contracts, providing support for H1A. Contractual safeguards and provisions decrease the threat of underinvestment by the vendor. This is important when greater task scope increases the measurement difficulty of outsourced tasks, in line with prior literature that posits that well-crafted contractual provisions can decrease the problems posed by a lack of measurability in outcomes (e.g., Jensen and Meckling 1992).<sup>14</sup> We find that task complexity is negatively associated with contract extensiveness, indicating support for H1B. Because complexity increases the cost of foreseeing and specifying contractual contingencies, incomplete contracts offer “strategic ambiguity” to decision makers (e.g., Bernheim and Whinston 1998, p. 903).

Controls: We also find that large transactions (involving higher contract value) and programmability of tasks are associated with more extensive contracts. When firms have a better understanding of the inputs to a process, they can be more effective in designing managerial control systems (e.g., Jensen and Meckling 1992).

## 4.2. Contract Duration

We find that greater task scope is positively associated with longer contract duration even though the relationship is not statistically significant (H2A is not supported). Our results indicate support for H2B; task complexity is negatively associated with long term contracts, which suggests that firms might be wary of the greater threat of inefficient bargaining posed by the vendor in a longer term contract (Figure 3(a)). Because task complexity cannot be managed by incorporating contractual safeguards and stringent contract provisions alone, firms outsourcing complex tasks cannot rely on managing holdup through longterm arrangements to manage vendor opportunism. We also find that exclusivity clauses are marginally associated with long-term contracts, i.e., restrictions on redeploying investments to the “next-best” alternative (e.g., Whinston 2001) might actually strengthen incentives to the vendor to make client-specific investments (e.g., Brickley 1999, Whinston 2001).

We observe that task complexity is positively associated with extendibility terms in contracts, indicating support for H3B (Figure 3(b); Table 6: Column C). Extendibility provisions represent a deliberate choice on the part of firms to foster cooperative vendor behavior—the “shadow of the future” (Axelrod 1984, p. 124) resulting in “calculative trust” (Williamson 1993, p. 463). By providing an option to the firm, extendibility terms offer a way to motivate unobservable vendor effort, which would be vital to the execution of complex tasks. Such terms also reduce the threat of inefficient bargaining by both parties (e.g., Noldeke and Schmidt 1995) through the allocation of decision rights to the firm (e.g., Edlin and Hermalin 2000). We find that vendor ownership is negatively associated with extendibility. This can be explained by the fact that asset ownership might increase vendor rent-seeking behavior, which may not be offset with the promise of greater control rights conferred on the firm through extendibility clauses.<sup>15</sup>

## 4.4. Controls for Contract Duration and Extendibility Estimations

Vendor reputation and the existence of prior relationships are not significantly associated with contract duration or extendibility provisions. When vendors are concerned about their reputation in the marketplace or with a trading partner, they may be less likely to underperform, thus reducing the need for extendibility terms. Contracts are more likely to be extendible when a firm is a dominant customer of a given vendor and with vendor size. This suggests that extendibility terms can address the threat of bargaining power of the vendor.

## 4.5. Relationship Between Contract Extensiveness, Duration and Extendibility

We find that contract extensiveness is positively associated with duration (H4A is supported) as shown in Table 6 (Column B). One possible argument is that the potential for holdup persists in spite of contractual extensiveness (e.g., Klein 1996). While an extensive contract details the terms of the relationship, enforcement of these terms is far from straightforward (e.g., Fama and Jensen 1983).<sup>16</sup> In other words, extensive contracts that delineate vendor’s responsibilities are still incomplete. Long-term arrangements lower demand uncertainty over the contracting horizon for the vendor. This makes the vendor the residual claimant when it is difficult to enforce detailed contract terms (e.g., Jensen and Meckling 1992), thereby reducing potential rent-seeking behavior.

We find that contract extensiveness is positively associated with extendibility, indicating support for H4B (Figure 3(c)). By increasing provisions for monitoring and facilitating incentive alignment, extensive contracts can motivate a vendor to make clientspecific investments. However, parties have limited ability to foresee criteria to observe, monitor and regulate contracts (Segal 1999). Moreover, it can be costly to enumerate all possible contingencies in detail, resulting in a detailed contract that may still be incomplete. This scenario is similar to discussions in contracting literature where parties abide by the detailed contract terms while also violating the original intent of the agreement (Klein 1996). Such incompleteness necessitates reliance on decision rights such as extendibility terms to reduce the likelihood of holdup by the vendor (e.g., Edlin and Hermalin 2000).

## 4.6. Testing for Simultaneity of Duration and Extendibility

To test for the possibility of simultaneity between contract duration and extendibility, we estimated a recursive simultaneous bivariate probit model (Greene 2000) including duration as an independent variable in predicting extendibility clauses. This specification allows us to treat the two choices as being determined by a single underlying decision making process, where managers determine contract terms based on an assessment of contracting hazards as evidenced by the explanatory variables. An exclusion restriction was used to identify duration with an exogenous covariate, the presence of exclusivity clauses. We conducted a likelihood ratio test of the correlation coefficient of the residuals in the two models (e.g., Knapp and Seaks 1998), the recursive-simultaneous bivariate probit model and a bivariate probit model (that ignores simultaneity) and find that the likelihood ratio statistic is not significant; thus we do not find evidence of simultaneity in the two choices. Results are presented in the online appendix.

## 4.7. Test for Ex Post Efficiency

## of Contracts Chosen

If contracts are chosen according to predictions from theory, we should find that subsequent performance is predicted by contractual structures. We evaluate the success of an outsourcing agreement as evidenced by observed contract renewal. A contract that is extended beyond the initial contract term is interpreted as a successful relationship. By dividing the sample according to the presence of extendibility clauses, we conduct a simple test of proportions (allowing for equal as well as unequal variances in the subgroups) and find that contracts with extendibility clauses are positively associated with renewals. We also conducted a logit estimation of contract renewal including as controls vendor reputation, whether the firm is a dominant customer, and firm and vendor size. For consistent identification, we also include as an exogenous covariate the presence of contractual coordination mechanisms that reduce costs of communication between organizations by specifying boundary-spanning roles and standard operating procedures (Dyer and Singh 1998), leading to greater success in the outsourcing initiative. Results are presented in Table 6, Column D.

We find that extensively detailed contracts are more likely to be renewed. This indicates that contractual parties who bear higher contracting costs (i.e., costs incurred in delineating contingencies) are likely to incur success in outsourcing initiatives. This also indicates that upfront investments in writing detailed contracts might pay off in the long run through collaborative value-reinforcing commitments. We also find that longer term contracts are less likely to be renewed. We find that extendibility clauses are positively associated with contract renewal (Figure 3(d)), supporting our arguments that the promise of future payoffs might lower the potential for underinvestment and inefficient bargaining. Extendibility provisions serve as important mechanisms to minimize the threat of holdup by rewarding vendors that make client-specific investments. Firms and vendors will find it optimal to sign contracts with extendibility terms when there is need for considerable relationship-specific investments that also carries with it the likelihood of rent appropriation and acrimonious bargaining.

## 4.8. The Role of Extendibility Terms in

## Contracting for New Systems Development

One potential bias with our estimation is that contracting for systems development may pose a systematically different set of challenges than contracting for ongoing activities such as maintenance services. This distinction might be reflected in the role of extendibility terms, which could play a limited role in contracting for new systems development. However, in our sample, we find that most contracts that involve new systems development include explicit extendibility terms. This could be for two reasons. One, when a vendor has successfully developed an application for a firm, this can lead to future opportunities to extend the use of the application developed in slightly related but different contexts. The client-specific investments made by vendors such as skills learned, knowledge of business processes in a client’s organization, and development expertise (e.g., design patterns) may then be transferable across other projects for the same client. Second, firms may also choose to leave open the potential for future scope expansion with an extendibility clause.

We find that development contracts with extendibility terms have contractual provisions to ensure that development takes place within a predefined deadline, a prespecified delivery schedule, and include penalties for late delivery. Contracts may also include termination rights where the firm can terminate the vendor based on the firm’s assessment of performance below expectations. Last, such contracts also include a service duration clause that details the duration of development effort, post development support, and other stages of the systems development process upfront. In other words, extendibility pertains to the interorganizational contractual relationship while strict deadlines pertain to the delivery and deployment of the developed system. Vendors then have incentives to exert effort to complete the first project within a reasonable timeframe so as to secure a future stream of revenue based on related similar applications of the project.

## 4.9. Implications for Practitioners and Academics

Figure 3 shows the impact of task complexity on contract duration and extendibility terms. Marginal effects from the bivariate seemingly unrelated regresssion (SURE) probit model are presented in Table 7. When it is difficult to specify means-end transformation processes, extendibility clauses protect firms from vendor nonperformance. Greater task complexity is associated with short-term extendible contracts (Table 7). Extendibility provisions are important when the outsourcing initiative is part of a strategic, rather than a transactional relationship, where a vendor’s investments in problem solving and knowledge discovery are critical to success in outsourcing and where the threat of holdup may not be mitigated through extensive contractual detail alone. When managers have limited ability to foresee contractual contingencies and specify the process of monitoring, extendibility terms can provide a strategic lever when the vendor’s ability to make specific investments is not proven. We find that increased task scope is associated with extensive contracts and long-term extendible contracts. Codifying contingencies in contracts formalizes operating procedures (Stinchcombe 1990). Well structured contracts that define due diligence and benchmarking performance obligations, when coupled with the possibility of continued rents in longer contract duration, strengthen vendors’ incentives to undertake specific investments.

## 4.10. Limitations

This study has a few limitations. The arguments in this paper suggest that extendibility terms are effective through the promise of future rents. However, we cannot rule out alternate explanations that impact the relationship between extendibility terms, task complexity and contract renewals, leading to the measurable outcomes that are similar to what we observe in the data. A “shadow of the future” between parties that decreases the returns from terminating the relationship (e.g., Axelrod 1984, p. 124) might create goal congruence between parties, increasing the vendor’s willingness to make a greater investment in clientspecific capabilities (e.g., Ethiraj et al. 2005). In other words, we cannot rule out whether it is the relationship quality between parties fostered through relationship continuity, rather than extendibility terms, that are responsible for contract renewals. While we control for prior relationships, we do not observe the emergence of solidarity and goal congruence between parties.

Because we do not have a direct measure of holdup, we can only infer the effectiveness of contract design through its impact on contract renewals. A direct measure of vendor opportunism obtained through surveys of vendors and firms could provide a richer assessment of the forms of ex post contracting challenges that could complement evidence from contract documents. Our research design also does not address the potential for strategic behavior on the part of the firm or the vendor triggered by the promise of contract extensions. As outlined by Tirole (1996), the vendor could be an opportunist who chooses to be reliable to obtain the benefits from longer term interaction with the client, and our estimation cannot distinguish between these contrasting motives for vendor behavior. We also do not consider the possibility that changes in market conditions can move transactors outside the self-enforcing range, i.e., the range of actions where the threat point is balanced against the risk of underinvestment, so that it becomes profitable to breach the contract (Klein 1996).

## 5. Conclusions and Future Research Questions

Prior research posits that IT outsourcing arrangements should have clearly defined processes and “tight” contracts (e.g., Lacity and Willcocks 1998). However, in outsourcing strategic and transformational IT tasks, the difficulty in benchmarking outcome performance and enforcing penalties for poor performance limit parties’ ability to delineate a set of desirable outcomes and enforcement mechanisms in the contract. Firms and vendors may also have limited foresight about the extent of contingencies that can arise. This again limits the effectiveness of extensively detailed contracts. One of the contributions of this paper is analysis of IT outsourcing agreements from an incomplete contracts perspective. We first examine the implications of detailing contractual contingencies to address the scope and complexity of outsourcing of IT services. We then assess the role of nonprice provisions, contract duration and extendibility clauses, in mitigating the impact of contract incompleteness.

While prior literature has highlighted the importance of repeated relationships between vendors and firms (Banerjee and Duflo 2000, Ethiraj et al. 2005), extant literature has not examined how contractual provisions should be designed to make contracts implicitly self-enforcing (e.g., Klein 1996). The promise of extendibility options is that firms can achieve success in IT outsourcing by offering the promise of future business payoffs, without either party having to invest in extensive contractual safeguards or in incurring costs of detailing extensive contracts. Our results underscore the importance of deliberate and well planned contract design choices, and in particular the strategic role of decision rights such as extendibility provisions to limit opportunistic bargaining and solve the underinvestment problem.

Prior work has posited that formal contracts and relational enforcement complement each other (e.g., Baker et al. 2002). Likewise, a rich literature on interorganizational relationships argues that interfirm agreements can be governed by the simultaneous use of mechanisms such as price, authority, and trust (Stinchcombe 1990). By using longitudinal data on the evolution of interfirm relationships in combination with contract data, future work can shed more light on the emergence of interfirm relational norms that accompany outsourcing of IT services.
