## Abstract

Electronic marketplaces (EMPs) are widely assumed to increase price transparency and hence lower product prices. Results of empirical studies have been mixed, with several studies showing that product prices have not decreased and others showing that prices have increased in some cases. One explanation is that sellers prefer not to join EMPs with high price transparency, leading highly price transparent EMPs to fail. Therefore, in order to be successful, EMPs might be expected to avoid high price transparency. But that strategy creates a catch-22 for EMPs on the buy side: Why would buyers want to join EMPs in the absence of price transparency and the benefit of lower prices? We argue that successful EMPs must provide compensatory benefits for sellers in the case of high price transparency and for buyers in the case of low price transparency.

To understand how EMPs could succeed, regardless of price transparency, we examined the relationships among EMP strategy, price transparency, and performance by analyzing all 19 EMPs that compete by selling a broad range of standard electronics components. We found that all EMPs pursuing a low cost strategy had high price transparency and performed poorly. All EMPs that performed well pursued strategies of differentiation, but, interestingly, not all successful EMPs avoided price transparency: Some EMPs succeeded despite enabling high price transparency. We therefore examined two differentiated EMPs in greater depth—one with high price transparency, the other with low price transparency—to show how they achieved strategic alignment of activities and resources and provided compensatory benefits for their customers.

Keywords: Electronic marketplaces, electronic market theory, electronic commerce, Internet, price transparency, strategic alignment, electronic components industry

## Introduction

Electronic marketplaces (EMPs $^{2}$ ) are independently owned, IT-enabled intermediaries that connect many buying organizations with many selling organizations. EMPs have long been of interest because of their potential to increase market efficiency and to lower product prices. Central in many theoretical explanations of how such outcomes could come about is the concept of price transparency, which is defined as “the degree to which market participants know the prevailing prices and characteristics or attributes of goods or services on offer” (Clemons 2002). The theoretical argument is that, for some product types (e.g., easy to describe commodities) and for some market structures (e.g., many buyers and sellers), the emergence of electronic intermediaries should lead to increased price transparency and lower product prices.

In order for an EMP to increase price transparency and thereby reduce product prices, two preconditions are necessary. First, the EMP must implement IT-enabled mechanisms that enable increased price transparency. Examples of such mechanisms are electronic catalogs with posted prices, electronic forward or reverse auctions, or an electronic price negotiation (Bichler et al. 2002). Second, the EMP must be successful—that is, both buyers and sellers must use the EMP, buyers by purchasing products through the EMP, sellers by making their product information available to the EMP.

These two preconditions result in a catch-22 for EMPs. High price transparency is not in the interests of sellers, and they are likely to avoid participating in EMPs with high price transparency (Bakos 1997; Zhu 2002, 2004), leading to poor EMP performance. Consequently, EMPs may attempt to restrict price transparency in order to attract sellers. Conversely, however, lower price transparency is not in the interests of buyers, and EMP performance might be negatively affected if buyers choose not to participate in EMPs with restricted price transparency.

Because of this catch-22, EMPs need to make strategic choices in order to position themselves to meet the conflicting interests of both buyers and sellers. Strategic management theory suggests that EMPs can choose to position themselves by pursuing a low cost or a differentiation strategy. A necessary, but not sufficient, condition for success is alignment of important elements of strategy, specifically, the EMP's value proposition, value activities, and target market. An EMP's choices about price transparency, as reflected in its value activities, are part of its overall strategic position. EMPs pursuing a low cost strategy are likely to enable a high degree of price transparency for buyers at the risk of alienating sellers. Such low-cost, high price transparency EMPs must, therefore, offer compensating value to sellers in order to attract and retain them. On the other hand, EMPs pursuing a differentiation strategy could choose either high or low price transparency, but they must similarly offer sellers or buyers value to compensate for the high or low price transparency. We argue that EMPs with a differentiation strategy are more likely to have the necessary resources to offer compensatory value to either sellers or buyers and hence are more likely to perform well.

In this paper, we test the plausibility of our arguments through a study of all 19 EMPs serving the electronics components industry that survived the dot-com crash. This research design controls for product type and industry structure, so that these factors can be ruled out as alternative explanations for the observed incidence of IT-enabled price transparency (or lack thereof). In addition, the highly fragmented $^{3}$ electronics components industry is conducive both to the formation of EMPs and to downward price pressure, since the product is a commodity. We found that EMPs pursuing low cost strategies invariably enabled high price transparency. These low cost EMPs, as well as EMPs without a clear strategic focus, performed poorly. On the other hand, EMPs with differentiated strategies were more likely to be successful. Together these findings support our contention that price transparency in EMPs is a strategic choice that is related to EMP success. Because EMP strategy helps explain IT-enabled price transparency in EMPs and EMPs' business success, one cannot fully understand the effects of EMPs on product prices without considering EMP strategy.

## The Catch-22 of Electronic Market Price Transparency

The economic concepts of markets and prices are inextricably linked. Markets are defined as means of coordinating economic activity via price mechanisms. Market efficiency is understood in terms of the extent to which prices reflect available information about product quality, supply and demand, and so forth (Fama 1970). According to economic theory, market transactions involve search, price discovery, and settlement (Lee and Clark 1996), where price discovery is the process of determining, via mechanisms such as auctions or negotiation, the prices at which demand and supply “clear” (Bakos 1998). Buyers are expected to continue searching for products that meet their needs at the lowest prices, until the costs of additional search exceed the potential gains (Rothschild 1974). When search costs are zero, buyers are able to obtain all price information needed to make informed decisions. In this situation, they can be said to enjoy complete price transparency. As search costs rise, buyers curtail their search efforts, and there will be sellers whose prices buyers will not investigate (Bakos 1991). Sellers are believed to exploit non-zero buyer search costs by raising product prices and reaping higher profits (Bakos 1998).

Because the concepts of markets and prices are so intertwined, it is natural that the emergence of electronic marketplaces has raised questions about their effects on the prices of products traded through them. EMPs are believed to reduce buyers' search and selection costs and increase price transparency, thereby increasing market efficiency and reducing product prices (Bakos 1998; Brynjolfsson et al. 1994; Gurbaxani and Whang 1991; Malone et al. 1987). Since then, a growing body of system and market design research has examined various IT-enabled mechanisms that could contribute to increased price transparency. For example, Kim and Lee (2003) discussed the importance for customer satisfaction of various attributes (variety, accuracy, etc.) of the product information in electronic catalogs; one important element of product information in this scheme is product price. In the area of electronic purchase negotiations, design classifications schemes include messages for price quotes (Lomuscio et al. 2003), and scenarios for counter-proposals (Li et al. 2002). Research on online auctions has emphasized how IT can be used to optimize auction design with respect to such attributes as the number of bidders, auction rules, choice of auction type (forward, reverse, etc.), bid constraints, auction duration, lot size, and so forth (Pinker et al. 2003). Brynjolfsson and Smith (2000) empirically observed lower prices in the online book market and suggest that Internet price search intermediaries that provide customers with easy price comparison could have contributed to this phenomenon.

Higher price transparency in EMPs is widely believed to benefit buyers at the expense of sellers (Bakos 1997; Zhu 2004). Therefore, whenever sellers have sufficient market power to do so (for example, when they sell highly differentiated products), sellers can be expected to avoid participating in EMPs that increase price transparency (Bakos 1997). Studies have suggested that EMPs will fail to attract sellers if they enable high price transparency (Bakos 1997; Christiaanse and Damsgaard 2000; Zhu 2004). Sellers prefer to join (or even form) EMPs that provide extensive product information, but not price information (Bakos 1997). They might also choose to join EMPs with IT-enabled mechanisms that keep price transparency low. Since EMP success requires a critical mass of both buyer and seller participation (Yoo et al. 2002), EMPs may choose to restrict price transparency in order to attract sellers. Some do not even provide price information (Choudhury et al. 1998), perhaps as a deliberate enticement for sellers (Damsgaard 1998). In addition, empirical studies find that electronic markets do not always decrease the prices of traded goods (Lee 1998), which is consistent with the hypothesis that EMPs will avoid increasing price transparency.

Intentional price transparency minimization has been observed in traditional markets. For example, traditional Dutch flower auctions utilize fast clock speed and selling in small batches (Kambil and van Heck 1998). These features increase cognitive complexity for buyers, making it difficult for them to keep tabs on different lots and prices, keeping price transparency low. Price transparency-minimizing features can also be built into electronic markets. LendingTree.com provides an interesting example from the business-to-consumer e-commerce world (Jacobides 2001). In the home mortgage lending industry, consumers benefit from lower interest rates and upfront costs; lenders benefit from higher rates and costs. Online mortgage referral agent LendingTree.com attracts consumers with the motto “when lenders compete, you win.” Using a database of lending criteria, LendingTree matches potential borrowers with various loan programs. The consumer who applies to LendingTree receives four different offers—and each one quotes a price (interest rates and upfront costs called “points”). But the quotes do not represent the same type of loan. One might be a 30-year fixed, the second an adjustable rate mortgage, the third a balloon, the fourth a fixed with a home equity line of credit. LendingTree does not help the borrower compare these loan packages, and comparison is not easy. Thus, the degree of price transparency and the degree of price competition among lenders participating in this EMP is less than initially appears to be the case.

In summary, the conflicting interests of buyers and sellers with regard to price transparency and the need to satisfy both buyers and sellers creates a catch-22 for EMPs. While high price transparency is attractive to buyers, it alienates sellers. EMPs' attempts to restrict price transparency, while making it more attractive to sellers, reduce the benefits to buyers. The degree of price transparency is a strategic choice for EMPs, with implications for EMP performance. Since EMPs need to attract a critical mass of buyers as well as sellers, successful EMPs that minimize price transparency have to provide something of value to buyers to compensate for the lack of price transparency, $^{4}$ whereas EMPS that enable high price transparency will have to provide other value to compensate sellers. In order to understand this side of EMP performance, we turn to strategic management theory, because of its focus on how firms' decisions and actions contribute to competitive advantage (Barney 2002).

## EMP Strategy, Price Transparency, and EMP Performance

The dominant paradigm in strategic management is Porter's theory of competitive advantage (Campbell-Hunt 2000; Hill 1988). More recently, there has also been a surge of research on the resource-based view (Ramos-Rodriguez and Ruiz-Navarro, 2004). We first give a brief review of Porter's theory, focusing on the concepts of strategic positioning, generic strategies, and strategic alignment. The concept of strategic alignment provides a bridge between Porter's theory and the resource-based view. We then apply these concepts to EMPs and derive propositions that relate (1) EMPs' strategies to EMPs' strategic choices about IT-enabled price transparency and (2) EMPs' strategic choices about IT-enabled price transparency to EMPs' performance.

## Strategic Management Theory

According to Porter (1980, 1985), industry structure determines average firm profitability, whereas the relative profitability of a specific firm is determined by its strategic position within the industry. Multiple, viable strategic positions exist in any industry (Peteraf and Bergen 2003), with some positions being more profitable than others. A firm's strategic position is its stance with respect to customers, suppliers, and competitors; strategic position involves the customers and product segments a company serves and the value it provides to them. Firms formulate and implement strategies to achieve their desired position. A firm's strategy is evident from the value proposition it offers to a targeted product market and the set of value activities it carries out in order to create and deliver the value (Porter 1996).

For more than two decades, strategic management scholars have found that the set of strategies taken by the firms in a particular industry can robustly be characterized in the two generic categories of low cost leadership and differentiation (Porter 1980, 1985). Although the two strategies are not necessarily mutually exclusive (Dess and Davis 1984; Hambrick 1983), meta-analysis of strategic management research reveals that most firms' strategies are either low cost or differentiation; relatively few firms exhibit “stuck-in-the middle” blends of low cost and differentiation (Campbell-Hunt 2000).

Low cost and differentiation strategies imply very different value propositions and value activities. Low cost strategy firms offer customers lower prices. Such firms seek above-average returns even with lower prices by keeping their cost of operations low (Miller 1988; Porter 1985). This requires the firm to design and conduct its value activities with a high degree of operational efficiency, exploiting economies of scale. Firms with a differentiation strategy provide products or services that customers perceive to be unique. Differentiation strategy firms seek superior performance by creating customer loyalty and price inelasticity (Miller 1988). Prior research suggests that a differentiation strategy requires a high degree of innovation and/or marketing. These requirements will affect the design and conduct of the firm's value activities.

Implicit in the preceding statements is the notion of strategic fit or alignment among the various elements of strategy, in particular, between the firm's stated value proposition and the value activities needed to deliver the promised value to the targeted market. Fit is a fundamental concept in strategic management research (Venkatraman 1989), and many studies have examined various types of fit, including fit between strategy and environment, as well as among the various elements of strategy. The general hypothesis is that good fit is necessary for superior performance. Porter (1996) emphasized that strong alignment between value proposition and value activities is essential for superior performance. Strong alignment enables the effective delivery of value to the targeted market.

The notion of strategic alignment also includes the firm's resources and capabilities. Researchers have suggested that a tight configuration of resources focused on strategic goals provides competitive advantage relative to more diffused competitors (Miller and Chen 1996). The competitive advantage arises from possession of strategic resources that are rare, unique, valuable, and inimitable (Barney 1986, 1991). The heterogeneous distribution of resources among firms (Rumelt 1984) means that moving first in acquiring strategic resources can create entry barriers. More recent work has also noted that strategic resources do not affect performance unless deployed in the value activities of the firm (Ray et al. 2004). It often takes years for firms to effectively leverage their resources and capabilities in the design and execution of value activities. Trying to acquire and leverage such resources and capabilities quickly may require more effort and costs because of time compression diseconomies. On the other hand, firms with sizable stocks of resources and capabilities may find it easier to acquire more of the same and easier to obtain complementary resources and capabilities (Dierickx and Cool 1989). Hence, firms that achieve alignment of value proposition, activities, and resources possess a source of advantage that competitors will find difficult to imitate.

Another important idea is that alignment requires tradeoffs. Firms that pursue differentiation, for example, need to invest significantly in resources for innovation, service, and marketing activities. This level and type of investment is likely to undermine a strategy of low cost leadership. Hence, firms that are stuck-in-the middle between the low cost and the differentiation strategies are expected to perform poorly. This idea may also explain why most studies have found relatively few firms pursuing both strategies with equal emphasis. In the next sections, we apply these notions to EMPs.

## EMP Strategy and Price Transparency

Vertical EMPs are intermediaries serving buyers and sellers in a particular industrial sector, such as metals, chemicals, and electronic components. Although there was a massive shake-out among EMPs following their explosive rate of formation in the early years of electronic commerce, several industrial sectors characterized by fragmented supply and demand still contain more than a few competing EMPs (Day et al. 2002). Like the buyers and suppliers EMPs serve, the set of competing EMPs in each industrial sector can be considered an industry group, and their competitive dynamics can be analyzed in light of the strategic management notions discussed above. For example, we expect that the strategies of EMPs in a particular industrial sector can be categorized as low cost or differentiation. And we expect that EMPs exhibiting a good fit between their strategic value proposition and their value activities will exhibit higher performance than EMPs with poor strategic fit.

What kinds of value propositions and value activities are relevant for EMPs with different generic strategies? As intermediaries, all EMPs promise their customers (buyers and sellers of products relevant to a particular industrial sector) the value proposition of improved access to sellers and buyers. Thus, creating linkages among buyers and sellers can be considered a “baseline” value proposition for EMPs. EMPs with only baseline value propositions do not have a strong strategic focus. And, without focus, it is difficult to determine appropriate value activities for strong alignment. Consequently, strategic management theory would view such EMPs as equivalent to stuck-in-the-middle firms, which are predicted to have low performance.

Beyond the bare minimum represented by the baseline strategy, EMPs can provide value in either of two main ways: (1) by proposing to lower buyers' costs through lower product prices and transaction costs (the low cost strategy) or (2) by offering differentiated services to buyers and/or sellers (the differentiation strategy). Although EMPs can differentiate themselves in numerous ways, Malone et al.'s (1987) seminal work identifying the three main benefits of electronic interconnection—communication, brokerage, and integration—suggests three ways that EMPs can offer a differentiated value proposition: (1) providing comprehensive information on suppliers and products, (2) effectively matching buyers and sellers, even for hard-to-find products, or (3) creating customized, relationship-specific information flows between established trading partners.

According to strategic management theory, each of these above-baseline strategies (low cost plus three types of differentiation) would require a different set of value activities to achieve the strategic alignment that contributes to superior performance. The value activities of EMPs can be IT-enabled or non-IT-enabled. An example of a non-IT-enabled EMP value activity is the consulting service by which FreeMarkets helps customers consolidate purchasing requirements prior to conducting an electronically mediated reverse auction. Among the IT-enabled value activities of EMPs are some that could directly contribute to increased price transparency and some that could not. Examples of IT-enabled value activities that would not directly contribute to increased price transparency are fulfillment-related features such as the ability to electronically request and track goods, inspection, insurance, and transportation services.

The IT-enabled value activities of EMPs that have attracted the most attention from economists and business people are those with the potential to affect the prices of the products traded through EMPs, specifically, their features related to “search and selection” and transaction execution (Choudhury et al. 1998). EMPs exhibit considerable variation in how and how much they provide IT support for activities potentially related to price transparency (Bichler et al. 2002). With regard to buyers’ search-related activities, prior research has noted that EMPs can vary in the amount of price and product information provided (Bakos 1991, 1997). IT-enabled support for buyers’ selection activities can include electronic catalogs (with or without posted prices), automated negotiation features such as requests for quotes, and online auctions with various mechanisms (forward, reverse, etc.) and design features (Kaplan and Sawhney 2000; Lennstrand et al. 2001).

The strategic management notion of alignment allows us to hypothesize an association between an EMP's above-baseline value proposition and its choice of appropriate IT-enabled price mechanisms in the value activities of search and selection. Other things being equal, one would expect EMPs with a low cost value proposition to select IT-enabled mechanisms (e.g., electronic auctions) with the potential to increase price transparency in search and selection activities, because this combination could contribute to strategic alignment. On the other hand, EMPs with a value proposition of differentiation could achieve alignment with or without IT-enabled price transparency in its value activities: Alignment would depend on precisely how they proposed to differentiate. For example, EMPs with a value proposition of differentiated brokerage could achieve strategic alignment through matching buyers and sellers via electronic auctions, which tend to have a high degree of price transparency. Another EMP may deliver on its value proposition of differentiated brokerage by using IT to help its sourcing specialists scan international sources of supply, suggest matches between requests to sell and requests to buy, and manage the online negotiation process. These activities could be designed so that the level of price transparency to buyers and sellers is relatively low. More formally,

Proposition 1: EMPs with a low cost strategy are more likely to have high IT-enabled price transparency than EMPs with a differentiated or a baseline strategy.

## EMP Strategy and Performance

The challenge for EMPs is that there is more to strategic alignment than just selecting IT-enabled value activities appropriate for a value proposition of low cost or differentiation. As intermediaries, EMPs must appeal successfully to both buyers and sellers if they are to survive and thrive. Therefore, one would expect successful EMPs that perform value activities with price transparency-increasing potential also to perform value activities that could provide compensatory benefits for sellers. Conversely, one would expect successful EMPs that do not perform price transparency-increasing value activities to perform value activities that could provide compensatory benefits for buyers.

Examples of both types of behavior can be seen in published accounts of successful EMPs. For example, Telcot was an early electronic marketplace for cotton that employed online auctions and in fact resulted in lower overall product prices (Lindsey et al. 1990). Telcot sellers participated despite that negative consequence, because they benefited from an increased number of potential buyers and from speedier settlement of trades. Previously, the sellers, mainly cotton farmers, had to rely on gin brokers, who would call a handful of buyers and attempt to negotiate a price. The negotiations were tedious and iterative processes of back and forth phone calls and faxes. Another potential compensatory benefit that price transparent EMPs can offer to sellers is extensive information about products and sellers; this information helps sellers to differentiate their offerings in the face of IT-enabled capabilities that promote price competition (Bakos 1997).

Conversely, Choudhury (1997) described an electronic marketplace for aircraft parts that provided buyers with benefits to offset its lack of price transparency. This EMP did not post prices, but buyers nevertheless used it, particularly when they had an aircraft “on the ground” and needed parts quickly. Buyers, like sellers, value market liquidity (Campbell et al. 1997). EMPs can provide buyers with increased liquidity by serving as brokers (quickly sourcing for sellers) or as dealers (selling from their own inventory stockpiles). Under conditions of urgent need or scarcity, buyers might even be willing to pay a premium for products sourced through an EMP with low price transparency. The provision of extensive product and seller information could also contribute to buyers’ ability to find the best seller quickly (Kauffman 1994). Finally, buyers as well as sellers might benefit from EMP services that support collaborative product design or forecasting.

In many cases, the acquisition of resources and the development of value activities capable of compensating sellers or buyers for the negative consequences of its stance on price transparency can be very costly for EMPs. Alibris, for example, spent millions developing a comprehensive structured database of product information and tools to help customers price their products appropriately (McAfee 2004). Other EMPs invest in warehouses, systems, and personnel in order to conduct comprehensive fulfillment services. Investing in such resources and capabilities increases an EMP's cost of operations and is, therefore, inconsistent with a low cost value proposition. Therefore, we expect that EMPs pursuing a low cost strategy will tend not to have compensatory benefits for sellers; yet, without compensatory benefits, highly price transparent EMPs will not attract seller participation and will therefore perform poorly. Also, as noted in the previous section, baseline EMPs are likely to perform poorly, regardless of price transparency, because they lack a strong strategic focus to drive the alignment of their value activities and resources. Differentiated EMPs, drawing on the strategic resources and capabilities that enable their particular brand of differentiation, are best placed to offer compensatory benefits for their chosen level of price transparency. These arguments suggest

Proposition 2: EMPs with a strategy of differentiation will outperform EMPs with a low cost or baseline strategy.

## Methods

To test the plausibility of our contention that strategic management theory helps explain price transparency in EMPs and EMP performance, we studied all EMPs competing in a single industry sector (electronics components). This research design has several advantages. First, it controls for product type and product market focus, thus eliminating critical sources of variation related to EMP performance. Electronic markets are generally expected to arise only in the case of commodity products (Dhawan et al. 2000; Kaplan and Sawhney 2000). Therefore, it makes sense to exclude differentiated products from our research focus. According to strategic management theory, EMPs serving different product markets could exhibit different performance levels; therefore, controlling for product market is necessary to avoid spurious results. Second, studying the population of EMPs in an industrial sector is useful given the relatively small numbers of organizations of this type. Because we are not sampling, but rather studying all comparable EMPs in the industry, we do not need to use inferential statistics (we use nonparametric statistics instead), and we do not have to worry about the statistical problems posed by small numbers of observations.

## EMP Selection and Data Collection

We chose to examine EMPs serving the electronics components industry because its large numbers of buyers and sellers and commodity-like products are conducive to the formation and persistence of electronic marketplaces (Dhawan et al. 2000) with high price transparency potential (Bakos 1997). A recent survey found that this industry had a relatively high rate of business-to-business EMPs survival following the dot-com crash (Day et al. 2002), and indeed, following procedures described below, we identified 19 "going concern" EMPs trading in electronic components. (See the appendix for a list of the EMPs and their URLs.)

The electronics components industry is global in scope, and there are thousands of buyers and sellers. The major sellers are component manufacturers (such as Intel, Analog Devices, and Fairchild) and distributors, including global players like Arrow and Avnet, but most are regionally focused. (In North America, distributors account for 35 percent of electronics components sales.) The major buyers are original equipment manufacturers (OEMs, such as IBM, Matsushita, HP, and Fujitsu) and contract (outsource) manufacturers (such as Solectron, SCI Systems, Celestica, NatSteel, and Flextronics). The electronics components product category includes a vast number of different products. The major component types traded on B2B EMPs are semiconductors, electromechanical components, optoelectronics, interconnectors, passive components, and other (e.g., power supply). Within each type, there are thousands of parts that vary in technical specifications such as voltage, thickness, coatings, etc.

We used an online directory of EMPs (http://directory.emarketservices.com) to identify relevant EMPs. The directory proved to be comprehensive: Alternative search methods (e.g., Google searches, academic database searches, industry magazines listings) yielded only one additional EMP. Although the directory listed 44 EMPs in the electronics components industry, only 19 EMPs met our criteria of trading in a broad range of the standard electronics components. Various EMPs were dropped from the sample, because their core businesses were different (e.g., they traded in finished electronic goods for consumers, rather than electronic components for the B2B market).

Data about the EMPs were obtained both from the EMPs' Web sites as well as from articles archived in online databases. The strategy of using public data sources is highly consistent with the tradition of strategy research. Triangulating data both from the firm as well as from external reports increased validity and reliability in the data collection process. We downloaded all relevant Web pages during the last 2 weeks of October 2002 as well as news articles relating to the EMPs from LexisNexis Academic online news database for the period 2001 through 2003. Press articles that made reference to any of the 19 EMPs were selected for analysis and the text of all articles was carefully read to verify the reference. In order to verify the comprehensiveness of our coverage, the authors performed similar online searches on Bloomberg as well as Factiva.com (online news archive by Dow Jones and Reuters). Both searches returned results highly similar to that of LexisNexis Academic.

## Construct Measurement

We developed metrics for the three major constructs in our study: EMP performance, EMP capability to increase price transparency, and EMP strategy, drawing on the e-commerce and strategy literature.

## Performance

Many EMPs in the electronics products market space are privately held companies, making it difficult to obtain credible information about profits or losses and numbers of participants and transactions. We therefore developed four qualitative indicators of EMP performance based on suggestions in the literatures on e-commerce and entrepreneurial firms—dynamism, longevity, visibility, and reputation. We calculated ordinal measures for each performance construct as of 2003 (two years after the dot-com crash), and we constructed a performance index from the sum of these measures. An important signal of an EMP's economic health is dynamism—the extent to which it continues to attract venture funding, sign up major new customers, strike strategic alliances with complementers, and extend into new markets. There is consensus in the literature (Applegate et al. 2002) that superior e-business performance is associated with such dynamism. Dynamism is often observed in the first year or two of an e-business, but many are unable to create businesses that are strong enough to continue attracting new customers, partners, and investors. Since many EMPs failed during the year 2000-2001 dot-com crash, EMPs that scored well on dynamism in 2003 are clearly stronger performers. The dynamism score was based on a count of the number of distinct press announcements in 2003 reflecting new funding, new major customers, new strategic alliances, and new market extensions. Two coders read through these announcements and counted the number of distinct events. To assess the inter-rater reliability in the coding, we compute the Cohen's Kappa score (Cohen 1960) for coded constructs. The Kappa score for this construct is 0.874, which is significantly higher than the acceptable level of 0.65 (Moore and Benbasat 1991).

Longevity is another indicator of the performance of entrepreneurial firms that has been employed in previous studies (Mata and Portugal 1994; Shaver 1998). For the same level of dynamism, long-lived EMPs can be considered more successful than short-lived ones. The longevity measure was computed from the number of years since the start of the EMP, as stated on its Web site. Since name recognition is also important to the success of an online business, we assessed the visibility of the EMP in terms of its cumulative coverage in the trade press from 2001 through 2003. By being more visible, firms are also more likely to forge ties with other market participants, thus facilitating economic exchanges (Hite and Hesterly 2001). Kappa scores are not computed for the constructs longevity and visibility as they do not involve coding and are quantified by objective measures. Finally, since press coverage can sometimes be negative (e.g., VerticalNet's announced layoff of 150 employees [January 19, 2001]), we also considered the balance between positive and negative press coverage over time as a surrogate for the reputation of the EMP. Prior literature establishes that corporate reputation is positively associated with firm's financial performance and firms with a good corporate reputation are able to sustain superior profits over time (Carmeli and Tishler 2004; Roberts and Dowling 2002). Two raters read a total of 1,028 press articles mentioning one or more of the EMPs for the period 2001 through 2003 and assigned a metric based on the relative numbers of positive and negative press articles. The Kappa score for the coding of this construct is 0.935.

We computed an overall performance index for each EMP by summing the values of the four dimensions. The indices showed a fair degree of variation from 0 to 7 (where higher scores indicate stronger performance). Thirteen EMs had a score of less than 4. Six high performers had a score of 5 to 7. We triangulated the performance ratings by comparing our list of high performers against Forbes 2003 Best of the Web and found a good degree of support. Five of our six high performing EMs were included on the Forbes list.

## Price Transparency Potential

EMP price transparency is even more challenging to measure than EMP performance. When exploring price disclosure in consumer-oriented EMPs with catalog pricing (e.g., online bookstores), it is a relatively straightforward matter to determine whether or not prices are posted for a sample of products and what those prices are. But several factors complicate the situation for B2B EMPs. First, B2B EMPs exhibit a range of price discovery mechanisms—including auctions (forward or reverse), price catalogs, and negotiation (request for quotation, bid/ask). In addition, many EMPs employ more than one mechanism. Any of these mechanisms could increase price transparency, but whether they could do so to the same extent is unknown. Second, as the growing field of market design research (Kauffman and Walden 2000) suggests, instances of the same mechanism (e.g., forward auctions) can exhibit widely different features (e.g., bidding rules; see Pinker et al. 2003), possibly contributing to large variations in price transparency within mechanism. Third, contextual conditions (e.g., buyers' preferences for timeliness; the number of bidders) can influence the levels of price transparency observed. $^{5}$

The general impression given by the literature is that auctions are the “purest” of price-driven markets (Biggart and Delbridge 2004), that they are more dynamic than RFQs and posted prices (Bichler 2002) and hence more likely to enable price transparency. For this explorative study, we decided to operationalize price transparency potential using that intuition. However, for reasons stated above, it is clear that this is a very crude measure, and as we discuss below, the operationalization of price transparency is an important area of future research.

Two coders examined each EMP Web site and coded the presence (1) or absence (0) of each of the three major IT-enabled price discovery mechanisms: auction (forward, reverse or both), posted prices, and RFX (request for information, request for quotation, etc.). EMPs were coded as having posted prices if public online searches (that is, searches that did not require e-mailed requests to, and responses from, particular suppliers) returned price information. All EMPs with auction mechanisms were coded as having high price transparency potential. In addition, all EMPs with both features for posted prices and RFX capability were coded as having high price transparency potential. EMPs with only RFX features were coded as having low price transparency potential, since RFX is usually implemented via e-mail. (No EMP had only posted pricing; all EMPs with posted pricing also had RFX or auction capabilities, presumably because some suppliers might wish to withhold prices on at least some of their products.) Using this approach, we identified 10 high transparency potential EMPs and 9 low transparency potential EMPs. The Kappa score for this coding is 0.895.

## EMP Strategy

According to Porter, EMPs, like all firms, can pursue either of the two generic strategies of low cost or differentiation, or they can fail to articulate a clear strategy (stuck-in-the-middle, or what we earlier called the baseline EMP strategy). A low cost strategy offers the value proposition of lower product cost for buyers. A differentiation strategy offers the value proposition of superior communication (e.g., comprehensive product and supplier information), brokerage (quick and accurate matching of buyers and sellers), or integration (collaboration and integration across value activities among trading partners).

EMP strategy was assessed by examining statements on the EMPs' Web sites about benefits they offer to buyers and sellers. Two independent coders reviewed the downloaded Web site pages of each EMP, identified all statements related to benefits, and then classified the statements as representing a baseline, differentiated, or low-cost value proposition. Statements that relate to greater access to more buyers and sellers were classified as supporting a baseline strategy.

Statements about benefits of “extensive database” of parts/supplier information, “experienced industry experts” who effectively match requirements, or “comprehensive fulfillment services” in logistics, quality, and payment were classified as supporting a differentiation strategy. Finally, statements relating to “market driven pricing” or “best prices,” for example, were classified as part of a low cost strategy. To ensure independence in coding and assessing, the authors were only involved in resolving coding disagreements and not in the actual coding process. We found that six EMPs had a baseline strategy, eight a differentiated strategy, and five a low cost strategy. Table 2 summarizes the frequency distribution of the 19 EMPs by performance, price transparency, and strategy. The Kappa score for this coding is 0.92.

## Data Analysis

We employed nonparametric analytical methods to test the propositions set forth in earlier sections. Nonparametric analytical tests, including the Wilcoxon ranked sum, the Mann-Whitney, and the Kruskal-Wallis tests, were chosen for several reasons. First, because our study involves all firms in the population of B2B EMPs in the electronic components industry, we do not need to estimate parameters and make distribution assumptions. Nonparametric tests, which do not support those processes, are more appropriate for analyses of whole populations. Second, even though we studied all firms in the relevant population, the number of firms in the population is rather small, and the subgroups by price transparency and strategy are smaller. The distribution-free nature of nonparametric tests places few restrictions on the sample size in contrast with parametric tests, which rely on asymptotic properties or normality of the sample distribution. Third, the ordinal performance measures used in the study called for the use of nonparametric methods, which yield higher power than corresponding parametric tests (DePuy and Pappas 2004; Nanna and Sawilowsky 1998). Finally, rank-based nonparametric tests are not affected by outliers (Hollander and Wolfe 1999) and hence are more suitable for the analysis of EMPs, where outliers are common. (For instance, Amazon.com is a clear outlier in the online book business based on volume measures of performance.)

## Findings

In this section, we present the results of our analysis for the two propositions on the relationships among price transparency, strategy, and EMP performance.

## EMP Strategy and Price Transparency

Proposition 1: EMPs with a low cost strategy are more likely to have high IT-enabled price transparency than EMPs with a differentiated or a baseline strategy.

The results support our proposition. Table 3 shows that all five EMPs with a stated low cost strategy have IT features that support high price transparency—either an auction mechanism or both RFQ and posted prices. By contrast, only three of the eight differentiated EMPs enabled high price transparency. We used the Kruskal-Wallis test to examine the differences in price transparency across the three EMP strategies. The Kruskal-Wallis test is the generalization of the Mann-Whitney test when there are more than two independent groups (Hollander and Wolfe, 1999). This test is the nonparametric analogue of the one-way, fixed effects ANOVA; with it, we tested against the null hypothesis of equal price transparency for all three strategies. Based on the test statistic of 5.8 we conclude that price transparency is significantly different across the strategy groups (at 0.05 significant levels).

We then performed comparisons between each pair strategy using the Mann-Whitney test and found that the low cost strategy is more likely to be associated with high price transparency than either the differentiated or baseline strategy. Table 4 summarizes the results of the nonparametric tests.

## EMP Strategy and Performance

Proposition 2: EMPs with a differentiated strategy will outperform EMPs with a low cost or baseline strategy.

Our tests of Proposition 2 suggest that EMPs are much more likely to succeed with a strategy of differentiation (and the value activities to back it up). Table 5 shows that all of the high performing EMPs have a strategy of differentiation. Although two differentiated EMPs were low performers, this is not inconsistent with our argument. A good strategy does not guarantee success—many other factors enter into the equation. In particular, effective execution of the strategy, in terms of value activities (and the underlying resources and capabilities) that deliver the value proposition, is essential. By contrast, none of the low cost or baseline EMPs exhibited high performance.

## Discussion: Strategic Alignment and Compensatory Benefits

Although the propositions were supported by our analysis, the overall pattern of findings raises several points of discussion. We found that two of three strategic groups of EMPs—baseline strategy and low cost strategy—performed poorly. The poor performance of the baseline group is relatively straightforward to explain with strategic management theory: The baseline group lacks a distinctive value proposition and therefore has no basis for crafting strong alignment. The baseline value proposition is not distinctive, because all EMPs claim to provide enhanced access to buyer and sellers.

The poor performance of low cost EMPs, however, is more challenging to explain with strategic management theory alone. These EMPs have a distinctive value proposition above and beyond the baseline, and the presence of high price transparency in all low cost EMPs suggests that they are seeking alignment around that strategic focus. Here, prior research on electronic markets supplements strategic management theory with the insight that EMPs must offer distinct value propositions to both buyers and sellers. A low cost value proposition supported by high price transparency may be attractive to buyers, but not to sellers. Hence alignment of the low cost value proposition with value activities that offer high price transparency is not sufficient for success. Sellers value the benefits of better marketing and reduced costs and risks in fulfillment. In-depth analysis showed that the marketing, seller/product search, and fulfillment services of our five low cost strategy EMPs were very basic. Conversely, analysis of EMPs that offer sellers significant value shows that providing this value is very costly. (See the Converge and Global Sources examples below). Typically, EMPs with a low cost strategy cannot afford to provide these services while still charging minimal fees and maintaining profitability. In addition, as Davenport et al. (2001) suggest, there may be inherent conflicts in trying to fulfill simultaneously the value propositions that appeal to buyers and the different value propositions that appeal to sellers. For example, the promotion of high price transparency might make it difficult for an EMP to promote seller differentiation at the same time.

These high performing, high price transparency EMPs pursued differentiated strategies. Indeed, all high performing EMPs in our study had differentiated strategies (see Table 5); among them, two had high price transparency, while four had low price transparency (see Table 2). This raises the question of how differentiated EMPs are able to provide compensatory benefits to sellers in the case of high price transparency or to buyers in the case of low price transparency. We therefore examined in greater depth two high performing EMPs, one with high price transparency (Converge) and the other with low price transparency (Global Sources).

## Converge: Differentiated Strategy and High Price Transparency

Converge was formed by a consortium of OEMs in 2000. In 2001, with the acquisition of NECX, an established electronics exchange, Converge's value proposition became one of differentiated brokerage. The economics of electronics assembly make brokerage a particularly attractive service. Electronic components have an average life cycle of just 6 to 10 months (Levin 1996), creating substantial challenges for both OEMs and for suppliers. Through better supply chain visibility, OEMs strive to avoid building up inventories of obsolete parts, but it is difficult to forecast demand for many new products. Thus, OEMs are also likely to find themselves without the components they need to meet unexpectedly strong demand. Converge's brokerage business model is designed to help OEMs manage the dual risks of short life cycles and unpredictable demand by helping OEMs rapidly sell excess component inventories or buy to meet shortfalls.

Converge's activities and resources were aligned to deliver this value proposition. Sellers could upload their listings of components for sale, whereas buyers sent in their requests to buy. In line with its differentiated brokerage value proposition, Converge offered many different ways to match buyers and sellers. It had an automated hourly matching of requests to sell with requests to buy. Buyers could also submit an RFQ to Converge, and one of Converge's sourcing experts would tap Converge's network of sellers worldwide and return a quote to the buyer. Buyers and sellers could also participate in forward or reverse auctions. The activities were also conducted in a manner that recognized the competitive sensitivity of their target market of OEMs seeking to get rid of excess inventory or meet inventory shortfalls. In all search and selection activities, buyer and seller anonymity was carefully maintained. Automated searches for a given component returned only a consolidated view of total supply and demand. Buyer and seller identity were not revealed in the search results. Converge quotes in response to requests to buy or sell also did not reveal seller/buyer sources. Auctions were also anonymous. During fulfillment, Converge had facilities where it inspected goods and “sanitized” them, removing all seller identification markings.

Although the auctions increase price transparency, Converge offered sellers several compensatory benefits—anonymous and quick disposal of inventory and end-to-end financial and logistics services. For example, in the case of “strategic sourcing” auction events, sellers participating in a closed auction have a higher likelihood of making a large sale. Converge employees work closely with the buyer to identify, qualify, and train suppliers for effective execution of the closed auction. To deliver on its value proposition (that is, to provide the relevant value activities), Converge has had to expend significant resources, substantially increasing its costs of operation. A critical resource is the sourcing expertise and the global network of suppliers that were the major assets transferred in the NECX acquisition. Other resources include shipping locations around the world, and facilities in the United States, Europe, and Asia for inspection, product anonymization, and tracking.

## Global Sources: Differentiated Strategy and Low Price Transparency

Global Sources was formed in the mid-1970s in Hong Kong as a directory publishing business. In the mid-1990s, the company started an electronic marketplace. It built on its reputation and expertise in directory publishing by offering a value proposition of differentiated communication, where the key benefits to buyers were access to comprehensive information on thousands of Asian suppliers, and, to sellers, marketing to large Western buyers.

Global Sources' value activities were tightly aligned with the value proposition it promised its target market. It hosted supplier “showrooms” with an attractively designed and comprehensive format that went beyond mere product listing. The showrooms also provided extensive information on suppliers, including their paid-up capital, manufacturing facilities, key customers, and so on. The company recognized that many smaller suppliers would require assistance going online and therefore employed several hundred representatives in many Asian cities to visit suppliers, armed with laptops and cameras, to capture and upload current information. To help overseas buyers navigate the large amounts of information on the EMP site, Global Sources also developed comprehensive search techniques. Unlike many other EMPs, where search was conducted via component part number and/or a broad product category, Global Sources also offered search by specifications (e.g., component types, weight, coating, voltage tolerance, etc.). This was particularly useful because each supplier has unique product codes, and

Western buyers are unlikely to be familiar with them. Just as important with regard to alignment was what Global Sources had chosen not to do. It did not execute transactions nor oversee fulfillment for its customers. Once they identify a potential partner through Global Sources, buyers and sellers bypass the site (by Global Sources' design) and transact with each other directly.

Global Sources' primary source of revenue was fees from sellers for listing their products and for hosting showrooms. Unsurprisingly, Global Sources did not alienate them by promoting price transparency. Sellers were not required to post prices, and although the showroom template would allow them to do so, most did not. Our searches of the site never returned price information. There is also no auction service. To obtain price information, buyers needed to contact the sellers. Global Sources facilitated this process through its online request for quote service. Given this low level of price transparency, what would large Western buyers find of value in Global Sources? Global Sources provides buyers with the means to access thousands of small Asian manufacturers who might otherwise remain unknown. At the time that Global Sources began its online operations, few Asian businesses had their own Web sites; for the smallest suppliers, this is probably still the case today. The Global Sources EMP aggregated these suppliers into a single source of reliable, consistent, and comprehensive information for Western buyers about potential new business partners. Global Sources also provides online vendor management tools for buyers (for a fee).

Global Sources needed significant resources to deliver on its value proposition. These resources included its network of relationships with suppliers throughout Asia, its hundreds of sales representatives in the field, its reputation among buyers for comprehensive and accurate information, and its knowledge and capabilities for creating and managing buyer-accessible content about sellers and products. The bricks-and-mortar Global Sources developed these resources and capabilities over a period of 20 years and successfully transferred them to, and leveraged them in, its electronic marketplace.

Converge and Global Sources are EMPs that successfully differentiate with and without high price transparency, respectively. They flesh out the notion of alignment between an EMP's value proposition for a target market, its value activities tailored to deliver the value proposition, and the resources and capabilities required. Converge and Global Sources also illustrate how EMPs can provide benefits to compensate sellers and/or buyers for a strategically chosen level of price transparency.

## Implications for Research and Practice

In this study, we found that strategic management theory adds analytic power to the intuitions of electronic markets research about the relationship between high price transparency and low EMP success. Specifically, EMPs pursuing low cost strategies are expected to enable high price transparency, since that combination represents alignment with a low cost value proposition. However, because low cost EMPs lack the resources to provide compensatory benefits to sellers, they are likely to be low performers. EMPs with only baseline strategies are also likely to be low performers, since they do not have a clear value focus, whereas EMPs with differentiated strategies are the most likely of the three strategic groups to succeed. Successful differentiated EMPs can choose to enable either high or low price transparency, but they will also have to espouse value propositions that attract both sellers and buyers by offering compensatory benefits to sellers or buyers to offset high or low price transparency. In successful differentiated EMPs, these value propositions with compensatory benefits will also be supported by tightly aligned activities and resources.

Our study examined EMPs within a single industry vertical, in order to control for industry- and product-level effects on price transparency and performance. Further research is clearly needed to assess the differences in EMP strategies across different types of industries and the effects of these different strategies on performance. At the same time, we believe that our key findings are likely to be found to generalize to EMPs in other industry verticals that are also characterized by large numbers of buyers and sellers and commodity-like products. In markets where there are few sellers or where the products are highly differentiated, EMPs (if they exist at all) are unlikely to have high price transparency. Few EMPs will thrive under such industry conditions, and the few that survive would probably offer differentiated strategies with low price transparency and have value propositions that either promote product differentiation through communication of product and seller information or lower risks and transaction costs through effective and efficient brokerage or integration.

A limitation of our study is its cross-sectional research design. One implication is that we were not able to examine changes in the EMPs' strategies over time. There is evidence that some EMPs have shifted strategies, in some cases repeatedly, as they struggle for profitability (Daniel et al. 2004; Ganesh et al. 2004). This change in the contours of the strategic groups of EMPs (Segars and Grover 1994) could reflect growing awareness of the unprofitability of the low cost EMP strategy. Alternatively, it could reflect the influences of factors outside the scope of this study. One such alternative explanation of EMP performance is network effects $^{6}$ (Kauffman et al. 2000; Yoo et al. 2002). Because EMPs need a critical mass of both buyers and sellers to succeed, earlier forming EMPs have a distinct advantage over late forming ones and might even capture the entire market, closing out new entrants. We are not able to test this alternative explanation of EMP performance with our data, because we were unable to obtain longitudinal data about the size of the EMPs' customer bases, and because we actually used EMP longevity as part of our success measure. A promising area of future research is to compare the strategic and the network effects explanations of EMP performance and to examine their potential complementarities.

For the purpose of testing the propositions in this study, we assessed price transparency in terms of the types of price discovery mechanisms used—posted prices, RFX, and auctions. In the process of coding price transparency for each EMP, we found that even within each type of mechanism, there were variations in the way EMPs designed the IT-enabled features. For example, some EMPs required that all RFQs be handled by the EMP, with the EMP sending the buyer a single “best” quote. In other EMPs, the RFQs were sent directly to multiple sellers, and the buyer received multiple quotes. The ease with which the buyer can compare the quotes from multiple sellers also differs. Some EMPs provide a standard template that sellers must use in responding to quotes. Many others allow unformatted responses. In some cases, the EMPs permitted sellers to send buyers responses via e-mail and fax as well as though the EMP, whereas others did not permit this loss of control. These variations in IT features suggest that price transparency can vary even within a single type of price discovery mechanism. Much more work needs to be done through a variety of approaches (design science, market design, formal modeling, empirical investigation, measurement research) to understand the linkage between IT features and concepts such as price transparency. Such research will provide insights into the processes by which EMPs achieve their strategic goals and succeed in the EMP marketplace. And it will provide insights to designers about effective and ineffective ways to enable certain capabilities.

The variations we observed in IT-enabled features went beyond the price discovery mechanisms to the entire set of activities performed by each EMP, such as information content creation and management and fulfillment. The variations are driven in part by different strategies. They also reflect effectiveness of strategy implementation. For example, two EMPs might pursue similar strategies of differentiated communication but might achieve different degrees of alignment between value activities and resources. One EMP may have very basic IT-enabled information search and/or information presentation, making it difficult for its customers to obtain the desired information, thus undermining the EMP's value proposition. The second EMP might successfully deliver on its value proposition because it has superior content creation and management capabilities through comprehensive online information that is easy to use. These differences in IT-enabled features are important because they not only contribute to the successful execution of strategy through alignment but also are highly consequential for the benefits buyers and sellers get (or don't get) from using an EMP. Further research is needed on the ways EMPs design IT-enabled activities to align with their strategy and the consequences for buyer and seller benefits and EMP performance.

## About the Authors

Christina Soh is Vice Dean, Nanyang Business School, Nanyang Technological University. Her areas of research include IT and strategy, interorganizational systems, enterprise package implementation, and e-government. She holds a B.Acc. (Honors) from the National University of Singapore and Ph.D. in Management from the University of California, Los Angeles.

M. Lynne Markus is the John W. Poduska, Sr. Chair in Information Management at the McCallum Graduate School of Business, Bentley College. Professor Markus conducts research on enterprise and interenterprise systems and IT and organization change. She is the author of three books and numerous journal articles. She was named a Fellow of the Association for Information Systems in 2004. She holds a B.S. in Industrial Engineering from the University of Pittsburgh and a Ph.D. in Organizational Behavior from Case Western Reserve University.

Kim Huat Goh is a doctoral candidate in the Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota, majoring in Information and Decision Sciences. He is currently on study leave from Nanyang Technological University where he serves as a senior tutor. His research interests include examining the strategic value of IT at the firm and industry levels, latency issues pertaining to IT payoff, and strategic analysis of B2B electronic markets.
