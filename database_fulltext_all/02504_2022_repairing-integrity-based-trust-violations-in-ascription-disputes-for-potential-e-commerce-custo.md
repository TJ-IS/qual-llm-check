# REPAIRING INTEGRITY-BASED TRUST VIOLATIONS IN ASCRIPTION DISPUTES FOR POTENTIAL E-COMMERCE CUSTOMERS<sup>1</sup>

Honglin Deng Advanced Institute of Business, Tongji University Shanghai, CHINA {denghonglin@tongji.edu.cn}

Weiquan Wang CUHK Business School, The Chinese University of Hong Kong, Shatin, Hong Kong SAR, CHINA {wwang@cuhk.edu.hk}

Kai H. Lim Faculty of Business, The Hong Kong Polytechnic University, Kowloon, Hong Kong SAR, CHINA {kai.lim@polyu.edu.hk}

Trust violations of online sellers are widely reported in customer reviews and are often ascribed to the sellers’ lack of integrity. These reported violations reduce potential customers’ trust in the accused sellers, given the critical role of seller integrity in e-commerce. However, accused sellers and buyers often dispute the ascriptions of trust violations (e.g., sellers may argue that a violation is due to their lack of competence instead of their integrity). The trust repair literature has inadequately focused on effective strategies to repair reported integrity-based trust violations in ascription disputes. Drawing upon attribution theory and individuals’ cognitive sensemaking process regarding trust violations, we propose an account-based approach through reascription and stability attributions, enabling accused sellers to repair potential customers’ trust in them in the event of such disputes. We theorize the effectiveness of this approach by considering the contingent role of the accused seller’s reputation. The results of our laboratory experiments confirm the effectiveness of our approach in repairing potential customers’ trust for sellers with a high reputation but not for sellers with a low reputation. We further investigate the effectiveness of disclosing substantive amends (i.e., financia compensation) made by the accused seller to the victim as an alternative approach to repairing potential customers’ trust in sellers with a low reputation. The results reveal the significant effects of disclosing substantive amends on repairing potential customers’ trust in the seller, regardless of the seller’s reputation.

Keywords: Trust repair, trust violation, ascription, attribution, integrity, social accounts, substantive amends, laboratory experiment

## Introduction

In e-commerce environments, customers frequently report sellers’ integrity-based trust violations (e.g., seller deceptions) via negative online reviews (Pavlou & Dimoka, 2006).<sup>2</sup> These reviews can be easily and widely spread to potential customers (i.e., readers of the reviews) who were not involved in the violations (Lee & Song, 2010). Such negative reviews can have devastating consequences for the accused seller because they can reduce potential customers’ trust in the seller and, accordingly, their willingness to transact with the seller (Bundy et al., 2017). Thus, effectively repairing the trust of potential customers is paramount for online sellers who have been accused of integrity-based violations.

Existing trust studies have examined the strategy of social accounts to repair trust in the aftermath of a trust violation, but they have overlooked the importance of ascription disputes. Social accounts include the violator’s verbal claims, such as acknowledging responsibility and regret for a trust violation (i.e., an “apology” in the literature) or declaring the violation to be untrue (i.e., a “denial”) (Kim et al., 2004). Extant studies on the effects of social accounts have primarily examined scenarios wherein the trust violation is presumed to be ascribed to a certain cause (e.g., a lack of integrity) and the accused violator does not challenge the correctness of the ascription (e.g., Harmon et al., 2015; Kim et al., 2009). Nevertheless, disputes over the ascription of trust violations occur widely in e-commerce. For example, delivery problems are often reported in online reviews and customers often ascribe cases of nondelivery to the seller’s lack of integrity. However, these problems might be mis-ascribed, because they may be caused by other reasons (e.g., a delay in order fulfillment due to poor planning) (Gregg & Scott, 2006).

Scholars and practitioners are particularly interested in ascription disputes that involve integrity-based violations (e.g., Wang & Wang, 2019; Wang et al., 2018) because integrity is associated with individuals’ moral character, and dealing with integrity-based violations is difficult, as people who behave immorally are typically judged as dispositionally immoral (Reeder & Spores, 1983). Although integrity-related negative reviews may be caused by misunderstandings, many sellers appear to inadequately deal with such misunderstandings.<sup>3</sup>

We contend that the extant social accounts for trust repair (e.g., apology or denial of the violation) are inapplicable to such violations. When a violation exists (although the ascription of the violation is disputed), the traditional denial in which a violation is completely denied is considered dishonest behavior in and of itself. In this study, we draw upon the cognitive sensemaking framework of trust violations and an attributional perspective of trust (Tomlinson & Mryer, 2009) to propose a two-step account-based approach for repairing an integritybased violation in ascription disputes. As discussed below, this approach first involves the accused seller’s response of denying the accused ascription of the violation, which is different from the traditional account of denying the violation altogether. Next, the seller ascribes the alleged integrity-based violation to another trust-related factor (i.e., re-ascription to competence or benevolence), and then attempts to repair the re-ascribed violation via a stability attribution account, indicating that similar violations will not happen again in the future. Our twostep approach focuses on potential consumers who are reading a reported integrity-based violation of an accused seller in an online review for the first time.<sup>4</sup>

We further examine the boundary condition of our two-step approach. We do so by arguing that the seller’s reputation is a contingent factor that determines the effectiveness of social accounts in repairing trust in an e-commerce context. The account-based strategies to repair the victim’s trust that have been studied in offline contexts may be ineffective for repairing the trust of potential online customers (e.g., Kim et al., 2004). In offline contexts, the victim often engages in recurring interactions with the violator. Thus, after a transgression, if the violator attempts to repair trust via social accounts, the victim can verify the truthfulness of the violator’s social accounts (e.g., Lount et al., 2008). However, in e-commerce settings, potential customers become a major target of trust repair and they lack direct interactions with the accused seller (Chang et al., 2015). A key premise of the current research is that for social accounts to be effective for trust repair, they must be perceived as believable. In the cognitive sensemaking framework, Tomlinson and Mryer (2009) noted that social accounts are “less than optimal” if they are considered unverifiable or even “cheap talk” (p. 101). Lacking prior interactions with the accused seller, potential customers often have difficulties verifying the truthfulness of an accused online seller’s account; in this case, they need to rely on additional heuristics (e.g., the seller’s reputation) to make their decisions (Kim & Dennis, 2019). Thus, drawing upon the literature on the source credibility of online information, we consider the accused seller’s reputation as a key contingency factor for the effectiveness of our proposed account-based approach (Yi et al., 2019). We contend that the social accounts of accused sellers with a low (i.e., poor) reputation will be ineffective in repairing potential customers’ trust.

Apart from social accounts, the extant trust research investigates the strategy of substantive amends for trust repair, such as financial compensation (Desmet et al., 2011) and penance and regulations (Dirks et al., 2011). Upon recognizing the ineffectiveness of social accounts for sellers with a low reputation, we further examine whether disclosing the substantive amends made by the accused seller to the victim (particularly financial compensation) to potential customers would be an effective alternative approach to our proposed social accounts (Haesevoets et al., 2018). The extant research focuses on the effects of financial compensation on the victim’s trust (e.g., Cui et al., 2018). Unlike the victim, potential customers receive no actual compensation but learn from the accused seller’s response that the seller compensated the victim of the trust violation. Whether disclosing financial compensation information can repair potential customers’ trust in the accused seller remains unclear. Furthermore, whether the effect of such disclosure is confined to sellers with a high (i.e., excellent) reputation also remains unclear.

To test our two-step account-based approach and the hypotheses developed in this study, we conducted four laboratory experiments in a C2C e-commerce setting in which customer reviews and sellers’ responses are widely accessible to potential customers. Through this work, we make the following five contributions from an interdisciplinary perspective. First, we extend the scope of trust repair research to an understudied research domain in which the ascription of a trust violation is disputed. Second, we refine the extant literature on social accounts by examining re-ascription and stability attribution for repairing disputed trust violations. Third, we advance the understanding of repairing the trust of potential customers in e-commerce settings. Fourth, we identify the accused sellers’ reputation as a contingency factor when examining the effects of social accounts on repairing the trust of potential online customers. Finally, we examine the disclosure of substantive amends as an alternative approach to repairing trust violations when social accounts are ineffective.

The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows. The next section reviews our theoretical background. The Hypothesis Development section presents the hypotheses. The Methodology and Results section introduces the four experiments and reports their results. The General Discussion section discusses the findings and their implications for research and practice.

## Theoretical Background

## Cognitive Sensemaking of Trust Violations

Tomlinson and Mryer (2009) outlined a cognitive sensemaking framework to explain the process through which a trustor interprets a trust violation. Subsequent studies have applied this framework to investigate the effects of verbal accounts (e.g., CEO’s apology) on trust repair in offline organizational contexts (e.g., Bagdasarov et al., 2019; Kim et al., 2013). Upon experiencing a trust violation, the trustor’s cognitive sensemaking would involve two key steps. The first is an ascription of the violation to a reason, referred to as a “causal ascription” in the literature (Weiner, 1985). A violation of trust is established when the trustor ascribes the negative outcome to a certain deficiency of a violator’s trustrelated factor (i.e., competence, benevolence, or integrity) (see Step 1 in Figure 1). Thus, the violation is referred to as a competence-, benevolence-, or integrity-based violation.

In the second step, following a causal ascription of a trust violation, the trustor further evaluates the potential cause of the outcome along with various attributions (i.e., Step 2 in Figure 1) (Tomlinson & Mryer, 2009). The literature has identified three major attributional dimensions, namely, locus, controllability, and stability. Locus distinguishes between causes that are generated internally (i.e., by the violator) or externally (e.g., by another actor); controllability indicates the degree of control that violators have over an outcome, and stability refers to the extent to which the cause is considered as fluctuating or constant (Jones & Shah, 2016; Weiner et al., 1987).

We focus on the stability attribution. Stability is the primary driver of expectations of what will likely happen in the future (e.g., whether the negative outcome will recur in the future) (Bansal & Zahedi, 2015; Weiner, 1985). The reoccurrences of similar trust violations matter for potential customers. If they attribute a trust violation to an unstable cause, they are unlikely to form a strong negative evaluation of the violator. An integrity-based trust violation implies that the locus of causality is internal to the violator (i.e., caused by the violator). In addition, integrity, benevolence, and many forms of competence (e.g., basic transaction skills and domain knowledge) are under the control of trustees (Tomlinson & Mryer, 2009). Other uncontrollable forms of competence (e.g., the seller’s IQ) are not critical for e-commerce transactions. Thus, we do not examine the attributions of locus or controllability in this study.

## Traditional Social Accounts for Trust Repair

Existing trust repair studies have investigated the effects of two types of social accounts—namely, denial of the violation and apology for guilt—on trust repair (e.g., Kim et al., 2006). With a denial, the accused entity denies accountability for the negative outcome, either by declaring the inexistence of the violation or by stating that they are not the perpetrator at fault (Bagdasarov et al., 2019). Denials are effective for repairing integrity-based violations (e.g., Kim et al., 2004). This effect can be explained by the argument that integrity is a stable individual trait; absent denial, a dishonest person is expected to behave dishonestly again in the future (Thatcher et al., 2011).

In the extant trust repair literature, the apology is an act of acknowledging and admitting full responsibility for a trust violation and showing remorse for such a violation (DiFonzo et al., 2020). Apologies have been proven ineffective in repairing integrity-based trust violations (Kim et al., 2004) because apologies indicate that the violator is admitting to a lack of integrity, leading to concerns of future deception by the violator (van Houwelingen et al., 2018). Disputed integrity-based trust violations do not acknowledge the transgression as reported in a negative review; thus, we exclude apology as a social account.

## Proposed Two-Step Account-Based Approach

The first step of our proposed approach is for the violator to respond to a reported integrity-based violation with a social account of re-ascription. Distinct from the traditional account of denial of the violation itself, this account aims to (1) accept responsibility for the violation but reject the accused integrity ascription, and (2) re-ascribe the mis-ascribed (from the violator’s perspective) integrity-based violation to either a competence- or benevolence-based violation and further attribute the lack of competence or benevolence to an unstable cause, i.e., the stability attribution (Tomlinson & Mryer, 2009). We theorize the effects of these two accounts in our approach, namely, re-ascription and the stability attribution, in the following subsections.

## Effects of Re-ascription on Integrity and Competence/ Benevolence

We postulate that repairing trust via a re-ascription account involves a belief-updating process, by which a potential customer’s trust beliefs about the seller are modified (Hogarth & Einhorn, 1992). If the seller’s re-ascription account is accepted by the potential customer, the latter’s perceived integrity regarding the accused seller will be enhanced. At the same time, the potential customer’s perceived competence or benevolence regarding the seller will be reduced, depending on whether the account reascribes an integrity-based violation to a competence- or a benevolence-based violation. The validity of re-ascription is based on the following two major arguments.

First, in our approach, re-ascription transcends the denial of the ascription of integrity because the seller accepts responsibility for the violation. The extant literature has shown that accepting responsibility is an effective way to demonstrate sincerity in restoring a broken relationship, and such sincerity is critical for the success of a repair effort (Kramer & Lewicki, 2010).

Second, previous research suggests that social accounts can update the perceiver’s initial moral judgment (e.g., about the seller’s integrity in our case) by shaping or shifting the perceiver’s understanding of the nature of a violation (Kim et al., 2006). Moral judgments (e.g., a lack of integrity) are often based on immediate intuitions and are followed, when needed, by slow, ex post factor reasoning (Haidt, 2001).

Initial moral judgments can be modified when additional information is received (i.e., a belief-updating process). For example, when a customer fails to receive an ordered product, the customer may blame the seller’s failure to fulfill the obligation to deliver the product (i.e., a lack of integrity).

This morality-related judgment has weak temporal persistence when confronted with counterpersuasion (Petty et al., 1999). Potential customers often lack confidence in their initial judgments when faced with different reviews or contradictory information about a seller (Marks & Kamins, 1988). Thus, a reported integrity-based violation may be updated in the minds of potential customers to a competence- or benevolence-based violation when the accused seller provides a response that re-ascribes the violation to a competence- or benevolence-related issue.

## Moderating Role of Seller’s Reputation

When using our two-step approach for trust repair, we must ensure that the account is believable rather than merely being “cheap talk” (Tomlinson & Mryer, 2009). The extant trust repair studies on social accounts in offline, organizational contexts implicitly assume that accusers do not challenge the veracity of the accused entity’s account (e.g., Bachmann et al., 2015). This assumption is based on the fact that the accuser can judge and verify the truthfulness of the account according to their prior and/or recurrent interactions with the accused seller in such contexts (Grover et al., 2014). However, in ecommerce contexts that lack prior or recurrent interactions with the accused seller, potential customers are likely to be concerned about the believability of an accused seller’s social accounts (Chen et al., 2013). The credibility of the information source is a key factor in determining the believability and hence the success of an online persuasive message (Cheung et al., 2009). Potential customers need extra information and may seek heuristic cues to assess the believability of a seller’s social accounts (Liu et al., 2021).

In our proposed approach, we identify the reputation of the accused online seller as a contingency factor for the effects of social accounts. Reputation is a key heuristic cue that weighs heavily on online users’ judgment regarding the credibility of an information source (e.g., Yi et al., 2019). In electronic markets, transaction partners are geographically dispersed and often lack prior interactions; thus, potential customers face high uncertainties and risks in transacting with online sellers (Dimoka et al., 2012). The seller’s reputation can serve as an effective risk reduction cue for customers to make judgments about them (Utz et al., 2009). Marketing research has shown that the same persuasive message provokes less skepticism and increased believability if a reputable company delivers it (Goldberg & Hartwick, 1990).

An account of re-ascription from a reputable seller is likely to be readily believed. Thus, potential customers would believe a reputable seller’s re-ascription that the seller did not deceive the victim, as reported in a negative review (i.e., perceived integrity of the seller increases), but the seller still retains responsibility for the trust violation along the lines of other causal ascriptions (i.e., competence or benevolence is reduced). By contrast, customers will not believe the accused seller’s social accounts if the latter has a low reputation (Zha et al., 2018). Online sellers with low reputations are indeed perceived as lacking integrity (Cowley & Anthony, 2019). As such, the re-ascription is unlikely to increase potential customers’ integrity beliefs about sellers with low reputations.

Given the difference in the effectiveness of the re-ascription across sellers with high versus low reputations, we posit that the effect of re-ascribing a reported integrity-based violation to a competence- or benevolence-based violation on repairing customers’ integrity beliefs about an accused seller is contingent on the reputation of the seller.

H1: The positive effect of an accused seller’s re-ascription of an integrity-based violation to a competence- (a) or benevolence- (b) based violation on potential customers’ integrity beliefs about the seller is moderated by the reputation of the seller. This effect is stronger for sellers with a high reputation than for sellers with a low reputation.

We posit a main, negative effect of the re-ascription on customers’ competence or benevolence beliefs about the accused seller. Regardless of the seller’s reputation, the seller will not benefit (i.e., through increasing customer beliefs about their competence of benevolence) from acknowledging their lack of competence or benevolence in the re-ascription. Customers are unlikely to carefully scrutinize an accused seller’s acknowledgment of guilt when such guilt is not the focus of accusation (i.e., integrity); rather, they are more likely to believe the seller’s ascription of low competence or benevolence (Kassin, 2005). The re-ascription will thus negatively affect potential customers’ competence or benevolence beliefs about the seller regardless of the seller’s reputation.

H2: An accused seller’s re-ascription of an integrity-based violation to a competence- (a) or benevolence- (b) based violation reduces potential customers’ competence (a) or benevolence (b) beliefs about the seller.

## Effects of Stability Attribution on Competence and Benevolence

The second step of our proposed approach is to increase the competence or benevolence beliefs that anticipate reduction through the re-ascription account in the first step. Drawing on attribution theory (Weiner, 1985), we propose the social account of stability attribution to repair the re-ascribed competence- or benevolence-based violation in the second step of our approach.

Following a re-ascription of an integrity-based violation to a competence-based violation, stability attributions are particularly relevant for increasing customers’ perceived competence of the accused seller primarily because many forms of competence, such as skill and expertise, can be improved through proper training or experience (Heneman & Judge, 2006). In addition, individuals’ evaluations of competence are often task- and situation-specific and can be revised when additional information is made available (Mayer et al., 1995). Therefore, when an accused seller attributes a competence-based violation to an unstable factor (e.g., a seller may provide a response, such as “I have learned from this case and have made the necessary changes. This situation will not happen again!”), the perceived probability of a future violation will likely decrease. In turn, this will increase customers’ competence beliefs about the seller.

Likewise, following a re-ascription to a benevolence-based violation, a social account with a stability attribution will likely repair the re-ascribed benevolence-based violation. The benevolence of a trustee (i.e., the seller, in this case) indicates care and concern for others’ well-being (Schoorman et al.,

2007). Social psychology research suggests that benevolence is often perceived as unstable in the early stages of a relationship (e.g., Hubbard et al., 2016). Entities will understandably act with an egocentric motive when they initially interact with another commercial party; the trustee’s concern for the other’s benefits may increase as the relationship progresses (Mercier & Deslandes, 2020). Since potential customers have limited interactions with online sellers (i.e., an initial relationship), we posit that if a seller provides a stability attribution account to indicate the instability of a re-ascribed benevolence-based cause (e.g., “I have reflected on the incident and will put customers’ needs first in the future. This situation will not happen again!”), potential customers will increase their benevolence beliefs about the seller.

We argue that the reputation of an accused seller is also a contingent factor for the effects of the stability attribution on competence or benevolence. Potential customers are likely to believe that an accused seller with a high reputation is telling the truth because a reputable seller would care about protecting their good reputation and would be unlikely to engage in the same kind of trust violation in the future. Therefore, we posit the following hypothesis.

H3: Following a re-ascription of an integrity-based violation to a competence- (a) or benevolence- (b) based violation, the accused seller’s reputation moderates the positive effect of the seller’s stability attribution on potential customers’ competence (a) or benevolence (b) beliefs about the seller. This effect is stronger for sellers with a high reputation than for sellers with a low reputation.

## Repairing Competence- and Benevolence-Based Violations through the Disclosure of Financial Compensation Made to the Victim

Two issues remain unsolved in our proposed account-based approach. First, for sellers with a low reputation, providing a stability attribution will not increase potential customers’ competence or benevolence beliefs about the seller, which have been reduced by the seller’s re-ascription of the reported integrity-based violation. Second, a low-reputation seller’s reascription will not increase potential customers’ integrity beliefs. To solve the first issue, we investigate whether the disclosure of substantive amends made by an accused seller to the victim could replace the stability attribution in Step 2 of our approach to increase potential customers’ competence or benevolence beliefs about the seller, particularly for lowreputation sellers.

Previous studies have examined substantive amends as a strategy in rebuilding relationships and restoring cooperation (e.g., Dirks et al., 2011). Substantive amends involve the violator paying a substantive cost and exerting effort to deal with a violation as an apologetic gesture and a retribution (Dirks et al., 2011).

Sellers involved in competence-based violations (after reascription) are expected to take measures to increase their ability to fulfill business transactions and prevent similar violations in the future (Xie & Peng, 2009). Previous research has suggested that substantive amends lead to trustors’ confidence in a violator’s willingness to change and improve (Ren & Gray, 2009), thus repairing competence-based violations by reducing the possibility of similar violations occurring in the future. Similarly, after re-ascribing an integrity-based violation to a benevolence-based one, satisfactory financial compensation made by the seller to the victim signals a benevolent gesture that the seller is willing to develop a good relationship with customers and avoid similar transgressions in the future (Gasparotto et al., 2018). Thus, a seller’s disclosure of financial compensation to the victim is likely to increase potential customers’ benevolence beliefs about the seller.

We further argue that regardless of the seller’s reputation, the seller’s disclosure of financial compensation can increase potential customers’ competence or benevolence beliefs about the seller. In e-commerce settings, the accused seller’s response is made public, and the victim can see and verify action-oriented responses (e.g., whether the seller took the action of paying compensation to the victim) (Desmet et al., 2011). As such, the seller is unlikely to provide a false response that can be verified by the victim. Such a response is different from a social account response. Without an actionoriented response, social account responses from sellers with a low reputation are likely to be perceived as unverifiable cheap talk (Chakraborty & Harbaugh, 2010). In addition, the disclosure of financial compensation can deter sellers’ cheap talk through the threat of the financial cost incurred by sellers through future violations (Ren & Gray, 2009). Thus, the disclosure of financial compensation can reduce potential customers’ concerns regarding the possibility of future competence- or benevolence-based violations even when the seller has a low reputation (Bitner, 1990). In short, the seller’s disclosure of financial compensation can reduce potential customers’ stability perceptions regarding similar future transgressions, thereby increasing their competence or benevolence beliefs about the seller. Thus, we posit the following hypothesis:

H4: Following a re-ascription of an integrity-based violation to a competence- (a) or benevolence- (b) based violation, an accused seller’s response that discloses financial compensation made to the victim increases potential customers’ competence (a) or benevolence (b) beliefs about the seller, regardless of the seller’s reputation.

## Repairing Integrity-Based Violations through Financial Compensation

The second unresolved issue of our account-based approach is that a re-ascription account cannot increase potential customers’ perceived integrity of an accused seller with a low reputation. We contend that as a replacement of the reascription account, the seller’s disclosure of financial compensation can increase potential customers’ integrity beliefs about the seller regardless of the seller’s reputation. In our research context, potential customers view the public exposure of disclosing financial compensation information as a strong signal of an accused seller’s sincerity in resolving a violation (Wojciszke et al., 1998). When facing countless online customer reviews containing different opinions about a seller, potential customers often have little certainty regarding their judgments (Liu & Tang, 2018). The sincerity demonstrated through the disclosure of financial compensation will likely trigger potential customers reevaluation of the initially low integrity beliefs formed on the basis of a negative review written by a prior buyer (i.e., a belief-updating process). As such, increases in customers’ perceptions of the sincerity of a seller can lead to increases in the perceived integrity of the seller (Tabak et al., 2012).

Furthermore, the financial cost paid by a seller to compensate the victim demonstrates the justice of the seller in dealing with customers and motivates potential customers to put their trust in the seller (Dawar & Pillutla, 2000). Justice is another important factor that influences an individual’s moral judgment of others, including integrity (Colquitt & Rodell, 2011). During the early stage of an economic exchange relationship, customers heavily weigh justice and exchange norms (Grégoire et al., 2009). In our context, potential customers view an accused seller’s sacrifice in the form of financial compensation paid to the buyer as demonstrating the justice of the seller and thus can enhance customers’ integrity beliefs about the seller. These arguments regarding the effects of disclosure of financial compensation are applicable for online sellers regardless of their reputation because such disclosed information is made public and can be verified by the victim, as discussed above. Thus, we posit the following:

H5: An accused seller’s response that discloses financial compensation made to the victim increases potential customers’ integrity beliefs about the seller regardless of the seller’s reputation.

## Methodology and Results

To test our hypotheses, we conducted four experiments in a computer laboratory located at a Chinese university. Experiments 1 and 2 examined the effects of the proposed two-step account-based approach in repairing an integritybased violation in an ascription dispute and the moderating effects of the accused seller’s reputation (i.e., H1, H2, and H3). The two experiments focused on the re-ascription of an integrity-based violation to a competence- and benevolencebased violation, respectively. Experiment 3 examined the effects of disclosing financial compensation made to the victim (following a re-ascription) on repairing customers’ competence and benevolence (i.e., H4). Experiment 4 tested the effects of disclosing financial compensation on repairing a reported integrity violation without re-ascription (i.e., H5).

## Experiment 1: Testing the Re-ascription to a Competence-Based Violation with and without a Stability attribution

To test H1a, H2a, and H3a, we employed a laboratory experiment with a 3 (seller response: no response vs. a reascription to a competence-based violation vs. a re-ascription to a competence-based violation followed by a stability attribution) × 2 (seller reputation: high vs. low) betweensubjects design (see Table 1). The participants in these groups received a manipulation of an integrity-based trust violation. Two control groups and two supplementary groups were also included. The participants in the control groups did not receive the manipulation of an integrity-based violation (and thus no seller’s response). The main purpose of the control groups was to check the effectiveness of the manipulation of the integritybased violation. In the supplementary groups, the participants read the seller’s response indicating a mere denial of the integrity ascription only (but no further ascription to a competence-based violation), which was part of the reascription response. The supplementary groups were used to verify our arguments that merely denying the ascription of integrity is ineffective for increasing customers’ perceived integrity of the accused seller.

For experimental purposes, we selected www.taobao.com, which is currently the largest C2C e-commerce platform in China and one of the most popular C2C e-commerce websites worldwide (Alexa, 2020), as the experimental platform.

## Experimental Participants, Task, and Procedure

We recruited 300 participants for Experiment 1, with 119 males and 181 females. The sample size was determined by a statistical power calculation with a power of 80%, a medium effect (f = 0.25), and a significance level of p = 0.05 (Chin, 1998). The ages of the participants ranged from 17 to 29 years. All the participants had over five years of internet use experience and had shopped at least three times on taobao.com in the last 12 months.

Upon the arrival of the participants at the laboratory, we briefly oriented them regarding the experiment and disguised the purpose of the study by explaining that it was an “investigation into how customers choose products and sellers from taobao.com.” We instructed the participants to assume the role of a customer searching for an online tailor at taobao.com to make a suit as a gift for one of their close friends. We also informed the participants that a seller named Tailor KC was in the search results and might fulfill their tailoring requirements.<sup>5</sup>

Thereafter, we presented a manipulated webpage of Tailor KC to the participants. The participants could freely browse the webpage as if they were surfing taobao.com as usual. Once they finished browsing the webpage, they were asked to complete a post-task questionnaire for the main and control variables. The entire experimental session lasted approximately one hour. The participants received RMB50 as compensation for their participation.

## Manipulations

Figure 3 shows a screenshot of the manipulated webpage presented to the participants (a re-ascription response provided by a seller with a high reputation followed by a stability attribution is presented as an example). Given that our experiments were conducted in China, we followed the back-translation method to develop the materials related to customer reviews, the seller’s responses, and questionnaires (e.g., Liang et al., 2013). The manipulated webpage of Tailor

KC contained part or all of the following manipulations, depending on the experimental condition.

## 1. Reputation information for Tailor KC (presented to all groups)

We first provided the participants with the reputation information about Tailor KC. Tailor KC’s reputation was manipulated through the provision of its past transaction record and overall customer ratings. We followed taobao.com to provide accumulated sales volumes and customer ratings for sellers, which have also been widely adopted as manifestations of an online seller’s reputation by prior research and in practice (e.g., Kim & Dennis, 2019; Li et al., 2019).

For the participants assigned to the “high reputation” groups, Tailor KC was described as follows: having a large number of successful transactions (i.e., 640,334 transactions with 99.21% 5-star ratings) and high average ratings for “Item as Described” (4.8/5.0 stars), “Seller’s Service Attitude” (4.9/5.0 stars), and “Shipping Services” (4.9/5.0 stars).<sup>6</sup> All these ratings were labeled “higher than other similar sellers.” For the participants assigned to the “low reputation” groups, Tailor KC was described as follows: only 130 successful transactions with 91.67% 5-star ratings and relatively low average ratings for “Item as Described” (4.2/5.0 stars), “Seller’s Service Attitude” (4.2/5.0 stars), and “Shipping Services” (4.1/5.0 stars). All these ratings were labeled “lower than the average level of similar sellers.” These transaction records and the overall customer ratings of our manipulated seller with high and low reputations (i.e., Tailor KC) were comparable to those of sellers with high and low reputations, respectively, on taobao.com. We conducted a series of pre-tests to ensure that the participants perceived Tailor KC to have a high or low reputation as expected.

## 2. Five positive reviews of Tailor KC (presented to all groups)

Five positive reviews about Tailor KC were presented to participants. We expected participants to use these reviews to form their initial trust in the seller.

## 3. A negative review of Tailor KC’s integrity (presented to all groups except the control groups)

The following negative review that reported a product nondelivery by a prior customer was presented to the participants: “The tailor committed to making a suit for me. I sent the materials to the tailor. She was deceptive and cheated for my materials. The suit should have arrived before the New Year, but I received nothing yet. She did not keep the promise! Unacceptable!” Nondelivery has been frequently reported by online customers, and customers often describe this issue as being an integrity-based trust violation (Agag & El-Masry, 2016).

4. Tailor KC’s response to the negative review (presented to all groups except the control and “no response” groups)

We provided the accused seller’s response to the negative review depending on the experimental condition. Table 1 presents the detailed response for each group. The reascription response contains a denial of the integrity ascription and admits a competence issue that led to the delivery delay. Following the re-ascription, a stability attribution response explains that the seller learned from this and made changes to prevent similar mistakes in the future.

## Measurements

Our dependent variables (DVs) include three trusting beliefs, namely, competence, benevolence, and integrity. We adapted the measurement items from the trust literature (McKnight et al., 2002; Wang & Benbasat, 2007). Perceived seller reputation was measured as a manipulation check using items adapted from the literature (Doney & Cannon, 1997; Jarvenpaa et al., 2000). Perceived stability of the violation (Klein & Dawar, 2004) and perceived believability of a seller’s response (Craig et al., 2012; Grazioli & Carrell, 2002) were measured as manipulation checks for the seller’s response. We also measured participants’ familiarity with the online shopping platform in the experiment (i.e., taobao.com) (Bhattacherjee, 2002) and their disposition to trust (McKnight et al., 2002) as control variables. All items used 7-point scales, with 7 representing the highest level of agreement. Table 2 presents the measurement items.<sup>7</sup>

## Results of Experiment 1

Manipulation checks: Table A1 in the Appendix presents the descriptive statistics of Experiment 1. We checked the effectiveness of our three manipulations: (1) an integritybased violation, (2) the seller’s reputation, and (3) the seller’s response. First, we assessed whether the participants recognized the nature of the violation as expected, i.e., an integrity-based violation. We compared the initial trust of the participants in the control groups with that in the “no response” groups. Upon reading the five positive reviews, the participants perceived the seller to have a certain level of integrity $( M ~ = ~ 4 . 6 7 , ~ S D ~ = ~ 1 . 1 3 )$ , and such perception significantly decreased after reading the negative review (M = 3.68, SD = 1.12, t = 4.83, p < 0.001). At the end of the posttask questionnaire, we also asked the participants (except for those in the control groups) the following question: “What did the negative review bring into question?” The options included “primarily the seller’s ability,” “primarily the seller’s benevolence,” “primarily the seller’s integrity,” and “none of the above.” All the participants successfully identified that the negative review concerned the seller’s integrity. Based on these results, we conclude that we have effectively manipulated an integrity-based trust violation in the experiment.

Second, for the reputation manipulation, a t-test showed a significant difference in the perceived reputation of the seller between the high (M = 5.45, SD = 0.81) and low reputation groups (M = 3.58, SD = 0.98, t = 8.40, p < 0.001). Our manipulation of seller reputation was thus effective.

Third, we checked the participants’ perceived believability of the seller’s response. Our results showed that their perceived believability of the re-ascription response from a seller with a high reputation (M = 5.38, SD = 1.16) was significantly higher than that from a seller with a low reputation (M = 3.41, SD = 0.97, t = 7.15, p < 0.001). Likewise, their perceived believability of the stability attribution from a seller with a high reputation (M = 5.54, SD = 1.21) was significantly higher than that from the seller with a low reputation (M = 3.69, SD = 1.16, t = 6.07, p < 0.001). In addition, participants perceived benevolence of the seller in the re-ascription conditions (M = 5.01, SD = 1.25) was not significantly different from that in the “no response” group (M = 4.82, SD = 1.17, t = 0.86, p = 0.394). Therefore, as expected, the “reascription to competence” did not reduce the participants’ benevolence beliefs about the seller. Meanwhile, for the seller’s stability attribution response, the participants’ perceived stability of the trust violation was significantly higher in the “no response” groups (M = 4.78, SD = 0.81) than that in the groups with a stability attribution response (M = 3.99, SD = 1.22, t = 4.14 p < 0.001). Thus, our manipulations of the seller’s response were effective.

## Discussion of Experiment 1

The results of Experiment 1 confirm the expected effects of our proposed account-based approach. As predicted, the reascription to a competence-based violation and the stability attribution are effective in repairing trust for sellers with a high reputation but not for sellers with a low reputation. We conducted Experiment 2 to examine the effects of another type of re-ascription (re-ascribing an integrity-based violation to a benevolence-based one) and those of the stability attribution on repairing the re-ascribed benevolence-based violation.

## Experiment 2: Testing the Re-ascription to a Benevolence-Based Violation with and without a Stability Attribution

To test H1b, H2b, and H3b, we conducted the second laboratory experiment with a 3 (seller response: no response vs. a reascription to a benevolence-based violation vs. a re-ascription to a benevolence-based violation followed by a stability attribution) × 2 (seller reputation: high vs. low) between-subjects design (see Table 3). Similar to Experiment 1, two control groups, in which the participants did not receive the trust violation manipulation (and thus no seller’s response), were also included.

The experimental task, experimental procedure, and measurement of the variables were the same as those in Experiment 1. The key difference was that we manipulated the accused seller’s responses to re-ascribing an integrity-based violation to a benevolence-based violation instead of a competence-based one. Table 3 presents the manipulation of the re-ascription and stability attribution we used. The re-ascription response contains a denial of the integrity ascription, followed by admitting a benevolence issue that led to a delay in delivery. Following this re-ascription, the stability attribution response explains that the seller has resolved to avoid similar mistakes in the future.

## Results of Experiment 2

Manipulation checks: Table A4 in the Appendix presents the descriptive statistics of Experiment 2. We conducted similar manipulation checks to those of Experiment 1. Compared to the participants’ initial beliefs about the integrity of the seller (M = 4.71, SD = 0.98), their perceived integrity beliefs decreased after reading the negative review (M = 3.53, SD = 1.06, t = 6.36, p < 0.001). The perceived reputation of the high reputation groups (M = 5.52, SD = 0.92) was significantly higher than that of the low reputation groups (M = 3.55, SD = 1.27, t = 6.53 p < 0.001). Furthermore, as expected, the re-ascription to a benevolence-based violation did not affect the participants’ competence beliefs about the seller (t = 0.80, p = 0.425). The participants’ perceived believability of the re-ascription (t = 4.28, p < 0.001) and the stability attribution (t = 2.60, p = 0.012) from a seller with a high reputation were higher than those from a seller with a low reputation. Thus, all our manipulations were effective.

Effects of re-ascription on integrity and benevolence (H1b and H2b): We conducted ANCOVAs to test the effects of the seller’s re-ascription to a benevolence-based violation (see Figure 6 and Table A5 in the Appendix) using data from Groups 1, 2, 4, and 5. Our results showed that, for a seller with a high reputation, the re-ascription response significantly increased participants’ perceived integrity (M = 4.84, SD = 0.76) compared to those in the conditions without a seller response (M = 3.84, SD = 0.97, F(1, 116) = 15.27, p < 0.001); however, this effect was insignificant for the seller with a low reputation (F(1, 116) = 0.68, p = 0.411). Thus, H1b is supported. Furthermore, the re-ascription decreased the participants’ benevolence belief regardless of the seller’s reputation (F(1, 116)<sub>high\_reputation</sub> = 15.40, p < 0.001 and F(1, 116)<sub>low\_reputation</sub> = 7.80, p = 0.006), thus lending support to H2b.

## Discussion of Experiment 2

Experiment 2 provides evidence for the hypothesized effect of our re-ascription account. The effect was contingent on the seller’s reputation. This finding is similar to that of Experiment 1. ## Experiment 3: Repairing Competence and Benevolence by Disclosing Substantive Amends Made to the Victim

## Experimental Design

We conducted the third laboratory experiment with a 5 (seller response: no response vs. a re-ascription to a competencebased violation vs. a re-ascription to a competence-based violation followed by a disclosure of financial compensation vs. a re-ascription to a benevolence-based violation vs. a reascription to a benevolence-based violation followed by a disclosure of financial compensation) × 2 (seller reputation: high vs. low) between-subjects design (see Table 4). Similar to Experiments 1 and 2, two control groups, in which the participants did not receive a trust violation manipulation (and thus no seller’s response), were included.

The experimental task, experimental procedure, and measurement of the variables were the same as those in Experiments 1 and 2. The key difference was that we used the disclosure of financial compensation to replace the stability attribution response following the re-ascription response. Product refunds and discounts for future purchases are typical forms of financial compensation that have been extensively used in practice and studied in previous research (e.g., Xie & Peng, 2009). Thus, we used refunds and discounts for future purchases in this experiment, and the following response was provided:

Response to disclosing financial compensations made to the victim (presented to the “re-ascription + compensation” groups): “As compensation, we will refund you the shipping fees. In addition, we will provide you a 40% discount for your next order.”

We conducted a pre-test with the participants who made frequent purchases at Taobao. The participants agreed that using financial compensation was a common practice on the platform to deal with transaction transgressions, and our level of compensation was reasonable for customers experiencing similar transgressions described in our experimental scenario.

## Discussion of Experiment 3

The results of Experiment 3 confirmed that, following a reascription, the accused seller’s response of disclosing financial compensation made to the victim increased potential customers’ competence and benevolence beliefs about the seller regardless of their reputation. We also observed that disclosing financial compensation increased the perceived integrity of the seller regardless of their reputation (F(1, 176)<sub>high\_reputation</sub> = 14.77, p < 0.001 and F(1, 176)<sub>low\_reputation</sub> = 20.86, p < 0.001). Another unresolved issue in our proposed account-based approach is whether disclosing financial compensation information can directly repair integrity without a re-ascription response from the seller. We designed Experiment 4 to test the related hypothesis (H5).

## Discussion of Experiment 4

The results of Experiment 4 confirm the effects of disclosing financial compensation information on repairing integrity, regardless of seller reputation, as hypothesized. Together with

Experiment 3, we demonstrate that a response with financial compensation information could repair potential customers competence, benevolence, and integrity beliefs about the seller. More importantly, the effects of such a response did not depend on the seller’s reputation, which matters for the effects of social accounts. This finding reflects a difference between social accounts and substantive amends for repairing potential customers’ trust.

## General Discussion

In e-commerce settings, victims of trust violations can openly voice and report the violations they experienced through negative online reviews. In turn, the accused sellers can openly respond to these reviews. Sellers and victims (buyers) often dispute the ascription of a trust violation. With the prevalence of online reviews, these reviews and sellers responses largely affect the trust of potential customers in the accused sellers.

This study focuses on repairing the trust of potential customers who learned about an integrity-based trust violation reported in a negative review of a previous buyer. Drawing upon the cognitive-sensemaking framework of trust violations and the social account literature, we proposed an account-based two-step approach (i.e., reascription and stability attribution) for an accused seller to repair reported integrity-based violations in ascription disputes. We considered the reputation of the accused seller as a contingency factor for the effectiveness of our accountbased approach. We also examined the effects of a seller’s response of disclosing substantive amends (i.e., financial compensation) made by the seller to the victim as an alternative approach to trust repair when our account-based approach is ineffective. The four experiments reported in the preceding sections demonstrated the effects of our trust repair approach and the role of the seller’s reputation as well as the effects of a seller’s disclosure of substantive amends when the account-based approach is ineffective.

## Implications for Research

This study offers several theoretical contributions. First, we contribute to trust research in the field of information systems (IS) by extending the scope of trust repair research to an understudied domain in which the ascription of a trust violation is disputed in e-commerce settings. Following early studies on trust violations and repair in social psychology, scholars in organizational behavior research have developed various strategies (e.g., social accounts and substantive amends) to repair different types of trust violations (e.g., competence- and integrity-based). Building from them, studies in other fields (e.g., communication and IS) have examined the effectiveness of these strategies in different contexts (e.g., public media and e-commerce) (please see Table 6 for a review). However, these studies have not yet examined the scenario in which an accused violator challenges the correctness of an accuser’s ascription of a trust violation (e.g., Kim et al., 2009). In e-commerce settings, integrity-based trust violations of sellers are frequently reported, whereas disputes over violation ascriptions often occur between buyers and sellers given the physical distance between them (Wang & Benbasat., 2005). Yet, not all extant trust repair strategies are applicable to repair disputed trust violations. To the best of our knowledge, we are among the first to fill this gap and extend the trust repair literature to e-commerce contexts by studying strategies for repairing such disputed violations.

Second, we refine the extant literature on social accounts for trust repair by examining two accounts: re-ascription and stability attribution. Thus far, the extant literature has extensively studied two basic accounts (i.e., apology and denial of the violation). However, these accounts may be inapplicable in addressing trust violations in ascription disputes. Instead of denying a violation, our re-ascription account is an effective solution for a reported integrity-based violation in ascription disputes (e.g., when the accuser misunderstands the actual cause of a trust violation). More importantly, our findings on the effects of re-ascription generally contribute to a better understanding of customers moral judgments. In particular, we find that negative moral judgments can be corrected to a non-moral-related cause via social accounts, especially when the accounts are from a reputable source. The findings of this study can serve as a basis for future studies in dealing with moral-related issues, especially when the accuracy of the reported issues is disputed.

The stability attribution is another account examined in our approach to repair a re-ascribed competence- and benevolence-based violation. Previous studies have focused on the attribution of stability in repairing trust in general but not on specific trusting beliefs (e.g., Chen et al., 2013). Our theorization of the stability attribution offers a theoretical angle with which to investigate relationship recovery for different types of trusting belief (integrity vs. competence vs. benevolence). We speculate that once a seller commits an integrity-based violation, the seller will be condemned as not trustworthy, because people consider integrity a stable individual trait (Simoms, 2002). However, for competenceand benevolence-based violations in e-commerce settings, sellers can increase customers’ competence and benevolence through trust repair efforts.

Third, we advance the understanding of repairing the trust of potential customers in an accused vendor. The extant trust repair studies focus on repairing the victim’s trust. However, potential customers become a major target of trust repair in online settings. Our investigation is helpful to e-commerce, because potential customers can readily observe how disputes and violations are handled in online environments (e.g., through online reviews) and change their trust in the accused seller accordingly. To our knowledge, we are among the first to propose a social account-based approach and theorize its effects on repairing the trust of potential customers.

Fourth, we identify a contingency factor related to the accused seller (i.e., reputation) when examining the effects of social accounts in our research context. This contingency factor is critical to e-commerce contexts for two reasons. First, potential customers have no prior interaction with an accused seller and, thus, would not know whether the social accounts provided by the seller are believable. The reputation of the focal seller can be used to serve as a basis for potential customers to evaluate the believability of that seller’s social accounts. This factor has not been investigated in offline contexts. Thus, whether it matters for various trust repair strategies in online (and offline) settings remains unknown.

Second, reputation signals and cues are readily available in online environments. By applying the theoretical perspectives of source credibility and believability, we discover a boundary condition for the effects of social accounts and inform the trust repair research by highlighting that individuals’ judgment on whether or not to believe an account should be considered when studying the effects of social accounts.

Finally, this study enriches the trust repair literature by examining the public disclosure of financial compensation information as an alternative approach to trust repair when social accounts are ineffective. The extant literature examines the effects of financial compensation made to a focal accuser to repair that person’s trust (e.g, Cui et al., 2018). Evidently, the effects of the public disclosure of such compensation information can be generally extended to potential customers. Furthermore, despite the importance of the violator’s reputation for social accounts, we uncover whether the violator’s reputation matters depending on the trust repair strategy used. Distinct from that of social accounts, the effect of disclosing financial compensation is not affected by the seller’s reputation. Public disclosure of substantive amends can overcome the constraint of being judged as cheap talk associated with social accounts from accused violators with a low reputation. Thus, revealing such a difference advances the trust literature on repairing strategies by providing an integrated and comprehensive understanding of the effectiveness of various strategies.

## Implications for Practice

This research has several practical implications. First, it outlines an account-based approach to addressing integritybased violations in ascription disputes. The accused sellers are often faced with the difficulties of whether and how to respond to mis-ascribed violations. <sup>8</sup> If a violation was indeed mis-ascribed and the accused seller does not reascribe the violation, appropriately judging the sellers’ integrity would be difficult for potential customers. In addition, “no response” is the least effective way of dealing with disputes based on our experimental results in the “no response” conditions. If a violation is mis-ascribed, the accused seller should provide customers with a re-ascription account despite the possible harm that the re-ascription may cause on other trust beliefs about the seller. The seller must also further repair the reduction of the other trust beliefs using additional strategies (e.g., a stability attribution or publicly disclosing substantive amends made to the victim if applicable). Therefore, our proposed account-based approach and the disclosure of substantive amends provide a clear guideline to help online sellers minimize the damages caused by trust violations with mis-ascriptions.

Second, we provide evidence to practitioners that customers do not blindly believe the social accounts of accused sellers. In the aftermath of an alleged trust violation, potential customers often refer to certain heuristics (e.g., the seller’s reputation) when judging social accounts from an accused entity. Online sellers should note that customers are unlikely to believe accounts from sellers with a low reputation. These sellers must exert additional effort to repair trust—for example, by providing substantive amends to victims and publicly disclosing such amends to address trust concerns from potential customers.

Third, our results of disclosing information related to the substantive amends made to the victim provide implications for practitioners to choose an appropriate strategy for trust repair. Although disclosing information about such amends can effectively repair integrity-based violations, even without additional social accounts, the financial cost of our proposed social account-based approach is considerably lower than that of making substantive amends. However, it is important to note that our proposed account-based approach is ineffective for sellers with a low reputation. Thus, these sellers should consider providing substantive amends to victims and disclosing such amends publicly to repair potential customers’ trust.

## Limitations and Future Research

This study has several limitations and offers multiple opportunities for future research. First, we collected data in a specific C2C e-commerce context using a laboratory experiment method. This context involved some limitations in ecological validity. In our experiments, we asked participants to browse a manipulated webpage that contained specific previous customers’ reviews and the seller’s responses. Thus, testing our hypotheses in an actual B2C or C2C site would be worthwhile. Such a field study could increase technical complexity, and the corresponding designs could reduce researchers’ control. Nevertheless, such attempts would provide a realistic environment for data collection and opportunities to investigate other potential factors on trust repair, such as the cultural differences of a heterogeneous sample. Individuals from individualistic cultures are often skeptical of commercial entities (Chang & Cheng, 2015); thus, social accounts, even from reputable sellers, may be less effective for these types of customers than for those from a collectivist culture. In addition, using a field study that involves a large sample size, and thus increases the statistical power, would allow researchers to better test H3b and they would be hence more likely to observe a significant interaction effect between stability attributions and seller reputation.

Second, to control for possible confounding issues, we presented only a single negative review on the seller’s integrity issue to the experimental participants. Potential customers may read multiple, different negative reviews about the seller—e.g., when a seller simultaneously violates multiple trusting beliefs. Thus, if a strategy cannot simultaneously repair all beliefs, we must consider a trade-off between different beliefs and identify a feasible way to strategically address these violations. Future studies could extend our work by investigating the needed strategies to repair trust in such a context.

Third, as this study focuses on trust violations and trust repair, we only investigated trust violations in disputes over trustrelated factors (i.e., integrity vs. competence or benevolence). Other types of disputes unrelated to trust factors, such as customers’ misunderstanding of service agreements, exist in ecommerce. Future studies can extend our work by examining the contexts of disputed violations beyond trust-related factors. In particular, accused sellers might apply attributions of locus and controllability to deal with different accusations. For example, a seller might attribute a transgression to an external actor (i.e., the locus attribution). Future research could examine the effects of these attributions in dealing with these different types of disputes.

Last but not least, this study does not examine factors related to those who make trust violation accusations in negative reviews. Instead, it focuses on potential customers as the target of trust repair. However, the effect of accusers’ reactions to sellers’ responses is a topic worthy of future investigation.

## Conclusion

Drawing on the cognitive sensemaking process of trust violation and attribution theory, this research proposes a twostep account-based approach to repairing the trust of potential online customers in a seller who was accused of an integritybased violation but disputed r the ascription of the violation. Our findings reveal that the effectiveness of our account-based approach is contingent upon the accused seller’s reputation. We also find that a seller’s disclosure of financial compensation made to the victim is an effective approach to repairing potential customers’ trust. Thus, we advance the understanding of trust repair strategies for potential customers. Furthermore, this research provides practical guidelines for accused sellers to deal with integrity-based violations in ascription disputes.
