# Third-party apps (TPAs) and software platform performance: The moderating role of competitive entry

Geng Zhou, Peijian Song

School of Business, Nanjing University, 22, Hankou Road, Nanjing, 210093, China

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Platform Apps Entry Platform ecosystem Two-sided markets

## A B S T R A C T

Noting the increasing importance of software platform, this article examines the e<sup>f</sup>ects of third-party apps (TPAs) on platform performance (PP) and how such effects differ before versus after competitive entry. The authors validate the hypotheses using data from the web browser market. The results show that the quantity of new TPAs leads to higher PP before than after competitive entry, whereas the quantity of TPA updates contributes a higher PP after than before competitive entry. In addition, the quality is more important and diversity is less important to PP after than before competitive entry.

## 1. Introduction

Software built on di<sup>f</sup>erent platforms is used in a variety of industries such as mobile systems (e.g., Apple App Store), web browsing (e.g., Firefox, Chrome), video games (e.g., Xbox, PlayStation), and social media (e.g., Facebook) [1–3], leading more researchers to emphasize the importance of platform-based strategies for establishing a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s competitive advantage and performance (e.g., [4]. Unlike traditional, nonplatform-based markets, software platforms extend product boundaries by encouraging diverse, complementary third-party apps (TPAs) to join them to help satisfy the needs of heterogeneous users [5,6]. For example, Apple’s App Store launched in early 2007 with approximately 500 TPAs; by 2015, this quantity had increased to 1.5 million TPAs, ranging across varied domains such as education, entertainment, games, health, productivity, and sports. Google Android, a competing smartphone platform, featured 1.6 million TPAs in 2015.

To make the software platform more appealing to customers, a platform can devise strategies for new TPAs to join it [1,7]. A platform, for example, can o<sup>f</sup>er development guidelines or standard development kits for TPAs [8]. To have successful TPAs such as Angry Birds, the platform might also impose screening criteria on TPAs before making them available [9]. At the same time, a platform can encourage or in centivize existing TPAs to upgrade, which is to leave core functionality intact, and to release with more features and better performance [10]. For example, with the encouragement from web browser platform, Adblock Plus, a TPA that helps end users block annoying ads, has upgraded its versions 62 times since its initial launching. However, these TPAs represent a double-edged sword for the platform [1,11]: On the one hand, a wide portfolio of complementary TPAs makes the platform more attractive, but on the other hand, managing a wide range of TPAs can be challenging in terms of both performance risks and end-user acceptance [5]. Therefore, it is both practically and theoretically important to understand the value implications of TPAs to software platform performance (PP). However, we still know little about how TPAs contribute to PP, leaving a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant research gap that this paper attempts to address.

In addition, the e<sup>f</sup>ects of new TPAs and TPA updates on PP may be di<sup>f</sup>erent with the entry of competing platforms, as many platformbased markets experience competitive entries. If the incumbent platform and new entrant use similar business models and are developed for the same end users, they are positioned in direct competition and the new entrant can be considered as competitive [12]. For example, in the video game market, Sony’s incumbent PlayStation platform experienced competition from Microsoft’s Xbox consoles. In the online group buying industry, the incumbent Groupon faced intense rivalry from the new entrant LivingSocial. The market share of the new, competitive platform has the potential to reach certain threshold that could a<sup>f</sup>ect the incumbent platforms. Competing platforms may even a<sup>f</sup>ect the survival of the incumbent platforms [13,12]. However, we know little about the impact of TPAs on PP under distinct market conditions (e.g., before and after competitive entry). The entry of a competitive platform might alter the behaviors of end users and TPAs, as well as their interplay [3], and thus the di<sup>f</sup>erent aspects of TPAs on PP before and after competitive entry may be quite di<sup>f</sup>erent.

Therefore, this paper explicitly examines two key questions: (1) What are the efects of new TPAs and TPA updates on platform performance? and (2) How do such efects difer before and after competitive entry? In particular, we focus on three aspects of new TPAs and TPA updates: quantity, quality, and diversity. The quantity relates to the number of new TPAs and TPA updates on the platform, quality is about the performance attributes of these new TPAs and TPA updates perceived by end users, and diversity refers to the extent to which these TPAs span across multiple subject domains. Empirically, we study the competition between two platform-based open web browsers: the early mover Mozilla Firefox and the later entrant Google Chrome. Firefox, introduced in November 2004, di<sup>f</sup>ered from traditional browsers such as Internet Explorer (IE), in that it operated with a platform ecosystem model that provided a development kit and application programming interface (API) to encourage TPAs (i.e., add-on applications by thirdparty developers to enrich Firefox’s performance). In December 2009, Google developed its Chrome browser and entered the market using a similar platform strategy. This entry provides an ideal setting to assess the e<sup>f</sup>ects of TPAs on the Firefox platform’s performance both before and after the competitive entry by Chrome.

Longitudinal weekly data, collected between 2008 and 2013, revealed the TPAs, PP, and competitive entry; by adopting vector autoregression with the exogenous variable (VARX) method, we incorporate dynamic and network e<sup>f</sup>ects between TPA and PP. In turn, we obtain several important insights.

(1) The quantity of new TPAs leads to higher PP before than after competitive entry, whereas the quantity of TPA updates contributes a higher PP after than before competitive entry.

(2) The quality of new TPAs and TPA updates is more important to PP after than before competitive entry.

(3) Before competitive entry, a diversi<sup>fi</sup>ed portfolio of new TPAs con tributes more to PP, whereas after competitive entry, a con centrated portfolio contributes more to PP.

These <sup>fi</sup>ndings accordingly contribute to extant literature in several important ways. First, our theoretical framework and empirical results suggest that assessing the value of complementary TPAs to the platform requires noting their unique characteristics and <sup>fi</sup>t with market condi tions (before and after competitive entry). Second, the literature of competitive entry in information system, strategic management, and economics mainly focused on the order-of-entry e<sup>f</sup>ect and market shares achieved by a new entrant or retained by an incumbent (e.g., [14,15], and disagreement exists about whether an entrant platform can gain or retain market share when it competes with an incumbent platform (e.g., [12]; we provide initial evidence on how an incumbent platform can use TPA strategies to respond appropriately. Finally, our empirical evidence implies that, when facing the entry of a competing platform, platform owners should shift their focus from new to existing TPAs and focus more on TPA quality. Therefore, the platform should balance control over and contributions by TPAs.

## 2. Related literature

The importance of platform strategies has prompted growing literature to focus on the determinants of PP, as summarized brie<sup>fl</sup>y in Appendix A. According to the constituents of platform ecosystem, the determinants of PP can be categorized as platform-related and supplyand demand-side factors. The former emphasizes how a platform can design appropriate strategies to improve its performance, while the latter two focuses on how end user and TPA characteristics may a<sup>f</sup>ect PP. Among the platform-related factors, prior studies have examined issues such as pricing, openness, and control. For example, some studies show that it is often pro<sup>fi</sup>t maximizing for a platform to undercut its price below cost on one side of the market to attract more consumers on that side and increase consumers’ willingness to pay (e.g., [16–19]. Furthermore, Maurer and Tiwana [9] found that tight development control is a double-edged sword that promotes knowledge integration on one hand but inhibits platform di<sup>f</sup>erentiation on the other. In ad dition, some theoretical studies suggest that selecting the appropriate levels of openness is crucial for platform success (e.g., [20–22]. Among demand-side factors, Chen and Xie [23] found that, unlike traditional market where higher consumer loyalty generally leads to higher pro<sup>fi</sup>ts, in platform market, where e<sup>f</sup>ect of cross-market network exists, a medium level of customer loyalty may lead to lower pro<sup>fi</sup>ts than a low level of loyalty. In addition, Hagiu and Halaburda [24] further examined end users’ expectations and found that di<sup>f</sup>erent mechanisms of expectation formation have distinct in<sup>fl</sup>uences on platform bene<sup>fi</sup>ts.

Surprisingly, existing studies have largely ignored the supply-side factors and their impact on PP. Thus far, only Cenamor et al.’s [25] study showed that end users’ adoption of platform is driven by the availability of complementary products on the platform, but their study did not explore how di<sup>f</sup>erent characteristics of TPAs may di<sup>f</sup>erentially contribute to PP. Furthermore, prior studies largely ignored the po tential entry of competing platforms, even though many platform-based markets experience competitive entries. The emergence of a competing platform is an environmental shock exogenous to an incumbent ecosystem. Although the relationship between environmental dynamics and platform evolution has received some attention in the theoretical literature (e.g., [3], it has received little empirical attention.

## 3. Theory and hypothesis development

## 3.1. Two-sided markets in platform ecosystem

The software platform organizes as an ecosystem that encompasses several distinct roles (refer to Fig. 1): (1) demand-side platform users, commonly called “end users”; (2) supply-side TPAs, which are adopted by demand-side users in tandem with the core platform and can be viewed as add-on products; and (3) the core platform, which serves as the primary point of contact for demand-side users and supply-side TPAs [26,27]. The performance of the core platform depends on the demand-side users, supply-side TPAs, and their interactions [21,3].

The software platform inherently operates as a two-sided market and exhibits a special form of cross-side network e<sup>f</sup>ects because of the interdependence between end-user demands for platforms and demands for their associated TPAs [7,28]. TPAs usually complement and add functionality to the core platform. The bene<sup>fi</sup>t that an end user derives for the use of the platform depends on the availability of TPAs. More TPAs on a platform lead to greater demand for that platform; at the same time, a larger installed base of consumers leads to a larger supply of TPAs [12,29]. In the presence of cross-side network e<sup>f</sup>ects, a size advantage on the TPA side normally leads to dominance on the user side, which is then fed back to the opposite side in an escalating fashion. Thus, a platform that has small lead on both sides of the market is likely to attract more users and more TPAs, and thus over time, it could take over the entire market [30,31].

In many cases, TPAs play a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in platform innovation and serve as the basis for platform leadership [5]. As a result, the focus of platform owners has shifted from in-house innovation to providing resources that support the development of TPAs [32,33], with the goal of o<sup>f</sup>ering future end users greater bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. For example, platforms are providing some base functionality on which developers can build their work [33]. Through the provision of APIs, developers can call up, with simple commands, rich sets of functionality of the underlying platform [8]. As developers can devote less attention to realizing the core functionalities of the platform, their resource commitment to develop a TPA is decreased.

## 3.2. Competitive entry

Most platform ecosystems are always subject to the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of disturbance that is exogenous to ecosystems. The emergence of a new, competitive platform is a source of disturbance and can disrupt the environment for the constituents of the existing platform ecosystem. Because of the interdependence between demand and supply sides, the emergence of a competitive platform in<sup>fl</sup>uences the behaviors of both end users and TPAs (refer to Fig. 1).

The entry of a competing platform will change behaviors of end users. First, end users’ attention to the incumbent platform gets interrupted, and the incumbent platform may lose its advantageous position for in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the attribute weights that consumers use to evaluate platforms [34]. Second, competitive entry will decrease end users’ stickiness toward the incumbent platform. In the context of imperfect information regarding platform quality, end users may rationally stick with the <sup>fi</sup>rst platform they encounter that performs their task satisfactorily Liberman and Montgomery 1988. When competitors emerge, with more information and choices, end users’ intention to stick to the incumbent platform may be decreased. Finally, with com petitive entry, end users can choose both incumbent platform and late entrants. Therefore, a multihoming behavior will also emerge, and it will be more di<sup>fi</sup>cult for the platform to lock-in end users [4]. In turn, the existing platform must reposition the TPA portfolio and di<sup>f</sup>erentiate itself from other platforms to combat competitive encroachment [19,35].

Competitive entry might also change the behaviors of TPAs in several ways. First, new TPAs have multihoming options, such that they can introduce their products in both incumbent and entrant platforms to reach a larger user base and hedge their risk with multiple competing platforms in the market [4]. This option is valuable when the ultimate dominance of any particular platform after competitive entry is uncertain and unpredictable [36]. When TPAs engage in multihoming behaviors, the incumbent platform gains less resource support after competitive entry. That is, the TPAs are less likely to dedicate speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments to one particular platform, which would lock them in and create higher costs for switching from that platform to another. In addition, when incumbent platform’s TPAs are multihomed on entrant platform, the incumbent platform may lose its di<sup>f</sup>erentiation [37]. Second, the emergence of competing platforms erodes the market share of the incumbent; as a result, competition among TPAs for end users intensi<sup>fi</sup>es, which should increase their motivation to improve their quality to compete better with other TPAs [38].

We maintain that in response to the changing behaviors of both TPAs and end users, before and after competitive entry, a platform may need to adjust its TPA strategies in terms of quantity, quality, and di versity. In the next section, we discuss how the e<sup>f</sup>ects of the quantity, quality, and diversity of new TPAs and TPA updates on PP di<sup>f</sup>er before and after competitive entry.

## 3.3. Quantity of new TPAs and existing TPA upgrades

More new TPAs increase the features and utilities o<sup>f</sup>ered by a platform. For example, more apps available in the Apple App Store provide end users with a greater variety of options and can better ful<sup>fi</sup>ll their heterogeneous demands, which increases the attractiveness of that platform. Because of cross-side network e<sup>f</sup>ects, more TPAs on a platform make the platform more valuable and attractive to end users. However, the emergence of a competitive platform may weaken this network e<sup>f</sup>ect in two ways. First, from the demand side, end users will show less attention to an incumbent platform with competitive entry. With the decreasing stickiness toward the incumbent platform and the option of multihoming, end users are less sensitive to and appreciative of the increased quantity of TPAs for the incumbent platform [39]. The competing platform may provide similar TPAs; hence, the value of additional new TPAs on the incumbent platform for end users likely decreases. For example, more TPAs available on Firefox may be less meaningful to end users if apps with similar functionality already exist on the competing Chrome platform. Second, from the supply side, with competitive entry, new TPAs have the option of multihoming and can work with both incumbent and entrant platforms [17]. The resulting similarity of the new TPAs on the competitive platform likely causes the incumbent platform to lose its di<sup>f</sup>erentiation advantage among end users [37]. Therefore, the quantity of new TPAs has a stronger e<sup>f</sup>ect on incumbent PP before, compared with after, competitive entry.

For TPA updates, they do not add to the total quantity of TPAs available on the platform, but these updates improve the e<sup>fi</sup>ciency and functionality of existing TPAs. In the ecosystem of software platform, TPAs are continuously evaluated and updated during their lifetimes. After an initial release of TPAs, end users usually identify vulnerabilities or malfunctions [40]. End users may also require new features, and hence, updates are developed to address such requests. Therefore, TPA updates increase the attractiveness of the platform among users. Furthermore, the continuous improvements are more important after competitive entry because improvements di<sup>f</sup>erentiate the platform from competing platforms [35]. End users already have more alternatives from which to choose after competitive entry; hence, a di<sup>f</sup>erentiation advantage based on continuous improvements of TPAs is more important for attracting and maintaining end users. Therefore, we predict:

H1. The quantity of new TPAs has a stronger e<sup>f</sup>ect on incumbent PP before, compared with after, competitive entry.

H2. The quantity of TPA updates has a weaker e<sup>f</sup>ect on incumbent PP before, compared with after, competitive entry.

## 3.4. Quality of new TPAs and existing TPA upgrades

In addition to quantity, the quality of new TPAs and TPA upgrades also makes the platform more attractive to end users, and such e<sup>f</sup>ect increases with competitive entry. After competitive entry, end users’ attention to the incumbent platform is distracted and their stickiness toward the incumbent platform is decreased. Cross-side network e<sup>f</sup>ects in two-sided market can help the incumbent platform to achieve <sup>fi</sup>rstmover advantage by allowing it to leverage its market sizes on one side to build dominant positions on the other side, creating entry barriers for new entrants [41,12]. However, as end users have more options and lower intentions to remain with the incumbent platform, the incumbent platform must evoke higher quality perceptions among end users to ensure its adoption. Similar to late entrants, the incumbent platform also has to “work harder” to generate awareness and acceptance from end users.

Product quality refers to the collection of features and characteristics of a product that contributes to its ability to meet given users’ requirements. Quality is an important factor for end-user adoption. With a high-quality TPA portfolio, the incumbent platform might establish its high-quality image among end users and gain a competitive advantage in the “race” for consumer attention. As a result, the contribution of quality for new TPAs and existing TPA upgrades on PP should be more important in the postentry period. Therefore, we predict:

H3. The quality of new TPAs has a weaker e<sup>f</sup>ect on incumbent PP before, compared with after, competitive entry.

H4. The quality of TPA updates has a weaker e<sup>f</sup>ect on incumbent PP

before, compared with after, competitive entry.

## 3.5. Diversity of new TPAs and existing TPA upgrades

A unique advantage of a platform is that it enables end users to access, purchase, and use diverse TPAs. For example, the Firefox eco system encompasses more than ten TPA categories, ranging from download management to language support to games and entertainment. Because of the heterogeneous nature of end users’ needs, any single category might not fully meet all their demands, and more diversity makes it more likely that each user will <sup>fi</sup>nd exactly the option he or she desires.

The e<sup>f</sup>ects of the diversity of new and existing TPA upgrades on PP decrease with competitive entry. First, after competitive entry, end users have more options, and their attentions to the incumbent platform may be diverted. With a concentrated TPA portfolio in certain domains, the incumbent platform might establish its distinctiveness among end users [35]. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, concentrated TPAs in selected domains (e.g., gaming and security on Firefox) can help to establish uniqueness of the platform, more important for PP when the platform faces greater “noise” created by competitive entry. Second, when most TPAs are in similar categories, intra-category competition intensi<sup>fi</sup>es such that each TPA competes with others for end users, which increases the pressure on the TPAs to improve their performance to meet end users’ needs better. The improvement pressure associated with low TPA diversity is more important after competitive entry because end users have more options and lower intentions to remain with the incumbent platform. Therefore, we argue:

H5. The diversity of new TPAs has a weaker e<sup>f</sup>ect on the incumbent PP before, compared with after, competitive entry.

H6. The diversity of TPA updates has a weaker e<sup>f</sup>ect on the incumbent PP before, compared with after, competitive entry.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Research context

We choose our empirical setting as the web browser industry, which o<sup>f</sup>ers software used to locate, retrieve, and display content on the Internet. IE by Microsoft has been dominant in this market; its market share peaked at more than 95% in 2002. However, IE is a closed system developed by Microsoft, with no complementary TPAs. By contrast, as a free and open source web browser, Firefox largely ignores product boundaries and encourages complementary, add-on applications (i.e., TPAs). It makes its API available to third-party developers, which can leverage the Firefox platform for their add-on applications, available for end users to download and complement the core platform. Such TPAs extend Firefox capabilities in a variety of ways, such as multimedia information, videoconferencing, and anti-phishing <sup>fi</sup>lters. Shortly after the release of Firefox 1.0 in late 2004, Firefox accounted for 7% of browser use; by August 2011, it had reached a 25% market share, with more than 450 million users worldwide.

Several years after Firefox entered the web browser market, it confronted a competitive entry by Google. On November 29, 2009,<sup>1</sup> Google launched Chrome with a similar platform ecosystem model, including a TPA center that encouraged third-party developers to produce applications for the Chrome platform, in direct competition with Firefox. After Chrome’s entry, Firefox’s market share dropped from 25% to approximately 20% in 2013, while Chrome’s market share increased to 16% in 2011 and 39% in 2013. It is currently the most widely used

web browser in the world.

This competition between an earlier mover, Firefox, and a new entrant, Chrome, o<sup>f</sup>ers an ideal setting for our empirical analysis. First, both platforms use similar, open business models, developed for the same operating systems (i.e., Windows, OS X, Linux, and Android). Therefore, they are positioned in direct competition. Second, they attract similar portfolio of TPAs, and these TPAs have the option of multihoming on both platforms. Third, both Firefox and Chrome are free to end users, which provides a clear context without price heterogeneity e<sup>f</sup>ects. That is, pricing strategies cannot confound PP. Fourth, Chrome’s competitive entry is largely exogenous to Firefox because Firefox had little in<sup>fl</sup>uence over Chrome’s decisions. This set ting minimizes concerns about endogenous entry decisions and enables us to observe the performance implications of TPAs in the Firefox platform both before and after the competitive entry. We also note that IE, as the main closed web browser, did not change its strategies signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in our observation period; hence, it appears largely exogenous to our empirical setting.

## 4.2. Data and measurement

We test our theoretical predictions using a longitudinal weekly data set pertaining to the Firefox from July 2008 to October 2013 (277 weeks). We chose weekly data for two reasons: Because daily information about TPAs and PP changes little, it cannot provide su<sup>fi</sup>cient time variance and using monthly data is too coarse to reveal the timevarying patterns of TPAs and platform performance. In addition, our data reveal that behaviors associated with adding new or updating existing TPAs occur on a weekly basis.

We collected information on TPAs and PP from the o<sup>fi</sup>cial website for add-on applications for Firefox (https://addons.mazilla.org/en-us/ <sup>fi</sup>refox). This site has recorded all add-ons since the launch of Firefox in 2004; for each add-on, it provides detailed information including the initial launch time, updating history (i.e., when and how many times), category, end-user rating, and the developer’s name. These add-ons only function on Firefox. They are freely available to end users as complements to Firefox’s functionality. Up to October 2013, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed more than 10,000 add-ons, spanning 14 categories, which we detail in Table 1.

By tracing the launch and update information for each add-on application and aggregating it at a weekly level, we obtained longitudinal weekly data about the quantity, quality, and diversity of both new and existing add-ons or TPAs. We measured the quantity of new TPAs (QN) as the total quantity of newly launched TPAs on the Firefox platform each week; the quantity of existing TPA updates (QU) was the quantity of existing TPAs in Firefox platform updated each week. In addition, we took the log form of these measures because the variables are right skewed. After transformation, both variables were normally distributed.

For quality of new TPAs and TPA updates, for each new TPA and TPA updates, we used the average rating from end users as a measure of quality. After downloading the TPA, end users can rate the target TPA according to their own experiences. The rating uses the 5-star scale (1 star: extremely low quality; 5 stars: extremely high quality). We then averaged these ratings for all TPAs and TPA updates each week to obtain our measure of quality of new TPAs $( \mathrm { Q } _ { \mathrm { u } } \mathrm { N } )$ and the quality of TPA updates $\left( \mathrm { Q } _ { \mathrm { u } } \mathrm { U } \right)$

Then, $p _ { j , c } = n _ { j , c } / \Sigma \ n _ { j , c }$ represented the proportion of occurrences of category j relative to the cumulative occurrence of new TPAs in that week. Competitive entry was measured as a dummy variable, equal to 0 for all weeks before November 29, 2009 (when Chrome entered the market), and 1 for all subsequent weeks. We gathered 75 and 202 weeks of observations before and after competitive entry, respectively.

For PP, consistent with the existing literature (e.g., [12], we use market share as a measure of PP. Market share captures the proportion of web browser users who are active users of Firefox. This measure has three advantages. First, it re<sup>fl</sup>ects not only the quantity of downloads but also the active usage of Firefox. Some end users may download multiple web browsers but choose to use only one of them. Second, the adoption of Firefox and other web browsers such as IE and Chrome is largely free to end users, which makes revenue-related measure such as sales revenues not applicable. Finally, market share measure relates to the usage of Firefox, as well as of other competing web browsers, and thus captures competitive dimension of performance. We collected weekly market share data from StatCounter, a leading web tra<sup>fi</sup>c and usage analysis tool (http://gs.statcounter.com/). Table 2 reports the constructs, measures, and summary statistics of all variables. The correlation matrix for these variables is shown in Appendix B.

We also include some control variables associated with the unique features of Chrome’s entry. The <sup>fi</sup>rst one is the market share of Google in search engine market (MSG). Because Chrome is developed by Google and search engine is Google’s core product, the market share of Google in search engine market may in<sup>fl</sup>uence end users’ choice between Firefox and Chrome. These time variant data were measured by the proportion of search engine users who chose Google as their main service provider. We collected this market share data from StatCounter, a leading web tra<sup>fi</sup>c and usage analysis tool (http://gs.statcounter. com/).

The second one is the quantity of web-based products provided by Google $\left( \mathrm { Q } _ { \mathrm { u } } \mathrm { G } \right)$ . After achieving success in search engine market, Google has extended its product line and provided a series of web-based products and services. Google can utilize advantage of these web-based products to help Chrome attract potential users and in<sup>fl</sup>uence the per formance of Firefox platform. This time variant variable was measured by the accumulated number of web-based products provided by Google until this week and was collected from wiki (https://en.wikipedia.org/ wiki/List\_of\_Google\_products).

The third one is the brand value of Google (BVG). Brand is a precious asset to <sup>fi</sup>rms because the users’ experience with the established pro duct, which provide users with the relevant information about the new product, could help reduce the uncertainties and perceived risks associated with the newly launched products. The brand value of Google may also in<sup>fl</sup>uence users’ choice between Firefox and Chrome. We collected this yearly time-variant brand value data from BrandZ top 100 most valuable global brand ranking (http://brandz.com/region/global/ ).

The <sup>fi</sup>nal one is the platform developers’ sunk cost (DSC). After the entry of Chrome, new developers may directly develop TPAs on Chrome without generating sunk costs for Firefox. While, for existing developers on the Firefox platform, they still provide updates because the sunk costs are already there. We used the percentage of existing developers in the platform as the measurement of developers’ sunk cost. It was calculated as the number of existing developers divided by total number of developers. New developers have low sunk cost in the platform, whereas existing developers have high sunk cost. As a result, a higher level of the percentage of existing developers suggests a higher developers’ sunk cost.

## 5.2. Hypothesis testing

In Model 1, we are interested in the short-and long-term interaction e<sup>f</sup>ects between competitive entry and TPA characteristics on PP. As Table 4 indicates, the interaction between competitive entry and the quantity of new TPAs has negative short-term (−0.064) and long-term (−0.039) e<sup>f</sup>ects on PP; this suggests that the quantity of new TPAs exerts a greater short-term and long-term e<sup>f</sup>ect on PP before than after competitive entry. These results support H1. With regard to the e<sup>f</sup>ect of the quantity of TPA updates on PP before and after competitive entry, Table 4 shows that the interaction has signi<sup>fi</sup>cant positive long-term (0.036) e<sup>f</sup>ect on PP. The implication is that the quantity of TPA updates contributes more to PP after than before competitive entry, in support of H2.

Both H3 and H4 refer to the e<sup>f</sup>ects of TPA quality on PP. Table 4 shows that the interaction between competitive entry and quality of new TPAs has positive short-term (0.044) and long-term (0.073) e<sup>f</sup>ects on PP. It indicates that the quality of new TPAs is more important to PP after than before competitive entry. Therefore, H3 is supported. In addition, H4 is supported because the interaction between the quality of TPA updates and competitive entry is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant for both short-term (0.079) and long-term (0.115) e<sup>f</sup>ects (see Table 4). The implication is that the quality of TPA updates contributes more to PP after than before competitive entry.

Finally, both H5 and H6 refer to the e<sup>f</sup>ects of TPA diversity on PP. Table 4 shows that the interaction between competitive entry and di versity of new TPAs has negative long-term e<sup>f</sup>ects on PP (−0.066). This result suggests that the diversity of new TPAs exerts a greater long term e<sup>f</sup>ect on PP before than after competitive entry, in support of H5. However, H6 is not supported because the interaction between the diversity of TPA updates and competitive entry is not signi<sup>fi</sup>cant for both short-term and long-term e<sup>f</sup>ects (see Table 4). This suggests that the diversity of TPA updates contributes equally to PP after than before competitive entry. A possible explanation is that rapid TPA develop ment and expansion on a platform may generate cognitive challenges to users. The user tendency of forgetting can contribute to the decreasing attractiveness of the concentrated TPA updates. Although the incumbent platform might establish its distinctiveness among end users with concentrated TPA updates, the decreasing attractiveness may counteract the moderating role of competitive entry on the relationship between diversity of TPA updates and PP, resulting in no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant moderating e<sup>f</sup>ect of competitive entry.

## 6. Discussions

The objective of this paper is to understand (1) What are the efects of new TPAs and TPA updates on PP? and (2) How do such efects difer before and after competitive entry? We <sup>fi</sup>nd that the quantity of new TPAs leads to higher PP before than after competitive entry, whereas the quantity of TPA updates contributes to higher PP after than before competitive entry. The quality of new TPAs and TPA updates is more important for PP after than before competitive entry. Finally, before competitive entry, a diversi<sup>fi</sup>ed portfolio of new TPAs contributes to higher PP, whereas after competitive entry, a concentrated portfolio of new TPAs helps PP more. These results have both theoretical and practical implications.

## 6.1. Theoretical implications

First, this study contributes to the literature of software platform. In particular, managing TPAs to improve PP is challenging because despite the fact that a wide portfolio of complementary TPAs makes the platform more attractive, a wide range of TPAs may increase performance risks and deter user acceptance [5]. The results suggest that the quantity of new TPAs is more valuable for the platform before than after competitive entry, whereas the quantity of TPA updates contributes to higher PP after than before competitive entry. The quality of new TPAs and TPA updates is more important for PP after than before competitive entry. In addition, before competitive entry, a diversi<sup>fi</sup>ed portfolio of new TPAs and TPA updates contributes more to the platform, whereas after competitive entry, a concentrated portfolio helps the platform more. Second, this article advances our understanding of strategic responses to entry, especially in platform-based markets. Several researchers o<sup>f</sup>er theoretical predictions about competitive response strategies when a new entrant to a market upsets the existing equili brium among incumbent <sup>fi</sup>rms, causing them to reposition themselves in response (e.g., [35]. However, existing studies of repositioning as a strategic response mainly focus on one-sided settings rather than con sidering how <sup>fi</sup>rms respond to entries in multisided markets. Using newspapers as an example of a multisided market, Seamans and Zhu [19] suggest that di<sup>f</sup>erentiation is a strategic response to entry and show that di<sup>f</sup>erentiation among newspaper platforms increases following a new entry (i.e., by Craigslist). To clarify the relative e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of such responses, we have demonstrated that, with competitive entry, more high-quality TPA upgrades in a concentrated domain exert a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant positive e<sup>f</sup>ect on PP. We thus o<sup>f</sup>er an empirical evaluation of the e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of a TPA strategy in a two-sided market and document how this repositioning occurs in tandem with other responses, including an increased quantity of upgraded TPAs.

Third, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings have important implications for the theory of complementarity. In strategy research focused on the relationships between complementarities and <sup>fi</sup>rm performance [57,58,11], most studies argue that a <sup>fi</sup>rm can gain competitive advantages by choosing an industry position characterized by a complementary set of value chain activities that positively reinforce one another [57,59]. The resource-based view recognizes that a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s system of complementary assets is more valuable, rare, di<sup>fi</sup>cult to imitate, and di<sup>fi</sup>cult to sub stitute for than a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s standalone assets [60]. Thus, complementary assets o<sup>f</sup>er important sources of competitive advantage. These argu ments assume that complementary relationships remain stable across di<sup>f</sup>erent contexts, such that competitive advantages gained through complementary assets should persist, even if the conditions change. Our theoretical framework and empirical results instead suggest that changes in market conditions can enable or inhibit the performance advantages associated with complementary assets. A “more is better” view is inaccurate; rather, the value of complementary assets depends strongly on their <sup>fi</sup>t with market conditions.

## 6.2. Practical implications

Our study has several important implications for business practice.

Platform owners should design their TPA strategies according to the market conditions. When there are no competing platforms, they should open their platform and encourage lots of new and diversi<sup>fi</sup>ed TPAs to join. The focus should be not on developing complementary products by themselves but rather on providing resources that support the development of TPAs. By establishing necessary resources and reducing skill requirements, the platform can deepen its prospective pool of TPA producers [61,62]. As an early mover in the smartphone market, Ap ple’s iPhone platform provided substantial resources for TPAs, including software development kits, APIs, and support with marketing and distribution [8]. Even individual hobbyists could access sophisticated TPA design tools. By partitioning the development task between TPAs and the platform, Apple enabled users to design new products and services with relatively little training and practice; a very basic TPA might require just a couple of weeks of part-time development work [63]. Such “democratization” of the digital innovation has helped make Apple the platform leader in the smartphone market.

In addition, the platform should balance control over and contributions by TPAs. Our empirical evidence implies that, when facing the entry of a competing platform, platform owners should shift their focus from new to existing TPAs and focus more on TPA quality. With its property rights over the platform ecosystem, the platform owner can control a broad set of TPAs [33], by modifying their rights, freedoms, and obligations through contracts or other rule-setting instruments [27]. There is a delicate tension between implementing such platform controls and encouraging TPAs to join the platform. Our research in dicates that, for new TPAs, platform owners should pursue some degree of closeness and restrict their development and commercialization. By contrast, for existing TPAs, platform owners should open or remove any restrictions on upgrades and help those TPAs that are especially relevant to end users, such that they can maintain or improve their competitive position over time. Yet platform owners also need to consider the positioning of these existing TPAs and strongly encourage those that match its own repositioning. For example, as the world’s largest social networking platform, Facebook has built a vast ecosystem for a range of TPAs. To respond to the emergence of competing platforms such as LinkedIn and Twitter, Facebook controlled the quantity of new TPAs while enhancing the positioning of its TPA portfolio related to socialization.

Finally, because the platform needs to adjust its TPA strategies according to market conditions, TPA developers should rearrange their development e<sup>f</sup>orts, respectively, especially in postentry periods. Even after the platform has transferred some design capacity to TPA developers, they still must evaluate the use of development strategies in relation to their strategic <sup>fi</sup>t with the platform’s positioning. For TPAs that strategically <sup>fi</sup>t with the repositioned platform, developers may prefer specialization, such that they are willing to contribute signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in vestments and e<sup>f</sup>ort. If TPAs fail to achieve such strategic <sup>fi</sup>t though, developers invest less e<sup>f</sup>ort and decrease their dependence on the platform, through multihoming behaviors.

## 6.3. Limitations and further research directions

This study is subject to several limitations and also could be ex tended in several ways. Our <sup>fi</sup>eld data came from the entry of Chrome that is developed by Google; hence caution is required before generalizing the <sup>fi</sup>ndings to other platforms. Although we have controlled several features associated with Chrome’s entry, contextual factors such as the strategy of new entrants and platform-speci<sup>fi</sup>c settings may shape the performance of the incumbent platforms. Because these e<sup>f</sup>ects have not been included in this study, extending this study to other contexts and collecting contextually rich data sets would help generalize the <sup>fi</sup>ndings to other platforms.

Although we found substantial support for the e<sup>f</sup>ects of di<sup>f</sup>erent TPA strategies on incumbent PP, we do not know the e<sup>f</sup>ects of these TPA strategies for late entrants. In the presence of cross-network e<sup>f</sup>ects, a platform with a small lead on one side of the market normally dom inates also on the other side, which enhances the initial side, in an escalating fashion. Once incumbency has been established in the market, the existing network e<sup>f</sup>ects put later entrants at a critical disadvantage [64]. More research should examine how a new platform can compete successfully with an incumbent, as well as explore how TPA strategies a<sup>f</sup>ect the performance of late entrants.

## 6.4. Conclusions

In conclusion, this study has attempted to reveal the e<sup>f</sup>ects of dif ferent third-party product strategies on PP and how they di<sup>f</sup>er, before versus after competitive entry. The outcomes are clear: Without competitive entry, having a greater quantity and quality of more diversi<sup>fi</sup>ed new TPAs in the platform’s ecosystem enhances the platform’s performance, but with competitive entry, a greater quantity of concentrated, existing TPA upgrades exerts more signi<sup>fi</sup>cant positive e<sup>f</sup>ect on PP.
