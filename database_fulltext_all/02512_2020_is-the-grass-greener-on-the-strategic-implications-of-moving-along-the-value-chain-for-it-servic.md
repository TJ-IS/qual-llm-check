# Is the Grass Greener? On the Strategic Implications of Moving Along the Value Chain for IT Service Providers

Anandasivam Gopal,<sup>a</sup> Sabari Rajan Karmegam,<sup>a</sup> Balaji R. Koka,<sup>b</sup> William M. Rand

<sup>a</sup> Robert H. Smith School of Business, University of Maryland, College Park, Maryland 20742; <sup>b</sup> Jesse H. Abstract. Information technology (IT) service providers are often advised to consider moving their service offerings along the value chain as a way to enhance their compet itiveness. On the basis of this advice, service providers operating successfully at the lower end of the value chain have tried to expand into higher-order consulting services, whereas those operating higher up on the value chain have sought to expand into more routinized services. In both cases, such efforts have been met with limited success. In this paper, we examine why this might be the case, using an agent-based model developed specifically for this purpose. As part of the model, we use the resource-based view of the firm to construct agents, representing individual IT services firms with three distinct strategic orientations (archetypes) operating at different parts of the value chain with varying resource endowments. We then examine the outcomes associated with these firms when they transition along the value chain. Although we find that moving along the value chain is generall risky, we identify specific conditions under which such moves may be favorable to firms. We find that firms moving up the value chain are likely to be successful only if such moves are accompanied by significant resource changes. In contrast, firms moving down the value chain are likely to be successful only if such moves are accompanied by learning capability arising out of higher absorptive capacity. We find that resource fungibility moderates these relationships. We conclude with a discussion of the managerial implications of our study as well as opportunities for future empirical research within the IT services industry based on our propositions.

## Introduction

In 2004, India-based Infosys Technologies, a leading offshore information technology (IT) services provider, founded Infosys Consulting, a U.S.-based subsidiary that would focus on higher-value and higher-margin consulting projects (Garud et al. 2006, Babu 2009, Burgelman and Schifrin 2011). Viewed as “a move up the value chain,” this strategic move was seen as a response to the common advice offered by industry observers that IT service providers should increase the proportion of higher-value consulting projects as a way to gain market share and enhance margins (Garud et al. 2006, Chari 2011). The new consulting entity, however, never achieved traction (Babu 2009) and was eventually shut down (Babu 2009, Sen 2014). More significantly, Infosys’s foray into higher-value consulting appears to have adversely affected its competitive position in its traditional business (Chari 2011). Why did this strategic move fail? Are moves up the value chain always prone to fail, or are there specific conditions under which such moves are likely to be successful? More importantly, are there specific strategic actions that a firm can undertake before such a move to enhance the odds of success? In this paper, we address these questions that remain at the core of strategic decision making within the IT services industry.

Moving up the value chain in the context of IT service providers refers to service offerings that are complex, knowledge based, of higher value, and more transformational (Linder 2004, Li et al. 2008). In economic terms, moving up the value chain involves executing projects where vendors get paid for solutions and not for headcount, thereby shifting the focus of engagements to the revenue rather than the cost side. Firms can also choose to shift down the value chain (Christensen et al. 2013). For instance, McKinsey & Company, a strategy consulting firm, sought to leverage its strengths at the strategic end of consulting engagements into more routinized data-oriented analytics projects using a subscription or license-feebased model (Christensen et al. 2013). Because these projects represent highly routinized work at lower billing rates, this particular initiative is effectively a move down the value chain for a highly differentiated service provider.

Movements up and down the value chain can be viewed as strategies for growth on the part of service providers. Prior research argues that growth, through such strategic moves (Meyer and Lehnerd 1997), helps the firm acquire and build on existing capabilities in new and positive ways (Su 2013) as well as serve as a deterrent to competitors (Greve 2006). However, as evidenced by the example of Infosys above, these initiatives are not without risk. As research from the strategic management literature points out, moves up and down the value chain imply deviations in the firm’s core strategy, which could have adverse implications for the firm (Porter 1980, Porter 1996). Such arguments are based on an internal logic that shifts in strategy result in internal inconsistencies between resource, processes, and actions, leading to poor performance. Thus, the advice from IT practitioners and researchers to IT services firms to move up or down the value chain to enhance their competitive position seems to contradict the theoretical arguments from the strategy literature.

There has, however, been little systematic work seeking to resolve this contradiction. Much of the evidence for success and failure of such moves is anecdotal and inconclusive. For instance, HCL, an Indian firm operating predominantly in the low-end application development and maintenance field, successfully moved into the SAP consulting market (Singal and Jain 2012). In contrast, IBM’s attempt to break into the Indian local market for application development and maintenance met with little success (Simhan 2012, Mishra 2014). There is thus a paucity of research addressing the outcomes associated with moves along the value chain, representing a significant gap in the literature on IT services.

In this paper, we take a resource-based view (RBV) of the firm perspective (Wernerfelt 1984, Dierickx and Cool 1989, Barney 1991) to address this gap by examining whether and how IT service providers can overcome these internal inconsistencies, which is necessary to successfully move along the value chain. Specifically, we examine the role of three key resources and capabilities (R&Cs) that may influence how successful a service provider may be in moving along the value chain. First, we consider the impact of the mode of movement along the value chain; the firm can choose to change its internal resource mix as a precursor to the move, or it can choose to move using its existing resource mix. By moving up or down the value chain through, for instance, acquisitions, the firm ends up changing its resource mix significantly On the other hand, organic movements may not start with a changed resource profile ex ante. Second, we consider the role of learning capabilities on the outcomes associated with the move. In certain cases, firms can invest in organizational learning processes as a precursor to moving that could mitigate some of the costs and uncertainties associated with the move (Ethiraj et al. 2005, Bapna et al. 2013). Finally, we consider the role of general-purpose technologies, and their advent, that may help increase the productivity of resources in general (Brynjolfsson and Mitchell 2017, Roos and Shroff 2017), leading to increased resource fungibility, defined as the ease with which resources can be deployed across projects of varying complexity. Although increased fungibility may be desirable, there are limits to how easily productive resources can be redeployed to alternative contexts; we consider the effects of resource fungibility in our work.

We study how these three key R&C factors—resource changes, learning capabilities, and fungibility—affect the outcomes of moves along the value chain by a focal IT services firm using an agent-based model. The use of an agent-based model allows us to simulate movements along the value chain by different types of firms with varying resource endowments under different conditions and contingencies, enabling us to examine performance variations of such moves. To this end, we first create agents (firms) associated with three distinct strategic orientations (archetypes) discussed extensively in the strategy literature—low-cost leaders (LCs), differentiators (DFs), and hybrids (Porter 1980, Dess and Davis 1984). Each firm operates in a specific part of the value chain, endowed with a set of resources that is consistent with its strategic orientation, as described in the strategy literature (Dess and Davis 1984, Campbell-Hunt 2000). Additionally, each firm takes on a portfolio of projects representing levels of complexity consistent with its location in th value chain. The LC executes projects at the lower end of complexity, with a focus on transactional projects, efficiency, and stability that can be accomplished by standard quality but well-trained resources. The DF, by contrast, operates at the other end of the spectrum, executing highly complex projects that are customized and knowledge-oriented and require flexi bility and improvisation, and consequently require higher-quality resources. Consistent with the literature, we create the hybrid midway on the continuum between the LC and DF firms. Within this simulated environment, moving along the value chain can be modeled as the outcome associated with the firm executing projects of a different complexity profile, under varying conditions of resource changes, learning, and fungibility.

Furthermore, each firm faces a set of cost and revenue implications in its marketplace that impact its performance, reflecting the firm’s supply and demand sides. Cost implications are driven by both the availability and costs related to acquiring capable and skilled resources in the marketplace (Aron et al. 2008, Langer et al. 2008, Bapna et al. 2013) as well as the ability to deploy existing resources onto projects different from their existing projects. Revenues, on the other hand, are influenced by the complexity of the project being executed, with higher complexity garnering higher revenues (higher prices), all else equal (Carmel and Tjia 2005, Abbott et al. 2012, Su 2013). Putting these pieces together allows us to answer questions relating to how the presence of learning, resource changes, or fungibility influence the potential success of a firm that attempts to move along the value chain, in the presence of competitors that already operate in the market (Ruckman et al. 2015). Contrasting different contingencies that emerge from these different R&C factors enables us to answer the broad research question we started with: Under what conditions can a service provider successfully move along the value chain? In addition, if a focal firm chooses to move along the value chain, it is possible for competing firms in the marketplace to respond with their own moves along the value chain. In some cases, these moves may be beneficial. In subsequent analyses, we explore these conditions by addressing the following research question: How should competitors respond when a focal firm moves along the value chain?

An agent-based model is particularly suitable to address this question. Simulation methods are suitable in contexts when the basic outline of theory is available but empirically validated evidence of pathways by which the theory is operationalized, including any nonlinear interactions of the central constructs, are still unclear or difficult to directly observe (Davis et al. 2007). Given our need to understand the implications of movements along the value chain in a structured manner, agent-based models provide the more reasonable means to run thought experiments about the contingent effects of moves along the value chain (Rand and Rust 2011). In addition to the paucity of theory necessary for developing testable hypotheses, empirical analyses using data from the IT services industry have significant limitations. At the outset, even the assignment of firms to a specific strategic orientation, usually through clustering methods (Miller and Friesen 1986, Kim and Lim 1988), is difficult because there is usually considerable withingroup variation on strategic dimensions that cannot be safely ignored. Additionally, specific data on the critical factors we study such as learning capabilities, resource endowments, and specific location on the value chain (representing average project complexity) is particularly hard to gather accurately. Finally, acquiring empirical project-level data over a significant period for a wide range of vendors is infeasible. By unpacking theoretical mechanisms in a datadriven manner (Davis et al. 2007), our agent-based model enables us to develop testable propositions that should be empirically validated in future research. We thus add to the growing research using agent-based models to examine issues of strategic importance within information systems (IS; Nan 2011, Oh et al. 2016, Nan and Tanriverdi 2017).

We find that in most baseline models within our simulation, the firm that moves from its niche experiences significant weakness in its market position relative to other firms occupying their own niches, providing support for the argument that firms that deviate from their pure strategies are inherently in weaker positions relative to their competition (Porter 1980). However, the simulations also show a significant dichotomy in how the presence of learning ca pabilities and resource changes influence the success of such moves. Specifically, we find that firms moving up the value chain, such as LCs, are likely to be successful only if such moves are accompanied by significant resource changes. In contrast, firms moving down the value chain, such as DFs, are likely to be successful only if such moves are accompanied by significant learning. These patterns hold for hybrids as well. We also find adverse consequences for adopting the wrong strategy. Thus, LCs and hybrids that seek to move up the value chain find that learning does not mitigate the significant loss in competitive advantage. Similarly, DFs and hybrids suffer significant adverse effects when their moves down the value chain are accompanied by resource changes only. Furthermore, we observe that in most cases, the best response to competing firms moving along the value chain is to not respond; if such moves are ill advised in general, the best competitive response is to, ironically, do nothing.

Our work makes three significant contributions to theory and practice in IS strategy, particularly with reference to IT services. First, our research addresses an important theoretical gap: When should an IT service provider contemplate moving up or down the value chain? Whereas conventional wisdom favors such moves, we draw on the resource-based view of the firm to present evidence as to when and how such a move, under varying conditions of resource changes, learning capabilities, and resource fungibility, can lead to success, particularly in the pre sence of competition. Second, we add to the body of research adopting the methodology of agent-based models to research on the IT services industry, while also highlighting the significant benefits that the methodology offers in conducting what-if experiments and generating testable propositions, in addition to formal modeling. Finally, we turn the lens of strategic orientations (Porter 1980) on IT services vendors for the first time, where prior work has studied mostly clients (Sabherwal et al. 2001, Aubert et al. 2008). For vendors within each strategic orientation, we show the potential limits to adaptability and expansion along the value chain.

## Theoretical Background The Value Chain in IT Services

The concept of moving along the value chain is rooted in the strategic management literature (Normann and Ramirez 1993, Gereffi et al. 2001, Kaplinsky and Morris 2001, Porter 2001). A firm’s value chain is composed of a system of interconnected activities. Competitive advantage over rivals is ensured if the firm can perform these activities at either a lower cost or differentiate itself in some systematic manner to command a premium price. The implications of moving up the value chain for a firm include higher margins, a greater focus on intellectual property and intangible assets, and greater market power (Porter 2001, Christensen et al. 2013). In contrast to moving up the value chain, Staats and Upton (2015) propose that firms should consider moving down the value chain by leveraging existing solutions to offer new solutions that are routinized in nature. This move would help the firm grow outside its core; execute standardized projects, which in turn provide clear visibility on returns on investment; and protect revenue and market share during downturns. Such a move would also help ward off competition that may emerge from beneath (Christensen et al. 2013).

In the IT services literature, moving up the value chain refers to providing clients with more higherorder value-added technical services that represent knowledge-oriented work and leverage the service providers’ intellectual property (Garud et al. 2006, Abbott et al. 2012, Perunovic et al. 2012, Singal and Jain 2012, Su 2013). At the lower end of the value chain, service providers tend to execute projects that are typically noncore and nonstrategic to the client, are routine and transactional in nature, and have limited margin potential. Several pathways have been identified within the IT services literature that enable firms to transition along the value chain. The most common path allows the firm to transition within the bounds of an existing client relationship, where higher-order work is offered to the service provider first (Levina and Ross 2003), as observed with Infosys Technologies (Garud et al. 2006). A second path, practiced by Chinese vendors, refers to moving along the value chain by adopting an internationalization strategy (Su 2013). A third strategy is through the process of acquisitions, that is, acquiring third-party firms operating higher in the value chain (Niosi and Tschang 2009). Finally, providers can transition organically by building resources that are different from those currently in the firm and creating business units to target new markets along the value chain, essentially acting like new entrants into a different location on the value chain (Chellappa and Saraf 2010, Lee et al. 2014). These strategies, however, involve significant challenges for the providers arising out of their existing strategic orientation.

## Challenges in Moving Along the Value Chain

The strategy literature has long recognized that firms within an industry are likely to differ in terms of the strategic choices they make and the means employed to achieve their business goals. These differences are captured through the concept of strategic orientation (archetypes), defined as the set of processes, resources, and technologies the firm adopts, the markets it chooses to participate in, and the products and services it develops (Ginsberg and Venkatraman 1985). Strategic orientation also involves a cognitive component, driven by the dominant logic held by upper management of the firm. Strategic orientation is thus a critical attribute of the firm that drives how managers conceptualize the business and make critical resource allocation decisions (Prahalad and Bettis 1986).

Firms that are consistent in their strategic orientation (i.e., their choices of technologies, resources, processes, and markets are in consonance with this un derlying strategic orientation) will show better performance (Miles and Snow 1996) because this fit between all activities is essential to achieving competitive advantage (Porter 1996). Thus, low-cost leadership and differentiation are identified as two broad strategic orientations that can be used to cat egorize most firms in any industry (Porter 1996). The organizational elements necessary for the effec tive pursuit of a low-cost strategy are distinctly different and mutually exclusive from those necessary to pursue a differentiation strategy. To the extent that resources and capabilities within the firm are coupled together coherently and consistently, the firm responds efficiently and effectively to its market needs (Weick 1979). However, these very characteristics— consistency between activities and fit between resources, processes, and actions—can also hinder movements along the value chain. Moving along the value chain necessitate activities that conflict with and contradict the firm’s strategic orientation. A lowcost firm seeking to appeal to a differentiated market needs to engage in activities such as branding, customization, and exclusivity that conflict with an existing focus on standardization and economies of scale. Similarly, a differentiated firm can lose its exclusive image in trying to reach a lower segment of the value chain. Being everything to everybody results in being stuck in the middle (Porter 1996). The empirical evidence provides some support for the notion that firms pursuing pure strategies (low cost and differentiation) exhibit higher performance on average, based on a meta analysis of two decades of research (Campbell-Hunt 2000). The role of strategic trade-offs in affecting performance receives support as well, showing that pure strategy firms always outperform hybrids (Thornhill and White 2007), even in some platform-based industries (Cennamo and Santalo 2013). However, the benefits of being stuck in the middle also exist, wherein the “all-rounder” strategy (Campbell-Hunt 2000, p. 149), with elements of low cost and differentiation, might work under some contingencies (Kim and Mauborgne 2005). Little prior research has, however, addressed the performance implications of firms that deviate from pure strategy orientations, indicating the need to better understand when such deviations may be successful.

Examining this question in the IT services literature assumes increased importance because vendors have largely been assumed to be homogeneous. Most vendors have been assumed to pursue similar objectives such as a desire to move up the value chain (Aron et al. 2008), acquire certification (Gopal and Gao 2009), and form deeper relationships with clients (Ethiraj et al. 2005, Ravindran et al. 2015) with similar constraints and challenges. Assuming that all firms have similar strategies and competitive positions has led researchers and practitioners to treat the firm as a “black box.” Adopting a strategic orientation perspective, in contrast, recognizes the heterogeneity in IT services firms as they pursue different strategies with different resources endowments and capabilities tailored to fit the specific needs of their customers. It suggests that different firms have different contingencies and trade-offs arising from their internal configurations.

This becomes important because any movement across the value chain will involve reconfiguring the existing resource mix, either by acquiring the resources from the external environment or by training existing resources (Normann and Ramirez 1993, Bapna et al. 2013). Alternatively, using the existing resource mix exposes the firm to inefficiencies and high opportunity costs (Porter 1980). In the absence of any competition, these inefficiencies may not be as critical. However, this is unlikely because, across levels of the IT services market, there exists significant competition (Ruckman et al. 2015). As clients increasingly choose to multisource, that is, engage multiple providers for their diverse IT and business services (Levina and Su 2008, Bapna et al. 2010), the presence of multiple vendors within the same client have, if anything, increased the salience of competition in the industry. For firms moving along the value chain, the presence of competitors enacting their own strategic orientations thus remains a significant obstacle. Thus, different strategic orientations in the market lead to variations in not only how each firm can move along the value chain, but also in the consequences associated with such moves.

## Some Anecdotal Evidence

There has been little empirical research on these effects of move along the value chain in the academic literature. In addition, there has been little emphasis on identifying the factors that could be important in influencing the success of these moves forcing us to examine the practitioner press to identify some factors. We identified several cases where firms in the IT or services industries have attempted to move up or down the value chain (Table A1 in the online appendix). In many instances, firms have attempted to move up the value chain through organic means, such as Hexaware Technologies in India, which tried to move its enterprise resource planning consulting practice up the value chain into an offshore development center. Alternatively, high-end providers of consulting services have moved down into analytics services, such as BCG’s move into BCG Analytics. Although an exhaustive review of the cases shown in Table A1 in the online appendix is not possible given space restrictions, we can extract specific insights that help characterize the problem faced by practitioners.

First, it is evident that firms enacting different strategic orientations do try to move both up and down the value chain as a way to enhance their market appeal Thus, both a differentiated firm like BCG and a relatively low-cost provider like HCL Technologies seem to see value in such movements. Second, there are different approaches that are espoused, ranging from acquiring (divesting) firms that occupy a specific location on the value chain to organically expanding service lines. In some cases, firms actually change their hiring processes and hire more highly trained or differently trained personnel (BCG, McKinsey), whereas in other cases, no changes in hiring are noted (Evalueserve). It is not clear how and why these strategies were chosen, simply based on available anecdotal evidence. Third, firms varied in terms of how prepared and organized they were before the attempted move. Some firms had deep expertise in their subject domain, which may have helped them deal with the risks of movement (Evalueserve), whereas others are generalist firms (Cognizant, Deloitte). Finally, we observe that some of these moves were considered successful, whereas others were not. There is no consistent evidence that either moving up or moving down is associated with improvements in firm performance. From the information available, it is not possible to explain these outcomes, resulting in a gap in the research that we seek to address. Indeed, many of the underlying theoretical concepts we address here (strategic orientations, resource acquisitions, learning, resource changes) are imperfectly observed, if at all, indicating that an empirically driven research methodology to address our research questions is unlikely to provide enough visibility to identify causal relationships. However, the cases allow us to identify specific resources and capabilities that may help (or hinder) firms moving along the value chain.

## R&C Factors In<sup>fl</sup>uencing Outcomes of Moves Along the Value Chain

We use the RBV of the firm perspective to identify critical resources and capabilities that could affect the outcome of such moves by IT service firms along the value chain. The RBV explains firm performance by looking internally at a firm’s R&C configurations. A fundamental tenet of the RBV is that unique resources and capabilities, and the manner in which they are deployed, leads to competitive advantage (Wernerfelt 1984, Barney 1991). Specifically, integrating and creating fit between the different capabilities over time provides the firm with a competitive advantage that is likely to be sustainable. Viewed this way, competitive advantage depends on both the “stock” of resources as well as an accumulating “flow” concept, where experience in combining and deploying resource stocks leads to building up capabilities over time. However, R&C configurations that are produced without creating the necessary fit with other R&Cs within the same firm adversely impact competitive advantage. Thus, the RBV provides clear a theoretical underpinning for why deviations from a clear strategic orientation may impact firm performance negatively. Under this larger framework of the RBV, we focus here on three key R&C factors based on the above cases and prior work in the IT services literature (Levina and Ross 2003, Ruckman et al. 2015). They circumscribe a set of resources and capabilities that the focal IT services firm may invest in strategically, as it plans to move along the value chain. In our model, we are agnostic about whether these R&C factors emerge endogenously through the firm’s agency or whether they are imposed exogenously—we consider only their effects on firm performance. The three factors we consider are the presence of resource changes, learning capability, and resource fungibility.

Resource Changes. The theory of strategic orientation presumes that the firm’s resources are a close and congruent fit with the firm’s processes and target market, thereby ensuring consistency (Ginsberg and Venkatraman 1985). However, as a firm chooses to move up or down the value chain, the firm has two choices with respect to its resources. First, the firm could choose to retain its current resource mix when extending its services into the adjacent market (Ruckman et al. 2015). In this contingency, the firm’s resource costs do not change in the short run, although in the long run, the possible mismatches between resources and newly realized demand in the firm’s new position on the value chain may detract from any value generated (Garud et al. 2006). In fact, in many cases where service providers choose to move up the value chain within the same client relationship, no new resources are marshaled in the short run (Levina and Ross 2003, Su 2013). However, the firm can choose to invest considerably in retraining its existing resources to meet the demands of the new customer base (Normann and Ramirez 1993), which is arguably cheaper than having to acquire new resources (Langer et al. 2008). Thus, one possible strategy to move into adjacent markets on the value chain without incurring excessive costs is to retain the same resources and shift only the nature of the firm’s demand side.

Alternatively, it is arguable that a successful move along the value chain requires a new resource mix that is consistent with the new customer profile facing the firm (Weick 1979, Abbott et al. 2012). Therefore, the firm may take on new resources externally from the market by hiring the relevant resource personnel (Bapna et al. 2013). Because the availability of such resources within the IT services industry is generally low (Aron et al. 2008), firms may also choose to gain them through acquisitions (Levina and Su 2008, Chatterjee 2017). Acquired firms located at a different point of the value chain provide the acquirer with a new resource mix, by combining existing resources with the newly acquired resources. Many of the firms shown in Table A1 in the online appendix do seem to make these resource investments, either through training or acquisitions, before moving up or down the value chain. Of course, this new resource mix may have implications for the focal firm’s performance in its home market as well, given the new levels of inconsistency between the firm’s resources and its home market needs. It is thus not clear from the literature whether moves along the value chain should always be accompanied, ex ante, by changing the resources of the firm, given the costs and benefits outlined above.

Learning Capability. Firms vary in their abilities to absorb new information and learn (Cohen and Levinthal 1990). A firm’s learning capability has been linked to prior experience and capabilities (Darr et al. 1995, Argote and Miron-Spektor 2011), deep technological expertise (Narasimhan et al. 2006), and absorptive capacity (Zahra and George 2002). Absorptive capacity enables the firm to learn to recognize new information or opportunities, assimilate this information, and apply this learning strategically through new technological innovations or processes that can shift the bases of competitive advantages (Lane et al. 2006).

Within the IT services context, the impact of learning from services alliances has been mostly recognized on the client side (Weigelt 2009, Mani and Barua 2015). More recently, researchers focusing on the vendor or service provider side have argued that certain firms are inherently better able to learn and improve their productivity by virtue of their service alliances as well. Alcacer and Oxley (2014) refer to this as “learning by supplying,” wherein service providers who have existing links to client firms with deep technological or domain expertise are likely to learn more from these alliances. Similarly, Ethiraj et al. (2005) show that service providers with repeated interactions with specific sets of clients are likely to learn from these interactions and improve their own capabilities. Indeed, learning from customers can occur not only at the individual level but also at the organizationa level, as shown by Clark et al. (2013) in the context of radiological services. Thus, one can differentiate between providers with high learning capability and those that lack this capability. Extrapolating to the present context, moving along the value chain necessitates new relationships with clients, newer technological issues, and unfamiliar project management and business challenges (Abbott et al., 2012), all of which can lead to failure of such moves. Learning capabilities enable the firm to become better and better at recognizing new opportunities and challenges, accumulate new stocks of resources to address such opportunities, and deploy this accumulated stock of resources to build new products and services in new markets. Service providers with the capacity for learning are thus likely to be able to better manage the organizational and market turbulence that may occur from moving along the value chain, relative to those that do not have the same capabilities (Mills and Friesen 1992). Learning arising out of experience, deep expertise, or technological innovations enables them to recover faster from these challenges and reach a steady state (Cohen and Levinthal 1990), enhancing the odds of success.

Resource Fungibility. The IT services industry relies heavily on skilled and competent human resources (Bapna et al. 2013). The more competent and skilled these resources are, the more service providers benefit from their productivity, and the greater the value created in client engagements (Langer et al. 2008). However, it is not always easy to access such highly productive resources, especially in emerging service areas like big data and cloud computing (Tambe 2014, Loebbecke and Picot 2015). Thus, most providers are forced to be creative with a resource mix characterized by variations in resource productivity, using disciplined processes as a way to ensure acceptable performance (Jalote 2000). However, technological progress and the introduction of generalpurpose technologies can help address this by in creasing the productivity of all resources.

Early work in the business value of IT literature argued that one of the ways in which the adoption of new IT has helped firms is through improved employee productivity (Bresnahan et al. 2002). Even though early benefits may have accrued from automating mundane and repetitive tasks, the true benefit kicked in when technology was put to use in more complicated and higher-order work, thereby en hancing the productivity of the workforce within the firm (Brynjolfsson and Hitt 2000). More recently, scholars have argued that the use of analytics and IT creates significant complementarities, with implications for firm performance (Aral et al. 2012), again suggesting that technological progress can help enhance the productivity of all resources within an organization. The current trend of progress in areas like machine learning, deep learning, and artificial intelligence technologies offers very similar benefits to organizations, wherein their widespread use can enhance the productivity of employees in their given tasks (Brynjolfsson and Mitchell 2017, Roos and Shroff 2017), as well as enable employees to expand the locus of work to more complex and knowledgeoriented work that may have been out of their capabilities in the past (Jordan and Mitchell 2015). Indeed, in fields like radiology, the use of technology in identifying diseases and the productivity improvements that this may bring about are already considered inevitable (Jha and Topol 2016).

In our context, we consider the role of generalpurpose information technologies as an instrument for increasing the productivity of all resources within the IT services industry. As these technologies are widely adopted, it is possible that the limitations associated with lesser trained or less capable personnel may be lifted. In other words, the productivity of resources assigned to complex engagements may be enhanced by complementary investments in these general-purpose technologies. Effectively, this leads to increased resource fungibility; that is, the same resources can be utilized in different projects and assignments with little or no reduction in productivity. Because these are typically general-purpose technol ogies in the field, we assume that the benefits of resource fungibility are likely to be available to all competing firms in the marketplace. Under conditions of high fungibility, movements along the value chain may be easier because resources can be deployed to more or less complex work easily.

In sum, our theoretical focus from the RBV perspective identifies the potential role of resource configurations and internal processes (in the form of resource mix, learning, and fungibility) in influencing performance outcomes when firms seek to move along the value chain. Because different firms have different internal configurations arising from their strategic orientations, we expect differences in how firms move along the value chain and the consequences associated with such moves. In adopting this perspective, we deviate from an alternative theoretical treatment of similar questions rooted in industrial organization (IO) that examines how industry structure may determine optimal firm-level decisions and, therefore, firm performance (Porter 1981, Fudenberg and Tirole 1989). With its focus on industry-level analysis, IO seeks to explain industry-level performance, drawing on the structure–conduct–performance paradigm (Porter 1981). Because firms are assumed to be largely homogeneous, and by extension, firm conduct is also assumed to be similar, researchers have focused on the relationship between industry structure and performance (Porter 1981, Mahoney and Pandian 1992). In contrast, our approach recognizes that firms are heterogeneous. Not only do firms differ in their strategies and resource endowments, the nature of competition for each firm is also different because it is a function of the interaction of both its own strategic orientation and the competitor’s strategic orientation. The attention to firm heterogeneity, even within the same industry subclass, and its interactions with competitors is addressed more directly in the RBV, which is hence the lens we adopt here. Using this firm-level strategy perspective allows us to understand whether and how firms should move along the value chain by examining each firm’s strategy relative to its specific competitive environment, leading to testable hypotheses or actionable implications. We address this gap in the literature through an agent-based model that we describe in detail next.

## The Agent-Based Model

## Conceptual Model—Context and Approach

In this section, we briefly outline the approach that we take to operationalize the agent-based model to address our research question. The objective here is to outline the simulation strategy and provide an overall summary of the model. We provide a detailed description of the actual parameter settings and the execution of the simulation runs in Online Appendix B.

Recall that our objective is to assess how IT services firms enacting different strategic orientations may choose to move along the value chain and the outcomes associated with these moves. Therefore, we start by simulating firms (agents) that enact three strategic orientations, operating in different parts of the value chain—low-cost leader, differentiator, and hybrid firms. LCs in this conceptualization provide routinized and codifiable services such as call centers, software, and telecom facility maintenance services, typically in cost-plus contractual scenarios (Gopal et al. 2003) with relatively low risk (Teng et al. 1995). Internally, the LC focuses on strong process-based systems, with limited discretion needed for the average employee. The firm typically stresses long training programs, significant socialization of employees into the firm’s processes, and a traditional compensation system. Hiring is based on straightforward skills and easily established base credentials; emphasis is laid on developing personnel whose career prospects depend on rigorously following established standards and practices (Jalote 2000) Control systems are based on feedback processes (Ebben and Johnson 2005), which enhance efficiency, scale, and cost reduction.

The DF, at the other end of the continuum, is innovative and seeks competitive advantage by offering new products and services, customized to fit customer preferences, while charging premium prices. Service providers in financial analysis services, software development services, and research and development (R&D) services are likely to be DFs because these need high levels of customization and fit (Grimpe and Kaiser 2010). A DF hires personnel for their innovativeness, adaptability, and problemsolving capabilities, because the elements of the working environment are incompletely specified. Control systems include a mixture of feed-forward and feedback systems designed for flexible evaluations and quick responses.

Within this continuum, we identify a third archetype located between the LC and DF: the hybrid. Hybrids are entrepreneurial and adopt elements of the LC and the DF to carve out market space for themselves.

## Parameter Settings—Overview

Each firm representing a strategic archetype is characterized by a set of parameters that capture the firm’s supply and demand sides, as well as its internal processes. The parameter settings are chosen so as to represent the ideal type of the strategic archetype, rather than strict optimality conditions. In other words, the parameter settings are chosen to reflect how managers within these firms are likely to operate, drawing from prior work in IS and strategy. To describe each archetype firm, we choose a project metaphor to describe the firm and its services, that is, the firm in the agent-based model executes a portfolio of projects at any point in time (Garud et al. 2006). In addition to IT services, project-based firms are reflective of a number of different industries, such as consulting, R&D, and product development (Hobday et al. 2005), and give us some grounding for the parameter settings and validation of the model, while allowing findings to be generalized beyond one particular firm or industry. The model assumes that new projects continuously enter the portfolio, whereas completed projects exit the portfolio. We assume no inventory and that revenue accrues to the firm only on project completion, characteristics that are consistent with the IT services industry in general (Carmel and Tjia 2005).

We assume that service providers operate in a simple, vertically differentiated market niche and are price takers in their specific market niche. We model this setting to eliminate the influence of other factors that may affect outcomes in horizontally differentiated markets such as product variety, innovation, quality, and regulation. By definition, the LC operates in the lower end of the vertically differentiated marketplace, executing routine, standard projects, whereas the DF operates in the higher end of the same marketplace, executing complex projects that are nonroutinized and of high quality (HQ). We view these offerings on a continuum, with standard projects at one end of the continuum and projects of increasing complexity higher up on the continuum. Because complex projects are, by definition, high-value, highmargin offerings, LCs would prefer to move up the value chain, offering projects that are more complex than their current offerings. Similarly, DFs may consider moving down the value chain and competing in the low-complexity market. The hybrid has the option of moving up or down the value chain, based on its midpoint location.

On the supply side, archetype firms are differentiated on the extent to which they possess average or HQ resources, captured by the Fraction-HQ-Resources parameter. High-quality resources are typically more skilled and experienced or encapsulate sophisticated and capable technologies that lend themselves to problem definition, designing creative solutions, and efficient implementation (Bapna et al. 2013). In contrast, average resources are meant to accomplish relatively simpler tasks efficiently. In the interest of parsimony, we assume that all firms have the same number of resources (25 units) but that the proportion of HQ resources varies. Thus, the LC firm is assigned a parameter value of 0.1 (10% HQ resources), whereas the DF firm is assigned a value of 0.9 (90% HQ resources). The hybrid is set at 0.5; that is, half the resources are HQ.

On the demand side, the firm’s project portfolio is characterized by the Fraction-Complex-Projects parameter. As described above, we assume that there are two types of projects—standard and complex projects.

Complex projects represent greater complexity and higher output quality. They necessitate high-quality resources and consequently provide higher value to the firm. The firm’s decision to move up or down the value chain is thus reflected in the proportion of complex and standard projects executed. The LC firm executes fewer complex projects (0.1, 10% complex projects), whereas the DF executes the highest proportion of these projects (0.9, 90% complex projects). Hybrids are set at 0.5.

Finally, we model the internal processes of the firms based on their task environment. Theory argues that LC firms operate in standardized and efficient task environments with little or no volatility (Miles and Snow 1984, Miller 1992). In contrast, DFs operate in environments with high volatility and variance Hybrids have a set of processes to balance out the contrasting needs for maintaining stability, while also operating in an uncertain environment. We capture these differences using three parameters, consistent with prior work in agent-based modeling (Nan and Tanriverdi 2017) and IT services outsourcing (Levina and Ross 2003). First, we vary the rate at which new projects enter the firm’s portfolio, using the New-Project-Entry-Rate parameter, representing the days between project additions. LC firms have predictable entry rates, whereas DF firms are highly variable, with the hybrid located in between. Furthermore, we vary the size of projects entering the portfolio using the Project-Size parameter; LC firms have smaller variance in terms of project size, whereas the DF varies widely, capturing the volatility inherent in the archetype. Again, the hybrid is located in between. Finally, we model volatility in internal processes through the concept of interruptions (Jett and Georg 2003). Interruptions disrupt progress on projects and result from unforeseen factors such as changes in resource availability, changes in customer specification or environments, and technical failures, among others (Perlow 1999). We model such random interruptions using the parameter Interruption-Rate. All projects in a DF firm stand a 5% chance of being interrupted, whereas projects within a hybrid firm are interrupted at a rate of 3% and LCs are interrupted at 1%. The effect of interruptions is modeled in person days lost on the project, necessitating additional work. This is set in steady state at 0.1; that is, the proportion of incomplete work outstanding on the project is increased by 10% for the interrupted project. Table 1 details the underlying parameter settings for the three firm strategies.

Our interest is in evaluating the outcomes associated with moves up and down the value chain. We thus model moving up the value chain as executing a higher proportion of complex projects, that is, an increase in Fraction-Complex-Projects compared with the base value. Similarly, moving down the value chain implies executing a higher proportion of standardized projects, indicated by a decrease in Fraction-Complex-Projects compared with the base value. Thus, for an LC, the short-run move up the value chain is modeled by increasing Fraction-Complex-Projects from 0.1 to 0.3. Conversely, the DF’s move down the value chain is captured by decreasing Fraction-Complex-Projects from 0.9 to 0.7. The hybrid can move up or down by adjusting Fraction-Complex-Projects across the range of possible values; we restrict these moves to one step (0.3 or 0.7) in our analysis. Because moving is generally considered disruptive to the firm (Lee et al. 2014), the moving firm experiences a higher penalty from interruptions, relative to the steady state of 10% discussed above. Therefore, when a firm moves up or down the value chain, the new interruption penalty is set to 20% of outstanding effort on the interrupted project, regardless of the archetype. Only the moving firm has a higher interruption penalty; other firms retain the 10% penalty because they experience no change in their status.

## Modeling Resource Changes, Learning, and Resource Fungibility

We consider the effects of three internal factors on the efficacy of moves along the value chain—resource changes, learning, and resource fungibility. To model resource changes, we allow the moving firm to change its resource profile. Therefore, for the LC moving up the value chain, we allow the Fraction-HQ-Resources parameter to shift to 0.3, representing a greater number of high-quality resources. Similarly, for the DF moving down, we set the Fraction-HQ-Resources to 0.7. The parameter is set at 0.3 and 0.7, respectively, for the hybrid moving down and up the value chain.

With respect to learning, the effects are typically observed in improvements in efficiency or productivity (Clark et al. 2013). In our context, we model learning by the speed with which the moving firm manages penalties arising from interruptions in its portfolio. We argue that learning manifests here by managing the new portfolio better, dealing with interruptions quickly, and managing resource scarcity more efficiently (Abbott et al. 2012). These capabilities may not influence the interruption rate, but will help the firm recover faster from interruptions. In our model, the learner firm starts at the interruption penalty of 20% but, by virtue of learning, ends at a penalty of 5%,<sup>1</sup> representing even better performance than the steady-state rate of 10%.

Finally, with respect to resource fungibility, it is clearly preferable to assign HQ resources to complex projects. However, this is not always possible. In such cases, average resources may not deliver the same levels of productivity when assigned to complex projects as they would on standard projects. We capture these effects in the model. First, we consider perfect fungibility, that is, when there is no cost to deploying average resources on complex projects, assuming that average resources have access to technology that can help them manage more challenging work on complex projects. We capture this using the Average-Resource-Penalty parameter set at 1.0. However, when this is not the case, we alternatively set this parameter to 0.7, imposing a cost on deploying average resources on complex projects.<sup>2</sup> In this setting, average resources deployed on complex projects only provide 70% (0.7) of the productivity they provide on standard projects, building on prior work studying human capital and performance in the IT services industry (Bapna et al. 2013). In either case, HQ resources are more productive than average resources; thus, HQ resources provide 125% productivity on standard projects and 100% productivity on complex projects, as shown in Table 2.

Having created these parameter settings, we execute each run of the simulation for 500 time steps (“days”) of activity on the portfolio of projects, covering roughly two “years” (Jalote 2000). In each set of parameter settings, we ran the simulation 100 times to generate a distribution of results and then analyzed the mean values across 100 runs for each set of settings, as is common in simulation-based research (Davis et al. 2007, Rand and Rust 2011, Nan and Tanriverdi 2017). At the end of each run, project- and portfolio-level data were recorded for each firm archetype. Specifically, the data collected included the total projects completed within each run, broken down by the number of complex and standard projects, as well as total billable effort (completed projects only) on complex and standard projects. In addition, the average schedule slippage across projects in each run, defined as the ratio of anticipated completion time when the project enters the portfolio (based on the available resources) to actual completion time, was recorded. Finally, the resource utilization across each run was noted. These were used to ensure the validity of the agent-based model, described next.

## Testing for Face Validity of the Model

An important step is to verify and validate that the model adheres reasonably to the real-world phenomenon and underlying theoretical logic (Davis et al. 2007). We conducted verification (comparison of the implemented model to a conceptual model) of the model through comprehensive documentation, programmatic testing, and test cases (Rand and Rust 2011), available upon request. The key metric for evaluating the validity of our parameters and model should be based on whether the specified parameters and model clearly differentiate between strategic archetypes in a consistent manner. Within each fungibility setting, we see that LCs complete, on average, more projects (62.84, 64.72) compared with hybrids (54.67, 60.93) and DFs (40.72, 42.94). Perfect fungibility leads to more projects completed across all archetypes, as expected because average resources are as productive as HQ resources on complex projects. Also, LCs complete a larger proportion of standardized projects, compared with hybrids and DFs. As expected, DFs complete more complex projects than the LC and hybrids. The LC moving up the value chain and operating in a more volatile and turbulent task environment should lead to higher schedule slippage and lower resource utilization. Starting with the cost side, we first create a baseline by assuming that each time step of resource effort by both average and HQ resource costs one unit.<sup>3</sup> Because we start with 25 resources and each run extends to 500 time periods, the baseline cost for all firms, regardless of its strategic orientation, is 12,500 units. We then vary the cost of HQ resources from 1.1 times that of the average resource to 2.1 times the average resource cost; that is, the cost premium associated with HQ resources varies from 10% to 110% above the cost of an average resource in 10% increments, providing 10 specific scenarios that may apply to a service provider. We refer to this as the high-quality resource cost premium because it drives the total cost incurred by a firm as a function of the proportion of HQ resources it possesses (Fraction-HQ-Resources) and the specific cost premium. This cost faced by each orientation in a run is fixed, given the parameter settings, and is incurred whether the firm completes projects or not. This parameter captures the financial cost of moving along the value chain for providers with a specific strategic orientation (Abbott et al. 2012, Su 2013).

On the revenue side, similarly, we create a baseline by assuming that both standard and complex projects result in revenue of one unit per completed effort; that is, complex projects are priced the same as standard projects. We then vary the prices that may be charged by the firm for complex projects, relative to standard projects, resulting in higher total revenues. We consider revenue per unit effort resulting from complex projects at 1.2 times that from standard projects (20% premium) to 3.2 times that from standard projects, in increments of 0.2. These variations give us 10 scenarios, each representing varying total revenues, for the same proportion of complex versus standard projects. We term the premium factor the complexity price premium, to capture the logic that complex projects reflect project portfolios positioned further up the value chain. In other words, revenue generated on a complex project, relative to a standard project, reflects the complexity and required quality of that project. Thus, a firm’s revenue per run is driven by three parameters—the proportion of standard proj ects completed, the proportion of complex projects completed, and the complexity price premium. Using the variations on cost and revenue models described here, we compute portfolio profitability for each orientation under different parameters.

We then compare profitability across firms enacting varying strategic orientations and generate heat maps (Figures 1–8 and A2–A5 in the online appendix) depicting relative firm profitability for different scenarios. A heat map shows the “map” of where firms enacting different orientations are dominant in twodimensional marketspace, with the high-quality resource cost premium on the x-axis and complexity price premium on the y-axis. The specific area in the map is shaded differently based on which strategic orientation has the highest profitability in that particular comparative scenario. The axes can be viewed as conditionalities—firms are likely to have a competitive advantage only if they command a particular leve of price premium or if the cost of HQ resources is at a certain level relative to average resources. Thus, the maps indicate regions where a firm enacting a strategic orientation can, or cannot, dominate the competitors. Comparing across heat maps as a firm moves along the value chain identifies how profitability changes for a firm enacting a strategic orientation, relative to its competitors.

## Results

In the interest of clarity, the simulated heat maps are presented in a staged manner. We first consider the LC, to examine the conditions under which moving up the value chain yields a positive outcome. In the first stage, we consider these outcomes when only two types of firms exist in the market—the DF and the LC. We examine whether the LC benefits in its move up the value chain under three conditions—resource fungibility, learning, and resource change (and their interactions). In the second stage, we introduce the hybrid into the market and analogously study the implications of movement along the value chain for the LC, effectively a three-firm analysis. We repeat the analysis for the DF and hybrid subsequently. Finally, we explore the implications of competitive responses: How should firms respond to such moves by their competitors? We limit this part of the analysis to the two-firm scenario in the interest of space. Examining the effectiveness of these firms in moving along the value chain, along these different conditions helps develop theoretical propositions translating to manageria insights as well as testable empirical hypotheses.

As a general rule, we find that firms are worse off when they move along the value chain. We formalize these results below:

Proposition 5b. Differentiated IT service firms can maintain or limit the negative consequences to their position in the market when they respond to moves up the value chain by low-cost firms by either not responding or by responding by moving down the value chain accompanied by significant learning capabilities.

## Discussion and Conclusion

Should IT service providers move along the value chain, in search of greater competitive advantage? Many experts in the field argue that moving up is an important pathway that service providers need to take to survive and thrive (Abbott et al. 2012). Using a contrarian perspective, Staats and Upton (2015) argue that service providers should actively consider moving down the value chain to prevent encroachment on their markets from below. Both perspectives relate to the larger question we address in this paper: Under what conditions can IT service providers move up or down the value chain successfully? We utilize an agent-based model to develop clear and actionabl insights to address this question. Although moving along the value chain is generally risky, we identify specific conditions under which such moves may b favorable to firms. We find that firms moving up the value chain (such as LCs) are likely to be successful only if such moves are accompanied by significant changes in the resource mix that the firm possesses. In contrast, firms moving down the value chain (like DFs) are likely to be successful only if such moves are accompanied by significant learning capabilities. We find this pattern holds for hybrids as well. Resource fungibility favors firms that compete in the complex end of the value chain. Importantly, we find that moves along the value chain sans firm-specific changes almost inevitably lead to loss of competitive space. Thus, when a firm chooses to move along the value chain, the best response from competing firms enacting other ar chetypes is often to do nothing; our analysis of competitive responses shows that such moves may often make conditions worse. In summary, we show that th outcomes from moves along the value chain are complex interactions of three factors—resource acquisi tion, learning capabilities, and strategic orientation— moderated by a fourth, resource fungibility.

It is worth considering explanations for the intriguing dichotomy in our results that appear consistent with prior arguments from the literature (Narasimhan et al. 2006). Firms (LCs) at the low end of the value chain have experience and knowledge of a standardized project environment, based on their strategic niche. Process management and process discipline, rather than knowledge-oriented skills, are more important in such an environment. Moving up the value chain exposes them to customer domains where knowledge-oriented skills, in addition to individual human capital, are critical for competitive advantage. In the absence of such capabilities internally, acquiring high-quality resources becomes necessary to compete effectively. This is a possible explanation for why we find LCs are more successful only when they move up the value chain by acquiring resources. However, acquiring these knowledge-oriented skills in the absence of prior experience in such domains is hard, and unlikely to occur, consistent with Cohen and Levinthal’s (1990) arguments. Thus, LCs moving up the value chain with learning face this interesting paradox—they need learning but are also in an unfavorable position to acquire it, which explains why moving up the value chain with learning alone is likely to have adverse effects. By contrast, DFs already have the knowledge skills to operate in multiple domains, by virtue of their resources and processes built for volatility. Adapting that knowledge to the lower end of the value chain by being adaptive (through learning capabilities) is actually more feasible (Cohen and Levinthal 1990, Mills and Friesen 1992). Acquiring average quality resources, on the other hand, actually detracts from their bases of competitive advantage because these resources are still a mismatch for the majority of projects completed by the DF, thus explaining our results. More focused empirical testing of these arguments is essential to further our understanding of these dynamics. Our simulation mode presents a framework incorporating elements that are not always visible in the field (learning, project complexity) with those that are discernible (resource changes), representing opportunities for further theoretical and empirical work in this area.

Although our focus has been on consequences for the focal firm, we also highlight the potential for opening up niches for hybrid firms or the competition when a provider chooses to move along the value chain. Indeed, such moves increase the odds that market niches open up not just for existing competitors, but also for new competitors such as the hybrids we model. Through such potentially ill-conceived moves, for instance, we see that the DF not only fails at the newer market, but also ends up ceding its original market to the competition, particularly when it moves down with a significant resource change. Intriguingly, our model also points to how the pursuit of competitive advantage aimed against existing competitors, by way of aggressive moves along the value chain, may actually provide opportunities for the entry of new competitors. Apart from highlighting market instability, our study thus emphasizes the need for firms to carefully assess the implications of their competitive actions, not just in terms of its impact on existing competitors but also how it might incentivize potential new entrants.

In summary, our work here raises questions about the value of the advice to move along the value chain in search of newer markets. The approach we adopt helps address some of these questions ex ante by asking a series of what-if questions to determine the advisability of such moves. The pressure to increase revenues is often cited as a valid reason to move along the value chain (Chari 2011); our perspective is that these revenue projections often come with unrealistic assumptions about changes in resource profiles, learning capabilities, fungibility, and competitor actions. Rather, we caution managers in such firms to first evaluate their strategic orientation, examine changing their resource profiles to suit the target mar kets, evaluate the importance of learning, and, finally, evaluate the fungibility of resources and their costs. Managers should keep in mind that resources and capabilities are neither perfectly fungible nor completely rigid, which influences the success of moves along the value chain. Finally, managers are wel advised to consider an exit strategy if the anticipated benefits from such moves into adjacent markets do not match reality. Indeed, in many cases where such moves are built around acquisitions, there is no easy exit strategy. Although we do not model the actual cost of acquisitions, these are consistent with the notion of moving with resource changes, and it is easy to envision conditions where such moves can backfire.

Within agent-based modeling, we constructed and validated our model using the guidelines discussed in the literature (Rand and Rust 2011), including an expansive set of robustness tests shown in Table 6. The appeal of agent-based modeling is based on the notion that complex systems with many interacting components can be modeled effectively, unlike for mal modeling or research based on archival data (Onik et al. 2017). Within the agent-based methodology (Marchi and Page 2014), there are several ways to further enrich our model for future research. First, we define the abstract concepts of firms and projects using a specified set of parameters, raising questions of the extent to which these choices may influence the results that were obtained. In order to establish validity, as described in the literature, we ran our models using a range of values for the key parameters in our model. Across a range of different settings for resource fungibility, resource costs, learning capabilities, strategic orientation definitions, run times, and revenues, we see consistent patterns. Nonetheless, additional mechanisms could be explored. Furthermore, our heat maps are based on static profitability comparisons between different firms rather than dynamic comparisons with multiple firms at the same time. We conduct some simple analyses of strategic responses, but a fuller treatment of this issue is beyond the scope of our work here. Devising appropriate best-response functions to parameterize dynamic responses between firms concurrently requires much more theoretical clarity and complexity, which we leave for future research. We opt here instead for a parsimonious modeling approach, allowing us to postulate novel and important propositions that can be subjected to empirical testing. Finally, we focus on resources, learning, and competition in this model; there are other R&Cs at play here, such as brand, reputation, and R&D, which we leave to future work.

The work we present here is essentially a theoretically driven model of firm behavior and, to that extent, does require more focused empirical testing to validate the propositions we derive. One of the benefits of agent-based modeling is to unpack theoretical mechanisms in a data-driven manner that can then lead to more systematic hypothesis testing (Davis et al. 2007), much in the spirit of formal modeling (Wan and Wu 2016). In that spirit, we believe that considerable research remains to be conducted in understanding how moves along the value chain, or movements into adjacent markets by incumbents, may lead to success using empirical data from the field (Abbott et al. 2012). These can be conducted through detailed case studies of firms that have made such transitions under different circumstances, such as recent work on Chinese service providers by Su (2013). Alternatively, a more quantitative approach at the level of the firm or business unit can be undertaken across years to understand how expansions in product or service lines may lead to success (Ruckman et al. 2015). Given the size of the IT services market and the changing dynamics in this market regarding multisourcing, it is expected that many leading service providers will see opportunities across the value chain for revenue generation. A clearer understanding of the implications of such moves, using theoretical work as well as empirical testing, is needed to ensure that the industry continues to thrive.
