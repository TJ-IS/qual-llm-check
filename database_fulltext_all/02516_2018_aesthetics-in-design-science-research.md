# Aesthetics in design science research

Richard L. Baskerville<sup>a,b</sup>, Mala Kaul<sup>c</sup> and Veda C. Storey<sup>a</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Computer Information Systems, Robinson College of Business, Georgia State University, Atlanta, GA, USA; <sup>b</sup>School o Information Systems, , Curtin Business School, Curtin University, Perth, Australia; <sup>c</sup>Department of Accounting and Information Systems, College of Business, University of Nevada, Reno, NV, USA

## ABSTRACT

Much research has focused on understanding design science research and providing guidelines for its successful execution. However, there is still a need for more work on the raison d’être of design science research, which is the development of artefacts that can be applied to solve realworld problems. The value of design science research thus far has been to provide practically applicable solutions, as well as a contribution to knowledge. This paper proposes aesthetics, in addition to truth and utility, as a way to advance this raison d’être. We identify the design science triad of analytics, synthetics, and aesthetics and justify why the aesthetics quality attribute should be appreciated. We then identify twelve quality criteria for articulating and assessing aesthetics in design science research. Doing so enables us to both recognise and achieve artful design so that rigour, utility, and aesthetic value can collectively justify a valuable design science research contribution.

ARTICLE HISTORY Received 05 June 2015 Revised 19 August 2017 Accepted 27 September 2017

KEYWORDS Design science research; aesthetics; synthetics; analytics; aesthetic dimensions

## 1. Introduction

We are conditioned to think of design science research in terms of its coupling of high relevance with strong rigour. It is a research approach that seeks to deliver new and innovative artefacts constructed through the strength of science. These twin aspects are widely viewed as the goal and distinguishing characteristic of design science research. Although design science research focuses on utility, it must also contribute to science by extending existing knowledge.

While this viewpoint seems indisputable, it is incomplete. A truly design-inspired approach can also include attributes of elegance or beauty (van Aken, 2007). If we consider design science research as an activity that incor porates theory, artefact development, creativity, and innovation, with the objective of solving a practical problem, we can recognise the improvement aspect of this form of research. This improvement is the desire to create something that is better than before. While we may achieve this notion of quality in varying degrees, we often instinctively connect quality to beauty. This aspect of design science research proceeds from the presence of design itself in the approach. It regards the intuitive human response to the characteristics of a new or innovative problem-solving creation that evokes a sense of wonder and pleasure. This aesthetic aspect of design, perhaps because of its artistic character, is less frequently the focus of design science outcomes than it’s more practical and scientific companions that focus on truth and utility. However, if we agree with Simon’s (1996) conceptualisation of an artefact as the interface between the inner and outer environments, then there must be an aesthetic quality implicit in the harmony of creating an artefact of design ingenuity that seamlessly fits into this environment (Liedtka, dt ogilvie, Friedel, & Liedtka, 2007).

In order to delineate and highlight the valuable attribute of aesthetics in design science research, we draw the reader to three important aspects of design science research: analytic, synthetic, and aesthetic. This is akin to a more cultural view of the adherents to the paradigm. The analytic aspect regards the value attached to the science of it all: the logic, the theory, the systematic, and the methodical. The synthetic aspect regards the value attached to the practicality of it all: the making of real artefacts that are actualised to fit into real environments. The third aspect is the one that has been less explored in design science research. It is the aesthetic aspect, which regards the wonder of it all: the pleasure of doing things with elegance and making things of beauty. Similar to arguments that theory and utility are inseparable in design science research (Hevner, March, Park, & Ram, 2004), we argue that these three aspects are also inseparable. Those conducting design science research immerse themselves in a research culture where science, practicality, and beauty intertwine.

The purpose of this paper is to explore how design science research can achieve outcomes that deliver practical artefacts and scientific knowledge in a way that is aesthetically pleasing. An important aspect of aesthetics is that of artistic expression and communication. Aesthetics is not just what a designer embodies in a design; much like any other form of artistic venture, an equally, if not more important aspect of any aesthetic, is its recognition and appreciation. This is dependent on both the properties or quality of the aesthetic object and its interpretation by the spectator (Cupchik & Heinrichs, 1981). This has an important connotation for the inclusion of aesthetics in design science research: that is, to highlight the aesthetic qualities, and also to be able to recognise these qualities. To elaborate this idea and its importance to design science research, we propose a framework consisting of the three important dimensions of design science research: synthetics, analytics, and aesthetics. We use this framework to explain how aesthetics, because of its close relationship with design, is one of the defining aspects of design science research. Aesthetics interacts with other aspects of design science research in the delivery of such outcomes as artefacts, scientific knowledge, and pleasure. With an objective of encouraging the discernment and appreciation of the aesthetic quality of design outcomes, we examine the quality criteria of design science aesthetics and propose how utility, scientific rigour, or aesthetic value can jointly justify a design science contribution.

The next section of this paper presents three aspects of design science research: analytics, synthetics, and aesthetics. Following that, we then explore principles for aesthetics in art and adapt them to propose some quality criteria for aesthetics in design science research. Application of these criteria is illustrated within a design science research project, where the aesthetic quality of an artefact was a dominant outcome. The discussion section highlights the potential contribution of recognising aesthetics in design science research, as well as some of the challenges in doing so.

## 2. The design science triad: analytics, synthetics, and aesthetics

There are three interlocking aspects or dimensions of design science research: analytic, synthetic, and aesthetic. Together, they suggest a defining culture of those engaged in the research paradigm. Two of these attributes, the synthetic and the analytic, are much more thoroughly treated in the design science research literature. However, despite astute calls for artefacts “that are aesthetically pleasing to both the designer and the user”, (Hevner et al., 2004, p. 86) and that are “elegant” (Chatterjee, 2015; Vaishnavi & Kuechler, 2015), the aspect of aesthetics has largely been left unexplored and is the focus of this paper.

For the benefit of a parallel comparison, we discuss both synthetics and analytics prior to treating aesthetics. Our choice of terminology is largely influenced by the work of Kant (1908, 1987), who distinguishes analytical forms of reasoning from aesthetic forms of reasoning. He also distinguishes analytical reasoning from synthetic reasoning. Table 1 details a comparison of these three aspects.

## 2.1. Synthetics in design science research

Synthesis regards learning by putting things together. It entails combining and unifying isolated sensations into an understandable whole (Kant, 1908). For Simon (1996), this unification often involves creating an interface that combines the isolated elements of the artefact’s internal and external environments. For design science researchers, it includes searching for, and assembling, the components of a satisfactory solution to the design problem. Synthesis is a key dimension of design science research because it is through synthesis that a workable, practical, valuable artefact ultimately must materialise.

## 2.1.1. Realm of synthetics

The realm of synthetics (by which we mean the sphere that synthetics controls, or within which it operates,) is often the particular context in which the design problem arises. It is usually bound to practice in the sense that the synthesised design solution, the artefact, must be relevant in solving the practical problem at hand (Hevner et al., 2004).

## 2.1.2. Paradigm of synthetics

The paradigm of synthetics is a primary application. It is this aspect of design science research that brings science to bear on producing practical results. Synthetics is a key aspect of many approaches to the study of design. After all, without actually putting an artefact together, the design remains essentially a somewhat unfulfilling thought experiment. Therefore, the synthetics paradigm (by which we mean the more general models of this aspect of design science that synthetics instantiates) incorporates at least two key existing paradigms. Work labelled “design research” will often centre its synthetics. Winter (2008, p. 471), for example, distinguished design research as “aimed at creating solutions to specific classes of relevant problems by using a rigorous construction and evaluation process”.

## 2.1.3. Domain of synthetics knowledge

The domain of synthetics knowledge is characterised by technê and poïesis, Aristotle’s conceptualisation of the kind of knowledge that provides a capacity to make things or accomplish a goal (technê). Because design science research often goes beyond just “the capacity” and actually crosses the threshold to the event itself, we include poïesis, the human activity of making or transforming things in our world (Kilminster, 2006).

## 2.1.4. Activities of synthetics

The activities that take place in synthetics centre on making things. These activities focus on such artefacts as constructs, models, methods, and instances (March & Smith, 1995). An engineering viewpoint would characterise these kinds of products as designed “works” (Owen, 1998). Synthetics involves the construction of the artefact, along with the evaluation of the artefact for fitness and utility. From the perspective of design science, synthetics is an important design activity. Situating an artefact in a real or artificial setting and testing/evaluating the artefact still resides within the design science cycle (Hevner, 2007).

## 2.1.5. Outcomes of synthetics

The outcome of synthetics includes the material artefacts, their utility, and the “how to” knowledge acquired in their construction and evaluation. Works and artefacts are the most prominent outcomes of synthetics, especially when these works are material in nature. For example, classic Simonean artefacts will feature an internal and external environment and an interface between the two environments (Simon, 1996). Information technologies such as operational software or an electronic communications device are the clearest examples. Further efects from the development of these works include the value added by the works to the context: the utility, usefulness, benefits, etc. Such further efects also include the practical, “how to” knowledge gained in the construction: the new technê and poïesis gained by the design researchers.

## 2.2. Analytics in design science research

Analysis regards learning through a detailed examination that determines the nature, structure, or essential features of a subject. It is usually highly theoretical, rig orous, precise, and logical. It typically entails a focus on language and philosophy, ie, ways to describe or explain a phenomenon (Lowe, 2005). For design science researchers, it regards the abstract or theoretical framing that, while possibly quite general in nature, guides the designer in their search for a design that solves the problem at hand in the particular situation.

## 2.2.1. Realm of analytics

The realm of analytics is often theoretical: an abstract, generalised form for which the particular problem context at hand arises only as an example or an instance. It is usually bound to descriptions and explanations for the relationship between the problem and the designed solution. The analytics typically takes the form of a design theory (Gregor & Jones, 2007; Walls, Widmeyer, & El Sawy, 1992), a set of design principles (Markus, Majchrzak, & Gasser, 2002), or a design logic (Simon, 1996).

## 2.2.2. Paradigm of analytics

The paradigm of analytics is inquiry. It is this aspect of design science research that seeks knowledge or information concerning the design problem or its solution. The presence of rigorous analytics contributes to the face validity of design science research. The science of design is distinguished as “a body of intellectually tough, analytic, partly formalizable, partly empirical, teachable doctrine about the design process” (Simon, 1988, p. 68). Although synthetics can be important for adding an empirical grounding, the analytics can add intellectual rigour and formalisms. Analytics helps distinguish “design research” from “design science” where the latter “aims at creating standards for its rigour” (Winter, 2008, p. 471).

## 2.2.3. Domain of analytics knowledge

The domain of analytics knowledge is characterised by notions of epistêmê: Aristotle’s conceptualisation of a kind of knowledge that is ideally universal and applies across time and space (Flyvbjerg, 2004). While often associated with science, it is known to have value for providing principles for practice. Indeed, the explanations in design science analytics tend to be functional or teleological rather than deductive (Baskerville & Pries-Heje, 2010). The most common examples of design science research will incorporate both analytics and synthetics, such that there are, for example, a design theory and a material artefact. However, there are examples of pure design theories that have made substantial contributions without developing artefacts per se (e.g., Kasper, 1996; Walls et al., 1992).

## 2.2.4. Activities of analytics

The activities that take place in analytics centre on understanding, describing, and explaining things. These activities involve rational and logical thought productions that ensue from reflection (Flyvbjerg, 2004). In design science, such reflection usually regards the relation between the problem and similar problems (reflections on a class of problems) and between possible solutions and similar solutions (prescriptions for a class of artefacts). The search aspect of design science research means that there is a process of discovery involved in comprehending the nature of the problem and the related nature of possible solutions. The analytics ultimately aims to produce results that are prescriptively useful. The activities are inevitably imperative: design theories, design principles, and design logic must indicate the features and functions of an artefact (Simon, 1996).

## 2.2.5. Outcomes of analytics

The outcome of analytics includes the design theories, design principles, and/or the design logic that prescribes the features and functions of the material artefacts. These intellectual outcomes may also necessarily prescribe the construction and evaluation process (Venable, Pries-Heje, & Baskerville, 2016). They will often include a rationale (explanation) for such features and functions, usually in terms of functional requirements (Simon, 1996).

## 2.3. Aesthetics in design science research

## 2.3.1. The aesthetics aspect

The oldest known theory on aesthetics can be traced to Vitruvius’ architectural theory comprising three fundamental principles: i. firmitas or strength, ii. utilitas or utility, and iii. venustas which denotes beauty or aesthetics (Kruft, 1994). In design science research, the concept of firmness can be found in the notion of robustness and rigour of building and evaluating design artefacts (Hevner et al., 2004); the concept of utility can be seen in the focus on relevance and utility (Gill & Hevner, 2013); the concept of venustas has been noted with regard to the importance of beauty and elegance. More recent inquiry into aesthetics is based on the study of beauty and the sublime in works of art, most notably through the writings of Baumgarten, Kant, and Vico (Bathurst, 2009). The modern usage of the term “aesthetics” is based on the idea of sensing response traceable to its root word aisthesis which means “sense perception” in Greek (Williams, 2014/2015).

In keeping with this historical perspective, our usage of aesthetics regards the aspects and experience of beauty and art. Perhaps, it is unseemly to introduce this aspect into a scientific enterprise, but in design science research, scientists design. In information systems, the act of designing has both an analytic and an artistic aspect. Both aspects are productions of the mind. The artistic productions of the mind emerge from a momentum of ideas into a figural schema that is more complete than nature (Kant, 1987). The result can sometimes be described as tasteful, harmonious, charming, elegant, etc.

A thorough review of the massive literature on aesthetics is beyond the scope of this paper. However, the writings of John Dewey (1934) regarding aesthetics are among those that are particularly valuable for design science researchers. Dewey was not only prominent in advancing the scientific method and leading light in the philosophy of pragmatism, but he also wrote of aesthetics. Dewey’s reconciliation of the scientific method and aesthetics is important for framing the aesthetics of design science research outcomes (Johnston, 2002). For Dewey, aesthetics is part of experience; it is part of how we experience an artefact and create an artefact.

Design science in information systems regards artefacts like software, digital devices, and communications systems. On the surface, such artefacts might be considered too industrial to engage aesthetics. But why should we expect these artefacts to be devoid of aesthetic impact?

Objects of industrial arts have form – that adapted to their special uses. These objects take on aesthetic form, whether they are rugs, urns, or baskets, when the material is so arranged and adapted that it serves immediately the enrichment of the immediate experience of the one whose attentive perception is directed to it … When this form is liberated from limitation to a specialised end and serves also the purposes of an immediate and vital experience, the form is aesthetic and not merely useful. (Dewey, 1934, p. 116)

Artefacts arising in design science have no lesser claim to bring an aesthetic experience than do the “spoon and carpet”. A utilitarian object may come to function as art, and a work of art may come to function as a utilitarian object (Goodman, 1977, p. 19).<sup>1</sup> Even a scientist may have an artistic capacity, the ability to work expressively in a medium. For design science, this worked medium may be that of an artefact or a design theory. These take on aesthetic form as a result of this design work (Dewey, 1934).

Aesthetics, because of its tight relationship with design, is perhaps one of the overlooked, yet defining aspects of design science research. The problem with ofering beauty a place in the foreground of design science research is its evaluation. Its quality is mostly a part of the experience of design science research and/or its results. Such an aesthetic quality must be noticeable in the experience. It is the ancient Greek homily: beauty is in the eye of the beholder; or as Shakespeare put it, “Beauty is bought by judgement of the eye”.<sup>2</sup> This notion of beauty is termed as immediacy, the idea that a judgment of beauty is based on an immediate sensory judgement rather than being based on the rational analysis (Shelley, 2015). In terms of design science, the immediacy of aesthetic judgement is the result of the immediate critical communication of certain features of an artefact (Isenberg, 1949). There is an aesthetic sensibility in which personal judgement delivers a rapid verdict about the value of an artefact’s aesthetic properties or qualities (Sibley, 2001). As Kant also suggested above, even though such aesthetic sensibility may be ungoverned by conditions, it still may be supported by reason (Shelley, 2015).

While we recognise that there is seldom a “perfect” design, there are designs that may be better than others; the potential for an artistic aspect in information systems design science research is, therefore, undeniable. All designers, even scientific ones, are human. While the main practical purpose of our designs may be the utility of the outcome, a little beauty in that outcome always appeals as an improvement. There are certainly no universal proscriptions that forbid aesthetically pleasing designs as part of the process or the outcome of design science research! As the field has evolved, we have dedicated little time to this aspect, perhaps because it is seldom part of the functional requirements. Experts in the sociology of art would agree: for them, the whole notion of art is itself regarded as “overdetermined” (Rifkin, 2006, p. 28). A focus on aesthetics, rather that art, at least provides a slightly better grip on this aspect. Aesthetics concentrates on the judgement-of-beauty aspect and more particularly the qualities by which we might make judgements about such beauty and art.

## 2.3.2. Realm of aesthetics

The realm of aesthetics is the perception, appreciation, and judgement of beauty and art. An aesthetic artefact has qualities “in accordance with principles of artistic beauty or taste; giving or designed to give pleasure through beauty; of pleasing appearance”<sup>3</sup>. Aesthetics regard not just the pleasure that arises in design science research, but any qualities related to such pleasure. For hedonic systems, it might be suggested that any principles relevant to this pleasure are a functional requirement (Hirschman & Holbrook, 1982; Van der Heijden, 2004). For other kinds of information systems, such a notion implies that the principles might be regarded as non-functional. These principles are generally regarded as highly subject to individual taste, although Emmanuel Kant is among those who proposed a widely read systematic framework for such principles (Kant, 1987). Kant argued that artistic thought was still within the domain of reason. However, the faculties of reason are aligned in fundamentally diferent relationships than in analytical reasoning.

Dewey proposed that aesthetic experience was a legitimate element of the empirics in human activity. Even the scientific method can produce aesthetic out comes. In Dewey’s sense, the aesthetic aspect of an artefact lies in our experience of it; the aesthetics regards the pleasure of making or using it. But this experience depends on its context. “Every well-constructed object and machine has form, but there is aesthetic form only when the object having this external form fits into a larger experience”. (Dewey, 1934, p. 341). The importance of this larger experience is consistent with Simon’s notions of the external and internal environments of artefacts. The environment of the artefact afects the aesthetic experience:

Even a crude experience, if authentically an experience, is more fit to give a clue to the intrinsic nature of aesthetic experience than is an object already set apart from any other mode of experience … The first great consideration is that life goes on in an environment; not merely in it but because of it, through interaction with it. (Dewey, 1934, p. 11)

Because aesthetics lies in the experience of using the artefact, any aesthetic qualities are mainly the properties of the artefact’s user, not just those of its designer or builder. The context of the design or use includes the “larger experience” of the designer or the user. The aesthetic aspect of the artefact user’s experience may (or may not) be intended by the designer, but its achievement is a quality of its outcome. Analysis of aesthetics is made possible because it is part of such experience. For example, Emmanuel Kant is among those who proposed a widely read systematic framework for such analysis (Kant, 1987). Like Dewey, Kant argued that artistic thought is still within the domain of reason. However, the faculties of reason are aligned in fundamentally different relationships than in analytical reasoning. It is neither free will nor pure reason, but a judging capacity that produces “pleasure without desire” (Tolstoy, 1913, p. 21). The notion of aesthetic qualities means that the realm of aesthetics for design science research is not just evaluative (the overdetermined art critic). It also has a role in guiding the aesthetic design or the design of aesthetic artefacts. Its evaluative role is actually a by-product of this guidance role.

The realm of aesthetic design encompasses the generative process of creating aesthetic artefacts. It regards the aesthetics innate in the process followed by the designer who is designing the aesthetic. This part of the realm regards the aesthetics of practising design science. We are accustomed to critiques that consider a scientific, methodical approach to design as a debasement of the design act. “Great designs come from great designers, not from great design processes” (Brooks, 2010, p. 231). Such a “method may be vital to the practice of science (where it validates the results) but not to the practice of design (where results do not have to be repeatable, and in most cases must not be repeated, or copied)” (Cross, 2001, p. 51). However, such critiques ignore the symmetry, the elegance, and the beauty of the design methods valued in design science research.

A scientific approach to design, one that appreciates a design method or a systematic way of designing, ofers a way of using science in doing design work. Qualities of such an approach include the systematic, the methodical, the repeatable, the complete, etc. Since some observers would regard these as the aesthetic principles of science (Glanville, 1999), we have to allow that the generative process of design science research itself has an aesthetic: it can be beautifully done according to its principles of science. We are not accustomed to taking note of a scientific project that has been beautifully done, but we cannot overlook the potential for aesthetics in the design science research principles.

Perhaps a little more obvious is the realm of the design of aesthetic artefacts. It encompasses the most material product of design science research: an artefact that pleases. The qualities that characterise a designed beauty are myriad (perhaps even “overdetermined”). But examples might include an artefact that is pleasing because it is simple, symmetrical, tasteful, harmonious, charming, elegant, etc., etc. Hence, the realm of design science research has at least two interrelated parts: aesthetic design and the design of aesthetic artefacts.

## 2.3.3. Paradigm of aesthetics

The paradigm of aesthetics is design. It is this aspect of design science research that creates and embodies the intentions or prescriptions for an artefact. The act of designing and the design it produces proceed from creative moments. While design science research embodies a doctrine about the design process (Simon, 1988, p. 68), it can only be a way of organising, perfecting or contextualising the design act and its product.

Information Systems design science research shares the aesthetics in design with myriad other fields. Engineers design. Architects design. Urban planners design. Commercial artists design. The list is countless, and all may share their aesthetic dimensions with design science research. These are closely linked to creativity in design, which itself is linked to inventiveness (Gregor & Hevner, 2015). Like aesthetics, such creativity is often mentioned in design science research but rarely are these actually linked to the aesthetics in design.

## 2.3.4. Domain of aesthetics knowledge

The domain of aesthetics knowledge is characterised by the artistic. Artistic knowledge is an overloaded term that includes the prevailing principles used to judge the beauty and art in a particular context and for a particular design process or artefact. There is also knowledge of how to go about creating artefacts in a way that invokes such principles. We could think of this knowledge as the underpinning of the skill or talent, or even a representation of style (Ding & Gero, 2001), that is required in creating an aesthetic of admirable merit or quality. Aesthetic knowledge is important because it ofers fresh insights and awareness, and although this special insight may be dificult to articulate, it enables us to see things in new ways (John, 2001).

Unfortunately, such knowledge is unstable. Principles of aesthetics and artistic knowledge can change radi cally over time and across diferent societies. That which is regarded as beautiful at one moment may quickly become regarded as ugly at another. The unstable characteristic of aesthetics and artistic knowledge can make it unsuitable for scientific study (Gopnik, 2012).

Nevertheless, in the context of design science research (as well as other fields), we find designs with creative and artistic character that are credited to the creative skills and talent of the designer. It is the designer’s artistic knowledge, that enables the skills and talent to deliver such aesthetically successful designs, with longevity that withstands the vagaries of fashion.

## 2.3.5. Activities of aesthetics

Activities that take place in aesthetics centre on creating, inspiring, and visualising. In design science research, creativity involves generating ideas that are both novel and useful (Csikszentmihalyi, 1996; Root-Bernstein, 1989; Root-Bernstein & Root-Bernstein, 1999). These activities bring a new, original form into existence. This involves applying the designer’s imagination, cognitive abilities, motivation, and knowledge (Amabile, 2012, 1988; Boland & Collopy, 2004; Guilford, 1983; Martindale, 1989). For creativity, a designer needs knowledge and skills in the domain of the design (Amabile, 2013, p. 135). Great designs may come to the designer as an inspiration, a creative leap that “puts together ideas in a way which defies expectation or convention” (Currie, 2001, p. 254). For some designers, visualising features of the design process or the artefact can lead to such inspirations and creative leaps (Verstijnen, van Leeuwen, Goldschmidt, Hamel, & Hennessey, 1998).

## 2.3.6. Outcomes of aesthetics

The outcomes of the aesthetic aspect of design science research consist of the aesthetic characteristics imbued in the design process or the artefact. Except in the case of hedonic systems, these characteristics add a nonutilitarian,<sup>4</sup> pleasurable aspect to the design (Mill, 1891). They might be described as beauty, style, taste, harmony, charm, elegance, etc. For those who are non-experts in aesthetic theory, such outcomes are dificult to prescribe and evaluate. They are often highly subject to individual tastes, highly situational, and very dificult to describe in any concise way. Indeed, the widespread attempts to describe and evaluate art in universal ways are partly to blame for the very over-determination found in the sociology of art. At issue is whether we should take a realist or relativist viewpoint in relation to the aesthetics of design science research (Schellekens, 2012). A relativist viewpoint would hold that such principles are too subject to individual experience and too situational for the determination of useful, universal characterisations. A realist viewpoint would hold that, at least at some level of abstraction, useful, universal characterisations can be observed.

In the following section, we discuss the issue of universal aesthetic dimensions and provide examples of such dimensions from the aesthetics literature.

## 3. Aesthetic dimensions of design science and its artefacts

The notion of declaring universal dimensions that characterise valuable art is certainly debated. On one hand, universal aesthetic dimensions seem to be impractical to many. Aesthetics<sup>5</sup> involve experiences that are so contextualised in individual experience that, even though an artefact may comprehensively demonstrate features of such dimensions, an individual user may regard both the dimensions and their results as lacking aesthetic value and appeal. That is, the character of the artefact may have dimensions that are widely acknowledged as those of beauty and pleasure, but its users may find its experience to be ugly and repulsive.

It is, therefore, unlikely that we can agree on a set of universal aesthetic dimensions for either producing or evaluating the beauty in the processes of design science research or the artefacts that these produce. As in other aesthetic areas, such dimensions assume there is a universal character to human experience, a common, stable set of interests and desires that span across all cultures. Neither a society’s artistic tastes, nor indeed the society itself, is suficiently predictable or singular enough to identify any technical dimensions of beauty that are going to hold in the future or around the globe (Dutton, 2001, p. 204; Schellekens, 2012).

Others, on the other hand, believe universal aesthetic dimensions are a necessity in order to have a shared language about the experience. Though Plato and Aristotle disagreed on what beauty is, they both regarded that it was not localised in the response of the beholder but rather could be achieved through the application of some general principles and using definite proportions or relations among parts, sometimes expressed in mathematical ratios, for example the “golden section” (Sartwell, 2016). Denying the lack of any collective viewpoints on the matter denies the widespread and common acceptance of many works as characterised by their aesthetic value. For example, Arruda-Filho, Cabusas, and Dholakia (2010) found that early adopters of the Apple iPhone were drawn to the product by the pleasurable experience of using it. There was a commonality in their pleasurable experience of this product.

American art philosopher Denis Dutton was a leading authority on aesthetic universals (Hickman, 2010). For Dutton, the aesthetic experience may be highly individual, but it also has aspects that are socially constructed. More importantly, there is an aspect that is akin to intuition and instinct (Dutton, 2009). This shared aspect, this universal aspect of aesthetics ofers grounds for recognising and assessing the aesthetic qualities of an artefact should at least be possible. Dutton build on cross-cultural studies and accumulates twelve universal criteria of art artefacts: direct pleasure, skill and virtuosity, direct pleasure, style, novelty and creativity, criticism, representation, special focus, expressive individuality, emotional saturation, intellectual challenge, art traditions and institutions, and imaginative experience.

We adapt Dutton’s aesthetic universals because they have a collective regard in the aesthetics literature and, if we assume aesthetic universals can exist, provide a usefu language for describing the aesthetic qualities of design science artefacts. Such dimensions help us to understand and explain the widespread aesthetic acclaim attached to certain artefacts. While none of these twelve dimensions is individually peculiar to aesthetics alone, and the list can be “adjusted at the edges” (p. 60), Dutton explains how they can govern investigations into the arts. These dimensions have some likelihood to be regarded as describing an aesthetically pleasing experience in some sense. Therefore, assuming a schema for the aesthetic experience of design science research artefacts is helpful, these twelve aesthetic dimensions have solid grounding. Each is quite general in form. In making this adaptation, we allow the dimensions to apply both in the experience of the outcome artefact and to the design science process. The former can be useful where aesthetic qualities are observed in a design science build-and-evaluate cycle. We allow for the latter, the aesthetics of the design science process, because this process is within the realm of aesthetics in design science research. The scientific process is an important distinction between design science and other kinds of design contexts (Baskerville, Kaul, & Storey, 2015). Allowing the process itself to be an experience with aesthetic value is inevitable: science is itself a pleasure.

These twelve dimensions are summarised in Table 2. Following this, we provide further context to each of these dimensions to elaborate how these dimensions pertain specifically to design science research, and apply these dimensions to a design science research example.

Skill or virtuosity: can be defined as the “types of technique that are derived from the knowledge of the skills needed for producing an artefact” (Reich, 1993). Designers bring to the design process, a set of formal aesthetic and technical judgement which is a composite of their experience, skill, and personal preferences and judgement (Harfield, 2007). Aesthetic criteria are embedded in the designers’ knowledge, expertise, judgement, and techniques, and these qualities are implicitly captured or manifested in their designs (Reich, 1993).

Direct pleasure: refers to the attributes of an artefact that have an appeal beyond pure functionality. At first glance, functionality or addressing a purpose, (problem-solving,) may inherently seem at odds with the basic concept of non-utilitarian pleasure. However, aesthetic choices in designing are important in solving real-world problems, especially when artefacts are considered within a socio-technical environment, and the aesthetic experience of artefacts have been found important in moulding user acceptance and user choices (Mettler, Eurich, & Winter, 2014; van der Heijden, 2003; Van der Heijden, 2004).

Style: The style of a designed artefact has been deemed important criteria for evaluation (Hevner et al., 2004). Aesthetic experiences often arise from pleasure by the association of style elements. Aesthetics or beauty can be easily perceived in the concept of order that is reflected through symmetry or coherence, seen for example in Vitruvian proportions, the Golden ratio, and the Fibonacci series (Reich, 1993). In art, the general rules of aesthetics suggest that an experience of novelty or surprise can be also introduced through contrast, and, that new, unfamiliar styles or stimuli can increase aesthetic appeal (Christensen & Ball, 2016; Leder, Belke, Oeberst, & Augustin, 2004). For example, Weick noted that good theory was “interesting rather than obvious … a source of unexpected connections … and aesthetically pleasing” (1989, p. 517).

Criticism: Critique is an essential aspect of design science research. It includes a critical evaluation of the artefact and the design process, as well as subsequent critique of the dissemination of results. Criticism is also important in the appreciation or evaluation of the experience of art. The presence of critique provides a level of collective credibility to various forms of artistic endeavours. Criticism is therefore invaluable in the judgement of the merit of the aesthetic attributes of design science research activities. Although this critique has a subjective aspect to it, expertise and knowledge in this area provide for generally impartial assessments. Sensitivity to the aesthetic elements of an artefact would also be extremely valuable to design science research eforts and its evaluation.

Representation: A widely accepted argument in aesthetics is the conundrum between novelty and typicality. While appreciating novelty, individuals prefer the experience present in the most typical examples of a category (Hekkert, Snelders, & Wieringen, 2003). If we can capture the qualities of a good design in the form of patterns and the subsequently apply those same qualities in a diferent context, it should enable the production of artefacts embodying these qualities (Petter, Khazanchi, & Murphy, 2010). The experience of novelty can only be taken when coupled with the experience of typicality.

“Special” focus: While the notion of aesthetics is most easily comprehended as the beauty or the physical characteristic of an artefact, it can also be expressed through the more intangible aspects that can provide certain special characteristics. This expression is similar to Alexander’s (1979) notion of “quality without a name” that possesses the experience of aesthetics or beauty. This experience is dificult to define, yet is able to elicit an emotional or intuitive response.

Imaginative experience: The aesthetic value can be described as the melding of the imagination of the designer and the manifestation of this imagination in the experience of such artefacts by the user. The aesthetic experience of the designer while designing is communicated through the artefact to a subsequent aesthetic experience of the user. In his aesthetic theory of art, Collingwood (1938) expertly links the imaginative experience of the viewer to the expertise of designer: “The painted picture is not the work of art … [However,] its production is somehow necessarily connected with the aesthetic activity, that is, with the creation of the imaginative experience which is the work of art” (p. 305). For the user of artefacts, the imaginative experience represents the continuation, the completion, of the designer’s imaginative experience through the user’s imaginative experience.

Novelty and creativity: While novelty, creativity, and originality may not appear among the functional requirements of a design science artefact, they are often highly valued as a result. Aesthetically pleasing artefacts should grab attention and surprise their users. In this dimension, an artefact may not only feature utility, but also express newness, genius, and imagination. The design may exploit otherwise unnoticed possibilities of the underlying technologies.

Expressive individuality: The exploratory aspect of science means that it is possible that the scientist faces a productive activity that is vague and open-ended. While there may be a methodology, the outcome may be unpredictable. This aspect of science is even more intensive in design science because the process of designing an artefact ofers an opportunity for individual expression. This dimension regards the innate talents of the designers and the aesthetic qualities of their minds.

Emotional saturation: The experience of an artefact may invoke intense emotions on the part of the users. This emotional aspect of aesthetics may embody a form of emotional communication from the designer to the user. For example, a warning message might be displayed in a way that fills the user with dread and fear. It is sometimes called the aesthetic emotion:

That there is a particular kind of emotion provoked by works of visual art, and that this emotion is provoked by every kind of visual art, [by various artefacts], is not disputed, I think, by anyone capable of feeling it. (Bell, 1972, pp. 6–7)

Intellectual challenge: Aesthetically pleasing artefacts may take advantage of a range of the perceptual and intellectual capacities of the users. Colour displays can be more pleasing than monochrome, video with sound can be more pleasing than a silent video, music more pleasing than voice, etc. Combinations of digital media can be more pleasing than sticking to a single medium. Combinations of input media, such as voice recognition, keyboard, or scanner not only may be more complex, but also more pleasing. A simple example would be the use of multiple video output screens as opposed to a larger single one. Many users are happier when they can organise diferent things on diferent screens. The use of more complicated media can be a pleasing challenge to user’s abilities.

Aesthetic traditions and institutions: Technological artefacts have long been regarded from the perspective that these various kinds of devices are social as well as technical constructions. The concepts of technical artefacts are drawn into widely debated social theories like socio-materiality, structuration theory, and actor– network theory. Such schools of thought provide the larger historical context in the aesthetic experience of technological artefacts. Technology companies such as Apple, Google, IBM, Microsoft, and Oracle have design traditions that influence the expectations (and thereby the aesthetic experience) of their customers. In other words, the aesthetics in the experience of many technical artefacts is shaped by history.

## 4. Application of dimensions where artefact (synthetics) is dominant aesthetic

The dimensions described above ofer a way to improve the reporting and evaluation of design science research results. In this section, we show how the report of an example of design science research would be improved by more careful treatment of its synthetics, analytics, and especially, its aesthetics. We selected a work reporting on the evaluation of an aesthetic artefact as found in Haugstvedt and Krogstie (2012). The outcomes produced by this design science research project are as follows: (1) a prototype of a mobile augmented reality system with cultural heritage resources (artefact) and (2) a technology acceptance model for this type of applications (analytics). The artefact under evaluation was a location-aware mobile information system that used mobile augmented reality to enable a user to view historical photographs of particular locations on a mobile device. The photographs that were delivered to the user were selected by the system, according to the geographic location as reported by the device.

The researchers conducted a technology acceptance study to assess the perceived enjoyment, or hedonic pleasure, in addition to the perceived ease-of-use and perceived utility. The results of the technology acceptance study found that, in addition to utility, perceived enjoyment was an important factor in explaining the users’ intention to use. This design science study indicated that, in the case of this mobile augmented reality application with cultural heritage resources, the enjoyment of the experience, worked in tandem with the achievement of the user’s learning goals.

We can demonstrate the usefulness of the aesthetic dimensions by examining the development of the Haugstvedt and Krogstie artefact (Haugstvedt & Krogstie, 2012; Krogstie & Haugstvedt, 2015) within the context of the first seven of Dutton’s dimensions. (We emphasise the first seven because these occur in both versions of Dutton’s lists (2001, 2009), and to illustrate the adjustability of his dimensions).

Skill or virtuosity: How did the process of designing and creating the artefact (the generative process) apply a specialised skill?

The researchers had developed and evaluated a number of applications in the last five years, which combined the display of local historical photographs on mobile devices with locational information. These applications worked by linking pictures from a large archive to the GPS locations. The researchers’ expertise within this domain prompted them to design a mobile application that would incorporate augmented reality to introduce a fun-element to this system. This dimension provides insights into the aesthetics of the research process itself. There was a surprising coherence in the research experience between the researcher’s methods and their depth of experience.

Direct pleasure: How did the generative process, or the artefact itself, aside from any practical, informational, or communicative experience, ofer a source of pleasure: a pleasurable experience?

The application system had non-utilitarian properties intended to provide enjoyment to the user in addition to learning goals. People not only wanted to use the application to achieve learning goals (utilitarian), but also because they enjoyed the experience. The study unveiled via an online and in-person survey reflected a mean between 5.45 and 6.17 (on a scale out of 7) in relation to application’s enjoyableness, pleasantness, excitement, and interest to the user. The field experiment data showed that the application provides non-utilitarian pleasure to the user while attributing to some goal. Further, the descriptions of the coherence of expertise and method in the research process subtly convey a degree of pleasure in the experience of doing design science research.

Style: How was the generative process, or the artefact itself, made (more-or-less) according to rules of form and composition for a recognisable style?

There was considerable extant research in mobile augmented reality projects in the cultural heritage sector. Contemporary advances in mobile technology applications, resulted in a shift from the use of headmounted displays in augmented reality, to the use of mobile augmented applications. The authors demonstrated precedence to the application of augmented reality projects and developed the design of their artefact based upon a system that used mobile augmented reality to support reflection on crowd management. Their artefact was built upon an existing platform called the CroMAR (Mora, Boron, & Divitini, 2012). This foundation establishes the artefact as a member of this genre or style. In addition, the research process followed a widely recognised “style”: the three-cycle build-and-evaluate framework of Hevner et al. (2004). In this dimension, the degree of fit with this style largely remains implicit in the research report. It is suficiently consistent with the framework to admit it as an example of this particular research genre.

Criticism: What critical language available whereby the generative process, or the artefact itself, can be subjected to a discourse about its aesthetic qualities?

While the ultimate critique lies with the users’ experience, the evaluation of the enjoyment as an aspect of the user’s acceptance provides a basis for a critical language to criticise the artefact. The established measures of hedonic construct (see their survey instrument for this critical language) also provide language to evaluate the artefact. Our set of dimensions also embodies yet another possible way to elaborate such an aesthetic basis for critical language.

Descriptions of the research process are functional: as with many scientific reports, these are direct, sterile, and routine. While there is a large literature that followed the Hevner et al. (2004) framework, it also mainly deals with the functional aspects. All of the elements of this research process appear to be mechanically present and working. While we may suspect there may have been joy in the experience of the framework, this is left to the imagination. There is not yet the critical language available for a discourse on the aesthetic quality of conducting design science research.

Representation: How does the generative process, or the artefact itself, relate closely to the real or imaginary experience of the world?

Augmented reality is a superimposed view of the real world. Virtual artefacts are laid over reality to create the sense that the virtual aspects are a part of the natural environment. In this regard, the definition of augmented reality is almost exactly related to the dimension of imitation. The artefact itself provides a virtual experience that augments reality for the users when they are in a specific location. The user is geo-physically positioned, partly real, partly virtual, in their location.

It is likely that the originators of the Hevner et al. (2004) framework would, themselves, be pleased by the way Haugstvedt and Krogstie executed this research. The process seems to emerge both in a natural way and in a way that is quite consistent with the framework. The research experience appears to relate closely to the imaginary world (the conceptual framework) and the real world (building and evaluating the artefact).

“Special” focus: How was the generative process, or the artefact itself, made in a way that was experienced as special and dramatic? How this experience was somehow bracketed of from a more ordinary generative process or artefact?

The dimension of “special” focus requires that the user be situated in an environment that can be special and dramatic. Augmented reality is based on the idea of creating a special experience within an ordinary situation. In this artefact, the goal was to create a special focus on the cultural heritage of the city by superimposing images onto the real-world view of locales. Such locales could include a city street, a museum, or a national landmark. It was bracketed of from remote, armchair visits. The user had to be situated to use the artefact.

In terms of process, the researchers were concerned with the outcome of the survey part of the evaluation. They describe a number of defects in the survey, suggesting this aspect of the process was perhaps less ordinary and dramatic because its outcome was less rewarding.

Imaginative experience: How did the generative process, or the artefact itself, engage the imagination of the designer and/or the user?

The augmented reality aspect of the artefact is intended to engage the imagination of the user as a means to meld the real with the virtual. The user is situated in a modern location and must use their imagination to connect with, and situate, the photos in the artefact as these might have appeared in older times. The user must imagine the experience of being in the location at diferent historical periods or events.

The excitement from the focus on cultural heritage would also infect the experience of using the Hevner et al. (2004), framework. Although it seems implicit in the report, Haugstvedt and Krogstie (2012) were deeply caught up in the experience of their study, considering such applications of technology as “powerful” and “ideal” (p. 247).

The above is an illustration of aesthetics as an element of value in the purpose of the design science research artefact. Note that aesthetics can be manifest in design science research in many diferent ways. While this is by no means a required addition to the goals of truth and utility, we propose some ways in which aesthetics can be incorporated as an element of value in design science research: (1) Introducing an aesthetic component to the solution of a problem; (2) Designing a solution where the purpose of the designed artefact is aesthetic; (3) Bringing aesthetics into the design of the solution such that the artefact or solution has a certain beauty or elegance that extends beyond the purpose or utility of the artefact; (4) Incorporating an aesthetic aspect to the design science research process; and (5) Identifying that there is an elegance or artistry to the knowledge outcomes of the design science research process.

## 5. Discussion

In illustrating the usefulness of aesthetic dimensions for the discussion of design science research outcomes, we reviewed Haugstvedt and Krogstie’s (2012) study of an augmented reality system. The aesthetic dimensions for design science research suggest that aesthetics can be in found in the artefact as well as in the development process of the artefact. Haugstvedt and Krogstie (2012) have not explicitly proclaimed the aesthetic aspects of their artefact or in their process. However, as we devel oped sensitivity to these nuances through a careful study of their research, we found evidence of aesthetics and elaborate these aesthetic qualities in section 4. By doing so, we have extended Haugstvedt and Krogstie’s original study in a fundamental way. This analysis is important, because Haugstvedt and Krogstie’s (2012) system failed to attract more than minimal interest from the users. It was technically successful, but not valued in use. Our review does not show that a lack of aesthetic caused the ultimate use-case failure of the artefact. The artefact did satisfy certain, known dimensions of aesthetic values.

Sensitivity to aesthetic nuances raises the issue of aesthetic perception. It is important to note the impact of communication on aesthetic perception. Communication relies not only on the properties of the aesthetic artefact, but also on the interpretation of these properties by the spectator (Cupchik & Gebotys, 1988; Cupchik & Heinrichs, 1981; Hester, 1975). This paper aims to sensitise design science researchers to aesthetic nuances. Design science research in information systems is reaching a degree of maturity that allows aesthetics to grow in its prominence in the scholarly conversation related to the paradigm. At this point in its history, utility remains the queen of design science criteria. Scholars working in this paradigm value both the functionality and the relevance of the problem-solving outcome: the artefact is useful within its external environment. Creativity, innovation, and invention are often mentioned in the design science literature, but have thus far remained underdeveloped ideas in the field. Our more elaborate development of these aesthetics values contributes an extension of such work as van Aken’s (2007) and Gregor and Hevner’s (2015) on innovation and creativity in design science research.

By elaborating the aesthetic aspects of design science research, we also ofer a view that contradicts existing notions that suggest that involving design in approaches to science will somehow cripple design creativity and innovation. Examples of this critique include works by Brooks (2010), Cross (2001), and Iivari (2007). By introducing aesthetic dimensions for design science research, our research provides a fresh language by which researchers in this paradigm can describe and discuss its aesthetic aspects.

Baskerville et al. (2015) describe design science research as a rich dynamic of difering research genres. Our research extends this dynamic to include the aesthetics aspect in these genres. Dewey (1934), along with others in the realm of aesthetics discussed above, explains how any industrial artefact (within which we include the design science artefacts) ofers an experience with an aesthetic aspect. There is already a plethora of design science research methods, guidelines, and criteria on ofer in the literature. Must we now pester our design scientists for a commentary on the aesthetics of their work?

Like the previous work of Baskerville et al. (2015), the richness of this dynamic makes the reporting of design science research results selective. Such reports cannot comprehensively report all the details of the creation and evaluation of an artefact (typically) intended for use in a complex social-technical environment. Rather, the research reports must be restricted to those aspects of the research that are important to the contribution of the work. If the aesthetics are an important outcome, then our twelve dimensions ofer a common language for discussing such aesthetics. If the aesthetics are only a secondary aspect, it might be wise to ignore them.

The aesthetic aspect allows us to discuss the aesthetics in design science research when this aspect is important.

It is not required unless it is an important aspect. The framework allows us to consider rich, unique individual experiences in the product and the process of this paradigm. It also allows us to describe these experiences in a language that some authorities regard as universal across the galactic range of aesthetic experiences, including now design science research outcomes.

The aesthetic aspect also allows us to consider aesthetic theory in evaluating design science research. Such theory can be nested in the framework. Dutton’s “Darwinian” theory of aesthetic universals is one such theory. Dutton lists criticism as one of the aesthetic universals. Such criticism might itself be based on other aesthetic theories. It this sense, we have the opportunity to nest aesthetic theory in our discussion of this aspect of design science research.

The aesthetic dimensions might be regarded by some as qualities. Others might consider their value as criteria. Dutton himself described them “cluster criteria” (Dutton, 2009, p. 51) some of which regard features of artefacts, while others regard qualities of the experience of the artefacts. In our view, their greatest value lies in their potential role as a common language with which we can describe, discuss, and compare the aesthetic aspects of design science research.

There are certain types of artefacts that can, or should, be evaluated for their aesthetics. In the example of the aesthetic artefact of Haugstvedt and Krogstie (2012), the artefact was a prototype of a mobile augmented reality system with cultural heritage resources. In this example, it is obvious that the enjoyment in the usage of the app goes beyond its utility. Therefore, in this case, one would expect that aesthetics, as an evaluation criterion, is appropriate. Aesthetic inquiry is also appropriate for research into the design of artefacts (Mohrman, 2007) pertaining to interactive technology, designing involving visual aesthetics, and usability linked design solutions (Lavie & Tractinsky, 2004; Tractinsky, 2004). At a more abstract level, aesthet ics have been long noted within the context of elegant theoretical contributions (Markus et al., 2002; Vaishnavi & Kuechler, 2015) and these dimensions could serve as a starting point for researchers in assessing the aesthetics of more abstract concepts. Types of studies where aesthetics is not necessarily the right evaluation criterion include a model that should be evaluated on its ability to represent the important aspects of the real world in the application domain; or a framework whose purpose is to capture high-level aspects of important issues in a field.

The aesthetic dimensions for design science research ofer a starting point for further development, most likely, using a design science research methodology to become principles. The general idea is to provide a design science researcher with “actionable advice” on how aesthetics can be accomplished in a design science research process. The aesthetics dimensions identified in this paper should be useful for designers during their design science research study. It also ofers reviewers of design science research outcomes, a basis for recognising aesthetics as a valuable contribution. Future research is needed to further apply and mature these dimensions into principles that can advance design science research towards a research culture in which science, utility, and beauty intertwine.

## 6. Conclusion

Relevance and rigour have been widely discussed and adopted in design science research. While these are undoubtedly the cornerstones of improvement research, the objective of this paper is to explicate the importance of aesthetics, an aspect that has often been recognised, yet generally left as implicit. Perhaps this oversight arises because the design science research tradition links design to problem-solving and functionality, whereas aesthetics is generally associated with non-utilitarian notions. Although design science research greatly values the pragmatic and scientific, we suggest that aesthetics is yet another way to advance design science research’s raison d’être of providing practical solutions to real-world problems and making a contribution to science. We propose a triad of analytics, synthetics, and aesthetics, representing ways in which to use knowledge in design science research. Each is distinguishable in terms of the activities, outcomes, and the paradigms in which they occur. We draw on prior research in art and engineering, to identify twelve aesthetic dimensions which have some degree of universality. These aesthetic dimensions are adapted for design science research and illustrated through their application to a design science research project. These dimensions are intended to serve as a guiding point for design science researchers to develop the aesthetic contribution of their work and to aid evaluators in characterising the resulting beauty and elegance. Doing so means that rigour, utility, and aesthetic value can collectively justify a valuable design science research contribution, even though aesthetics is not always an absolute requirement for quality.
