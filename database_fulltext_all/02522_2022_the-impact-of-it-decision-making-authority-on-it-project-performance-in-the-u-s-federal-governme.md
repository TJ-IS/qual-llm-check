# THE IMPACT OF IT DECISION-MAKING AUTHORITY ON IT PROJECT PERFORMANCE IN THE U.S. FEDERAL GOVERNMENT<sup>1</sup>

Min-Seok Pang Department of Management Information Systems, Fox School of Business, Temple University Philadelphia, PA, U.S.A. {minspang@temple.edu}

Gwanhoo Lee Department of Information Technology and Analytics, Kogod School of Business, American University Washington, DC, U.S.A. {glee@american.edu}

Many information technology (IT) projects in the U.S. federal government have been plagued with failures and overruns in recent years, wasting a large amount of tax revenues and undermining the public’s confidence in the government’s ability to manage such resources and deliver on its commitments. What affects the performance of IT projects in the federal government? We find that prior literature on IT project management from the private sector setting is inadequate to answer this question. In addition, to the best of our knowledge, information systems (IS) research thus far has not considered how the decision-making authority of an IT unit influences IT project performance at the organizational level. We leverage several unique characteristics of the public sector, vis-à-vis for-profit firms, in developing our theory and hypothesis. Utilizing official datasets on IT projects and human resource records, we find that the more IT managerial officials and supervisors a federal bureau has, the lower time overruns it experiences in its IT projects. Interestingly, this relationship is weakened when the bureau has a larger number of ongoing IT projects. Our findings offer new theoretical insights for the IT project management literature and provide meaningful policy insights into government IT management.

Keywords: The U.S. federal government, IT decision-making authority, IT project performance, IT project management, public sector IT management

## Introduction

The role of information technologies (IT) in the public sector has become more prominent than ever before. IT has been transforming the government’s operating models and administrative processes (Lee & Kwak, 2012; Pang et al., 2014b). According to the Federal IT Dashboard (www.itdashboard.gov), IT spending in the U.S. federal government has risen from \$78 billion to \$91 billion in 2017-2021. However, many government IT projects fail, wasting taxpayers’ money, harming citizens’ well-being, and undermining the public’s confidence in government.

An industry study shows that IT projects in government have a failure rate that is higher than in any other industry (Standish Group, 2015).

What drives the performance of IT projects in government? As a series of decisions must be made throughout a project lifecycle, an IT manager’s decision-making authority plays a crucial role in meeting project goals (Lappi & Aaltonen, 2017). Although prior research tends to support the positive impacts of IT decision-making authority on IT project performance in the private sector (Lee & Xia, 2010; Tiwana, 2009), it is unclear whether this is the case too for public-sector IT projects due to the unique characteristics of the public sector environment (Mishra et al., 2016; Patanakul et al., 2016), such as a diverse group of stakeholders, bureaucratic requirements, and the absence of competitive motivations. Furthermore, while the extant literature offers extensive knowledge about the management of individual IT projects, it provides limited knowledge on IT project programs or portfolios at the organizational level. Whereas individual projects deliver tactical and focused outcomes, programs and portfolios contribute to an organization’s long-term objectives (Roy et al., 2021). Therefore, it is crucial to complement the literature by advancing our knowledge of project programs and portfolios.

To address these understudied problems, this research investigates how an IT unit’s decision-making authority in a federal bureau influences the organization-wide performance of its IT projects. IT project performance has two distinct dimensions: process performance and product performance (Nidumolu, 1995; Wallace et al., 2004); the former refers to the effectiveness of the implementation process undertaken in terms of on-time and within-budget completion (Deephouse et al., 1996), and the latter refers to the performance of the resulting system, such as quality and functionality (DeLone & McLean, 2003). This study focuses on process performance because time and cost overruns are particularly critical problems for government IT projects (Calvo et al., 2019); nearly 25% of the federal IT projects face moderate-to-severe problems in meeting their schedule and budgetary targets (Mishra et al., 2016).

We test our hypotheses with datasets compiled from multiple official sources, including the Federal IT Dashboard and federal human resource (HR) records. We find that when a federal bureau has more IT managerial officials and supervisors, the bureau’s IT projects experience lower time overruns. This effect is weakened when the bureau has a larger number of ongoing IT projects. However, we do not find a significant effect of the IT managerial officials and supervisors on its IT projects’ cost overruns.

This study contributes to the information systems (IS) literature in three significant ways. First, the decision-making authority directly and indirectly affects how IT professionals interact, negotiate, and coordinate with project stakeholders to make decisions (Levine & Rossmoore, 1994). However, prior research offers little understanding of how an IT unit’s overall level of decision-making authority influences its IT project performance. This study fills this knowledge gap. Second, the IT project management literature in the IS field primarily focuses on for-profit firms as the organizational context, and only a handful of studies investigate public-sector IT projects (e.g., Heeks & Stanforth, 2007; Mishra et al., 2016; Pan et al., 2006). Although there are some similarities between organizations in the private and public sectors, there are significant differences (Crawford & Helm, 2009; Mishra et al.,

2016; Patanakul et al., 2016). The differences create theoretical tensions in applying knowledge obtained from for-profit firms to the public-sector context and warrant new theorizing and rethinking of IT project performance in the government setting (Davison & Martinsons, 2016; Mishra & Browning, 2020). Our research contributes to the literature by taking a step in this direction. Third, while most prior research on IT projects has studied what affects an individual project’s performance (e.g., Lee & Xia, 2010; Maruping et al., 2009; Tiwana, 2009), few studies have investigated organization-wide aggregate IT project performance. We seek to discover new insights on the driver for the organization-wide performance of IT projects.

In what follows, we theorize the effect of an IT unit’s decisionmaking authority level in a federal bureau on the organizationwide time and cost overruns in the bureau’s IT projects. We present our findings and discuss their implications for research and practice.

## Theoretical Backgrounds and Hypotheses Development

## The Public Sector Context for IT Projects

Researchers in public administration have extensively discussed the distinctions between public and private sector organizations (Coursey & Bozeman, 1990; Perry & Rainey, 1988; Rainey & Bozeman, 2000), as well as the management practices in the two sectors (Boyne, 2002; Noordegraaf & Stewart, 2000). The major differences include organizational structures, incentives, the extent of public scrutiny, and environmental competitiveness, among others (Boardman et al., 2010; Bozeman, 2000; De Graaf & Van der Wal, 2008).

Similarly, IT projects in the public sector differ from those in the private sector in several ways (Crawford & Helm, 2009; Mishra & Browning, 2020; Mishra et al., 2016; Patanakul et al., 2016). First, stakeholders of an IT project in the government are typically more diverse than in the private sector (Metcalfe, 1993), and they often have disparate, vague, and conflicting goals (Boyne, 2002). Such stakeholders encompass the general public, business firms, interest groups, legislatures, contractors, and other government agencies, each of which has different priorities, interests, and demands. Furthermore, governments are expected to pursue distinctive goals such as equity and social welfare, which are more vague and difficult to measure than the objectives of for-profit firms (Ferlie et al., 1996; Pang et al., 2014a). As a result, IT projects in the public sector should meet varied, complex, ambiguous, and even conflicting requirements from a diverse set of multiple stakeholders (Lee & Brumer, 2017).

Second, public managers are subject to public scrutiny and supervision by lawmakers, media, and government watchdogs and thus must demonstrate greater accountability and transparency than private-sector managers (Crawford et al., 2008; Heeks & Stanforth, 2007). By the same token, government IT projects are subject to more oversight and compliance requirements (Calvo et al., 2019). Third and for the reasons above, public officials, compared to managers in the private sector, tend to be risk-averse, bureaucratic, slow in decision-making, exercise limited autonomy, and resist change (Bellante & Link, 1981; Bozeman & Kingsley, 1998; Pang, 2017). Fourth, since governments are not subject to the same degree of competitive pressures as for-profit firms (Ferlie et al., 1996; Mishra & Browning, 2020; Patanakul et al., 2016), public-sector officials are less incentivized to expedite decision-making or reduce costs (Boyne, 2002). Despite these unique characteristics, which warrant contextspecific theorizing, public-sector IT projects largely remain as a blind spot in the IT project management literature (Mishra & Browning, 2020; Mishra et al., 2016).

## An IT Unit's Decision-Making Authority and IT Project Performance

An organization can be viewed as a system of positions and their associated roles (Karimi et al., 1996). Organizations define a certain hierarchy to differentiate roles and authority levels vertically and horizontally and delineate functional responsibilities and formal power dependencies among organizational actors (Cummings & Cross, 2003). The rank of an individual in the hierarchy reflects the formal authority and power inherent to the position. Authority refers to the right to make particular types of decisions for an organization (Yukl, 2002). The higher the rank, the greater authority one has to influence the organization’s actions (Hambrick, 1981).

Much of the IT governance research has studied the impact of the IT unit’s decision-making authority on firm performance (Sambamurthy & Zmud, 1999; Tiwana & Konsynski, 2010) or IT project performance (Tiwana, 2009). Decision-making authority encompasses two classes of decision rights: decision control rights and decision management rights (Tiwana, 2009). The former includes, for example, approval of project setups and monitoring of performance measurement criteria, and the latter focuses on the utilization of organizational resources and execution of ratified decisions (Fama & Jensen, 1983). An IT unit possesses both classes of project decision rights to some extent, and its authority for making those decisions is found to affect IT project performance (Tiwana, 2009). However, to the best of our knowledge, few prior studies have investigated the performance implication of decision-making authority for an IT program or portfolio (Gregory et al., 2015).

In this research, we develop two competing hypotheses for the effect of the IT unit’s decision-making authority on organization-level IT project performance in government. First, we posit a positive effect for the following reasons. Decision-making authority empowers IT managers to effectively influence the behaviors of their subordinates, peers, contractors, and superiors. According to interpersonal influence theory, an actor’s influence can flow downward, upward, or laterally (Enns et al., 2003; Higgins et al., 2003). The actor uses pressure and exchange tactics to modify the behaviors of a subordinate (Allen et al., 2016); relies on such tactics as exchange, rational persuasion, personal appeal, coalition, and inspirational appeals to influence peers (Enns et al., 2003); and uses expertise, rational persuasion, upward appeals, and consultation tactics to influence superiors (Farmer & Maslyn, 1999). IT managers with greater decisionmaking authority, compared to non-managerial IT employees, can exercise greater interpersonal influence over all levels of stakeholders to coordinate multiple projects more effectively by optimally allocating shared resources, mobilizing necessary resources, and resolving conflicts (Enns et al., 2003; Gregory et al., 2015; Ngwenyama & Nielsen, 2014).

Top management support is consistently found to be a critical success factor for IT projects (Aladwani, 2002; Biehl, 2007; Liang et al., 2007; Ravichandran & Rai, 2000). IT managers are likely to accrue greater social capital with top management than non-managerial IT employees because they have more opportunities to interact with top management through management meetings and social functions. Behavioral control theory suggests that managers can leverage their social capital to secure top management support for their initiatives (Eisenhardt, 1989). Higher-ranking IT leaders can spend more time interacting with those outside the IT unit or with senior executives (Ives & Olson, 1981; Stephens et al., 1992). Liu et al. (2015) found that creating and mobilizing social capital through repeated interactions with top managers helps obtain their support. Raghunathan and Raghunathan (1989) find that IT projects have a higher chance of success when IT managers are hierarchically close to a chief executive.

When more managers in the IT unit are endowed with decision-making authority, the locus of project governance moves from a business unit to the IT unit. In other words, IT managers, rather than business managers, can make critical decisions necessary for making IT projects successful. Many decisions for IT projects are technical in nature and interdependent within and across the projects (Hahn & Lee, 2021; Tiwana, 2009). IT managers’ knowledge in the technology domain and their work relationships with IT professionals enable them to make complex technical decisions better and faster than their counterparts in the business unit (Hahn & Lee, 2021). Furthermore, thanks to their frequent interactions with a wide range of users and stakeholders, IT managers are likely to possess broader business domain knowledge than non-managerial IT professionals. This allows them to make informed trade-off decisions that would improve the performance of all IT projects. For the reasons mentioned above, we posit:

Hypothesis 1: The IT unit’s decision-making authority level in a federal bureau is positively associated with the organization-wide time and cost performance of the bureau’s IT projects.

However, empowering the IT unit with more decisionmaking authority can have a negative impact on IT project performance in the public sector for two reasons: (1) diversity in stakeholders and their interests, and (2) decision accountability and compliance. First, as mentioned above, public-sector IT projects involve various stakeholders who have divergent interests and conflicting goals that are difficult to measure (Boyne, 2002; Ferlie et al., 1996). For example, an IT system for a new healthcare policy program would have to meet requirements from individual patients, physicians, care providers, Congress, and other officials within the government (Lee & Brumer, 2017). Since officials in a business unit typically have more interactions with many stakeholders than do IT officials, they are better positioned to balance different interests and resolve conflicts. This problem is more pressing in the public sector than in the private sector because the stakeholders’ objectives are more difficult to define and measure (Boyne, 2002). As such, prioritizing and balancing different interests and requirements would be a more challenging task for IT officials than for non-IT officials. This would slow down IT managers’ decision-making, which is confounded by the lack of incentives for improving efficiency in the public sector. Therefore, the IT unit’s greater decision-making authority relative to the business unit can negatively impact IT project performance.

Second, in the federal government, managers are held accountable for their decisions against a range of mandates imposed by various stakeholders such as agency inspectors general, Congress, the White House Office of Management and Budget (OMB), the General Services Administration, the Government Accountability Office, and external watchdog or nonprofit groups (Calvo et al., 2019). This becomes a concern for IT managers because of onerous requirements for transparency and oversight, which are not as great a burden as in the private sector. Upon being endowed with more decision-making authority, IT officials, rather than their counterparts in business units, are held accountable for their decisions and for ensuring compliance with oversight requirements from these overseers (Meier, 2010). As a result, federal IT managers exhibit risk-averse behavior in decision-making. In addition, increased oversight requirements and accountability often lead project teams to allocate resources away from project-specific activities to compliance-specific activities. Hence, we posit:

Hypothesis 2: The IT unit’s decision-making authority level in a federal bureau is negatively associated with the organization-wide time and cost performance of the bureau’s IT projects.

## Data Sources and Methods

## Data Sources

Our unit of analysis is the federal bureau-quarter. At the top of the federal governmental hierarchy are federal agencies such as the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) or the Department of Transportation (DOT). Under the federal agencies, there are bureaus that carry out more specialized policy functions, including the Immigration and Customs Enforcement under the DHS or the Federal Railroad Administration (FRA) under the DOT. We measure IT project performance at this level.<sup>2</sup>

We used the Federal IT Dashboard to obtain data on federal IT investments, projects, and performance in 2010-2019. It covers 24 federal agencies under the purview of the OMB, and provides a detailed profile of IT projects, budget amounts and actual cost accrual, and schedules. Examples of IT projects are a Laboratory Information Systems implementation project by the Center for Disease Control and Prevention and a National Fire Plan Operations and Reporting System (NFPORS) project by the Department of the Interior (DOI).

We measured the decision-making authority of an IT unit using the profiles of IT personnel in each bureau and data from the U.S. Office of Personnel Management (OPM).<sup>3</sup> This dataset offers a snapshot of the entire federal personnel in each quarter (March, June, September, and December), with approximately 2 million records of individual employees in each quarter. Each personnel record consists of agency, bureau, location (state), age range, education level, tenure range, and base pay. The records also offer each employee’s information on hierarchy (managerial official, supervisor, or staff member) and occupation classifications. Integrating these data sources, we build a panel dataset of more than 2,000 bureau-quarter observations from 91 federal bureaus from the fourth quarter of 2010 to the second quarter of 2019.

## Measurement of IT Project Performance: Time and Cost Overruns

Time and cost overruns are widely used in the IS literature for IT project performance (Rai et al., 2009). We use both as dependent variables (Table 1). In the Federal IT Dashboard, an IT project consists of what it calls “activities.”<sup>4</sup> For each activity, we found a planned start date, a planned end date, an actual start date, and an actual end date, which we used to calculate a planned duration in days from the difference between the planned start date and the planned end date and an actual duration from the difference between the actual start date and the actual end date. The time overrun of each activity was measured by the actual duration minus the planned duration in days. For each activity, the IT Dashboard also offers a planned cost and an actual cost in millions. Cost overrun was measured by the actual cost minus the planned cost.

We aggregated time and cost overruns of each activity to a bureau level. We used two measures of time overrun for the dependent variables: Time Overrun Average and Time Overrun Total. For Time Overrun Average, we calculated the average of activity time overrun in days weighted by the cost planned. Time Overrun Total was calculated by the sum of time overruns of all activities within the bureau. Similarly, we calculated two measures of cost overruns: Cost Overrun Average and Cost Overrun Total. Cost Overrun Average is the average of activity cost overrun weighted by the cost planned, and Cost Overrun Total is the sum of cost overruns of all activities in the bureau. The calculations of Time Overrun Average and Cost Overrun Average are consistent with the earned value management metrics (Fleming & Koppelman, 2016), which are widely used to evaluate the performance of public-sector projects (Kwak & Anbari, 2012).

## Measurement of the Decision-Making Authority of the IT Unit

The OPM records provide occupational codes for the entire federal workforce. Examples include general attorneys (0905), personnel managers (0201), and intelligence officers (0132). To identify IT workers in each bureau, we used the following six codes: information systems management (2201), information systems management trainee (2299), office automation clerks (0326), computer operations (0332), computer specialists (0334), and computer clerks (0335).<sup>5</sup> Federal employees with all the other occupational codes are considered non-IT personnel.

For our independent variable of interest—decision-making authority—we measured how many IT employees are appointed with the status of managerial officials or supervisors in a bureau. The OPM defines a managerial official as a person responsible for managing a program or program segment whose duties include formulating, determining, or influencing policies for the assigned program scope. Managerial officials “direct the work of an organizational unit, are held accountable for the success of specific line or staff functions, monitor and evaluate the progress of the organization toward meeting goals, and make adjustments in objectives, work plans, schedules, and commitment of resources.”<sup>6</sup> In other words, a managerial official is a high-ranking executive who oversees a large function or unit within a federal bureau. On the other hand, a supervisor is defined as “an individual who has an authority in the interest of an agency to hire, direct, assign, promote, reward, … or remove employees,” and a major duty of the supervisor is “planning and scheduling work; assigning work to employees; accepting, amending or rejecting completed work; … appraising performance and recommending performance standards and ratings.”<sup>7</sup> A managerial official has a higher rank in the federal hierarchy than a supervisor, and most managerial officials also assume supervisory authority. The construct IT Manager is the percentage of bureau IT employees with a managerial official or a supervisory status to all IT personnel in a bureau. As of March 2019, 15.1% of the federal IT employees have managerial or supervisory positions.<sup>8</sup>

## Control Variables

To account for other observable factors that might affect the performance of IT projects, we used a range of control variables (Table 1). First, we controlled for the characteristics of IT workforce at each federal bureau. We used the educational attainment levels of federal IT professionals as control variables (IT Bachelor and IT Master). Since project performance is likely to be affected by the experiences of IT workers, we controlled for the average age of IT employees. In addition, we measured the length of tenure and external experiences of IT professionals in federal bureaus. For all federal employees, the OPM records provide a “length of service” in years<sup>9</sup> in the federal government, which indicates the number of years an employee has worked in the federal government. We calculated the average length of service of IT employees in a bureau for a control variable (IT Tenure). To control for external work experience, we measured the length of the career of IT employees outside the federal government using three attributes: age, length of federal career, and educational attainment.<sup>10</sup> Then we calculated the average length of the non-federal career of IT employees in years for a control variable (IT Out Fed).

Second, we controlled for the number of IT employees in a bureau and their average base pay level. Also, we accounted for the bureau-level total numbers of ongoing projects, activities within the projects, and outsourcing contracts associated with the bureau. Control variables include bureaulevel IT investment measures such as the size of total bureau IT funding and the share of new IT development, which the Federal IT Dashboard calls development, modernization, and enhancement (DME) (Pang, 2017). Third, control variables for bureau budget characteristics are included in our estimations. We used the federal budget database from the OMB. In addition to the budget size, we used the percentage of four budget functions (defense, public welfare, law enforcement, government management) vis-à-vis overall budgets as controls, as different bureau missions may have different impacts on IT project performance. We also controlled for budget complexity using a Herfindahl index of budget functions. Fourth, using the OPM database, we controlled for non-IT workforce characteristics such as the number of employees and non-IT employees’ federal tenure, pay level, managerial status, and education levels in each bureau. ## Econometric Methods and Instrumental Variables

In addition to the control variables above, our estimations include bureau-fixed effects, which account for unobserved time-invariant heterogeneity, such as IT/project management capabilities, HR policies, and management structures. We also include year/quarter-fixed effects for government-wide temporal changes in policy, political, and fiscal environments. To further capture time-variant unobserved heterogeneity such as changes in leadership, agency-specific policies, or funding, the estimations include agency-specific linear time trends.

The control variables, the fixed effects, and the linear time trends still may not completely rule out other endogeneity concerns. For example, reverse causality can be an issue with respect to project performance and decision-making authority. A federal bureau might empower its officials with more authority when its project performance deteriorates. There might be other time-variant unobserved heterogeneity that affects both independent and dependent variables, potentially causing bias in estimations.

To rule out these endogeneity concerns and ensure causal identification, we employed instrument variables (IVs) using the characteristics of non-IT employees in sister bureaus and offices under the same agency, excluding a focal bureau. For example, for the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) under the DOT, we used the characteristics of non-IT employees of other DOT bureaus and offices (e.g., the FRA, the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration), excluding the FAA, as IVs. Specifically, the IVs are the percentage of non-IT managers to non-IT employees, their average base pay and age, the percentage of bachelor’s degree holders, and the percentage of those with senior executive service (SES) appointments (Table A1 in Appendix).<sup>11</sup> The rationales are as follows. For bureaus that belong to the same agency, the HR characteristics of the bureaus under the same agency are highly related to each other because a central office develops agency-wide HR policies and oversees policy implementations across the agency;<sup>12</sup> in other words, the decision-making authority of IT officials in the FAA is likely to be correlated with the characteristics of non-IT employees in the sister bureaus within the DOT. However, after accounting for bureau-level fixed effects and agency-specific linear time trends, it is unlikely that the characteristics of non-IT employees in the sister bureaus outside the FAA significantly affect the IT project performance of the FAA.<sup>13</sup>

## Results

Table 4 presents the main estimation results. Table A1 in Appendix presents the first-stage estimation with the IVs. Kleibergen-Paap LM tests show that the IVs are significantly correlated with the endogenous independent variable (IT Manager). In three of the four estimations in Table 4 (except Column 3), Hansen J tests fail to reject the null hypothesis that the IVs are significantly correlated with the residuals, satisfying the exclusion restriction conditions. These empirical tests broadly support the conceptual arguments regarding the relevance conditions and the exclusion restrictions of the IVs.

In Table 4, Columns 1-2 show that the level of decisionmaking authority of an IT unit is negatively associated with time overruns; the more IT managerial officials and supervisors a federal bureau has, the lower time overruns it achieves in its IT projects. Economically, a one-percentage point increase in the ranks of IT managers (e.g., from 10% to 11%) is associated with a decrease in average time overrun of project activities by 13.8 days (Column 1) and a decrease in total time overrun in the bureau by 160 days (Column 2). On the other hand, we did not find a significant effect on cost overrun (Columns 3-4). One explanation for this finding is that the efforts to resolve conflicts, mobilize resources, and remove barriers within and across projects may demand a substantial and costly commitment of managerial resources, which may offset cost reductions occurring from a decrease in time overruns.

We performed a post hoc analysis with the number of projects and contracts as potential moderators.<sup>14</sup> We choose these variables because public-sector organizations tend to be less scalable or responsive to increasing workload than for-profit firms, in part due to administrative rules and policies that they are obliged to follow (Feeney & Rainey, 2009; Manring, 1994; Rodrik & Zeckhauser, 1988). Table 5 suggests that the effect of the decision-making authority is more nuanced than one would expect. Specifically, when a bureau has more ongoing IT projects and external contracts, the impact of decision-making authority on total time overrun weakens. In Column 1, the coefficient of the interaction of IT Manager and the number of projects indicates that when a bureau has more than five projects (mean = 4.61), an increase in the share of IT managers actually leads to an increase in total time overruns (Figure 1). Similarly, Column 2 suggests that the effect of decision-making authority reverses when the number of contracts is over 67 (mean = 47.2). These results indicate that when the bureau undertakes too many ongoing IT projects, elevating more IT professionals to managerial or supervisory ranks increases time overruns.

A plausible explanation for the findings in Table 5 and Figure 1 is as follows. As discussed for Hypothesis 2, there are two major reasons for the negative effect of decision-making authority: (1) stakeholder complexity and (2) decision accountability and compliance. As the number of ongoing projects increases, so do the number of stakeholders and the amount of the compliance and accountability burden. Given the divergent, ambiguous, conflicting nature of stakeholders interests and goals and the high standards for accountability and transparency in federal IT projects, the complexity of managing these stakeholders and meeting burdensome compliance and oversight requirements increases exponentially as the number of projects grows (Meier, 2010). As the IT unit is endowed with more decision-making authority, IT managers assume more responsibilities to deal with project stakeholders such as Congress and are held more accountable for the decisions they make by the stakeholders. However, IT managers, compared to business-unit managers, may not be well-suited for managing complicated stakeholder relationships and navigating through complex compliance and oversight requirements. Therefore, when the number of projects grows, the costs associated with diverse stakeholder interests and the accountability burden outweigh the benefits that arise from the empowerment of the IT unit. Therefore, our findings demonstrate how the unique characteristics of the public sector influence the relationship between IT decisionmaking authority and IT project performance.

## Discussion and Conclusion

By integrating datasets for IT project performance and HR characteristics of the U.S. federal government, we obtained a new, interesting finding that the level of decision-making authority of the IT unit in a federal bureau is negatively associated with bureau-level time overruns in IT project delivery. In addition, our analyses find that the impact of such decision-making authority on time overruns reverses when there are too many ongoing IT projects in the bureau.

The present study makes important theoretical contributions to the IS literature. First, to the best of our knowledge, our study is the first to identify the positive effect of the level of an IT unit’s decision-making authority on IT project performance. While prior studies have investigated top management support, the CIO’s influence, team autonomy, and centralization/decentralization of decision rights as antecedents for IT project performance, the overall level of empowerment in the IT unit is a related but distinctly different theoretical construct. This construct, despite its theoretical and practical significance, has been understudied and undertheorized in the IS literature. This study takes a step forward toward a better understanding of the role of the IT unit’s decision-making authority in IT projects.

Second, our study is one of the few in IS research to look at the performance of IT projects in government. We offer unique theoretical contributions by incorporating the distinct characteristics of government into theorizing. Although the IS community has extensively studied IT project management, it has overlooked the differences in the public and private sectors in theorization. The extant literature implicitly assumes that research findings from the private sector would be generalizable to the public sector by default. We challenge this assumption and argue that the unique characteristics of public-sector organizations create theoretical tensions and warrant new theorizing and rethinking IT project performance in the public sector.

Take agile approaches as an example. One of the agile principles is self-organizing teams empowered by decisionmaking autonomy. Prior research finds that team autonomy (Lee & Xia, 2010) and decentralized decision rights (Tiwana, 2009) positively affect IT project performance. However, it is unclear if the same positive effect would be observed in public-sector IT projects, owing to the sector’s deeply ingrained culture of command-and-control, hierarchy, and risk aversion. Another example is project escalation, which prior studies try to understand with several theories such as avoidance theory, agency theory, prospect theory, and self-justification theory (Keil et al., 2000). Key drivers for project escalation derived from these theories include psychological self-justification, sunk cost effect, goal incongruency, and completion effect. While these constructs might still be useful for explaining project escalation in the public sector, some important constructs unique to the public sector are overlooked, such as public scrutiny for failed projects.

Our research indicates that the effect of the IT unit’s decision-making authority on IT project performance is more complex and nuanced than would be expected in the private sector. As managers interact with various stakeholders and function in a complex organizational environment to achieve a set of goals, theorizing their decision-making authority requires consideration of the unique organizational context in different sectors, such as a complex network of multiple stakeholders with different interests (e.g., Congress, interest groups), objective ambiguity in government, and onerous accountability and oversight requirements. Our findings reinforce the recent calls (e.g., Mishra & Browning, 2020; Mishra et al., 2016) for taking the public sector context more seriously in project management research.

Third, this research is one of the first empirical studies investigating organization-wide IT project performance rather than an individual project’s performance. Individual project performance is not automatically translated to the performance of an organization’s IT program or entire project portfolio, because one project’s success may come at the expense of that of others. In particular, IT programs typically encompass a set of interrelated IT projects, which compete for scarce organizational resources (Jiang et al., 2014). As such, effective program management requires seamless coordination and execution of multiple projects via optional resource allocation and the resolution of conflicting requirements among projects (Gregory et al., 2015).

Our study offers meaningful managerial and policy implications for practitioners. Our study suggests that policy makers should endow greater decision-making authority to IT units to improve the time performance of IT projects. At the same time, it is necessary to ensure that the number of project activities is not excessive; otherwise, the positive effect of the decision-making authority can be weakened or even reversed. This study also has policy implications for recruitment and career development for government IT personnel with respect to a balance between specialists and managers. More managerial positions might mean fewer opportunities for developing one’s career as a specialist. By contrast, an excessive focus on acquiring and developing specialists could have negative repercussions for IT project performance because of the IT unit’s lack of decisionmaking authority. Governments should take these factors into account when striking the right balance in the composition of their IT workforce.

This study carries a few limitations. While our data sources provide objective, high-quality datasets for federal human resources and IT projects, due to data availability, we are not able to identify which IT official is involved in which IT project. In addition, the data does not allow us to find what causes cost or time overruns in each individual project. Project-level investigations would offer additional insights beyond what this study is able to provide. Further, we do not have quantitative data on compliance or oversight-related bureaucracy, which would have allowed us to examine the relationship between the decisionmaking authority and compliance/accountability costs. Despite these limitations, our study reports an important finding on federal IT project management that has crucial implications for the benefit of the public.
