# A typology of user liability to IT addiction

Isaac Vaghe<sup>fi</sup>,\* Liette Lapointe<sup>†</sup> & Camille Boudreau-Pinsonneault<sup>‡</sup>

\*School of Management, State University of New York at Binghamton, Binghamton, New York USA, email: svaghe<sup>fi</sup>@binghamton.edu, <sup>†</sup>Desautels Faculty of Management, McGill University, Montreal, Canada, email: liette.lapointe@mcgill.ca, and <sup>‡</sup>Department of Neuroscience, McGil University, Montreal, Canada, email: camille.boudreau-pinsonneault@mail.mcgill.ca

Abstract. To date, information systems (IS) research mainly has provided a monolithic view of information technology (IT) use, considering it to be a desired behaviour with positive outcomes. However, given the dramatic increase in the use of technology during the last few vears. susceptibility to IT addiction is increas ingly becoming an important issue for technology users and IS researchers. In this paper, we report the results of a study that focuses on identifying variations in user liability to ITaddiction, which re<sup>fl</sup>ects the susceptibility of individual users to develop IT addiction. First, a review of the literature in different disciplines (e.g. health, psychology and IS) allows us to better understand the concepts of IT addiction and liability to addiction. The literature review also provides an overview of the antecedents and consequences associated with IT addiction. Then, building on the analysis of 15 in-depth interviews and 182 exploratory open-ended surveys collected from smartphone users, we apply the concept of liability to addiction in the IT use context and propose a typological theory of user liability to IT addiction. Our typology reveals <sup>fi</sup>ve ideal types; each can be associated to a user pro<sup>fi</sup>le (ADDICT, FANATIC, HIGHLY ENGAGED, REGULAR AND THOUGHTFUL). Building upon both the extant literature and our results, we put forth propositions to extend the theoret ical contributions of the study. We <sup>fi</sup>nally discuss the contributions and implications of our paper for research and practice.

Keywords: IT addiction, liability, typology, user pro<sup>fi</sup>le, smartphone

## INTRODUCTION

Despite the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant number of studies on information technology (IT) usage (Davis, 1989; Venkatesh et al., 2003), little attention has been paid to identifying and understanding excessive and problematic forms of ITusage. The extant literature mostly provides a monolithic view of the IT use phenomenon, treating all users’ behavioural manifestations equally (Bagayogo et al., 2014). In most cases, IT use is considered a desirable behaviour that is typically the ultimate goal of system designers and implementers. Accordingly, from an information systems (IS) research perspective, IT usage is referred to as a common indicator of IS success (Delone &

McLean, 2003). Although in most cases continued IT use helps individuals improve their pro ductivity and results in signi<sup>fi</sup>cant economic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts for organizations (Jasperson et al., 2005), the use of technology – in its various forms – can have a <sup>fl</sup>ip side and lead to undesired outcomes especially when it is excessive, compulsive and uncontrolled. In fact, anecdotal and research evidence from psychology, health and IS research hints that excessive and compul sive IT use tends to cause serious challenges for individuals, organizations and even societies (LaRose et al., 2003; Block, 2008; Turel et al., 2011b). As an example, pathological use of the internet was found to undermine academic performance, create social dif<sup>fi</sup>culties and cause several psychological problems such as depression and loneliness (Caplan, 2002; Billieux et al., 2008).

The adoption and use of IT has dramatically increased during the last few years (Alexander, 2011) and has become a social phenomenon (Turel & Serenko, 2010; Brus, 2013). Recent statistics show that the use of internet, social media and mobile-based technologies is at an all-time high (Internet World Statistics, 2014). For example, almost three-quarters of adult online users have a pro<sup>fi</sup>le on at least one social networking site (Brenner & Smith, 2013; Davenport et al., 2014). Also, the growing ubiquity and immediate accessibility of internet and social networks, especially provided by mobile technologies, have expanded users’ opportunities for long-term engagement with technology (Lundquist et al., 2014). As an example, a survey of users in eight industrialized countries showed that most individuals (84%) cannot spend a day without using their phones and one-<sup>fi</sup>fth of them check their phones every 20 min (Gibbs, 2012), even when there is no sound or noti<sup>fi</sup>cation. Despite the worrying levels of ITuse recently reported (Gibbs, 2012), with the exception of some recent efforts (e.g. Turel & Serenko, 2010; Turel et al., 2011b: Xu et al., 2012: Lee et al., 2014: Turel. 2014). there is little research in the extant IS literature on the excessive and compulsive use of IT, and there is no study that speci<sup>fi</sup>cally looks at the susceptibility of users to develop addiction. Given the increase in the use of technology and the potentially disturbing effects associated with IT addiction, understanding the risks of developing IT addiction and its associated impacts is timely and critical.

In this study, we borrow from human genetics and drug addiction literature to introduce the concept of liability to addiction (Falconer, 1965; Dawes et al., 2000; Vanyukov et al., 2003; Conway et al., 2010; Vanyukov et al., 2012), which corresponds to the susceptibility of individuals to develop an addiction. Based on this stream of research, all users would be susceptible to develop IT addiction, but at different degrees. Hence, we extend and empirically examine variations of liability to IT addiction, as manifested by users. Compared with existing addiction research that tend to provide a dichotomous view of addiction (addicts vs. non-addicts divided by a threshold), studying liability to IT addiction helps to understand the variations in behaviours exhibited by a population of users and explains users’ relative susceptibility to develop addiction. In this study, we aim to answer three important research questions: (1) What is user liability to IT addiction? (2) What are the variations in IT addiction liability among users? (3) What is the nature of the antecedents, behaviours and consequences associated with each type of IT addiction liability?

To answer these questions, we conducted a study to investigate user liability to IT addiction. Our study involved interviews and a qualitative written questionnaire (open-ended survey), collected from smartphone users. The qualitative analysis of the interviews <sup>fi</sup>rst allowed us to clarify the underlying dimensions of liability to IT addiction. Then, building on this analysis, we proceeded to qualitative and quantitative analyses of the survey data to develop a typology that re<sup>fl</sup>ects the variations in user susceptibility to IT addiction within the population of users. For each type of user liability to IT addiction, we conducted further analyses to identify the associ ated user pro<sup>fi</sup>le and developed propositions.

This study has contributions to and implications for the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld. First, it introduces in IS research the concept of liability to addiction, which is critical to a better understanding of the susceptibility of users to develop IT addiction and identi<sup>fi</sup>es its underlying dimensions. Second, this study extends the theoretical insights on IT addiction by developing a typology of user liability to IT addiction that includes <sup>fi</sup>ve ideal types. Third, our analysis identi<sup>fi</sup>es the associated user pro<sup>fi</sup>le for each type, including antecedents, usage behaviours and consequences. The paper further extends the existing knowledge by providing a proposition for each type of user liability to IT addiction. Our results hence contribute to the emerging <sup>fi</sup>eld of IT addiction that seeks to portray a more comprehensive picture of IT use, one that takes into account both normal and problematic IT usage. From a managerial standpoint, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of our study could be used to identify users’ level of liability to IT addiction and even design mechanisms that effectively detect, prevent or treat users who show higher levels of liability to IT addiction.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. In the next section, we review the literature and provide a foundation for studying user liability to IT addiction. Then, we describe and explain the methodology used in this study. The results section presents our conceptualizing of user liability to ITaddiction, and the typology resulting from our qualitative and quantitative analyses of the open survey. Next, we develop propositions for each type of user liability to IT addiction. Finally, we discuss the implications of our study for research and practice and identify avenues for future research

## LITERATURE REVIEW

In this section, we review the existing literature on IT addiction from IS and other reference disciplines (e.g. health and psychology), to provide a de<sup>fi</sup>nition of IT addiction, to review its ante cedents and consequences and to introduce the concept of user liability to ITaddiction (for details on the review method, see Appendix A).

## Information technology addiction

The concept of addiction is rooted in the psychology literature where it has been referred to as a loss of control over a behaviour that serves either to produce pleasure or to escape from internal discomforts (Potenza, 2006) and that is typically associated with negative consequences (Goodman, 1990) For example, addiction to drugs and alcohol has been found to cause cancer, mental illness and social dysfunctions and/or to result in convictions (Cartwright, 2000; Volkow & Li, 2005; Potenza, 2006). Despite the focus on substance addiction in classic addiction research, this concept also has been used to describe non-substance addiction such as gambling addiction, compulsive shopping and eating disorders (Becona et al., 1996; Davis & Claridge, 1998; Blaszczynski & Nower, 2002).

In recent years, IT has been found to be a source of addiction. In spite of clear advantages of using technology to facilitate tasks and speed up communications, it has been shown that the dramatic increase in adoption and use of ITand communicative tools such as computers, mobile devices and social media may cause some dif<sup>fi</sup>culties for users (Chóliz, 2010). Similar to substance addiction, excessive and compulsive use of technology has been shown to be linked with risky behaviours such as ignoring important professional and personal life duties. Also, recent neurobiological <sup>fi</sup>ndings showed that there are similar neural mechanisms subserving substance and technology-related types of addictions (Turel et al., 2014). As a result, concepts and theories developed for substance addiction research (e.g. alcohol abuse, excessive smoking and opium addiction) were extensively borrowed from other disciplines (e.g. psychology, health and neuroscience) to theorize IT addiction and bring novel insights on excessive and compulsive IT use (Young, 1998; LaRose et al., 2003; Turel et al., 2011b).

Information technology addiction is a broad concept that has been extended to many excessive IT use behaviours such as online game addiction (Xu et al., 2012), internet addiction (Young, 1998; Armstrong et al., 2000; Byun et al., 2009), media consumption addiction (LaRose, 2010) and online auction addiction (Turel et al., 2011b). In this study, we drew on our review of the literature and the existing de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of addiction in previous research (Table 1) and identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the elements that were repeatedly mentioned in different conceptualizations of IT addiction. These elements are helpful in identifying a problematic behaviour and address both the psychological and physical aspects of addiction-driven behaviours. We argue that ITaddiction is a type of behaviour that embeds four essential elements: (1) feelings of dependency<sup>1</sup>; (2) excessive use; (3) failure to stop; and (4) signi<sup>fi</sup>cant negative outcomes. Building upon these elements and drawing upon the existing conceptualization of addiction, in this study, we define IT addiction as ‘a psychological dependency on IT that involves excessive and compulsive use despite signi<sup>fi</sup>cant negative consequences’.

## Antecedents and consequences of information technology addiction

In the last few years, research on technology addiction has increased in the IS discipline. Some efforts have been made to integrate the addiction construct into mainstream IS theory and to examine some of the antecedents and consequences that are associated with it. Using the Technology Acceptance Model, Turel, Serenko and Giles (2011b) showed how addiction may alter the belief system of users and increase their intention to use IT by changing perceptions about usefulness, ease of use and enjoyment of IT. In another study, perceived enjoyment and prior use of social networks were found to facilitate the development of IT habits, which can eventually lead to addiction (Turel & Serenko, 2012). Xu et al. (2012) proposed a model that illustrates the key functional needs that encourage online game addiction (such as the need for relationships and advancement), as well as mechanisms that could counterbalance them (such as parental monitoring and increasing the perceived costs of online games). Turel & Serenko (2010) also suggested that some characteristics of mobile phones (e.g. ubiquity and conve nience of email access) represent possible reasons for their widespread usage and even addiction, but they did not test these effects in their study. In terms of consequences, IS research has shown that IT addiction may increase the perception of work overload and decrease the commitment of employees, leading to technology–family and work–family con<sup>fl</sup>ict (Turel et al., 2011a), technostress (Lee et al., 2014) and burnout (MacCormick et al., 2012).

Despite the recent attention in the literature on the causes and consequences of ITaddiction, this research stream is still developing, and there are several calls to further investigate the fac tors associated with problematic technology use (Byun et al., 2009; Turel et al., 2011b; Xu et al., 2012). In this study, we aim to broaden the pool of potential antecedents and consequences of IT addiction by integrating research in the psychology, health and computer science literature. Based on our review of the literature across disciplines, we discuss three types of antecedents (demographics, individual differences and technology features) and three types of consequences (social, professional and well-being) associated with IT addiction (Table 2).

## Antecedents

Early studies focused on the demographics of users and tried to establish links between technology addiction and factors such as gender, age, education and economic status (Shotton, 1991; Young, 1998; Grif<sup>fi</sup>ths et al., 2004). For instance, early results showed that computer and internet addicts were usually young male introverts (Brenner, 1997; Grif<sup>fi</sup>ths et al., 1999; Morahan-Martin & Schumacher, 2000) who were computer savvy and educated (Shotton, 1991). Additional studies resulted in contradictory <sup>fi</sup>ndings. For instance, it was found that women could be 20% more addicted to the internet than men (Young, 1998), while other studies showed no speci<sup>fi</sup>c distinction between different demographic groups (O’Reilly, 1996; Armstrong et al., 2000). Such inconsistencies motivated researchers to look for other drivers of technology addiction, i.e. beyond socio-demographic factors.

Researchers further scrutinized the individual differences between users and their role in the development of addiction. In this stream of research, many examined the role of personality traits and dysphoric moods on the development of internet and computer addiction (e.g. Marlatt et al., 1988; Kreek, 1992; DeJong et al., 1993; Nelson-Zlupko et al., 1995; Caplan, 2002). For example, Grif<sup>fi</sup>ths & Dancaster (1995) argued that users with type A personalities (e.g. competitive, time urgent and more hostile) are more susceptible to addiction. Other studies linked depression, loneliness, shyness, self-esteem, social anxiety and impulsivity to problematic internet use (Armstrong et al., 2000; Caplan, 2002, 2006; Ha et al., 2007). Despite the abundance of studies on personality types, a review of the literature shows that, except for loneliness, there is no evidence that establishes a clear link between the personality of users and technology addiction (Byun et al., 2009). This calls for further investigation of these factors, especially in new technology use contexts.

It should be noted that ‘not all ITartifacts are equally addictive’ (Turel & Serenko, 2012). There has been growing interest in the role of the technology itself on addiction, meaning that speci<sup>fi</sup>c technology features could make IT attractive and addictive. As Green<sup>fi</sup>eld (1999) argued, the combination of features available in a technology (e.g. speed and stimulation potency) can provide a ‘highly psychoactive experience’ that can change users’ moods and in<sup>fl</sup>uence their behaviours (Morahan-Martin & Schumacher, 2000). Depending on its characteristics and features, users tend to engage with a technology and become dependent as long as it remains interesting (Chóliz, 2010). For instance, the communicative capacity of the internet and the high level of accessibility to information and services have been found to accelerate the subjective passage of time and provide a potential for addiction (Shaffer, 1996; Grif<sup>fi</sup>ths et al., 1999). Also, online gambling addiction was found to exist at a higher level compared with real gambling owing to the anonymity and accessibility provided by the technology (Grif<sup>fi</sup>ths & Parke, 2002). In addition, the results of a study by Ng & Wiemer-Hastings (2005) showed that advances in three-dimensional graphics and virtual representation are reasons why game players develop addiction to online games.

## Consequences

As discussed earlier, IT addiction generally causes new dif<sup>fi</sup>culties or exacerbates existing problems (Beard, 2005). First, a visible consequence of technology addiction is the effect it has on the social life of users when IT use brings dif<sup>fi</sup>culties such as a lack of communication with others, relationship con<sup>fl</sup>icts and marital discord (Goldberg, 1996; O’Reilly, 1996; Young, 2004). As users spend most of their time using technology and connecting to people in the online environment, they have less time and a decreased tendency to have real-life socia interactions (Beard, 2005). This may limit their interpersonal relationships substantially. Similar effects have been seen in different contexts such as video gaming (Lo et al., 2005), instant messaging (Huang & Leung, 2009) and cybersex (Cavaglion, 2009).

Second, depending on the intensity and purpose of use, IT can have negative effects on users’ professional life. In the case of technology addiction, excessive use of IT for non-workrelated purposes (e.g. personal email, chat and online games) can have adverse effects on the productivity and performance of users (Yellowlees & Marks, 2007) and increase the likelihood of job turnover for professionals and academic failure for students (Davis et al., 2002; Porter & Kakabadse, 2006; Peters & Bodkin, 2007). The amount of time and effort spent on non-productive ITuse, and the resulting distractions usually take away from the time users should spend on valuable tasks and is likely to lead to professional problems over time (Young, 2004).

Finally, previous research has shown that long-term stimulation resulting from technology addiction can undermine both the mental and physical well-being of users. As users become more dependent on technology, the resulting positive emotions (e.g. excitement and enjoyment)

become entangled with technology use. However, when emotions become saturated, increasing IT usage will no longer satisfy the addict’s desire (Beard, 2005). The associated frustration may lead to mood disorders, loneliness and depression (Young & Rodgers, 1998; Murali & George, 2007; Goodman, 2008). Also, disconnection from family and friends – caused by the overuse of technology – can result in similar disorders such as loneliness (Kraut et al., 1998). In addition, prolonged use of technology and lack of physical activity leads to physical problems such as fatigue or lack of sleep (Douglas et al., 2008). Long-term patterns of sleep disorder and fatigue may increase the chances of other severe impairments such as backache, neck pain, eyestrain and carpal/radial tunnel syndromes (Young, 1999).

## Liability to addiction

Early on, scholars have measured addiction using lean measures and classi<sup>fi</sup>ed individuals as addicts vs. non-addicts, based on diagnostic criteria (Shaffer, 1996; LaRose et al., 2003; Yang & Tung, 2007). In this dichotomous view of addiction, there is an underlying assumption stating that the characteristics of behaviours are similar within the two groups, i.e. for non-addicts (individuals below a given diagnostic threshold) or addicts (beyond this threshold) (Conway et al., 2010). Based on such a view of addiction, there is no distinction between a user who falls slightly over the threshold and someone who experiences severe addiction. At the same time, all users who are below the addiction threshold will be considered the same. For example, no action is usually taken by clinicians and policy makers for non-addicts (Conway et al., 2010; Vanyukov, 2012), even though some of these users may carry a high risk of becoming addicts in the future.

Despite the popularity of this approach in addiction research (Beard, 2005; Murali & George, 2007), some studies in general addiction research have shown meaningful differences among individuals within each group of users, whether below or above the diagnostic threshold (Conway et al., 2010). In other words, while we have insights into the between-group differences, little is known about within-group differences among users and their behaviours. Furthermore, although there are studies in the general addiction literature hinting that the behaviours of individuals within the cohort of addicts or non-addicts are not equal by nature (Porter & Kakabadse, 2006; Conway et al., 2010), they do not provide detailed information or an in-depth analysis of these behaviours. One element that varies within groups is the susceptibility to addiction. Understanding the differences in susceptibility could inform and advance extant IT addiction theories and has important practical value.

To study these variations, the concept of ‘liability’ is highly relevant. Indeed, liability to addic tion refers to the degree to which an individual is prone to addictive usage. In extant addictior literature, liability has been used as a multifactorial concept representing the property of a behaviour that is associated with the development of disorders (Vanyukov, 2012). More specif ically, the concept is intended ‘to express not only the individual innate tendency to develop or contract the disease, i.e. his or her susceptibility in the usual sense, but also the whole combi nation of external circumstances that make him more or less likely to develop the disease (Falconer, 1965, p. 52). Based on this assertion, liability can be conceptualized as a multifac torial (non-substance-speci<sup>fi</sup>c) construct that takes into account the combined effect of differ ent factors (e.g. social and psychological) related to the individual and the environmenta condition around usage (Dawes et al., 2000; Vanyukov, 2012). While in psychology, neuroscience and health, different labels have been used to discuss the development of disorders (such as susceptibility, vulnerability or resilience), liability has been accepted as a more heuristic, consistent and parsimonious construct to represent the addiction-proneness property of a behaviour (Vanyukov et al., 2003).

Extending liability to the IT addiction context allows us to have a more granular view of users behaviours (their usage in different forms) and understand the heterogeneity among users in their susceptibility in developing and exhibiting IT addiction. Compared with the core concept of addiction that, by de<sup>fi</sup>nition, deals with users with excessive and compulsive behaviours (i.e. addicts who fall above the threshold of diagnostic criteria), liability is a much more inclusive concept (Iacono et al., 2008; Vanyukov, 2012), including those who show no sign of the disorder and those who exhibit severe ones (Vanyukov et al., 2012). In other words, in the IT addiction context, liability would apply to the entire user population and their IT use behaviours, rather than just those who suffer from addiction at a given point in time.

In the general addiction literature, the liability construct has been used to investigate the likely development of several addiction types, including drug abuse (Collins et al., 1984), work addiction (Robinson & Post, 1997) and stimulant addiction (Foltin & Fischman, 1991). Despite the theoretical advances in understanding liability in different contexts (Hicks et al., 2012; Conway et al., 2006: Neale et al., 2006). owing to the complex. latent and multifactorial nature of the construct, the assessment of liability has been shown to be a methodological challenge (Dawes et al., 2000; Vanyukov et al., 2003, 2012). As Falconer (1965) argues, liability, once measured, can provide us a graded scale for the degree of normality or of affectedness, with these two categories divided by a threshold. Accordingly, to overcome the challenges in quantifying liability, some researchers have focused on the gradations of normality or of affectedness and identi<sup>fi</sup>ed two underlying dimensions that help assess liability (Conway et al., 2010; Vanyukov et al., 2012). First, the gradation of normality shows the expected variations that exist in risk (also referred to as propensity) of a behaviour turning into severe addiction. Second, the gradation of affectedness (also referred to as severity) corresponds to the various degrees of severity of addiction manifested by an individual (Robinson & Post, 1997; Conway et al., 2010; Vanyukov et al., 2012). While propensity refers to the probability or the risk of addiction being developed below the threshold of addiction (the subthreshold phenotypes), severity refers to the degree of obsessive and compulsive behaviours exhibited by an individual, when such behaviours fal above the threshold of addiction (the suprathreshold phenotypes). For example, in the case of severity, the Addiction Severity Index (McLellan et al., 1980) was proposed to evaluate the need for addiction treatment within the key areas of functioning (e.g. medical, legal and employment). Another instrument (Substance Problem Scale) was developed by Dennis et al. (2003). This scale consists of items for diagnosing substance addiction in addition to other screening items such as the amount of weekly usage, complaints from peers and continued use despite confrontations. Other examples of existing scales for severity are Drug Use Screening Inventory, Global Appraisal of Individual Needs (GAIN), Severity of Alcohol Dependence Questionnaire and Substance Dependence Severity

In the case of propensity, the instruments for measuring the risks of developing addiction are few and far apart (Conway et al., 2010). Still, an instrument (Transmissible Liability Index) was put together by the Center for Education and Drug Abuse Research, using items from numerous psychological and psychiatric scales to characterize users’ behaviours/personalities and thei likelihood of substance use disorder (Kirisci et al., 2009; Vanyukov et al., 2009).

Despite the existence of measures for propensity and severity, these dimensions have been generally looked at separately, and there is no single instrument that combines the measurement of both propensity and severity, for example to generate a total score for liability. Such a take on liability perpetuates the dichotomous view of addiction where severity only applies to users who fall above the diagnostic threshold (i.e. addicts) and propensity to those below the diagnostic threshold (i.e. non-addicts). Therefore, only looking at either propensity or severity will not be suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to investigate the variations between users’ behaviours. The need for a <sup>fi</sup>ner-grained understanding of liability is further justi<sup>fi</sup>ed by existing research in IS discipline that has treated addiction as a continuous variable and, thus, did not specify a threshold for problematic IT use (e.g. Turel & Serenko, 2012; Soror et al., 2015).

Accordingly, in this study, we extend propensity and severity to the IT use context by consid ering them as continuous indices that could vary from very low to very high. We argue that, in the context of ITaddiction, propensity refers to the risk of developing extreme addiction. Accordingly, a higher degree of propensity means a higher point along the X axis. At the same time, severity corresponds to the gravity of the compulsive behaviours that is manifested by a user, and higher severity translates into a higher point along the Yaxis. Compared with the traditional dichotomous view of addiction, in this conceptualization, a degree of propensity and severity can be attributed to any type of usage whether above or below a theoretical threshold

Extending the propensity and severity dimensions can be helpful in providing an in-depth understanding of users’ behaviours and their liability to addiction. Although past research has indicated that severity varies among users who are above the threshold (Conway et al., 2010), a more granular understanding of the level of propensity to addiction can also be speci<sup>fi</sup>ed for these users. At the same time, for users who are below the threshold, while there are variations in their propensity to addiction, it would also be valuable to understand the variation in severity, although the level of severity for these users is expected to be lower compared with that of addicts. Hence, our study focuses on the liability of users to IT addiction by studying the meaningful variations that exist in users’ liability levels, by examining both the propensity and severity levels for all users.

## RESEARCH METHODOLOGY

Given the aims of our study and in light of our literature review, a mixed-method strategy (Creswell, 2003; Teddlie & Tashakkori, 2003) was chosen to provide a rich understanding of liability and develop novel theoretical accounts about the variations that exist between users and their liability to IT addiction. The purpose of using a mixed-method approach was developmental (Venkatesh et al., 2013) and aimed <sup>fi</sup>rst at identifying liability dimensions in the context of IT addiction and then developing a typology of IT users.

Because we do not have a clear conceptualization of severity and propensity in the context of IT addiction, we <sup>fi</sup>rst used an exploratory approach and conducted interviews in order to determine these dimensions in the context of ITaddiction. Then, to develop a typology of user liability to IT addiction that re<sup>fl</sup>ects the existing variations in users’ susceptibility to develop such addiction, we sequentially used qualitative and quantitative analysis techniques to make sense of the data gathered using an open-ended survey. Finally, we conducted additional multi-method analyses to identify the user pro<sup>fi</sup>les associated with each liability type, including antecedents, behaviours and consequences.

## Context of the study

As noted in previous research (Turel et al., 2011b), because of the broadness of the topic, examining general ITaddiction in one study is impossible. Thus, in this study, we collected data in one speci<sup>fi</sup>c context: smartphone usage. Smartphones are portable tools that combine a mobile phone with a collection of software applications, which were traditionally accessed via computers (e.g. email, Web browser and social networks) as well as other novel applications solely designed for this device (e.g. location-based apps) (Wei, 2008). In line with general technology trends, smartphone adoption has recently experienced rapid global growth. Statistics show that more than 1.75 billion people worldwide own smartphones (Emarketer.com, 2014), and the average daily use of the smartphone ranges from 10 to 200 times with a length of use between 10 and 250 s (Falaki et al., 2010). Popular media also stressed the excessive use of and dependency on smartphone and its embedded applications by coining terms such as crackberry (constant checking of Blackberry email) and nomophobia (extreme anxiety and fear of losing a phone). Given the global growth in smartphone usage and wide range of usage (very low to very high), using smartphone as a context for our study on liability to IT addiction was deemed timely and relevant.

Smartphone usage has received recent attention in IS research (e.g. Soror et al., 2015) and was deemed a valuable phenomenon to study given the objectives of our research. It should be noted however that by focusing on smartphones, we take a slightly different approach compared with addiction studies that solely focus on one system or application (e.g. internet, email, social network and game). Indeed, smartphones represent a collection of several applications, some of which have been found to be addictive. Smartphone addiction hence refers to the collective pathological interactions between user and applications embedded in smartphones. Despite the limited empirical studies focusing on smartphone addiction, research evidence on the problematic use of smartphone and mobile phone is growing (Billieux et al., 2008; MacCormick et al., 2012; Salehan & Negahban, 2013; Chiu, 2014; Lee et al., 2014; Lundquist et al., 2014), and our study will add to this stream of research.

## Data collection

In this study, we relied on two rounds of data collection. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst round, we used a theoretica sampling strategy (Patton, 2002) to select eight female and seven male heavy smartphone users for face-to-face, semi-structured interviews. The interviewees were selected from faculty members and graduate students between the ages of 26 and 38 in a large North American University. To conduct our interviews, we used an interview guide (Appendix C) that was developed based on the extant literature. This interview guide was further re<sup>fi</sup>ned in the <sup>fi</sup>eld through three pilot interviews with two users and with a qualitative IS researcher who is an expert on the subject.

The <sup>fi</sup>nal interview guide included 21 open-ended questions (with some probes and prompts that could be used as needed). It began with a very general question about smartphone usage and then moved on to more speci<sup>fi</sup>c questions about the antecedents, consequences and use-related behaviours. We also used an eight-item questionnaire (borrowed from Young’s, 1998, study) to assess users’ dependency, which covered the symptoms described in Appendix B. The interview guide ended with an open question that allowed interviewees to provide additional insights on smartphone usage and addiction. Each interview took between 20 and 40 min, was recorded in its entirety and was transcribed verbatim. Overall, interview data were analysed and used to better delineate liability to addiction in the context of smartphone use by identifying its propensity and severity dimensions.

We then proceeded to the development of an exploratory written questionnaire that was based on both the insights derived from the extant literature and the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from the analysis of the interviews. Although they are not typically used in IS research, an open-ended survey was particularly useful in the context of our study because it allowed users to re<sup>fl</sup>ect on their own behaviours in a less intrusive way (Lewis-Beck et al., 2004). The survey also helped us obtain rich and detailed qualitative data from a large sample of users. Hence, because we are studying a phenomenon that may not be perceived as socially desirable, such a qualitative survey was deemed effective because it allowed for collecting more honest responses from a large sample and maintaining full anonymity of the respondents.

The <sup>fi</sup>rst version of the questionnaire comprised <sup>fi</sup>ve open-ended questions about smartphone usage and its associated outcomes. Nine additional questions allowed us to probe the respondents in order to identify their relevant individual characteristics and perceptions about smartphone features and capabilities. Other questions focusing on liability dimensions and sub-dimensions were added. As we did for the interviews, Young’s (1998) questionnaire was used to assess dependency. While this questionnaire has been used to assess addiction in several studies (Byun et al., 2009), we use it in a way similar to Young, i.e. in order to determine whether respondents were dependent on their smartphone. To check for conten and face validities, we pretested the questionnaire with two senior researchers and then with six smartphone users who had pro<sup>fi</sup>les similar to those of our respondents (university students). The pilot group was asked to re<sup>fl</sup>ect on the questions and comment on their readability, clarity and relevance. Some items were reworded based on the pilot results (see Appendix D for the survey questions).

The survey was administered to all 275 students that were enrolled in an undergraduate-level class at a large North American university. One hundred and eighty-two questionnaires were returned (for a response rate of 66.2%). Respondents were between 17 and 29 years old; 43.4% were male and 54.9% female (three respondents did not specify their gender). On average, it took 30 min to <sup>fi</sup>ll out the questionnaire. Students voluntarily participated in the survey; no incentive was given. We chose the student population because it is typical of the fastestgrowing smartphone user segment (Test Kitchen, 2010) and represents a large proportion of current smartphone users (Smith et al., 2010). Indeed, based on Google statistics for the USA, Germany, France, UK and Japan, it represents between a quarter and one-third of smartphone users in developed countries (Google Think, 2013). A recent survey indicates that more than two-thirds of US college students currently own smartphones, which should increase to 90% by 2016 (Fredricksen, 2012).

## Data coding and analysis

We first focused on the interview data and used NVivo9 (QSR International., Melbourne. Australia) to support the content analysis of the transcripts. Data were coded using the standardized methods of qualitative thematic analysis (Patton, 2002). We relied on the interviews to achieve a meaningful conceptualization of the liability dimensions (propensity and severity) in the contex of smartphone use. First, we devised codes for each of the factors that appeared meaningfully salient and could potentially be associated to liability (e.g. personality traits, dependency, use behaviours, intensity of use, technology characteristics and negative consequences). A second round of coding involved grouping and combining the relevant coding categories with the same content and meaning and developing new categories that were, as appropriate, associated with user liability to IT addiction. The analytical process was repeated until saturation was achieved (Patton, 2002), at which point additional analysis repeatedly supported previously made interpretations. In reporting the analysis, the most revealing quotes were selected to illustrate the results. We made sure that the quoted interviewees remained anonymous by assigning a single code to each interviewee. Overall, the analysis of interviews revealed that liability can be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by mapping the elements of ITaddiction to its underlying dimensions.

## Typology development

Once we had developed a better understanding of liability in the context of IT addiction, we proceeded with a qualitative analysis of the survey data to build a typology (Doty & Glick, 1994) that would re<sup>fl</sup>ect the different types of user liability to IT addiction. The use of typologies is not uncommon in IS research. For example, a similar approach has been pursued to investigate multidimensional and interdependent phenomena in different contexts such as types of trust in e-commerce relationships (McKnight et al., 2002) or types of control in IS development projects (Cram & Brohman, 2013). As de<sup>fi</sup>ned by Doty & Glick (1994), a typology is composed of ‘a conceptually derived, interrelated set of ideal types’, ‘each of which represents con<sup>fi</sup>gura tions of a set of dimensions’ (p. 232). In this way, a typology is different from a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation, which is simply ‘an exhaustive set of mutually exclusive categories’ (Shaw et al., 2005, p. 10). Typologies allow researchers to organize complex relationships that exist between factors and associated outcomes (Doty & Glick, 1994). It also integrates the <sup>fi</sup>ndings into coherent theoretical accounts in a parsimonious manner (Fiss, 2011).

In a typology, ideal types are constructed based on a unique combination of dimensions. In general, there are three ways to specify ideal types (Doty & Glick, 1994). First, ideal types can be modelled based on the theoretical speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of dimensions made by expert raters. Second, the extremes of the dimensions (low to high) can be used to model ideal types. In both methods, the researcher will <sup>fi</sup>rst deductively construct ideal types and then use empirical data to assess the <sup>fi</sup>t between the conceptualization of the ideal types and the observations (Bailey, 1994). The third method is to empirically specify ideal types based on a qualitative assessment of the data. In this case, the aim was to <sup>fi</sup>rst empirically construct ideal types and then to conceptualize its theoretical speci<sup>fi</sup>cations.

Given the nature of the study, we followed the third suggested approach. In order to identify the variations in user liability to IT addiction (i.e. ideal types), we used the dimensions of liability (propensity and severity) identi<sup>fi</sup>ed via the qualitative analysis of our interviews. Based on this analysis (as described later), for propensity, we took into account users’ dependency and self-regulation levels. To specify severity, we considered the intensity of usage and the magni tude of negative consequences. Each dimension can be situated on a continuum, varying from very low to very high. Then, through a qualitative assessment of our survey data, we empirically built a typology of user liability to IT addiction that embeds a unique combination of these dimensions, along <sup>fi</sup>ve different user types.

Afterwards, in order to validate this typology and re<sup>fi</sup>ne our assessment of the ideal types, we relied upon cluster analysis (available in SPSS software, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Cluster analysis refers to a set of technique that aims to assign observations into distinct groups, based on high homogeneity within a group and high heterogeneity between groups (Romesburg, 2004). In the context of our study, clustering techniques were used to identify clusters<sup>2</sup> that can be distinguished within our data. Our strategy was to compare the results of cluster analysis with the initial typology developed through the qualitative analysis. The variables used for cluster analysis were similar to the dimensions and sub-dimensions of liability, which were used in the qualitative analysis and the initial typology.

For the purpose of cluster analysis, each user’s data point was represented by a vector of four variables or liability sub-dimensions (dependency, intensity of use, self-regulation and magnitude of negative consequences). Following guidelines by Hair et al. (2006) and Kaufman & Rousseeuw (2009), we <sup>fi</sup>rst conducted a hierarchical agglomerative clustering (HAC) and applied Ward’s minimum variance technique to specify the number of clusters (see Appendix E for more details on the cluster analysis). Thus, we input the four variables into the cluster analysis and then observed clustering coef<sup>fi</sup>cients, which basically show the overall proximity between data points in each cluster. A lower coef<sup>fi</sup>cient means that clusters have highe homogeneity. To determine the number of clusters, we used a plot that illustrates the change in the coef<sup>fi</sup>cients as a function of the number of clusters. From this plot, we chose the optimum number of clusters in a way that adding an extra cluster does not provide suf<sup>fi</sup>cient improvement to the model, creating an elbow. Once the number of clusters was determined, we used a K-means clustering technique to assign data points to clusters (Burns & Burns, 2008). Furthermore, we used analysis of variance (ANOVA) to check the null hypothesis that ‘there are no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences between the cluster centroids’. Upon the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of clusters, we compared and contrasted these results with the ideal types identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the previous round of analysis. All in all, using the results of both qualitative and cluster analyses, we proposed a typology of user liability to IT addiction types.

Finally, for each liability type, we analysed the survey data to identify the associated user pro<sup>fi</sup>le (which consists of antecedents, behaviours and consequences). Where appropriate, we again used thematic qualitative analysis techniques to analyse the qualitative data collected using the open-ended survey to complement the results of quantitative analysis (Appendix F).

## STUDY FINDINGS

## Conceptualizing liability to information technology addiction: propensity and severity

The interview data analysis gave us some insights on user liability and how it can be de<sup>fi</sup>ned in the context of IT addiction. To clearly conceptualize user liability to IT addiction, we focused on the two known underlying dimensions of liability: propensity and severity. Based on our review of the literature, we know that propensity refers to the risk of developing IT addiction by a user. At the same time, severity refers to the degree of obsessive and compulsive use manifested by that user. Based on our analysis and the evidence provided by our data, it appears that, in the context of IT addiction, propensity can be conceptualized using ‘dependency’ and ‘selfregulation’ and that severity depends on the ‘intensity of IT usage’ and the ‘magnitude of negative consequences’ experienced by users.

With regard to user propensity to ITaddiction, our qualitative data analysis <sup>fi</sup>rst suggests that it is associated with a user’s dependency on technology, which can be de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a need that is so strong that it becomes necessary to have access to that technology in order to function properly:

I guess there are different components to smartphone addiction. One part would be you are reliant on it, reliant on it in a behavioral sense that you feel an urge to have it … like a companion, right. You rely on it more and more and that thing becomes an unhealthy relationship with the device, in which case it’s just like a person to person interaction where you depend on someone and it becomes unhealthy. (Interviewee B)

Consistent with the extant literature and existing measures of addiction and liability (McGovern & Morrison, 1992; Young, 1998, 2004; Miele et al., 2000), we used symptoms (e.g.

preoccupation, mood and tolerance) in determining the level of dependency. An interviewee was considered highly dependent on IT if she or he had several ‘yes’ answers to the diagnostic criteria. The lower the number of symptoms reported, the lower the dependency on technology.

A second insight that emerged from the interview analysis is that the level of self-regulation regarding ITuse is also related to propensity. By self-regulation, we mean the capacity to control one’s impulses to behave in a certain way. On the one hand, we found that individuals with low self-regulation tend to ignore or deny problems or disorders that they might experience as a result of their smartphone usage. Our analysis indeed revealed that some interviewees never made an effort to reduce their use, despite the high levels of use and long hours of engagement per day they reported. On the other hand, individuals with high self-regulation appear to assess their behaviours regularly and to have a high degree of control over their behaviours, as illustrated in the following interview excerpt:

I know that I can get addicted to things, but I know how to control. For example, TV watching became a big addiction for me when I was younger. From the morning until the evening or until night, I would watch TV – every program. So, once I started watching it wasn’t dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for me to kind of just keep watching. I did that for a couple of months, or maybe three or four months – and then I realized ‘I’m not doing anything else’. So, then I took a resolution that I’m not going to watch the TV for one year and then I did not. I think that for me obviously to avoid doing something in excess – I limit it or I just don’t do it. I tend – because I know that I can get addicted to that. (Interviewee A)

With regard to severity, it appears possible to conceptualize the severity of IT addiction by examining the intensity of use and the magnitude of negative consequences. First, our results show that intensity of usage, that is the amount of time spent interacting with the technology, is critical to understanding severity. Considering usage intensity to determine severity is in line with existing scales of addiction severity such as GAIN (Dennis et al., 2003), which included weekly usage measures, or SDSS (Miele et al., 2000), which had 30-day period usage indica tors. Accordingly, our results point out that when users spend long hours using a technology, the severity of their problematic use behaviours is more salient:

Waking up to text on your phone, or just constantly having your phone on you … Constantly checking things on your phone – even if you don’t need to have your phone, just constantly checking the time. Even doing that or constantly checking your e-mails, even though nothing new has come – But just having it on you in your hand at all times… That is when I say you have a problem. (Interview K)

Also, our analysis indicates that the magnitude of negative consequences – i.e. the degree to which technology use affects a user's personal. social and professional life – appears to be linked to severity. Indeed, in line with our literature review, interview data show that the severity is associated to the amount of negative consequences that are experienced. When users suffer no or very few negative consequences from their smartphone usage, the degree of affectedness (i.e. the importance of the impingement of the behaviour) is low. In contrast, when users report higher levels of negative personal, social and/or professional consequences (i.e. high magnitude of negative outcomes), they appear to have a high degree of affectedness:

Like people have told me before when I had my Blackberry that, ‘Look, you’re addicted to your Blackberry’… That was like being scolded, right, and that was when I was checking something when we were out at a dinner or something, or when it’s pure family time. (Interviewee J)

In summary, our analysis indicates, as expected from our literature review, that user liability to IT addiction is associated with propensity and severity. Our analysis further reveals that in the speci<sup>fi</sup>c context of IT addiction, propensity is associated with dependency and self-regulation and that severity is associated with intensity of use and magnitude of negative consequences.

## A typology of user liability to information technology addiction

In developing our typology, drawing from our review of the literature and in light of the results of the interview data, we considered the two underlying dimensions of liability, i.e. propensity and severity. Following the guidelines for typological theory development (Bailey, 1994; Doty & Glick, 1994), we used these dimensions and their sub-dimensions to uniquely identify each ideal type and its configuration. Here. idea/ types refer to the archetypes of user liability in the population. Variations within ideal types were in part based on the level of dependency (how many symptoms is associated with their behaviour) and self-regulation reported by users. Both dependency and self-regulation appear to vary from very low to very high. Together, these two sub-dimensions were used to qualify the level of propensity to ITaddiction. In addition, we found that the intensity of use as well the magnitude of negative consequences varied from very low to very high for each user. Looking at these factors together allowed us to qualify the degree of severity. All in all, we used qualitative content analysis techniques to <sup>fi</sup>nd the common con<sup>fi</sup>gu rations of the propensity and severity dimensions that exist among users. Based on these variations, <sup>fi</sup>ve ideal types emerged.

For each type of user liability to IT addiction, we analysed the survey data to identify the associated user pro<sup>fi</sup>le. ## Fanatic

For the second type of user liability to IT addiction (type II – high liability), the associated user pro<sup>fi</sup>le was labelled fanatic. Fanatic users have a high dependency on technology (lower than addicts), and medium–low self-regulation (higher than addicts). Although their intensity of use is at a medium level (lower than addicts), there is a high magnitude of negative consequences that even exceeds that of addicts. While fanatics have hence a different pro<sup>fi</sup>le than that o addicts, their IT usage is problematic and results in signi<sup>fi</sup>cant negative impacts on their lives. Our results show that despite spending a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant amount of time per day using their smartphone, fanatic users tend to be unconcerned about the intensity of their usage.

They generally deny having a problem and believe that they do not need to control their behaviours.

Further analysis of the characteristics of fanatics reveals that these users also are mostly female (67%) with an average age of 19.6 years. Concerning their personality types, these users reported to be self-reliant (90%) individuals with an active social life (86%) and high need for approval (76%). They use their phone very often throughout the day, especially when feeling bored (95%), lonely (81%), anxious (76%), emotional (48%) or shy (48%). Similar to addicts, fanatics mentioned that smartphone characteristics such as usefulness (90%), ease of use (90%), portability (90%), access to the internet (86%), email (81%), text messages (90%) and Facebook (86%), being fast (71%), being fun (67%) and nice design (81%) are the reasons underlying their very high level of usage. In terms of negative consequences, results show that fanatics experience a higher magnitude of negative consequences from their usage (2.28), compared with addicts (2.12). Fanatic users reported con<sup>fl</sup>icts between their use of the technology and the completion of their tasks (81%) or, sometimes, their interaction with others (19%). When they are away from the device, fanatic users feel stressed (76%), frustrated (67%) and uncomfortable (90%). In the words of two fanatic users:

People get mad sometimes when you do not give them your full attention because you’re on your phone. (Fanatic user)

It de<sup>fi</sup>nitely has an impact on studying as it is a constant source of distraction. (Fanatic user)

I feel like I lack some social skills because of it [smartphone]. (Fanatic user)

Fanatics use their phones on most occasions such as in class (100%), during meetings (81%), when socializing or eating (100%), when they just woke up (95%) or while driving (76%), as illustrated in the following quotes:

When I go to a restaurant with my family and I stay on the phone, not talking but texting, and my mother is upset. (Fanatic user)

Even with others around, if I had a text conversation going on, I have the urge to check my phone. (Fanatic user)

## Highly engaged

For the third type of user liability to ITaddiction (type III – medium liability), we labelled the associated user pro<sup>fi</sup>le as highly engaged. These users have medium dependency and medium self-regulation regarding their smartphone usage. Also, their intensity of use is medium, and they experience a medium magnitude of negative consequences. Highly engaged users use smartphone very often during the day but are able to regulate it in a way that it brings less negative consequences on their lives overall.

Our data analysis of the characteristics of highly engaged users shows that they are 59% female users (with an average age of 19.6 years old) with highly perceived self-esteem (71%), who use IT especially when they feel bored (94%), anxious (59%), lonely (68%) and, to a lesser degree, shy (50%). They experience lower adverse consequences associated with their behaviours. For these users, smartphone use rarely con<sup>fl</sup>icts with their social life (6%) and causes less stress (29%) or frustration (26%). Looking at their usage behaviours, we found that highly engaged users use their phones at almost any time, e.g. during class (94%) and meetings (59%), while eating or socializing (94%) and right after they wake up (91%). As mentioned by one user:

I may use it in class because the teacher is really boring, and you want to text your friends, play a game, or do other things to pass the time. (Highly engaged user)

## Regular

The fourth type of user liability to IT addiction (type IV – low liability) is associated with a pro<sup>fi</sup>le of users that was labelled regular. This pro<sup>fi</sup>le encompasses the largest number of users. Regular users exhibit low dependency and high self-regulation. In most cases, when these users try to reduce their smartphone use, their efforts are successful. The intensity of use for this pro<sup>fi</sup>le is low, and these users experience very low magnitude of negative consequences.

Regular users have a reasonable level of interaction with smartphones, typically using them only when required (e.g. occasionally sending text/email, making a phone call here and there or using applications at times).

Regular users’ demographics re<sup>fl</sup>ected on the characteristics of the population (56% women and 44% men with an average age of 19.8 years old). These users are generally upbeat (86%) and optimistic (75%) individuals, who use their smartphone especially when they are bored (95%). Looking at the technology-related antecedents associated with use, regular users are inclined to use smartphones because they are useful (88%), easy to use (84%), easily accessible (77%), portable (91%), convenient (92%), fast (61%) and ef<sup>fi</sup>cient (69%). Also, connectivity (72%), access to emails (69%), text messages (95%) and Facebook (64%) are important smartphone characteristics for this type. Regular users generally did not repor negative consequences associated with their smartphone use, except some being uncomfortable when away from their phone (66%). However, in some cases, smartphone use may con<sup>fl</sup>ict with their daily life tasks (39%). In terms of usage behaviour, some tend to text while driving (41%), and they generally use their phones during classes (97%), while eating or socializing (88%) and when they just woke up (86%). As explained by one user:

I use it, because it’s necessary in today’s society … it is nothing unusual. It’s in my pocket. use it when I am free. (Regular user)

## Thoughtful

Finally, the last type of user liability to IT addiction (type V – very low liability) corresponds to what we labelled the thoughtful pro<sup>fi</sup>le. Thoughtful users have the lowest dependency and highest self-regulation. Our results show that for this type of users, the intensity is at the lowes level and the magnitude of negative consequences is very low. Based on our analysis, the thoughtful users are very cautious and extremely watchful of their smartphone usage and proactively limit any extended interaction with this technology. For most users falling in this type, having a smartphone was equal to having a regular cellphone and therefore was being used minimally (e.g. calling or texting occasionally).

Our data analysis shows that thoughtful users are the only category with higher percentage of male users (51%) and slightly older than the other categories (average age of 20.1 years old). We found that they are highly upbeat (84%), are optimistic (84%), have high self-esteem (80%) and use their smartphone most often when they are bored (78%). In terms of technology features, thoughtful users use smartphones because they are useful (94%), easy to use (78%), portable (78%), convenient (84%) and ef<sup>fi</sup>cient (63%) devices that provide access to the internet (69%) and text messages (92%). In terms of consequences, being away from their phone may bring an uncomfortable feeling to some users (43%).

Thoughtful users closely monitor their own usage behaviours, although they do tend to check their phones in class (86%), during social events (76%) and when they wake up from bed (84%). However, all levels of these behaviours are below average when compared with other user types. The following quote provide additional insights in reference to the thoughtful user’s view of smartphone use:

I do not really like my smartphone because it’s destroying humanity’s de<sup>fi</sup>nition of social life, connectedness between people and our world around us. A phone and virtual doing is not the same as actually doing things.I hate when people are texting or using their phone while we’re talking or hanging out, I <sup>fi</sup>nd it impolite. ## Developing theoretical propositions

As explained earlier, following Doty & Glick’s (1994) guidelines, we proceeded with our theory development by <sup>fi</sup>rst constructing a typology of user liability to IT addiction based on propensity and severity, with <sup>fi</sup>ve types (addict, fanatic, highly engaged, regular and thoughtful) ranging from very high to very low. For each type, we also identi<sup>fi</sup>ed an associated user pro<sup>fi</sup>le. To extend our theory development, based on our data and in light of the literature, we now develop a theoretical proposition for each type of user liability to IT addiction

Looking at the <sup>fi</sup>rst pro<sup>fi</sup>le, the addict, we found that 7% of smartphone users have very high liability to ITaddiction. For these users, not only the propensity or risk of addiction (to smartphone or other technological innovations) is high, but also the severity of addiction is very signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. Although, there is no study that looks at the rates of low to high user liability, our results are consistent with previous research on technology addiction, which has generally reported a 5–10% severe addiction rate (Young, 1998; Armstrong et al., 2000; Turel & Serenko, 2010).

By comparing and contrasting important factors across pro<sup>fi</sup>les (i.e. addressing those that have variability across ideal types), we found that some demographic factors and individual differences stand out, especially when user liability is very high. Indeed, we found that the addict pro<sup>fi</sup>le includes more female users. Previous research on mobile phones has shown that women are more likely to become addicted than men (Jenaro et al., 2007) and have higher preference for indirect communication (via text, email, etc.) (Toda et al., 2006). Furthermore, this type includes IT users who are conformist, are obsessed and generally have high needs for approval and fear of rejection. Such traits are typically associated with depression and may increase the probability of addiction to other technology types, such as internet or online games (Young & Rodgers, 1998; Chou et al., 2005). We also found that addict users tend to interact with IT excessively, especially when they feel lonely, anxious and emotional. Earlier research suggested that technology-based communication allows such users to conceal their personalities through anonymous or carefully devised interactions and, thus, evade social discomfort in the presence of others (Caplan, 2002; Chak & Leung, 2004; Beard, 2005; Byun et al., 2009). Therefore, users with depressive personality types (e.g. fear of rejection, need for approval and lonely) tend to become dependent on technology for communication.

Beyond individual differences, our results show that technology features can explain varia tions between users’ liability to IT addiction. Consistent with technology acceptance models (Davis, 1989; Venkatesh et al., 2003), usefulness and ease of use (and other related factors such as portability, convenience and easy access to the internet and text messages) were deemed critical factors by all types of users. Addicts in particular reported the speed, ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, design and access to emails and Facebook as reasons for their excessive and compulsive IT usage. These results are in line with previous <sup>fi</sup>ndings that hint the importance of technology in related contexts such as video game addiction (Ng & Wiemer-Hastings, 2005). In addition, the users who belong to the addict pro<sup>fi</sup>le tend to use a smartphone because it is fun and entertaining. Perceived enjoyment has been found to be associated with long-term and sometimes excessive IT usage in other contexts such as in internet and mobile phone usage (Park & Lee, 2011; Turel & Serenko, 2012). Based on the theoretical insight in intrinsic motivations, hedonic motivations are critical in forming IT habits (Van der Heijden, 2004). Also, extant literature indicates that gaining experience and increasing the level of habitual usage may lead to addiction (Turel & Serenko, 2012; Turel, 2014).

In terms of consequences, our results indicated that users who belong to the addict pro<sup>fi</sup>le hence have very high liability to IT addiction. They spend signi<sup>fi</sup>cant time per day on smartphone use, which causes con<sup>fl</sup>icts with other productive tasks. This is akin to previous <sup>fi</sup>ndings in IT addiction research that showed that the loss of time, plus distractions associated with the excessive use of technology, may cause work impairments and reduced commitment (Turel & Serenko, 2010) as well as academic failure and job termination (Davis et al., 2002; Nalwa & Anand, 2003; Beard, 2005; Byun et al., 2009). We also found that the excessive use of smartphones exhibited by users in this pro<sup>fi</sup>le may cause negative consequences regarding users’ psychological well-being, causing, for example, frustration and stress when they are deprived of their smartphones. Despite a few comments mentioning a lack of sleep and fatigue, we did not <sup>fi</sup>nd any other signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect of excessive smartphone use on the physical wellbeing of users. Finally, the users in the addict pro<sup>fi</sup>le are highly dependent on smartphones. These users declared using the device in almost all situations, such as when they are in meetings, in classes or at social gatherings and when eating, or in bed and just waking up, and playing online games in inappropriate situations. ## DISCUSSION

Extant IS research has largely focused on IT use as a positive and desired behaviour and hence spent signi<sup>fi</sup>cant efforts to <sup>fi</sup>nd ways to increase acceptance and usage of technology (Venkatesh et al., 2003), assuming that more usage would help reap more bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (Delone & McLean, 2003; Jasperson et al., 2005). More recently, there has been calls to better understand the ‘dark side of technology use’ (D’Arcy et al., 2014), especially with regard to the ITaddiction phenomenon (Turel et al., 2011b; Xu et al., 2012). With this study, we aimed to contribute to this growing stream of literature by introducing the concept of user liability to IT addiction and by proposing a typology that takes into account variations in liability to IT addiction.

In our study, based on the analysis of our interviews, we were able to conceptualize liability to IT addiction and identify its dimensions (propensity and severity) and sub-dimensions (dependency, self-regulation, intensity of use and magnitude of negative consequences). Hence, our research extends the traditional addicts/non-addicts dichotomy often adopted in IT addiction studies and takes into account propensity and severity of addiction for all users. Although liability and its underlying dimensions have not been previously studied in IS research as such, the importance of these factors in understanding problematic IT usage has been recognized. In the case of propensity, dependency has been used repeatedly in distinguishing between norma usage behaviours and addiction (Young, 1998; Armstrong et al., 2000; Byun et al., 2009). In some cases, dependency even has been used as a proxy for addiction-driven behaviours (Faze et al., 2006; O’Brien, 2011). For self-regulation, which was identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as a sub-dimension of propensity, previous research indicates that a de<sup>fi</sup>ciency in self-regulation, ‘a state in which conscious self-control is relatively diminished’ (LaRose et al., 2003, p. 232), can lead to the development of bad habits and, at times, to addiction (Heatherton & Baumeister, 1991; Bandura, 1999; Soror et al., 2015). Such addiction may occur because of the failure to suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently monitor the intensity of use, adequately judge usage behaviours and properly react and adjust it to normal levels (LaRose et al., 2003; Caplan, 2006). Conversely, other users implement selfregulatory mechanisms in order to limit and control their usage of technology (Peters & Bodkin, 2007), which limits the risk of developing IT addiction. In the case of severity, the intensity of usage was found to be a key sub-dimension of liability. In previous studies, intensity of use was found to be associated to future usage intensity (Marlatt et al., 1988) and can be used to estimate the likelihood of developing problematic behaviours. Some existing measures of severity also have used intensity as part of their assessment for severity of addictive consumption (e.g. GAIN and SDSS). In the context of technology addiction, usage intensity has been used to monitor the level of daily engagement required with technology usage, which can be used to assess the leve of chronic, repetitive behaviour (Ferraro et al., 2007). Finally, magnitude of negative consequences also has been recognized in recent literature as a critical element in the study of addiction. Indeed, in contrast to the view that assumes addiction simply to be equal to dependency, some researchers have argued that for a behaviour to be called an addiction, negative consequences must be exhib ited (Morahan-Martin & Schumacher, 2000; Caplan, 2002). As Charlton & Danforth (2007) put it, even a high degree of technology usage is non-pathological unless it is associated with negative consequences.

The results of ANOVA and a comparison of the F-values for these dimensions (Table 5) imply that dependency and intensity of use play important roles in distinguishing between addiction liability types. Although these effects could partly be related to the way we evaluate these dimensions, they lend partial support to recent research <sup>fi</sup>ndings showing that technology-related addiction is correlated to impairments in the impulsive system of the brain (Noel et al., 2013; Turel et al., 2014) Hence, while technology addiction appears to be associated with both the dependency and automaticity aspects of a behaviour, substance addiction is known to be triggered by both impulsive and inhibitory control brain systems (which is responsible for regulating own behaviour) (Noel et al., 2013; Turel et al., 2014). Because the salient consequences of technology addiction (e.g. productivity losses) are perceived as less severe and dangerous than those related to substance abuse (e.g. health related), these <sup>fi</sup>ndings could mean that technology users are less inclined to control their behaviours (for instance due to lack of motivation), although they have the capacity to do so (Turel et al., 2014). Additional research will be needed to further clarify this important issue.

Through both qualitative and quantitative analyses of our data, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed <sup>fi</sup>ve user liability types. Our results showed that while all types of users are ultimately susceptible to develop IT addiction, only a small proportion of users fall into the types that have high or very high liability to IT addiction and exhibit severe excessive and compulsive use behaviours. These results are consistent with the extant literature (Xu et al., 2012) where most users were found to have regulated interactions with technology and whose use did not interfere with important life functions. To further our understanding, we used survey data to specify the profiles of users associated with each liability type. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings were mostly consistent with previous claims that individual differences (e.g. personality traits or moods during usage) could help discriminate between addiction and non-addiction (Armstrong et al., 2000; Caplan, 2002, 2006; Byun et al., 2009). For instance, we found that having a fear of rejection or need for approval is more common in high-liability types (addict and fanatic pro<sup>fi</sup>les). In addition, our study reveals that some technology features appear to be associated with high liability. For example, while in the addict pro<sup>fi</sup>le, users emphasized the importance of being able to use communication and social media, users belonging to the thoughtful pro<sup>fi</sup>le emphasized text messaging as the key feature of smartphones.

Other results from our study were counterintuitive. For example, among users who have very high liability to addiction and who actually exhibit excessive and compulsive use behaviours, fanatics (type IV) reported a higher magnitude of negative consequences than addicts (type V). One possible explanation could be the non-linear occurrence of negative consequences. It is possible that as addiction starts to emerge, users experience higher level of negative consequences, and as time goes and as they increase their engagement with technology, they better handle excessive technology use, and its negative consequences. Given the recent attention to non-linear relationships between constructs in IS discipline (e.g. Brown et al., 2012), further research is required to see whether such effect may exist in the context of addiction-driven ITuse. Another reason for this <sup>fi</sup>nding could be related to the way we assessed negative consequences in this study, which was based on the number of consequence types (social, professional and well-being) reported by the users. As a result of our design, it is possible that addict users experience more severe consequences (e.g. depression, anxiety and frustration), but because these consequences fall under one type (e.g. well-being), the magnitude have been reported as one. Future research should aim at providing a more <sup>fi</sup>ne-grained view of the nature of negative consequences associated with high liability to addiction.

All in all, our results con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that liability to IT addiction is a valuable concept and that it adds to IT addiction research by revealing new insights vis-à-vis users’ susceptibility to IT addiction. Rather than focusing solely on addicts (i.e. users exhibiting pathological and excessive usage of technology), studying liability to addiction allows researchers to study the susceptibility of an entire population of users to develop IT addiction, which adds to our current knowledge. Previous research on liability in other disciplines has shown that liability is a dynamic concept that can change over time and should be viewed as a developing trait that can be tracked down in different intervals (Tarter & Vanyukov, 1994). Hence, liability to IT addiction could be useful to monitor users over time. This suggests that depending on the change in the conditions of use (e.g. user psychological state or technology features), liability to IT addiction might vary. As Tarter et al. (2006) stated, the variation in liability helps explain the typical transition of users from ‘softer’ to ‘harder’ usage. In addition, liability to a substance (e.g. a speci<sup>fi</sup>c drug) has been found to be helpful in predicting the likelihood of developing addiction to other similar substances (e.g. other drugs) (Iacono et al., 2008; Van Leeuwen et al., 2011). Although our data do not allow for longitudinal analysis or comparisons between IT types, these issues pave the way to further research on liability to IT addiction.

## CONCLUSION

We acknowledge that this study has some limitations. Our data were primarily collected from students in a large North American university. Not surprisingly, we did not <sup>fi</sup>nd any difference between types with regard to age and education, given that the respondents were all attending undergrad uate programmes and there was not much variety with respect to those factors. Even though the sample was deemed suitable given the context of our study, it is important for our results to be replicated in other contexts and with populations of different ages, education, professions and background. This would advance our understanding of user liability to IT addiction and improve the generalizability of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Future research should also aim at providing an instrument that offers a more precise measurement of user liability to addiction (e.g. a user liability to ITaddiction scale). Finally, this study involved solely the use of smartphones; additional research should consider different technologies and speci<sup>fi</sup>c applications to see if our results hold true.

Despite its limitations, our study makes important contributions to IS theory and practice. First, we introduce to IS research the concept of ‘liability to addiction’ and empirically conceptualize it in the context of IT addiction. In this way, we contribute to the existing research on IT addiction, by investigating the susceptibility of users to develop IT addiction and the risks of exhibiting excessive IT usage. The findings can be useful for researchers to better distinguish between pathological and non-pathological ITuse behaviours (Charlton & Danforth, 2007; Turel & Serenko, 2012), improve the capacity and precision in assessing both problematic and non-problematic behaviours based on an assessment of liability (Conway et al., 2010) and allow for enacting speci<sup>fi</sup>c strategies to encourage or discourage different forms of usage.

Second, we used typology theory (Doty & Glick, 1994) and mixed-method analyses to provide a rich and detailed explanation of user liability to addiction, as well as a systematic overview of different liability types. While some users were found to have very low liability to IT addiction and seem wary of technology (thoughtful), others show very high or high levels of liability to IT addiction (addicts and fanatics). Our typology helps extend the theoretical understanding of the IT addiction by providing a novel and complementary perspective of the factors associated with the risk of developing ITaddiction and the severity of the behaviours associated with each levels of risk. Hence, our results suggest a pro<sup>fi</sup>le-based understanding of factors associated with addiction. This means that rather than considering a <sup>fi</sup>xed set of factors that can predict or be predicted by addiction for the whole population of users, our typology suggests that a unique association between these factors exists for each liability type.

Third, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the pro<sup>fi</sup>les associated with each type of user liability to IT addiction by distinguishing distinct antecedents, behaviours and negative consequences associated with each pro<sup>fi</sup>le. Despite signi<sup>fi</sup>cant research on addiction in other research disciplines (e.g. psychology and mental health), past IS research has mentioned the limited knowledge and the need to further explore the potential antecedents and consequences of IT addiction (Ture et al., 2011b; Xu et al., 2012). Our study hence extends research on these factors and allows us to understand how they vary depending on the level of liability to IT addiction.

Finally, to further our theoretical contributions to this stream of research, we developed prop ositions for each user liability type. These propositions can be used as a baseline to formulate the relationship between the associated constructs and, in future research, to ground hypotheses that can predict causal relationships (Gregor, 2006) to be tested using large data set in addiction-related contexts.

The practical implications of our study should be considered with caution, as further research in organization contexts will be needed to truly allow for generalization of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. But assuming that the student population is representative of organizational users, these <sup>fi</sup>ndings could be used to better understand the differences between users in terms of susceptibility and risks of developing IT addiction. This is becoming increasingly important because many companies are using new technologies, for example to improve business communications. Many of these technologies could prove to be addictive. Examples are internal social media networks or providing phones for employees (Turel & Serenko, 2010; Xu et al., 2012). Depending on the level of user liability to ITaddiction (i.e. propensity and severity), different strategies could be suggested to avoid or limit the negative outcomes associated with excessive and compulsive technology use.

For type I (associated with the addict pro<sup>fi</sup>le), liability was found to be very high. Because the behaviours of users associated with this pro<sup>fi</sup>le are already problematic and are tied to signi<sup>fi</sup> cant negative consequences, intervention programmes are likely necessary. In extreme cases, treatments should be offered to reduce IT addiction liability to lower, healthier levels. Users belonging to type II (i.e. the fanatic pro<sup>fi</sup>le) also require interventions in order to control their usage, as there is a risk of falling into the addict pro<sup>fi</sup>le, where dependency and usage intensity are very high. This can be carried out for example by limiting, in all or in part, their access to technology, increasing their self-regulation (e.g. usage reports and reminders) and controlling their level of dependency (e.g. monitoring usage behaviours). Type III users (belonging to the highly engaged pro<sup>fi</sup>le) do not fall in the threshold of high liability (i.e. addiction) but still exhibit fairly high intensity of usage. Because highly engaged users mentioned experiencing some drawbacks, due to their high IT usage, it would be important to design preventive mechanisms to ensure that their usage will not progress to more problematic levels, where they may experi ence additional negative consequences from their IT use. Users with low liability to IT addiction (type IV; regular pro<sup>fi</sup>le) maintain a non-problematic level of IT usage that is mostly bene<sup>fi</sup>cial, is positive and is generally not associated with negative consequences. As long as their ITusage remains at the same level, there is no need to intervene. Lastly, in the <sup>fi</sup>fth type (very low liability; thoughtful pro<sup>fi</sup>le), users exhibit a very low level of interaction with IT. Thoughtful users’ minima ITusage inhibits them to take advantage of the many bene<sup>fi</sup>ts associated with technology usage. Hence, in contrary to types I, II and even III, further ITusage should be encouraged for this type through various promoting mechanisms, such as providing training, incentives and rewards.

All in all, technology usage is undoubtedly on the rise. Given the potentially damaging effects of excessive and compulsive ITusage, there is a need for further investigation and theorizing of addiction-related behaviours. This study contributes to a better understanding of the risks and dangers of developing addiction by users of technology. Depending on the user liability type, different antecedents, behaviours and consequences can be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed. Further theorization is needed to expand IS theories with regard to the dark side of IT use and IT addiction and design intervention programmes to reduce problematic IT usage and to avoid unintended, non bene<sup>fi</sup>cial consequences. For example, future work is needed in order to test and validate our typology and propositions, using large data sets, collected from IT users in different contexts (e.g. online gambling, online games or social networks). Another interesting avenue for future research would be to look at the typology from a longitudinal perspective. For instance, one may seek to know whether a type III user may fall into type V category over time. Although our data do not allow for such analysis, future work could explain how the usage behaviour of users may evolve with time, leading to change in liability level. We hope that our study serves as a stepping stone for further research and theorization on IT addiction in IS research.

## Appendix E: Cluster Analysis

Following suggestion by Hair et al. (2006) and Kaufman & Rousseeuw (2009), we used two common techniques to categorize and assign our observations to ideal types: HAC and K-means clustering.

HAC: We <sup>fi</sup>rst ran an exploratory analysis to <sup>fi</sup>nd out the number of clusters in our data. Hierarchical clustering is a maior statistical method that builds hierarchy of clusters based on the idea that data points that are close together in distance are more related to each other than others that are far. In HAC, the analysis starts at a point in which each observation is considered as one cluster. Following each step, data points merge to the closest pair of clusters to form new clusters (also known as bottom-up approach). The dissimilarities (distances) between observations were used in order to form the clusters (Burns & Burns, 2008). To merge data points into clusters, we used Ward’s minimum variance technique (Punj & Stewart, 1983; Loo, 2004; Hair et al., 2006). Ward’s technique is believed to be an ef<sup>fi</sup>cient technique that uses the ANOVA approach to evaluate the distances between clusters (Puni & Stewart. 1983). In this method. cluster memberships are calculated based on the total sum of squared deviations from the mean of a cluster, summed over all four dimensions (dependency, intensity of use, selfregulation and magnitude of negative consequences). Each data point joins a cluster that produces the smallest increase in the total sum of squared deviations. The algorithm stops when all data points are assigned to only one cluster. At each step, cluster coef<sup>fi</sup>cients are calculated.

K-means clustering: Although HAC is an ef<sup>fi</sup>cient technique to obtain the number of clusters, it may not be precise in allocating the data points to clusters (Norusis, 2012). To overcome this, we used non-hierarchical K-means clustering to move users across pro<sup>fi</sup>les to reach convergence through maximizing between-cluster distances and minimizing within-cluster distances (Sireci et al., 1999; Loo, 2004). In K-means clustering, the number of clusters (initial clustering points) should be prede<sup>fi</sup>ned; therefore, it is not possible to perform this analysis before running HAC (Hair et al., 2006). Once we set the number of clusters at <sup>fi</sup>ve, we ran K-means clustering to calculate each cluster’s centroids (the average values for each dimension in each cluster) and the distances between data observations and each cluster centroid (intra-cluster distance). Through multiple iterations, we randomly assigned new cluster centroids and calculated clusters till the convergence was reached; this means that assigning random cluster centroids does not change the allocation of data observations. At this point, the <sup>fi</sup>nal clusters were formed, in which data points are nearest to the centre of their clusters and farthest from other cluster centroids (Kaufman & Rousseeuw, 2009).

A review of cluster analysis techniques indicates that there is no consensus in extan research on what is the most suitable clustering technique for each study and context. However, the rejection of null hypothesis (assuming that all clusters are equal) using an ANOVA test and the ability to provide meaningful interpretation of the results are two main ways to assess the ‘goodness’ of cluster analysis (Bapna et al., 2004).
