# IMPACT OF GAMIFICATION ON PERCEPTIONS OF WORD-OF-MOUTH CONTRIBUTORS AND ACTIONS OF WORD-OF-MOUTH CONSUMERS<sup>1</sup>

Lei Wang SCIS Department, Smeal College of Business, Pennsylvania State University, 426 Business Building, University Park, PA 16802 U.S.A. {luw21@smeal.psu.edu}

Kunter Gunasti Marketing and International Business Department, Carson College of Business, Washington State University, Todd 386, Pullman, WA 99163 U.S.A. {kunter@gmail.com}

Ramesh Shankar OPIM Department, School of Business, University of Connecticut, 2100 Hillside Road, Storrs, CT 06269 U.S.A. {Ramesh.Shankar@uconn.edu}

Joseph Pancras Marketing Department, School of Business, University of Connecticut, 2100 Hillside Road, Storrs, CT 06269 U.S.A. {Joseph.Pancras@business.uconn.edu}

Ram Gopal Information Systems and Management Department, Warwick Business School, The University of Warwick, Coventry, CV4 7AL UNITED KINGDOM {Ram.Gopal@wbs.ac.uk}

Gamification has been shown to encourage contributions of user-generated reviews (word-of-mouth: WOM) in various domains, including travel and leisure related platforms (Foursquare, TripAdvisor), e-commerce (Amazon), and auctions (eBay). WOM contributors write reviews about products/services provided by business venues and WOM consumers read reviews and use them to form attitudes and make purchase decisions. Gamification elements such as points and badges, awarded to WOM contributors for various reasons, and displayed to WOM consumers, have a dual role in WOM context. First, points awarded for user contributions help motivate WOM contributors to increase their participation. Second, badges awarded to users for visiting business venues signal prior experience or competence, and they help determine how WOM consumers per ceive WOM contributors and form their judgments based on the reviews. While the first role of gamification (i.e., motivating users) has been widely studied, the impact of WOM presented along with gamification elements on the perceptions and behavior of the target audience, WOM consumers, has not been examined. This is important to businesses that are looking to attract customers. Drawing on social psychology literature, we show that gamification symbols signaling experience that accompany WOM leads to perceptions of positive WOM contributors as more competent. This leads to important changes in behavioral outcomes such as willingness to visit/buy and willingness to recommend the reviewed outlets.

Keywords: Gamification, word-of-mouth, badge, competence

## Introduction

Gamification is defined as using game-design elements in non-gaming contexts (Deterding et al. 2011; Werbach and Hunter 2012; Wu 2011). It is a growing trend that has drawn a lot of attention from leaders in business and education, as well as government and policymakers (Hamari et al. 2014; Huotari and Hamari 2012; Schumpeter 2012; Werbach and Hunter 2012; Wu 2011). Gamification has shown great potential in fostering good learning habits, skill acquisition, attitude formation and behavior change in domains like education, commerce, health, and exercise (Gibson et al. 2015; Hamari et al. 2014; Santhanam et al. 2016). More recently, gamification elements such as badges have been used to signal online reviewers’ prior experience on online platforms such as Foursquare, TripAdvisor, and Yelp. Specifically, reviewers (word-of-mouth contributors) write reviews about a product or service provided by a business venue. In turn, potential customers (word-of-mouth consumers) read these reviews and use them to form attitudes and make purchase decisions. We focus on how the presence of gamification elements (particularly badges) alongside reviews (word-ofmouth; WOM) affect consumers’ perceptions of the reviewers’ competence, the quality of their reviews, and subsequent consumer behavior such as liking the reviewed business, intention to visit the business or recommend the business to other consumers.

Gamification elements such as badges are visual tokens of recognition (Liu et al. 2017) that have direct effects on WOM contributors and indirect effects on WOM consumers. While direct effects of gamification on the WOM contributors (e.g., motivation to contribute) have been widely studied (e.g., Seaborn and Fels 2015), the indirect effects of gamification elements on the WOM consumers’ judgments and decisions, such as their intention to visit a business, have not been examined. Another stream of prior work (Zhu and Zhang 2010) has studied how user reviews influence consumer behavior and sales. Different from both these streams and the marrying WOM and gamification literatures that have evolved separately, we focus on an indirect effect of gamification that has not been studied. Specifically, we examine how WOM presented along with gamification elements can affect how WOM consumers perceive the WOM contributors and their reviews when making judgments about businesses. Thus, we frame our research question as follows: What is the impact of reviews provided along with gamified elements (by WOM contributors) on the responsive behavior of WOM consumers?

Consider, for instance, Foursquare (a popular mobile platform), where people have to physically visit business venues (e.g., restaurants, cafes, bars, stores) to collect badges. When WOM contributors post reviews about a restaurant, potential consumers who read those reviews can also view WOM contributors’ visits to similar restaurants captured through gamification badges that appear in contributors’ profiles. Consumers can now be informed of not only the review content but also of the prior shopping history and experience of the WOM contributors. Thus, gamification provides a unique opportunity for WOM consumers to assess the qualities of WOM contributors such as their experience and knowledge level about a restaurant they have reviewed. We show that this in return affects consumers’ behavioral outcomes of interest to businesses, such as liking or visiting the restaurant or recommending it to friends.

This paper makes three contributions to research in Information Systems. First, we show that the presence of gamification elements in reviews significantly affect behavioral outcomes such as the development of the attitudes toward the business venue, intent to shop at the venue, and willingness to recommend the venue to others. Next, we examine the underlying psychological process and show that the effects of gamification are serially mediated by the perceptions of the WOM contributor’s level of competence in the WOM domain, and by the perceived usefulness of the review. Third, we find evidence for the moderating role of WOM valence by documenting that the impact of gamification on consumer behavior is mediated by the perceived competence of the WOM contributor only for positive reviews, but not for negative reviews. Thus, our research sheds light on the effects of gamification on both WOM contributors and WOM consumers, as well as its important outcomes for businesses.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows. In the next section, we survey prior literature and situate our work and contribution in this context. We then present our hypothesis development, after which we discuss our methodology and present our main experimental study based on user interaction on Foursquare, a popular mobile platform offering gamification features along with WOM. We close with a discussion of our findings and managerial implications.

## Theoretical Foundation

Broadly, we situate our work in the context of information systems that facilitate gamification, and its impact on consumer behavior. Gamification, which is of increasing interest to businesses, engages players on an emotional level and motivates them (Burke 2014; Zichermann and Linder 2013). In contrast to reward programs that are more transactional, gamification can engage consumers by drawing on the intersection of consumer loyalty programs, game design and behavioral economics (Zichermann and Linder 2013).

Within this broad context, our specific goal is to study how gamification elements impact the effectiveness of WOM with regard to the behavior of consumers who are the end-users of WOM with gamification elements. Our focus is on a specific type of WOM, badges, which signal consumption of products or services, such as visiting a restaurant. Other types of gamification such as loyalty points, leaderboards, and so on are not the focus of this paper as they are more distantly related to WOM. In our context, WOM is not gamified as the user does not earn points for posting reviews; instead, activities such as visiting a restaurant are gamified and badges are awarded to participants. When a user contributes a review, their restaurant visit badges are in turn displayed alongside their review. We refer to this as “reviews with gamified elements” or “WOM with gamification elements.” We focus on how WOM with gamification elements (as compared to standalone WOM) can foster instrumental outcomes (Liu et al. 2017).

Instrumental outcomes, also referred to as behavioral outcomes, are preferred behavioral changes such as higher sales or more participation in activities and learning (Hamari et al. 2014; Huotari and Hamari 2012; Schumpeter 2012; Werbach and Hunter 2012; Wu 2011). In our context, by “behavioral outcomes,” we mean the extent to which WOM consumers (1) develop more positive (or negative) attitudes about the venue, (2) are more (or less) likely to visit the business venue, and (3) recommend the venue to their friends (or not), as a result of reading reviews that are presented with or without gamification elements. To further clarify, we are not referring to promotional messages, but to reviews that are effective in spurring readers to action.

Our work draws upon prior literature in three main areas: (1) online reviews (WOM), (2) gamification, and (3) social cognition. Below, we expand upon these three streams of research and situate our work in their context.

## Online Reviews

There is considerable literature on the impact of online reviews on consumer behavior. King et al. (2014) conduct a survey of 190 studies on electronic word-of-mouth (WOM) and present a multidimensional analysis of eWOM communication. Cheung and Thadani (2012) employ a social communication framework to summarize and classify prior work in electronic WOM. Neither paper lists any prior work that studies the impact of gamification or reviews with gamified elements on consumer behavior. Similarly, despite the existence of a vast literature on the effects of gamification on WOM contributors (for a review, see Liu et al. 2017), the role of reviews with gamified elements on WOM consumers (the end users) has not been examined. While the question of how reviews influence consumer behavior and sales has been studied extensively (e.g., Zhu and Zhang 2010), the impact of gamification of reviews on the effectiveness of WOM with regard to consumer behavior has not.

Our focus on reviews with gamified elements is conceptually novel, because gamification signals the experience of the review contributor, and can affect how the review is perceived. Therefore, gamification can change consumer behavior in ways not anticipated by prior work which has focused on reviews alone. In our context, WOM with gamification elements consist of reviews that also display badges earned by the consumers who posted the reviews.

## Gamification

Gamification is defined as the incorporation of game mechanics into non-gaming contexts to increase user engagement and loyalty (Hamari et al. 2014; Werbach and Hunter 2012; Xu 2011). There are two components in this definition: (1) game mechanics and (2) non-game context. Gamification mechanics is defined as “elements that are characteristics of games” (Deterding et al. 2011, p. 3). Table 1 presents a classification of gamification mechanics and their definitions based on a review of the literature. The most commonly used game mechanics include badges, points (redeemable or social), levels, leaderboards, and rewards (Hamari and Eranti 2011). Raftopoulos et al. (2015) have found that three key game mechanics have been widely used in previous research, including achievements such as badges/trophies (52%), points (43%), and currencies/rewards (35%). In this study, we focus on badges, the most commonly used game mechanic that is applied in the real world (Raftopoulos et al. 2015). A badge is an indicator of a specific accomplishment or conquest of a specific task or skill.

Previous literature has found that gamification can be applied in enterprises to engage employees and increase job performance (Herzig et al. 2012; Mollick and Rothbard 2014), and it is a promising technique to increase customer engagement over the web (Bista et al. 2012). Carignan and Lawler Kennedy (2013) have tried to identify end-users’ interests in a gamified sales application provided by SAP and their findings provide insights into the design process for the gamification of enterprise software. Kumar (2013) proposes a player centered design for product designers to incorporate the principles of gamification into their software.

As noted earlier, prior research on gamification has extensively studied the impact of gamification on the engagement of the recipients of gamification. In this context, we focus on the impact of gamification elements, not on the engagement or motivation of the recipients of the elements (in our case, the WOM contributors), but rather on the consumers of the WOM (reviews) generated, when the consumers see the recipients’ gamification elements (badges) alongside the reviews. Specifically, we focus on badges awarded to reviewers: a specific type of gamification signaling consumption of a product or a service (such as visiting a restaurant). We examine how gamification elements (badges) affect WOM consumers’ perceptions of WOM contributors and their review usefulness, as well as WOM consumers’ potential behavioral intentions (such as liking, visiting, and recommending the business venue being reviewed).

## Social Cognition Theory

Social psychology research recognizes perceived competence and warmth as the two universal dimensions of social cognition (Cuddy et al. 2011; Fiske et al. 2007). The perceived competence dimension reflects traits that are related to perceived ability, including experience, knowledge, intelligence, skill, etc., whereas the perceived warmth dimension captures traits that are related to kindness, sincerity, trustworthiness, friendliness, etc. Although competence and warmth have been introduced as two universal dimensions of social judgment, competence is judged more often when individuals try to make inferences about others based on their work, speech, creation, or production (in our case, online WOM), whereas warmth is more important in face-to-face interactions (Cuddy et al. 2011). In our context, users assess reviewers based on their written output—reviews of restaurants—as well as the reviewers’ activity and experience vis-à-vis prior restaurant consumption, as reflected in badges. There is no face-to-face interaction. Therefore, consistent with prior theory, we focus on perceived competence but not warmth.

Unlike warmth, competence is not perceived as easy to fake (Reeder et al. 2002). As Cuddy et al. (2011, p. 19) put it,

If we initially perceive a person as incompetent, we presume that however much they may want to, they will never demonstrate competence. Thus, as Tausch et al. (2007) demonstrate, competence impressions respond more quickly to positive competence information because competence (unlike warmth) cannot be intentionally turned on unless the individual possesses the requisite skills.

Tausch et al. discuss that objective tokens of achievements (in our case badges) are highly diagnostic of competence but less diagnostic of warmth, which can be easily faked or manipulated more subjectively.

Competence is about the ability or effectiveness in the context-dependent task (Read and Miller 1989). Starting with Hamilton and Zanna (1972), numerous adjectives have been used for measuring competence ranging from intelligent, experienced, knowledgeable, ingenious, creative, skillful, and industrious to determined, practical, cautious, efficient, scientific, or persistent (also see Wojciszke et al.1998). It has been widely accepted that the dimensions of competence vary depending on the specific task and social context (Bales 1970; Peeters 1983, 1992, 1995). In the WOM context, the most important dimensions used to judge WOM contributors are knowledge and experience of the WOM contributors (Chaiken and Maheswaran 1994; Hovland and Weiss 1951) as they help WOM readers decide how to evaluate the WOM and whether to act on it. Thus, we use knowledge and experience to capture WOM contributors’ competence level.

## Research Model and Hypothesis Development

## The Impact of Gamification

We study restaurants as business venues. We focus on badges as gamification elements because they are the most typical and widely used gamification elements in numerous contexts (Liu et al. 2017). Badges tend to identify and verify the knowledge and experience of a person with certain tasks depending on the domain (e.g., staying at a hotel in Trip Advisor). In the WOM context, badges are visual tokens of recognition that serve to remind the WOM consumers that the WOM contributors have visited restaurants and businesses similar to the one being reviewed. Thus, badges become a significant source of information about the characteristics of WOM contributors. Dou et al. (2012) argue that an online review from an identifiable source such as an independent product review site (as opposed to an anonymous source) will result in more positive evaluations of the review by readers. They identify expertise as a reviewer characteristic that influences review evaluations by readers, and conceptualize “experience competence” as a key component of expertise that is signaled by a visible source of an online review. Badges earned by review contributors, and displayed alongside their contributed reviews, could be viewed as a gamified version of this notion of experience competence.

Status is viewed as the social reward for competence (Mitchell et al. 2003; Son Hing et al. 2002). Status and competence judgments are correlated at 0.77 across 19 countries (Cuddy et al. 2007; Cuddy et al. 2009; Fiske et al. 2002). People judge higher-status others as more competent (Fiske et al. 1999; Kay and Jost 2003; Phalet and Poppe 1997; Poppe and Linssen 1999) and competence is often inferred from status cues (Darley and Gross 1983; Oldmeado and Fiske 2007). Lower status cues are judged as lazy, inexperienced, or ignorant, while higher status cues are associated with knowledge, experience, and competitiveness (Jost and Banaji 1994; LeVine and Campbell 1972; Tajfel 1981). In gamification, status is achieved via elements such as badges. Thus, badges serving as status cues are expected to increase perceptions of knowledge and experience which serve as natural proxies for the competence of a WOM contributor.

Based on several streams of literature on persuasion, impression formation and social cognition theory, we propose that when consumers are exposed to WOM provided with badges, badges help to increase the consumers’ perception of the WOM contributors’ competence level, the usefulness of WOM content, and the consequent consumer behavior. Thus, we hypothesize:

H1: Badges are positively related to the perceived competence of WOM contributors.

H2: Badges are positively related to the perceived review usefulness.

H3: Badges are positively related to the likelihood of the potential behavioral outcomes, such as liking the restaurant, visiting the restaurant, and recommending it to friends.

## The Impact of Perceived Competence

Prior work suggests that consumers evaluate the WOM contributor first, which then informs their evaluation of the WOM content (Greer 2003; Ivkovic and Hans 2013; Pornpitakpan 2004; Sundar 1998). Accordingly, when we are exposed to an evaluation of an unknown situation (e.g., the quality of a restaurant), we first form an opinion about the source (e.g., WOM contributor), which then helps us decide how to process the evaluation provided by that source.

In marketing, Pornpitakpan (2004) reviews five decades of literature on the effect of source credibility on persuasion and concludes that “the main effect of source credibility on persuasion seems to indicate the superiority of a highcredibility source over a low-credibility source” (p. 234). In the legal literature, Ivkovic and Hans (2003) find that jurors evaluation of expert testimony is influenced by the jurors evaluation of the experts themselves. In the context of mass communications, Greer (2003) finds that participants rely significantly on source credibility in evaluating online news stories. Again, in the context of online news, Sundar (1998) finds that participants rate news stories with quotes from sources (experts) “significantly higher in credibility and quality than identical stories without quotes” (p. 55). All of this literature suggests that consumers of information evaluate the source first, and then the content of information.

In our context, gamification elements signal the review contributor’s past actions and achievements and, therefore, they are conjectured to help consumers form an opinion of the review contributor. This, in turn, helps consumers decide how to process the review of the restaurant (e.g., whether it will be considered as useful information) that finally leads to consequent preferred behavioral outcomes (e.g., liking and visiting the place). We hypothesize:

## H4: Perceived competence of WOM contributor is positively related to the perceived review usefulness.

H5: Perceived competence of WOM contributor is positively related to the likelihood of the consequent behavioral outcomes such as liking, recommending, or visiting the restaurant.

Previous WOM literature has established a positive association between the perceived review usefulness and the consequent consumer behavior. When a review is perceived as useful, it is highly likely that consumers buy the product or service. Thus, we hypothesize that:

H6: The perceived review usefulness is positively related to the likelihood of the potential behavioral outcomes, such as liking or visiting the restaurant, and recommending it to friends.

Businesses are highly likely to be interested if customers react differently to positive and negative reviews based on their perceptions of WOM contributors and the role of gamification elements on such perceptions. Positive WOM is expected to increase the liking of a venue as well as the tendency to recommend the place and intentions to go there. On the other hand, negative WOM is expected to have the exact opposite effect in general. There is also conflicting evidence about the level of experience of positive versus negative WOM providers. Some studies suggest that negative WOM is perceived to be more experienced (Ward and Ostrom 2002) because they must have had more elaborate experiences with the product. Accordingly, negative information tends to be more diagnostic and influential than positive information in communication (Baumeister et al. 2001; Herr et al. 1991; Richins 1983). However, some recent studies suggest the opposite: “Senders of positive WOM are also viewed as having greater experience and evidence of their claims than are senders of negative WOM” (Martin 2017, p. 1). Also, there is research suggesting that positive WOM is more useful than negative WOM for hedonic products while negative WOM is more useful for utilitarian products (Sen and Lerman 2007). Salehan and Kim (2016) study review sentiment and find that negative reviews tend to be attributed to the reviewer more than the product, and are, therefore, less likely to be read whereas there is a bias toward positive reviews. Such work highlights the importance of understanding how consumers perceive the review contributor, as distinct from the review content. While we do not have formal hypotheses about the potential differential impacts of review valence on consumer responses (with the exception of the direction of attitudes), we do test our research model on positive and negative reviews separately to identify how the impact of gamification on WOM differs between positive and negative reviews.

## Methodology

To address our research questions on how gamification elements of WOM contributors can influence the preferred behavioral outcomes of WOM consumers, we demonstrate the effectiveness of gamification in terms of increasing the WOM contributors’ perceived competence, enhancing the perceived review usefulness and the preferred behavioral outcomes, such as their feelings about the restaurant, and also the likelihood to recommend and intention to visit the restaurant. In order to fully understand the underlying mechanism for the effects of gamification and the behavioral outcomes, we run the mediation model shown in Figure 1, which allows us to examine the mediating roles of the social cognition of WOM contributors (perceived competence) and the perceived review usefulness.

As discussed earlier, a separate analysis of WOM based on its valence is warranted. Accordingly, we also test the effect of WOM valence and explore how it affects the relationship between gamification and behavioral outcomes. Before we provide the details of this study, we first present a pretest conducted to identify the reviews appropriate for a coffee shop setting to be used in our experiments. We test the perceptions of the following statements:

Positive WOM: “What I like about this cafe is their amazing fresh-brewed coffee, their broad selection of beverages and their excellent service.”

Negative WOM: “This cafe is not good at all. Their coffee is not fresh-brewed, their selection of beverages is limited, and their service is just okay.”

One hundred Amazon Mechanical Turk workers (50% female, 69.6% between 25 and 44 years old) participated in this pretest for \$0.60 compensation and they all completed the survey. After reading the reviews, participants answer two questions designed to measure the representativeness of the reviews:

(1) Is this a typical review of a coffee shop/ restaurant? (1 = extremely atypical, 5 = neutral, 9 = extremely typical).

(2) Is this a realistic review of a coffee shop/restaurant? (1 = extremely unrealistic, 5 = neutral, 9 = extremely realistic).

## The Impact of Gamification

On Foursquare, badges are earned by checking in at different venues. Once a badge is earned, it remains in the user’s profile permanently. A user has to be physically present at a place to “check in” on the Foursquare app on her mobile device, and she cannot falsify her movement histories in order to earn badges. Therefore, badges provide an objective and verifiable symbol of the WOM contributor’s experience and knowledge level of the visited venue. Therefore, badges can have an impact on the perception of the WOM contributor’s competence level, and further on the preferred behavioral outcomes.

In this study (referred to as the “main study” to distinguish it from the two follow-up studies in the appendices), we focus on the relationship between gamification and the preferred behavioral outcomes (intention to shop at, recommend, and have positive feelings for the place). We explore the mediating role of the perceived review usefulness and the perceived competence of the WOM contributor for both positive and negative reviews.

## Method

The experiment has a 2 × 2 between-subject factors design, with two levels of gamification (control and badge), and two levels of the WOM valence (positive and negative). The subjects (participants) of the experiment represent the WOM consumers, who read WOM posted by various contributors and are also shown the badges earned by the WOM contributors. The preferred behavioral outcomes are participants’ intention to visit the place, to recommend the place to friends, and to have positive feelings about the place.

## Results

In this study, we focus on how gamification affects the preferred behavioral changes through WOM contributor’s social cognition (i.e., how consumers perceive the WOM contributor along the dimensions of competence). We first discuss the three outcome variables we collected to capture the preferred consumer behavior after reading the reviews. Recommendation measures the intention to recommend the coffee shop to others. Intention to visit measures the intention to act on the advice presented in the WOM, such as to visit the coffee shop after reading the comments. Feeling positive captures participants’ evaluation of the coffee shop. As shown in Figure 1, there are two mediation routes between badges and the preferred behavioral outcomes. First, perceived competence is modeled as a single mediator between badges and behavioral outcomes. Second, the perceived competence of WOM contributors works as a mediator between badges and the perceived review usefulness, which then mediates the impact on the behavioral outcomes (intention to visit, recommend, and liking). This is consistent with the past findings that negative reviews are diagnostic on their own and are already perceived to be very competent (Baumeister et al. 2001; Ward and Ostrom 2002).

This study demonstrates the two mechanisms through which gamification has an impact on the preferred behavioral outcomes. Gamification first helps to increase WOM contributor’s perceived competence, which (1) directly leads to higher intentions for the preferred behavioral outcomes, and (2) leads to higher perceived review usefulness and then better-desired behavioral outcomes later (intention to visit, recommend, and like). Such mechanisms only work for positive reviews but not for negative reviews. We do not find any direct relationship between gamification and preferred behavioral outcomes for positive or negative reviews (see Figure 3).

## Conclusion

In this paper, we utilize Foursquare, a popular gamified location-based service platform, as the empirical context (in combination with experiments on mTurk) to illustrate how gamification can enhance the impact of WOM on consumer behavior. Our work is (to the best of our knowledge) the first to study the impact of gamification on WOM consumers perception of WOM contributors’ competence level, WOM usefulness and the preferred consumer behavior. We construct a rigorous model grounded in rich theory, wherein the impact of gamification on consumer behavior is mediated (1) only by the WOM contributors’ perceived competence level, or (2) by both the WOM contributors’ perceived competence level and the consumers’ perception of WOM content. We run separate analysis on the positive and negative WOM.

Figure 3. The Direct and Indirect Effect of Badge on Behavioral Outcomes

We identify two mechanisms through which gamification impacts consumer behavioral outcomes. In the first mechanism, WOM contributors’ perceived competence works as a mediator between gamification and the preferred behavioral outcomes. In the second mechanism, both WOM contributors’ perceived competence and the perceived WOM usefulness work together as mediators. Our results have positive and negative implications for businesses. First, positive reviews are written by customers who actually come to the venue and earn badges, which lead to more favorable outcomes. Second, the gamification elements do not seem to play a major role in the undesirable outcomes of negative reviews. Specifically, negative WOM is equally effective regardless of whether customers have more or less experience with similar venues. This can be bad for businesses since even the negative reviews generated by the people who have limited experience visiting similar businesses can lead to unfavorable outcomes. However, the potential good news is that negative reviews created by regular customers are not effective any more than those generated by non-regulars and do not lead to any further undesirable outcomes for the business.

As consumers of WOM can also earn badges just like the contributors of WOM, we run a robustness check analyzing how the results change if the consumers themselves have badges (see Appendix B for details). We find that the WOM consumers’ badge status moderates the impact of badges on the perceived competence of WOM contributors for the positive WOM. The indirect effect of badges on the preferred consumer behavioral outcomes through WOM contributor’s perceived competence are significant and higher when the WOM consumer has a small number of badges, than when the consumer has a large number of badges.

The significance of this research for practitioners is clear. Companies that capitalize on these gamification possibilities can reap substantial rewards such as deeper engagement with consumers, increased customer loyalty, and enhanced customer lifetime value. Our findings suggest that online or mobile retailers may be able to increase product sales by incorporating gamification into their sites and platforms. Game designs need to take badges seriously and requires players to expend sufficient efforts acquiring competence in order to earn badges.

One limitation of our research is that we only focus on badges, the most popular gamification element. Future research can explore different gamification mechanisms such as points and leaderboards, and test their impacts on different aspects of user behavior. Recent research has shown that the scales used for measuring success can affect people’s perceptions of goal accomplishment (Gunasti and Ozcan 2019), thus, it will be interesting to explore how the scales of the point systems affects the perceived competence of WOM contributors. The impact of badges on negative reviews may be mediated by other social cognition measures and the examination of the underlying mechanism of gamification in the case of negative reviews can be an interesting avenue for future research.

Given that badges essentially consist of visual images and that we have shown the effectiveness of badges, one may wonder whether this effect is accounted for by the visual components of the badges. We conducted additional studies to address this (see Appendix C for details). Finally, our findings are constrained by surveys performed on a sample of participants on Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk), so that we are unable to check for the impact of gamification on the actual shopping outcomes such as total expenditure.

## Follow-Up Study 1: The Impact of WOM Consumer’s Earned Badges

On Foursquare and other review platforms, not only have WOM contributors earned badges, WOM consumers have also collected badges. Thus, WOM consumers may have basic knowledge about the meaning of badges and how to earn them. When a WOM consumer who has collected badges reads a review provided with badges earned by a WOM contributor, it is highly likely that the WOM consumer’s own badge collecting experience affects how he evaluates the WOM contributor’s competence level.

![](/api/attachments/QMRGQGBP/fulltext/images/0230f25210d85c1692506bbd3ff384775dc8b15921d148f9ae07fe12bc4b0690.jpg)  
Figure B1. Research Model for the Moderated Mediation Model

If a consumer has earned a lot of badges, he may downplay the impact of the badges earned by the WOM contributor and perceive the WOM contributor to have a low level of competence. On the other hand, if the consumer has earned just a small number of badges or no badge at all, he may perceive the WOM contributor who has badges to be highly competent. Thus, in this follow-up study 1, we are interested in finding out whether the WOM consumer’s badge status will moderate the impact of badges on the perceived competence of WOM contributor. We hypothesize that the WOM consumer’s badge status serves as a moderator between gamification and the perceived competence of WOM con tributor. The higher the number of badges collected by the WOM consumer, the lower the perceived competence of WOM contributor will be.

In our experiment, subjects are asked to report the number of badges they have collected on the platform. The median number of collected badges is five. Thus, we use five as the cut-off value to create a binary variable named as WOM consumer badge status (1 = high, 0 = low) and use it as the moderator in our analysis. In our main study, we have tested (1) how the perceived competence of WOM contributor works as a mediator between gamification and the perceived review usefulness, which then mediates the impact on the behavioral outcomes (intention to visit, recommend, and liking), and (2) how the perceived competence of WOM contributor works as a mediator between gamification and behavioral outcomes. The uniqueness of the model we test in Figure B1 is that we add the WOM consumer’s badge status (High/Low) as the moderator between badges and the perceived competence of the WOM contributor. We aim to identify the impact of the WOM consumer’s badge status on the relationship between gamification, the perceived review usefulness and behavioral outcomes. We use the Process Macro in SPSS by Hayes (2013) to estimate our model for positive and negative reviews separately.

Consistent with our findings in the main study, the perceived competence of the WOM contributor serves as the mediator between badges, the perceived review usefulness, and behavioral outcomes. When the review is positive, badges help to increase the perceived competence of the WOM contributor, which then leads to higher perceived review usefulness and better behavioral outcomes, such as higher intention to visit, higher intention to recommend the place and better feeling about the venue. Badges do not have a direct impact on the preferred behavioral outcomes. However, it has indirect impact on behavioral outcomes through the two routes we identified in our main study.

From the indirect effect results summarized in Table B2, it is clear that there are two mediation paths between badges and behavioral outcomes: (1) badge has a positive impact on the perceived competence of WOM contributor, which then directly have a positive impact on the three preferred behavioral outcomes, and (2) badge increases the perceived competence of the WOM contributor, which first leads to higher perceived review usefulness, and then increases the likelihood of three preferred behavioral outcomes.

From the analysis results summarized above, we find that the WOM consumer’s badge status plays a significant moderating role. The mediated relationship between badges and behavioral outcomes via a mediator variable, the perceived competence of WOM contributor, depends on the WOM consumer’s badge status. As the results summarized in Table B2, the impact of badges on WOM contributor’s perceived competence is higher and significant when the WOM consumer has a small number of badges instead of a large number of badges. One possible explanation is that when a WOM consumer has few badges, he has less knowledge and experience with badges and how to collect them. He may perceive that badges have higher value and demonstrate a user’s prestigious role on the platform. Thus, when this WOM consumer reads a review provided with badges, he may feel that this review is generated by a very competent WOM contributor who has more experience and knowledge about this type of business. On the other hand, if a WOM consumer has more badges, he may downplay the symbolic value of badges and has a lower evaluation of the WOM contributor’s competence level. Thus, the indirect effect of badges on behavioral outcomes through WOM contributor’s perceived competence is significant and higher when the WOM consumer has a small number of badges.

The critical dependent variables remain the same as the main study. This follow-up study 2 has a non-comparative design exposing participants to a single review comment in each condition. Participants are asked to fill out an online questionnaire<sup>2</sup> containing a review comment about a coffee shop, and assess the review comment given that they are looking for a coffee shop. Figure C1 shows the reviews and information elements (Verified Information or Badges) presented to subjects in each condition. In this follow-up study 2, we focus on badges and try to differentiate the information effect and the gamification effect. By comparing these two types of information elements, we can tease out the impact of verified information and badges that contain the equivalent information.

To summarize, the goal of this follow-up study 2 is to differentiate the gamification effect and the information effect by comparing badges and the verified information. When the review is positive, the gamification effect is no different from the verified information effect. However, if the review is negative, the gamification effect outperforms the verified information effect. The mechanism through which gamification has an impact on the preferred behavioral outcomes is different from the main study for a negative review. To be more specific, the use of gamification increases the perceived review usefulness which ultimately leads to better behavioral outcomes. Thus, in this follow-up study 2, we find that only the perceived review usefulness works as the mediator between gamification and behavioral outcomes, not the perceived competence of WOM contributor.
