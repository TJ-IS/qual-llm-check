# Impact of Average Rating on Social Media Endorsement: The Moderating Role of Rating Dispersion and Discount Threshold

Xitong Li<sup>a</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Information Systems and Operations Management, HEC Paris, 78351 Jouy-en-Josas, France Contact: lix@hec.fr, http://orcid.org/0000-0002-8005-7212 (XL)

Received: March 23, 2016 Revised: November 10, 2016; March 23, 2017 Accepted: April 26, 2017 Published Online in Articles in Advance: April 19, 2018

https://doi.org/10.1287/isre.2017.0728

Copyright: © 2018 INFORMS

Abstract. This paper examines how a restaurant’s online review ratings afect consumers to endorse deal vouchers sold by the restaurant via social media before they redeem the vouchers. While the efect of the average of review ratings is straightforward, we focus on examining how the efect is moderated by the dispersion of review ratings and the discount threshold set for the group-buying deals. A comprehensive literature review suggests that a large rating dispersion can deliver two diferent messages to consumers (uncertainty in product quality and uniqueness of product taste) and thus may either positively or negatively moderate the efect of average rating on social media endorsement. Discount threshold may serve as a quality signal, reinforcing the efect of average rating. The empirical results show that the efect of average rating is greater when review ratings are more dispersed and the discount threshold is relatively large. The findings generate important managerial and research implications.

## 1. Introduction

In February 2009, Facebook.com launched the “Like” button, allowing users to share any product webpage. Once a user clicks the button, the action is immediately displayed to her Facebook friends via newsfeeds, showing that she has endorsed the product webpage to all of her Facebook friends. Since then, more and more consumers “like” or share products via social media platforms, such as Facebook and Twitter (Lee et al. 2018). The phenomenon of social media endorsement has become increasingly important to firms (Aral et al. 2013). Recent studies show that social media endorsement can increase product awareness (Aral and Walker 2011), drive additional sales (Li and Wu 2018, Chen et al. 2015), and enhance brand loyalty (Rishika et al. 2013). Based on the estimate by Li and Wu (2013), one Facebook Like on a deal, on average, is associated with two additional voucher sales, equivalent to an increase of \$215 in revenue. In addition, social media endorsement can be used to predict firm equity value (Luo et al. 2013). Therefore, engaging with consumers through social media has become “a critical element of any organization’s marketing strategy” (Malhotra et al. 2013, p. 20), and social media endorsement (e.g., Facebook Likes) is an important indicator and influencer of firms’ business performance (Luo et al. 2013, Miller and Tucker 2013).

Social media endorsement is a new phenomenon, because the motive and cost of endorsing a product via social media are diferent from buying it for one’s own consumption. Consumers share and endorse a product via Facebook, perhaps because they think it is a good deal and want to inform their friends of the deal, or because the product is interesting (unique, special) and they want to express their preferences for it publicly.<sup>1</sup> From a cost perspective, social media endorsement is done with no monetary cost (consumers do not need to buy the product before sharing it) and minimal involvement (a simple click on the Facebook “Like” button), but consumers put their self-image at risk, because endorsing a product of low quality or a product purely for monetary rewards may undermine one’s self-image (Wojnicki and Godes 2008).

Because of the importance and distinction, the determinants of social media endorsing behaviors deserve to be investigated separately. Yet, despite the impacts of social media engagement and endorsement on various outcomes (Aral and Walker 2011, Miller and Tucker 2013, Rishika et al. 2013, Chen et al. 2015, Li and Wu 2018), research on the antecedence of social media endorsement is lacking in the literature (Lee et al. 2018). In this paper, we examine if online review ratings can serve as an antecedence of social media endorsement. While a large number of prior studies estimate the valence of online reviews on sales in different product categories, including books (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006), videos/DVDs (Ghose and Ipeirotis 2011), movies (Chintagunta et al. 2010), and restaurants (Anderson and Magruder 2012, Luca 2016), little work explores if the average of review ratings afects social media endorsement for products.

Although the efect of average rating may be expected, researchers underscore the importance of examining contextual factors that moderate the efect, such as reviewer identity (Forman et al. 2008), product popularity, and consumer Internet experience (Zhu and Zhang 2010). As many leading e-commerce websites with online reviews—including Yelp.com, Amazon.com, and Walmart.com—are making the dis tribution of ratings salient to consumers, rating dispersion (e.g., measured by variance) is an important contextual factor that can moderate consumers’ response to online reviews and has drawn recent scholarly attention (Sun 2012, Wang et al. 2015). To our knowledge, how rating dispersion would interact with average rating in afecting consumers’ perceptions of product value is underexplored, and there seem to be competing predictions based on diferent assumptions. On one hand, a large rating dispersion may suggest that the product is unique and niche (Wang et al. 2015), which stimulates consumers to share it via social media because people want to talk about something unique and interesting (Berger and Schwartz 2011). Therefore, one may expect a niche product of a higher quality to stimulate more social media endorsements than a mainstream product, suggesting a positive interaction between average rating and rating dispersion. In addition, many consumers are risk averse and dislike dispersion in critic review ratings (Meyer 1981). While consumers’ perceptions of product value increase with average rating, the increase is greater if the variance is large. Accordingly, the efect of average rating is expected to be greater when rating dispersion is large. On the other hand, however, the cue diagnosticity theory (Feldman and Lynch 1988) suggests that consumers would be less responsive to average rating as a quality signal when there is more disagreement in critic reviews, because they may find this quality signal noisy and nondiagnostic (Basuroy et al. 2006). Therefore, when rating dispersion is large, consumers would reduce their reliance on average rating and seek alternative quality signals. Besides that, Sun (2012) develops a game-theoretical model to examine the informative role of the variance of ratings and shows that a large variance corresponds to more subsequent demand if and only if average rating is low. Consistent with the analytical model, she also finds empirical evidence suggesting a negative interaction between average and variance of ratings. More discussions on the moderating role of rating dispersion are presented in Section 2.2. Therefore, whether rating dispersion pos itively or negatively interacts with average rating in afecting consumers’ perceptions of product value is an empirical question, and the answer to this question would suggest diferent strategies to e-commerce websites with product reviews. For example, if the interaction between average and variance of ratings is positive, merchants of niche products (with a large rating dispersion) would benefit more from allocating resources to maintaining favorable online reviews. By contrast, if the interaction is negative, merchants of mainstream products (with a small rating dispersion) would benefit more.

This paper presents an explanatory study that examines how online reviews (average rating and rating dispersion) would afect consumers’ social media endorsements of products. We choose daily-deal businesses as the research setting. The popularity of selling deal vouchers as a new marketing vehicle has dramatically increased in the past decade (Dholakia 2012). As of April 2012, consumers in North America have spent approximately \$7 million a day (more than \$2.5 billion a year) on deal vouchers, and it is projected to reach \$5.5 billion a year by 2016.<sup>2</sup> Many deal sites (e.g., Groupon) have the group-buying feature and require voucher sales to exceed a threshold for the deal to be “tipped.” Otherwise, the transaction will not succeed and the payment will be refunded (Dholakia 2012). Wu et al. (2015) show that the discount threshold has a significant efect on the difusion of group-buying vouchers. Local restaurants are major players in deal businesses and have online reviews about them on third-party sites such as Yelp.com. In this paper, we explore how online reviews about restaurants and discount threshold afect social media endorsement of deal vouchers sold by the restaurants. Specifically, we seek to answer the following research questions:

(1) Does a higher average of review ratings about the restaurants increase social media endorsement (Facebook Likes) of deal vouchers?

(2) Do rating dispersion and discount threshold moderate the efect of average rating on social media endorsement?

We first establish the causal relationship between the average of restaurants’ review ratings and social media endorsement of deal vouchers using a regression discontinuity (RD) design suggested by Luca (2016) and Anderson and Magruder (2012). Moving a step forward, we examine the moderating role of rating dispersion and discount threshold. The empirical results show that a restaurant’s higher average rating (displayed Yelp star rating) increases the total number of social media endorsements (Facebook Likes) for the restaurant’s deal vouchers, but only when the number of reviews is suficiently large. Note that most deals are only on sale for one day (Dholakia 2012) and that consumers usually redeem the purchased vouchers after a few days or weeks. Therefore, the causal efect of online reviews on Facebook Likes that we have identified in this study cannot be explained by the alternative explanation that favorable online reviews induce more consumers to buy and they come back to “like” the deals after they redeem the vouchers. For the moderating factors, we find that average rating has a greater impact on social media endorsement when review ratings are more dispersed. In addition, we find that the efect of average rating is greater when the discount threshold is larger.

To our knowledge, we are one of the first to empirically quantify the causal impact of average rating about a seller on social media endorsement (Facebook Likes) for the seller’s product. The results imply that consumers take into account their perceptions of product value when they consider endorsing a product via social media and that their perceptions are influenced by online reviews. This study contributes to the emerging literature on social media endorsement (Aral and Walker 2011, Miller and Tucker 2013, Rishika et al. 2013, Chen et al. 2015, Lee et al. 2018). This study is also distinct from the works by Moe and Schweidel (2012) and Muchnik et al. (2013), because the motive and cost of endorsing a product to one’s peers via social media are diferent from those of writing a positive online review (Moe and Schweidel 2012) or voting up a news article (Muchnik et al. 2013).<sup>3</sup>

The findings show that the efect of average rating on social media endorsement is greater for restaurants with more dispersed review ratings. Because review ratings of mediocre-quality products or niche products are likely to be more dispersed (Clemons et al. 2006, Sun 2012), the findings suggest that merchants who sell mediocre-quality or niche products benefit more from allocating resources to maintain a favorable valence of online reviews.<sup>4</sup> Therefore, those merchants should invest more in maintaining favorable online reviews. The findings also show that the efect of average rating on social media endorsements is greater for deals with a higher discount threshold. Because discount threshold is a decision variable that merchants can choose, merchants shall use it to manage the efect of average rating at their discretion. Merchants with a high average rating need to choose a relatively large discount threshold so that they can enjoy a greater impact of average rating and gain maximal social media endorsements for the products, whereas merchants with a low or mediocre average rating may need to choose a smaller discount threshold.

## 2. Hypothesis Development

In this section, we present the hypotheses for empirical testing. Figure 1 illustrates the conceptual framework of the hypotheses.

## 2.1. The Efect of Average Rating

Most daily-deal sites implement the Facebook “Like” button on the deal pages, allowing consumers to share and endorse the deals by clicking the button no matter whether they buy it or not. While diferent motivations may be expected to drive consumers’ sharing and endorsing behaviors in the context of daily deals, we argue that two motivations are particularly relevant:<sup>5</sup> one is to increase social capital and the other is to enhance self-image. No matter which is the primary motivation, consumers are expected to take their perceptions of product quality into account when they consider endorsing a product to their peers via social media, and accordingly, consumers are more likely to endorse a product when they perceive it to be of high quality.

On one hand, the products sold by daily-deal sites are deeply discounted vouchers; the average discount rate of restaurant deals is 51%, and the average value saving is \$15. Knowing the deep discounts, consumers may want to inform their friends by sharing via social media, because they can gain “social currency” if their friends appreciate the sharing (Berger and Schwartz 2011). The social currency is valuable as it increases one’s social capital (Lin et al. 2001) and is useful for social exchange in reciprocal relations (Ellison et al. 2007). For example, one may expect her friends to reciprocally tell her an opportunity of value saving in the future if her friends enjoy the deals because of her sharing and endorsing. If the primary motivation is to increase social capital, consumers would be more likely to endorse a product to their friends if they perceive the product to be of high quality, because their friends are more likely to buy a product of high quality and appreciate the endorsements.

On the other hand, when consumers endorse a product to their peers, they communicate not only information about the product but something about themselves (Berger and Schwartz 2011). Because people enjoy an enhanced self-image (Akerlof and Kranton 2000), consumers who want their peers to think highly of them will endeavor to associate themselves with interesting and superior products (Berger and Heath 2007). Sernovitz (2006, p. 6) notes that “nobody talks about boring companies, boring products, or boring ads,” and the most important rule of word-of-mouth (WOM) marketing is to be interesting. Accordingly, the theory of WOM as self-enhancement (Wojnicki and Godes 2008, Berger 2014) suggests that consumers prefer to initiate WOM about interesting and high-quality products. For example, when a restaurant is new or the deep discount of the restaurant’s deal is uncommon, consumers are likely to share the deal with their friends via social media if they perceive the restaurant to be of high quality. Even if their friends do not buy the restaurant’s deals, consumers may still enjoy an enhanced selfimage by associating themselves with an interesting, high-quality restaurant.

Because most daily-deal shoppers are new customers (Dholakia 2012) and the quality of restaurant deals, as experience goods, is dificult to ascertain before consumption (Hong et al. 2017), consumers’ perceptions about the value of a restaurant deal would be influenced by any available quality signals. The average rating of online reviews about the restaurants as a quality signal is particularly useful in updating consumers’ perceptions about the value of restaurant deals. Prior researchers have shown that a higher average of review ratings increases consumers’ perceptions about product value and make consumers perceive a product to be of higher quality (Roberts and Urban 1988, Erdem and Keane 1996, Chintagunta et al. 2010). Supporting this theoretical mechanism, a considerable number of prior studies show that a higher average rating of online reviews has a positive impact on product sales (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006, Ghose and Ipeirotis 2011, Anderson and Magruder 2012, Luca 2016).

Nonetheless, we note that online reviews would play a smaller role in updating consumers’ perceptions of product value when the number of reviews is small (Roberts and Urban 1988, Erdem and Keane 1996, Chintagunta et al. 2010). If a restaurant has only a few review ratings, consumers may doubt the representativeness of the few review ratings and ignore the quality signal of average rating. Therefore, if a restaurant has enough online reviews, we expect that a higher average review rating can significantly increase consumers’ perceptions of the deal value and their propensity to share and endorse the restaurant deals via social media. Thus, we formulate the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1 (H1). For restaurants with online review ratings, a higher average rating leads to more social media endorsements of the restaurants’ deal vouchers.

## 2.2. The Moderating Efect of Rating Dispersion

While a large number of prior researchers investigate the efect of average rating, only a few studies (Sun 2012, Park and Park 2013) focus on how rating dispersion moderates the efect of average rating. To examine the moderating efect of rating dispersion, it is important to understand what a large dispersion of review ratings reveals to consumers. Based on our literature review, we argue that a large rating dispersion can deliver two diferent messages: uncertainty in product quality and uniqueness of product taste. On one hand, it has been long recognized that there is variation in almost every stage of a production process— so-called “inherent product variability” (Roberts and Urban 1988, p. 170). In the case of restaurants, inherent product variability means the quality level of restaurant foods or services is subject to fluctuation. In addition, consumers may not be able to perfectly evaluate the quality of a product that they receive, and thus, there is variation in product evaluation by different consumers—so-called “idiosyncratic evaluation variability” (Erdem and Keane 1996, p. 5). Because of the two kinds of variability, the quality signal provided by review ratings is subject to uncertainty even if all consumers have homogeneous product preferences. Accordingly, a large dispersion of review ratings may indicate a high uncertainty in product quality. On the other hand, consumers are likely to have heterogeneous product preferences, especially for experience goods like restaurant deals. In this case, consumers are likely to have diferent food tastes or preferences about restaurant environment and service. Hong et al. (2017) show that for experience goods, the variance of review ratings is an important indicator of the systematic differences in consumer preferences. Therefore, even if there is no variation in product quality and consumers are able to evaluate product quality perfectly, there is variation in the evaluation due to the heterogeneity in consumer preferences. Accordingly, a large variance of review ratings reveals that the product taste is unique (niche) and diferentiated in consumer preference space (Clemons et al. 2006, Sun 2012).

Knowing the two diferent messages delivered by rating dispersion, we note that it is unclear ex ante whether rating dispersion would positively or negatively moderate the efect of average rating on social media endorsement. The efect of average rating could have a greater impact on social media endorsement for restaurants with a larger dispersion in review ratings. First, as we discuss above, a large dispersion of review ratings may suggest that the product is unique and niche. The product is thus more likely to be shared because people want to talk about something unique and interesting (Berger and Schwartz 2011). Therefore, a large dispersion of review ratings shall stimulate consumers to share the product via social media and reveal their unique preferences publicly to their friends. While a higher average rating signals a higher product quality, we expect a niche product of a higher quality to stimulate more social media endorsements than a mainstream product. For a mainstream, wellknown product, consumers may find it less interesting to talk about no matter whether the product quality is high. Therefore, we can expect that the efect of average rating on social media endorsements is greater for niche products (with a large rating dispersion) than that for mainstream products.

Second, many consumers are risk averse and respond negatively to uncertainty in review ratings. Meyer (1981), for example, shows that consumers dislike dispersion in critic review ratings and use the critic information by averaging the critic opinions first and then discounting the average to adjust for opinion disagreement. Models in the Bayesian learning literature (Roberts and Urban 1988, Erdem and Keane 1996, Kwark et al. 2014) explicitly consider how consumers dislike variance in review ratings and predict well consumers’ purchase decisions in diferent contexts. Based on the assumptions widely used in the Bayesian learning literature, we develop an analytical model and show that the cross-partial derivative of consumers’ perceptions of the product value w.r.t. the average and variance of review ratings is positive (the proof is given in the online appendix). The intuition lies in the fact that risk-averse consumers’ utility function is concave. While their expected utility of a product increases with average rating, it increases more dramatically with the average rating if the variance is large (Figure A in the online appendix illustrates this intuition). Accordingly, we expect the efect of average rating to be greater when rating dispersion is larger.

Finally, because the distribution of review ratings is often J-shaped (Hu et al. 2009), a large rating dispersion is associated with extreme and more negative review ratings that actually have a greater impact on consumers’ product evaluation (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006). The dissent in online reviews may trigger consumers’ curiosity about the product (Berger and Heath 2007) and thus make them more likely to read the online reviews closely. Thus, consumers are more likely to be influenced by the average review rating when review ratings are more disperse and trigger their curiosity.

Based on the preceding discussions, we therefore hypothesize:

Hypothesis 2 (H2A). The efect of average review rating about a restaurant on social media endorsements of the restaurant’s deal vouchers is greater when there is a larger dispersion in the restaurant’s review ratings.

Conversely, the efect of average rating could have a smaller impact on social media endorsement for restaurants with a larger dispersion of review ratings. First, the cue diagnosticity theory (Feldman and Lynch 1988) suggests that consumers’ reliance on and use of a specific cue in a decision depend on its diagnosticity. If a cue is nondiagnostic, consumers will turn to alternative cues that they find to be diagnostic. As we discuss above, a large rating dispersion may indicate a high uncertainty in the quality signal provided by the average rating. A quality signal of high uncertainty is less diagnostic and more costly to be digested. Given a large dispersion of review ratings, consumers are less likely to use average rating as a quality signal and turn to look for other quality signals such as narrative description about restaurant deals.

Second, the prospect theory by Kahneman and Tversky (1979) suggests that when a decision outcome is considered as a gain (above the reference point), consumers tend to be risk averse, preferring a certain outcome over an uncertain outcome with equivalent expected value. Conversely, when a decision outcome is considered as a loss (below the reference point), consumers tend to be risk seeking, preferring an uncertain outcome over an equivalent certain outcome. According to prospect theory, when the average critic rating of a product is relatively low and below consumer expectation (below the reference point), consumers evaluate a product more favorably when there is a large dispersion in critic ratings about the product, because the large dispersion increases the chance of meeting or exceeding consumer expectation. Accordingly, West and Broniarczyk (1998) find a negative interaction efect of the average and variance of critic ratings on consumers’ perceptions of product value, suggesting that rating dispersion negatively moderates the efect of average rating.

Finally, because a large dispersion of review ratings may indicate that the product is unique and niche (Clemons et al. 2006, Sun 2012), the efect of a large rating dispersion may depend on the average rating. Sun (2012) argues that when average rating is high and most consumers favor the product, the dominant efect of a large variance of review ratings is to drive away marginal consumers. Yet when average rating is low and only a few consumers favor the product, the dominant efect of a large variance is to indicate the nicheness of the product and thus to attract consumers who have well-matched preferences. Using an analytical model, Sun (2012) shows that a high variance of review ratings improves consumers’ perception of product quality if and only if average rating is low. Consistent with the analytical model, she also finds empirical evidence suggesting a negative interaction efect between the average and variance of review ratings.

Therefore, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 2 (H2B). The efect of average review rating about a restaurant on social media endorsements of the restaurant’s deal vouchers is smaller when there is a larger dispersion in the restaurant’s review ratings.

The above discussions suggest that rating dispersion may positively or negatively moderate the efect of average rating on social media endorsement, depending on which mechanisms dominate the others. The sign of the net moderating efect is thus an empirical question.

## 2.3. The Moderating Efect of Discount Threshold

Many group-buying sites set a discount threshold for the deals that is the minimum number of voucher sales required for the deep discount to be valid. Consumers who are interested in a deal would try to promote it in their social circles so that the sales are more likely to exceed the prespecified threshold. Despite the practical importance, only a few studies (Kaufman and Wang 2001, Wu et al. 2015) examine the efect of discount threshold. For example, Wu et al. (2015) find that the threshold has two types of efects on Groupon sales: one refers to a substantial increase in the number of sales around the time when the threshold is reached, and the other refers to a stronger positive relation between the number of sales and prior cumulative sales before the threshold is reached. Diferent from the prior studies (Kaufman and Wang 2001, Wu et al. 2015), we examine if there is any interaction efect between discount threshold and average rating on social media endorsement for restaurant deals.

In practice, discount threshold is chosen carefully under the joint work of Groupon experts and restaurant managers with the purpose of boosting voucher sales (Dholakia 2012). Thus, we assume that the threshold is set at a reasonable level<sup>6</sup> and consumers are motivated to share a deal via social media to help sales meet or exceed the threshold (Dholakia 2012). When the average rating about a restaurant is high, consumers’ motivations of sharing would be amplified. It is thus expected that the interaction efect between discount threshold and average rating is positive. In addition, discount threshold may serve as a quality signal, because it is often larger when a restaurant is of higher quality or its deal is of greater value or more precious (Dholakia 2012). For example, a high-quality restaurant may not be interested in getting just a few voucher sales but want to attract a certain amount of new customers by selling deep-discount vouchers. In this case, a higher discount threshold may signal the restaurant quality or deal value. If the discount threshold of a restaurant deal is minimal, consumers do not necessarily perceive it to be a great deal. Prior research suggests that diferent quality signals are likely to be complements (Kirmani and Rao 2000), because they often reinforce each other in updating consumers’ perceptions of a product. When consumers see a larger threshold suggesting a restaurant is of higher quality or its deal is of greater value, a higher average rating about the restaurant shall reinforce their perceptions that it is a great deal, further motivating them to share it via social media. Therefore, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 3 (H3). The efect of average review rating about a restaurant on social media endorsements of the restaurant’s deal vouchers is greater when the discount threshold of the restaurant deal is larger.

## 3. Data and Empirical Identification 3.1. Data

We collect the data about restaurant deals from two sources: a data set provided by Byers et al. (2012) (named BMZ) and a commercial daily-deal aggregator. The BMZ data set contains a nationwide sample of deals distributed from 19 major cities across the United States. The BMZ data set includes the characteristics (e.g., vendor, discounted voucher price, discount threshold), voucher sales, and number of Facebook Likes of each deal. For each restaurant deal, we manually check if the restaurant has a profile on Yelp.com.<sup>7</sup> We exclude restaurants for which we could not confidently find a profile<sup>8</sup> and those with no reviews on their Yelp profiles. Then, we use a computer program to automatically extract all individual reviews (including numeric ratings, textual contents, and dates) that are posted up to the date of the deal promotion. Eventually, we assemble a cross-sectional data set consisting of 2,545 restaurant deals and 129,129 individual review ratings (from one to five stars).

In this study, we use the total number of Facebook Likes that consumers endorse for a restaurant deal (Likes) as the dependent variable. We collect explanatory and control variables at two aspects. One is about deal characteristics, including voucher price, discount rate, threshold, the number of days that a deal promotion lasts, and a dummy indicating whether it is from Groupon (coded as 1) or LivingSocial. The other is about restaurant characteristics, including the displayed Yelp star rating, number of individual ratings, and the mean and variance of individual ratings. We code a proxy variable for a restaurant’s business age by calculating the number of days from when the restaurant’s earliest Yelp review was posted to the promotion date. ## 3.2. Empirical Identification Method

In nonexperimental studies, identifying the causal efects of online reviews is a challenging task because of the potential endogeneity problem; the average rating is often correlated with unobserved heterogeneity that afects consumers’ responses. For example, unobserved marketing expenditure is likely correlated with both average rating and consumers’ social media endorsement. Without reasonably controlling for such unobserved heterogeneity, average rating may just serve as a predictor of consumer preference rather than an influencer (Eliashberg and Shugan 1997). To identify the causal efect of average rating, we need variation in average rating that is uncorrelated with any deal or restaurant characteristics. Only the changes in consumers’ social media endorsement produced by such variation could allow us to identify the causal efect.

Fortunately, Luca (2016) and Anderson and Magruder (2012) have provided an RD design to identify the causal efect. In this study, we employ the RD design suggested by them. Yelp’s institutional feature of displaying the average rating scores provides an opportunity for the identification. For a restaurant with multiple review ratings (each ranging from one to five stars), Yelp calculates the average of these ratings and rounds it up or down to the nearest half star. For example, one restaurant with an average rating of 3.74 is rounded down and displayed as a 3.5-star Yelp rating, while the other with an average rating of 3.76 is rounded up and displayed as four stars. As a result, there is a halfstar diference between the displayed Yelp average ratings of the two restaurants, although their true average ratings are fairly close. The rounded average rating is prominently displayed on the restaurant’s Yelp profile, while the true average rating is not displayed.

For restaurants whose true average ratings fall in a small “window” centered on a cutof (in the above case, 3.75), whether one gets rounded up or down is likely to be merely subject to random chance such that they appear to be randomly assigned around the cutof.<sup>9</sup> The only diference between the restaurants on the left and right of a cutof, on average, would be a halfstar diference in the displayed Yelp average ratings. Therefore, any possible discontinuity in consumers’ social media endorsements could be attributed to the extra half star in the displayed Yelp average rating (Anderson and Magruder 2012, Luca 2016).

Let $r _ { i }$ be the true average Yelp rating of restaurant deal i that may fall in a small (e.g., 0.2-star) bandwidth of a cutof c. The value of c can be 1.25, 1.75, 2.25, 2.75, 3.25, 3.75, 4.25, or 4.75. Each of the cutofs with a 0.25- star bandwidth corresponds to one rating range, such as $( 3 . 7 5 \pm 0 . 2 5 )$ . In total, there are eight rating ranges between one and five stars. We pool the data from the eight rating ranges<sup>10</sup> and use the 0.2-star bandwidth in the main analysis; we also use diferent bandwidths in robustness checks and get similar results. We use the standard local linear regression (Imbens and Lemieux 2008) as specified in Equation (1) to estimate the causal efect of displayed Yelp average rating

$$
\begin{array}{r} y _ {i} = \alpha_ {0} + \beta \times I (r _ {i} \geq c) + \alpha_ {1} \times (r _ {i} - c) \\ + \alpha_ {2} \times (r _ {i} - c) \times I (r _ {i} \geq c) + \gamma X _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i}, \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

where the outcome variable $y _ { i }$ is the natural logarithm of the number of deal i’s Facebook Likes. If $r _ { i } \geq c ,$ then $I ( r _ { i } \ge c ) = 1$ and the restaurant’s average rating is rounded up to the nearest half star; otherwise, $I ( r _ { i } \geq c ) = 0 _ { . }$ , and it is rounded down. Because the discontinuity in outcome $y _ { i }$ is likely to be merely induced by the indication function $I ( r _ { i } \geq c )$ , the coeficient $\beta$ estimates the causal efect of an extra half star displayed Yelp average rating. In a valid RD design, including control variables is not necessary for estimating the causal efect, because restaurants around a cutof are “locally” randomized (Imbens and Lemieux 2008, Lee and Lemieux 2010, Hartmann et al. 2011). We include a vector of baseline covariates $X _ { i }$ about deal and restaurant characteristics as controls to improve estimation precision. The full set of baseline controls include city, promotion duration, weekday, the natural logarithm of voucher price, the natural logarithm of review volume, whether it is a Groupon or LivingSocial deal, restaurant age proxy, and a categorical variable indicating the restaurant’s rating range.

Following the prior studies (Sun 2012, Park and Park 2013) that all use variance as the measure of rating dispersion, we use variance in the main analysis and alternative dispersion measures in the robustness checks. To examine the diferential efects of average rating for restaurants with a large or small dispersion of review ratings, we follow the median split method that is commonly used in the literature (Demers and Lewellen 2003, Efendi et al. 2012, Rishika et al. 2013). That is, we create a dummy variable $d v _ { i }$ indicating if the variance of restaurant i’s ratings is above the median of the sample. We estimate the moderating efect of $d v _ { i }$ using Equation (2), in which $\beta _ { 2 }$ identifies the diference between the RD estimates for restaurants with a large or small variance of ratings. If $\beta _ { 2 } > 0$ in Equation (2), H2A is confirmed; if $\beta _ { 2 } < 0 ,$ H2B is supported

$$
\begin{array}{r} y _ {i} = \alpha_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \times I (r _ {i} \geq c) + \beta_ {2} \times I (r _ {i} \geq c) \times d v _ {i} + \beta_ {3} \times d v _ {i} \\ + \alpha_ {1} \times (r _ {i} - c) + \alpha_ {2} \times (r _ {i} - c) \times I (r _ {i} \geq c) + \gamma X _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i}. \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

Similarly, to examine the diferential efects of average rating for restaurants with a large or small discount threshold for the deals, we create a dummy variable $d t _ { i }$ indicating if the discount threshold of restaurant i’s deal is above the median of the sample. We estimate the moderating efect of $d t _ { i }$ using Equation (3) in which $\beta _ { 2 }$ identifies the diference between the RD estimates for restaurants with a large or small discount threshold for the deals

$$
\begin{array}{r} y _ {i} = \alpha_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \times I (r _ {i} \geq c) + \beta_ {2} \times I (r _ {i} \geq c) \times d t _ {i} + \beta_ {3} \times d t _ {i} \\ + \alpha_ {1} \times (r _ {i} - c) + \alpha_ {2} \times (r _ {i} - c) \times I (r _ {i} \geq c) + \gamma X _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i}. \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

## 4.1. Balance Check on Baseline Covariates

Before presenting the estimation results, we show a balance check on baseline covariates to support the “local randomization” assumption required by a valid RD design. We report more robustness checks in Section 5.

If restaurants are truly “locally” randomized around a cutof, we expect all observed baseline covariates of the restaurant deals on the left and right of cutof would appear to be balanced. In addition to the dummies of cities and weekdays, we collect 17 baseline covariates about deal and restaurant characteristics, such as voucher price, number of reviews, and the true average rating. Table 2 reports the results of balance check and shows that the averages of the 17 covariates of restaurant deals above and below cutof are all balanced. The balance check on the observed covariates also enhances our confidence that restaurant deals around cutof are comparable even in terms of unobserved heterogeneity. For example, unobserved marketing expenditure is likely correlated with some observed covariates, such as the number of reviews. Table 2 shows that the number of reviews for the restaurants above and below cutof are quite close (3.30 versus 3.37), suggesting that unobserved marketing expenditures between the two groups are plausibly comparable. Moreover, we use the Kolmogorov– Smirnov test to examine the equality of distributions of the covariates and find that their distributions between the two groups are not significantly diferent. Therefore, the balance check gives us confidence that the RD design in our study is valid and that the estimated efects of average rating can be interpreted as causal.

## 5. Robustness Checks

## 5.1. Diferent Bandwidths

A narrow bandwidth would make the RD estimates more convincing in terms of “local randomization” and “local linearity,” but it reduces the sample size and may lead to an insignificant estimate even if there is a true efect. Yet, a wide bandwidth allows more observations in the analysis but may make “local randomization” or “local linear regression” less likely to be valid. Thus, RD estimates may be sensitive to bandwidth selection (Imbens and Lemieux 2008, Lee and Lemieux 2010). To verify if the estimated efect of displayed Yelp average rating is robust, we choose a number of diferent bandwidths to analyze the data. If the RD estimates are relatively stable with the selection of diferent bandwidths, the findings would be more credible.

Using diferent bandwidths from the smallest (0.05 stars) to the widest (0.25 stars), Table 5 reports the RD estimates of the efect of displayed Yelp average rating on Facebook Likes for restaurants with at least

20 reviews. As Columns (2)–(5) show, the coeficient estimates of $I ( r _ { i } \geq c )$ are all positive and significant when the bandwidth increases from 0.10 to 0.25. Even though the coeficient estimate of $I ( r _ { i } \geq c )$ in Column (1) is insignificant with the smallest bandwidth of 0.05 (in this case, only 253 observations are used in the analysis), the point estimate is still comparable with those in Columns (2)–(5). Considering the small number of observations used in Column (1), the positive efect on Facebook Likes is likely there. Therefore, the results in Table 5 suggest that the findings about the efect of displayed Yelp average rating are robust with the selection of diferent bandwidths.

## 5.2. Alternative Measures for Rating Dispersion

Most prior studies (Sun 2012, Park and Park 2013) use unbiased sample variance as the measure of rating dispersion, which we do as well in the main analysis. Here, we verify if our findings are robust to two alternative measures forrating dispersion.

First, the Herfindahl–Hirschman index (HHI) developed in the economics literature is a measure of market share concentration and has been used to capture the consensus in movie critics’ reviews by summing up the squares of proportions of pro, con, and mixed opinions (Basuroy et al. 2006). Since HHI is a measure of opinion consensus, we can use the inverse of HHI as an alternative measure of dispersion of review ratings. Second, entropy, a concept from information theory (Shannon 2001), is an alternative measure of dispersion in a random variable (Ebrahimi et al. 1999). For a discrete random variable $X ,$ each possible value $x _ { i }$ is realized with a probability $p _ { i } ,$ then the entropy of X is defined as: $H ( { \bar { X } } ) = \Sigma _ { i } p _ { i } \operatorname { l { \dot { o g ( } } } p _ { i } )$ . Entropy is maximized if $p _ { i }$ is equal across all possible realizations, and it is minimized as zero for a certain value. In the literature, entropy has been used for measuring the dispersion of diferent opinion groups (Dellarocas et al. 2007) and for mining online product reviews (Zhang and Tran 2008).

## 6. Discussion and Conclusion 6.1. Managerial Implications

This study generates several important managerial implications. First, as engaging with consumers through social media has become “a critical element of any organization’s marketing strategy” (Malhotra et al. 2013, p. 20), understanding how online reviews afect consumers’ social media endorsements is vitally important to firms that rely heavily on social media marketing. While online reviews and Facebook Likes are both generated by consumers, we show that there is direct causal relation between the two $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ the average rating and the number of Facebook Likes), at least for the restaurants with enough online reviews. The results suggest that firms can dedicate resources to maintain favorable online reviews and use the average rating as a lever to boost consumers’ social media endorsements for their products.

Second, the findings show that there is heterogeneity in the efect of average rating on social media endorsements, and the efect is greater for restaurants with more dispersed review ratings. There are two kinds of products for which the review ratings are likely to be more dispersed: niche products and mediocrequality products. The results suggest that merchants of niche products or mediocre-quality products may benefit more from allocating resources to maintain a favorable valence of online reviews. It is critically important for those merchants to devote more resources, because average rating has a greater influence on consumers’ social media endorsements for their products. For example, merchants of niche or mediocre-quality products may consider motivating consumers to write favorable reviews about the products (Mayzlin et al. 2014). They may also need to devote more eforts to avoiding the decline of the ratings about their products or to mitigate online criticisms.

Third, merchants of both niche and mainstream products need to understand that the marginal impact of average rating on social media endorsement would decrease when rating dispersion reduces as they keep increasing the average rating beyond the mediocre level. If merchants can maintain the highest quality for the products (therefore, the variation in product quality is minimized), the impact of average rating on social media endorsement would largely depend on the characteristics of the products, such as product uniqueness;

the impact would be greater for niche products of more dispersed review ratings than mainstream products of less dispersed review ratings. In this case, merchants of niche products need to consider making the dispersion of review ratings more salient to consumers so that they can enjoy the greater impact of average rating while maintaining the highest level of product quality.

Last, the findings also show that the efect of average rating on social media endorsement is greater for group-buying deals with a higher discount threshold. Because the threshold is a decision variable that merchants can choose, merchants can use it to manage the efect of average rating at their discretion. For merchants of a high average review rating (no matter whether their products are niche or mainstream), they need to choose a relatively large threshold so that they can enjoy a greater impact of average rating and gain maximal social media endorsements for the products. This is a crucially important and useful strategy for the merchants of high average rating, because the impact of average rating may decrease when the average rating rises beyond the mediocre level and rating dispersion becomes smaller. Conversely, merchants of a low or mediocre average rating may need to choose a smaller discount threshold so that the average rating has a smaller influence on consumers’ social media endorsements for the products, and thus consumers would not be discouraged by the low or mediocre average rating.

## 6.2. Research Implications

This study also yields several research implications. First, this study contributes to the emerging literature that investigates the importance of consumers’ social media activities (Aral et al. 2013). For example, Rishika et al. (2013) find that consumers’ participation in a firm’s social media efort leads to an increase in consumer visit frequency. Kosinski et al. (2013) show that Facebook Likes can be used to predict sensitive personal attributes. While the extant studies demonstrate the importance of Facebook Likes, little is known about the determinants of consumers’ decisions of endorsing via social media. Egebark and Ekström (2011) conduct one of the first studies in this research stream by showing that conformity could afect one’s decision to “like” a Facebook status update. While Egebark and Ekström (2011) explore the consumer-side influence on “liking” Facebook status updates, our research examines the seller-side influence on “liking” commercial products. Our study is also distinct from the works by Moe and Schweidel (2012) and Muchnik et al. (2013), because the motives and costs of endorsing a product to one’s peers via social media are diferent from that of writing a positive online review (Moe and Schweidel 2012) or voting up a news article (Muchnik et al. 2013). To our knowledge, this study is one of the first to establish and quantify the causal impact of a seller’s online reputation (the valence of user-generated reviews) on consumers’ decisions of endorsing commercial products to their peers with established social ties. Our findings suggest that consumers take into account their perceptions of product value when they consider endorsing a product via social media.

Second, the extant literature documents mixed empirical findings about the efect of average review rating. While a considerable number of studies document that a higher average rating could increase product sales (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006, Chintagunta et al. 2010), it has been recognized that average rating may not necessarily reveal the true product quality (Hu et al. 2009) or influence consumers’ purchasing decisions (Eliashberg and Shugan 1997) because of at least two reasons: (1) consumers may realize that online reviews could be posted by biased consumers (Li and Hitt 2008), and (2) firms have incentives to manipulate their online reviews by posting fake reviews (Dellarocas 2006, Mayzlin et al. 2014). It is thus not surprising that Liu (2006) finds that the valence of movie messages has little explanatory power for movie revenue. Duan et al. (2008, p. 1007) show similar findings and conclude “online user reviews have little persuasive efect on consumer purchase decisions.” Our findings show that the efect of average rating is in fact moderated by the dispersion of review ratings. Ignoring the moderating role played by rating dispersion may lead to misleading results. For example, we find that the positive efects of displayed Yelp ratings are only detected for restaurants with a large dispersion of review ratings, and not for those with a small rating dispersion. Our results provide a plausible explanation for the seemingly inconsistent empirical findings about the efects of average rating (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006, Liu 2006, Duan et al. 2008, Chintagunta et al. 2010), because the efect of average rating is contingent on rating dispersion.

Third, understanding the efect of the dispersion of review ratings on consumer product evaluation has drawn much attention in the literature (Sun 2012, Park and Park 2013), and diferent theories yield competing predictions for the moderating efect of rating dispersion. For example, the cue diagnosticity theory (Feldman and Lynch 1988) suggests that consumers would reduce their reliance on average rating as a quality signal when the variance of ratings is large, because they may find the quality signal is noisy and nondiagnostic (Basuroy et al. 2006). Consequently, the average rating would have a smaller influence when review ratings are more dispersed. Conversely, a large dispersion of review ratings may suggest that the product is unique and niche, and thus stimulate consumers to share the product via social media, because they may want to reveal their unique preferences publicly to their friends (Berger and Schwartz 2011). Accordingly, a niche product may stimulate more social media endorsements than a mainstream product, suggesting that the efect of average rating on social media endorsement is greater for niche products with a large rating dispersion than that for mainstream products. The empirical findings in this study show that the dispersion of review ratings positively moderates the efect of average rating and thus contribute to the emerging literature on the moderating role of rating dispersion (Sun 2012). In Section 2.2, we discuss several plausible underlying mechanisms that contribute to the moderating role of rating dispersion. Future research can focus on disentangling the underlying mechanisms, perhaps by conducting controlled lab experiments.

Finally, discount threshold is a unique feature of group-buying businesses and has drawn attention from academia (Kaufman and Wang 2001, Wu et al. 2015). Wu et al. (2015), for example, find that the threshold has two types of efects on Groupon sales: one refers to a substantial increase in the number of sales around the time when the threshold is reached, and the other refers to a stronger positive relation between the number of sales and prior cumulative sales before the threshold is reached. Our study complements prior research and shows that the threshold not only has a direct impact but can positively moderate the efect of average rating on social media endorsement.
