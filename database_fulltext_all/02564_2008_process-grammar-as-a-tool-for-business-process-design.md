## Abstract

The space of design alternatives for a business process is typically very large, with technology, location, and other factors combining to generate seemingly endless possibilities. This paper introduces a set of artifacts that support process designers in their efforts to manage this critical business problem: (1) a grammar-based method to generate and manage business process design alternatives and (2) a softare prototype that provides support for the use of this method. The method and prototype software are demonstrated with a grammar for a sales process. The method improves on existing approaches by offering the generative power of grammar-based methods while addressing the principal challenge to using such approaches in the design of business processes: the limitations on automated evaluation of alternatives and thus the need to provide designers with tools to manage the potentially overwhelming array of choices generated by design grammars.

Keywords: Design science, process design, design space, business process design, business process reengineering, business process alternative generation, grammatical approach, grammar-based design

## Introduction

Process design is a critical business problem. For many processes, standard solutions are embodied in readily available commercial software, but the possibility of gaining competitive advantage through innovative processes has also generated interest in tools for inventing new processes (Bernstein et al. 1999; Malone et al. 1999). These approaches are based on the idea of using an abstract, hierarchical representation to represent a process design space.

In this paper, we describe a grammar-based approach to generating process design alternatives that extends this line of work. Grammar can be defined as a lexicon plus a finite set of rules or constraints that specify allowable combinations of the elements in the lexicon. While originally developed to describe the structure of languages (Chomsky 1986), grammar has been used as a design tool in many disciplines including mechanical engineering (Rinderle 1991; Schmidt and Cagan 1997), manufacturing processes (Baldwin and Chung 1995; Kwon et al. 2002), two-dimensional and three-dimensional art (Stiny and Gips 1972), architecture (Alexander 1979), software engineering (Gamma et al. 1995), VLSI (Jones and Simon 1986; Oberg 2000), and a range of other topics (e.g., Honavar and Slutzki 1998; Miclet and de la Higuera 1996). As a design tool, grammar is attractive because of its ability to generate a wide range of design alternatives while enforcing design constraints. Because a grammar is an inherently generative mechanism, it enables the systematic exploration of a design space (Stiny 2006).

While grammar provides a natural way to express business processes (Pentland 1995), there is a fundamental difference between business process design and design in art or engineering. In art or engineering, design grammars generate precise “blueprints,” which can be used to construct the object being designed (Stiny 1991). For business processes, design artifacts such as flow charts and written procedures do not play an analogous role (Pentland and Feldman 2007; Suchman 1987). For example, a participant may choose to consult a flow chart or checklist, but typically has tremendous freedom in how to interpret such artifacts and how closely to follow them in practice, if at all.

Thus, the output of a grammar of processes (henceforth a process grammar) cannot be treated as a blueprint for a specific, static artifact (like a sculpture). Rather, it represents a family of possibilities, because participants who perform a business process have a role in how the process is actually carried out. These performances inevitably entail variations, improvisations, and exceptions (Feldman and Pentland 2003). The extent of these variations is likely to increase when participants have access to modular, recombinable information and communication technologies (e.g., Web 2.0). Pentland and Feldman (2007) argue that the “Lego-like” quality of modern information and communication technologies allows participants to design their own business processes (or at least significant aspects of those processes). In short, the space of possibilities is growing and getting increasingly complex.

Process grammars are well suited for representing this design space, but searching the space presents daunting challenges for any grammar-based design method.

1. The method cannot present an exhaustive listing of all possibilities generated by the grammar since even simple grammars would generate far too many alternatives to be evaluated by a process designer.

2. While the method can include functionality to automatically filter out alternatives which are physically impossible or which violate other nonnegotiable constraints, the method cannot use automatic techniques to determine which of the remaining plausible alternatives are “better” or “best.” This must be so, both because (a) the potential of any given alternative is likely to be highly dependent on the specific context and (b) designers may in some cases find implausible alternatives to be the most interesting and useful.

These two limitations imply that a designer will typically need to search through a vast space of possible alternatives. Any usable method must therefore provide support to the designer in choosing which parts of the space to search and in keeping track of interesting alternatives.

This paper describes a method and a software prototype that together satisfy these requirements and thus enhance the potential of grammar as a tool for process design. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: In the next section, we discuss other applications of grammar to design and compare our work with some well-known examples in the literature. We then define the elements of a process grammar. We give specific techniques for representing process design problems as grammars and using those grammars to represent important design constraints while generating a wide range of possibilities. We then describe a software prototype that supports the use of these techniques in an interactive process of exploring the space of possibilities generated by such grammars. Following this, we describe a method that details how to construct such a grammar and incorporate it into process design. As an illustration of the strengths and limitations of this approach, we show how it might be applied to the design of a sales process. We evaluate the proposed methods and prototype by considering the extent to which they meet the criteria articulated in this introduction. After discussing issues for future research, we conclude the paper by evaluating this study with respect to seven guidelines for design science research (Hevner et al. 2004).

## Previous Work

There is a considerable body of work on the use of grammar in many types of design. This work demonstrates both the potential of grammar as a tool for design and the means by which it can be applied to domains outside of linguistics.

In the area of mechanical design, Rinderle (1991) used attribute grammars to represent a design artifact as a set of components, and showed how grammar can be used to generate alternative configurations. Rosen et al. (1994) used graph grammars to represent the features of a design artifact from multiple viewpoints, including manufacturability. Rosen et al. took a “bottom up” approach, starting with the complete geometry of an artifact. Grammatical models have also been applied in a top down fashion to generate the assembly plan for an artifact (Schmidt and Cagan 1997).

In certain domains of engineering design, such as mechanical or VLSI design, there are well-established principles for creating a design that meets a given set of specifications (Suh 1998). For example, Jones and Simon (1986) used an extended version of an attribute grammar to develop a hierarchical design editor for VLSI circuits. Oberg et al. (2000) proposed the use of a grammar to specify a communication protocol and synthesize a tool that automatically recognizes the protocol.

Stiny and Gips (1972) developed the notion of a shape grammar, a grammar that includes a set of two- or three-dimensional shapes. While the shape grammar was originally proposed as a tool for producing and analyzing paintings and sculpture, it has since been applied to architectural design (Mitchell 1990) as well as product design (Hsiao and Chen 1997).

Pattern languages (Alexander 1979; Alexander et al. 1977) offer a less formal approach for bringing grammatical ideas into design. A pattern language is a collection of interrelated patterns, each of which describes a design problem, the context which gives rise to it, and a solution to the problem. The term pattern emphasizes that the particular solution can be implemented in a wide range of circumstances, just as a geometric pattern can be expressed at many scales and constructed from a range of materials. Patterns are most useful if the reader is given a memorable name for the pattern, a rich description of the context in which the pattern applies, the underlying forces which govern its application, and examples of the pattern in practice. This approach differs from Stiny and Gips's use of grammar in that there is no formal (symbolic) representation of the thing being designed.

While Alexander's work was developed to support design in architecture, the pattern language approach has also been applied in software engineering, particularly in object-oriented software development (Gamma et al. 1995). There is an active community of practitioners who write patterns, and many development environments now include functionality to support the use of patterns. Software patterns occupy an intermediate place between the informal patterns of Alexander and the formal approach taken in grammar:

such patterns are applied to formal representations of systems (either design artifacts such as class diagrams or the underlying code itself) and in that sense more precision is both possible and necessary. There is, however, considerable leeway offered as to how a pattern is to be applied, and the overall software design typically includes many elements that are not created or modified using patterns. In addition to patterns for software artifacts, pattern languages have been developed for the software development organization and the development process (Coplien and Harrison 2005).

Many process planning systems employ grammatical representations. For example, Baldwin and Chung (1995) have developed a system for manufacturing and design integration that uses a process grammar to represent and generate process alternatives (see also Kwon et al. 2002). However, their work is focused on the problem of actually selecting particular alternatives in the manner of an expert system. Likewise, there has been a great deal of work on generating process plans from sets of rules (Bond and Gasser 1988; Tate and Hendler 1994). Again, most of this work seeks to elaborate the details of a particular process (e.g., how to generate code for a numerically controlled machine tool). Our approach differs in that our emphasis is on generating a design space for a process, an example of what Archea and Kaylay (1987) refer to as “puzzle-making.”

Salancik and Leblebici (1988) proposed that grammar could be used to generate and analyze alternative organizational forms and developed such a grammar for restaurant transactions. While Salancik and Leblebici do not offer an exhaustive analysis of the 300 alternatives generated by this grammar, it seems feasible that one could be carried out. The challenge remains of how to provide support to designers who may not be in a position to attend to 300 alternatives and who may be confronted by many more alternatives for more complex examples (in the sales example used later in this paper there are over 14 trillion alternatives to be considered).

Pentland (1995) explored the importance of the grammatical metaphor in organization studies (e.g., Barley 1986; Weick 1995) and suggested the possibility of formalizing the metaphor into a more explicit system. Grammatical models seem especially promising because they provide a natural way of describing the layering and nesting of actions that typify organizational processes.

The process recombinator (PR) generates alternative process designs by allowing a designer to explore alternative specializations for each activity in the process as well as alternative methods for coordinating their sub-activities (Bernstein et al. 1999). In addition, the PR allows users to combine the features of several alternative versions of a process so as to generate a new alternative. The PR is not grammar-based, but shares much with the approach we propose here. As such, we provide a detailed comparison between our work and the PR later in the paper.

## Elements of a Process Grammar

Readers unfamiliar with the concept of formal grammar may find the terminology burdensome and difficult to follow, but the basic idea is simple. A grammar is a way of describing all and only the valid possibilities in a given domain. In the case of natural language, a sentential grammar describes the set of all the valid sentences in a language. Since this set is potentially infinite, direct enumeration is impossible. A grammar represents this vast set of valid possibilities by generating them from a lexicon and a set of rules. The process of generating valid possibilities is called rewriting.

A grammar can be described mathematically (Chomsky 1957), which permits formal analysis of its properties as well as facilitates the development of software to generate and analyze grammars. However, in the interest of space and readability, we have chosen to present our grammar in a less formal way below.

Our approach to process grammar will include a lexicon and rewrite rules as well as other elements common to most grammars.

\- Lexicon (terminal symbols) – e.g., Advertise, TakeOrder, ReceivePayment. These are referred to as terminal symbols because, once they appear in an expression, they remain unchanged. These symbols are the building blocks we will use to construct process descriptions. Terminal symbols may include the names of activities, their attributes, and attribute values.

\- Nonterminal symbols - e.g., ATTRIBUTE, ATTRIBUTE-LIST. These symbols appear in our grammar but are eventually replaced by other symbols. For example, in the sales grammar example (discussed later in the paper), the attribute list symbol (ATTRIBUTE-LIST) would eventually be replaced by a list of actual attributes. We use nonterminal symbols to represent as yet undetermined choices of particular activities or attributes.

\- Starting symbol – e.g., S. The starting symbol is a nonterminal symbol which is used to initialize the derivation of a process description. The starting symbol provides a common starting point for exploring various process alternatives. The starting symbol is replaced with other symbols using rewrite rules.

\- Rewrite Rules – e.g., ATTRIBUTE → Who=VAL. Each rewrite rule consists of one or more symbols (both terminal and nonterminal) on the left-hand side of an arrow and a set of one or more symbols on the right-hand side. Such rules are a principal means by which context-specific knowledge is introduced into the grammar. A rewrite rule is enabled for a given expression if the left-hand side of the rule matches a set of symbols in the expression. For example, the rule above would be enabled for the expression Advertise[ATTRIBUTE], TakeOrder. When a rule is applied to an expression, the matching symbols (the left-hand side) are removed and replaced by the symbols on the right-hand side of the rule. For example, if we apply the rule ATTRIBUTE → Who=VAL to the expression above we would generate the expression Advertise[Who=VAL], TakeOrder. If there are no symbols on the right-hand side, the symbols matching the left-hand side are simply removed.

All rewrite rules should satisfy the requirement that any terminal symbols (all elements with mixed case or consisting of symbols such as “=”) that appear on the left-hand side must appear unchanged on the right-hand side.

Many grammars include the option to specify alternatives for the right-hand side. For example, ATTRIBUTE → Who | What would indicate that the nonterminal ATTRIBUTE can be replaced by either Who or What. A rule of this type may also be expressed as a collection of distinct rules, one for each alternative (e.g., ATTRIBUTE → Who and ATTRIBUTE → What).

Together these elements form a grammar which can generate a set of expressions that are sometimes referred to as the language generated by the grammar. In our case this language consists of a set of process descriptions that constitute a set of alternative versions of the process under consideration. We will require that any process grammar conform to a standard approach to describing processes which we will refer to as a process expression.

## Process Expressions

A process expression describes the activities that comprise a process along with associated attributes (such as who carries out a given activity).

We describe a process by listing the activities that take place in the process and the attributes associated with those activities. A process expression is a comma delimited list of one or more activities. Each activity takes the following form: ActivityName[attributes], where attributes is an optional list of attributes, each of which has the form AttributeName=AttributeValue. For example, we might include Who=Salesperson as an attribute of the activity TakeOrder to indicate that this activity is carried out by a salesperson: TakeOrder [Who=Salesperson].

## Attributes

We use attributes to specify additional information about the activities in a process. Such attributes may specify the actors and resources involved as well as the relative timing and location of the activities. This additional information is sometimes characterized in terms of cases (Fillmore 1968). There are many proposed sets of cases (Fillmore 1968, 1975; Grimes 1975; Simmons 1973). For our purposes we have chosen to use a set of attributes that are intuitive and also consistent with the original set of Fillmore cases (1968): Who (agency), What (object), WhereStart and WhereEnd (location), When and Prior (temporality), Using (instrument), Output (result), and Beneficiary (stakeholder).

Who denotes the actor who carries out an activity. What specifies the object on which an activity acts. WhereStart and WhereEnd specify the locations where the activity begins and ends. (These would be identical for stationary activities.) When denotes the time at which an activity starts relative to the previous activity or external events. Prior specifies whether a specific activity must precede this one. Using identifies a resource employed in carrying out an activity. Output identifies a particular resource or outcome produced by this activity. Beneficiary identifies a stakeholder who benefits from this activity. The choice of which attributes to include in a process description depends on the context as well as the interests and concerns of process designers and stakeholders.

## Process Grammar Defined

A process grammar generates process expressions of the kind just described. Table 1 describes each of the elements in our grammar.

Since process descriptions are intended to be used in an informal, intuitive way by process designers to support their exploration of interesting design alternatives, using a process grammar to generate an exhaustive list of alternatives is likely to be of limited value: even simple grammars will generate many more alternatives than can be reviewed by a user, and only some parts of this design space will be relevant to the user. This suggests that one particularly effective way to employ a process grammar in practice might be to allow a designer to interact with the grammar, choosing which rules to fire, which alternatives to consider, and so forth. We support this kind of interactive search by means of a software prototype described in the next section.

## Software Prototype

Our prototype is a web-based application implemented using the Ruby on Rails framework (rubyonrails.org). The prototype consists of two major components: a grammar editor, which allows a grammar author to define a lexicon and rewrite rules for a process grammar, and a process explorer, which allows a process designer to explore a set of alternative process designs generated by the grammar.

The grammar editor (Figures 1 and 2) allows an author to include an informal description of the process as a whole, as well as descriptions of individual symbols and rewrite rules. Descriptions support the Textile markup language (textism.com/tools/textile/) which allows users to add images, formatting, and hyperlinks.

The process explorer component (Figure 3) is the means by which a process designer can engage in an interactive search of the design space generated by a process grammar. At each point in this search, the process explorer displays the current process expression along with a list of those rules which can be applied to this expression. Each rule is accompanied by a description provided by the grammar's author. Thus at each point in the search process, the designer is presented with all available transformations along with informal guidelines as to their applicability.

In addition, the designer can add notes to each alternative. These notes are available on subsequent explorations. A list of all alternatives visited during a session is also provided at the bottom of each screen including the notes recorded for each alternative. The designer can click on any alternative in the search history in order to revisit it. This allows the designer to easily backtrack and explore other paths.

## Exhibit 1. Method 1: Building a Process Grammar

This method applies to processes which are to be described as a list of top level activities, each of which appears at most once in the activity list. This is suitable for high level analysis of a process or the design of a relatively small process. Processes which are described at multiple levels or which include repeating activities would require different methods beyond the scope of the current paper. This method assumes the grammar author will construct the grammar using the prototype described in the previous section. The steps which follow are in the form of instructions addressed to the grammar author.

## 1. CREATE NEW PROCESS GRAMMAR

Use the prototype to create a new process grammar entry. Name the process and include a brief description of the process as well as a statement of any specific assumptions about industry and available technology.

## 2. IDENTIFY POTENTIAL ELEMENTS FOR THE LEXICON

If there is an existing ontology (e.g., an XML application such as ebXML or BPRL, knowledge base, semantic web) for the domain of interest, examine it to identify activities and possible values for their attributes (e.g., who, what, and when).

If there is no preexisting ontology (or if it is necessary to supplement an existing ontology), examine existing processes to identify activities and attributes. Given the wealth of process modeling and systems analysis methods available, we will not give detailed guidance on how this is to be accomplished but rather refer the reader to reference works on these topics.

Create a new symbol entry in the grammar for each potential element (activity or attribute). This entry should include a name for the element that is consistent with the naming rules described earlier in the paper. Add a description of the element including whether it is an activity name or a possible attribute value. For attribute values identify the attribute(s) for which this value makes sense.

Note that these are potential elements of the lexicon and that some may be eliminated as we proceed with the analysis.

## 3. ADD REQUIRED SYMBOLS AND RULES

Add the symbols S, ATTRIBUTE, and ATTRIBUTE-LIST.

The following rules for ATTRIBUTE-LIST should also be added:
ATTRIBUTE-LIST → ATTRIBUTE ATTRIBUTE-LIST
ATTRIBUTE-LIST →

In addition, symbols should be added for any attribute which will be included in any of the activities. (It is not necessary to include all attributes for all activities and, for a particular grammar, the author might decide not to include a given attribute at all, in which case it should not be added to the lexicon.)

## 4. DETERMINE SYMBOLS AND RULES FOR ACTIVITIES

The goal of this step is to determine which activities belong in the top level decomposition of the process and to add symbols and rules to handle any alternative specializations of those activities.

Eliminate symbols corresponding to any activities (identified in Step 1 above) which are not actually in the scope of the process under consideration. Such out-of-scope activities may have been added because an existing ontology or preliminary analysis assumed a broader scope.

Similarly, remove symbols for any activities which are part of the decomposition of other activities on the list. Such activities are part of the process but are not going to be a part of any description generated by this grammar since it is limited to top level activities.

Create a nonterminal symbol for each activity (in addition to its terminal symbol, created in step 2 above). This nonterminal will be used to include activities in the process expression before we add their attributes.

We now create a list of potential top level activities which initially includes all these activities. As we proceed, we will remove some of the activities from this top level list (but not from the lexicon). When we finish this analysis, we will have the list of actual top level activities which we can use to create appropriate rewrite rules to generate the decomposition.

For each activity on the list, identify any other activities on the list that are substitutes for that activity. One activity is a substitute for the other if it can replace the other in a process decomposition. Any such group of substitutes can be thought of as a set of alternative approaches to accomplishing some common generic task. Identify this common task (the “generalization”) and create a nonterminal symbol for it (i.e., a symbol using uppercase letters). Create a set of rules which allow any of the alternative activities to replace the generalization. The generalization should be added to the top level list and the alternatives should be removed from this list (but not the lexicon).

For example, if the list included the activities COOK-DINNER and PURCHASE-DINNER we would create the generalization PROVIDE-DINNER and add the rules

PROVIDE-DINNER → COOK-DINNER

PROVIDE-DINNER → PURCHASE-DINNER

For the activities that remain on the top level list, identify all possible groupings of those activities which can appear in the process (ignoring the sequence in which the activities occur). If all the remaining activities are required, there will be only one such grouping which will include all the activities. For each possible group of activities, create a rule of the form $S \rightarrow$ list of activities.

Note that we do not need rules to generate activities in different sequences because sequencing is handled by the Prior attribute.

## 5. DETERMINE SYMBOLS AND RULES FOR ATTRIBUTE VALUES

For each attribute Att which is included in the lexicon, review the lexicon for all possible values of that attribute. Note that not all of these values may apply in the context of a particular activity, so for each activity Act in the lexicon, consider which of the possible values make sense. We call the set of allowable values of attribute Att for activity Act the value set of Att for Act.

Once we have identified the value sets for all attributes and activities, we may find that some activities share the same value set (with the simplest case being a single value set that applies to all activities). Add a new nonterminal symbol for each distinct value set, using the following convention: a value set that applies to all activities is given the symbol VAL. A value set which applies to a single activity is given the symbol VAL-<act> where <act> is an abbreviation of that activity name. A value set which applies to a group of activities is given the symbol VAL-<act-list> where <act-list> is an abbreviation for the group of activity names.

Create a set of rules for each of these value sets of the form

<att-name>=<value-set-name>→<att-name>=<value>

(A rule of this form should be created for each of the values in the given value set.)

## 6. DETERMINE REQUIRED ATTRIBUTES

For each activity, identify any attributes with a value set other than VAL. These attributes must be included in the default attribute list for this activity. To these, add any other attributes which are considered required (i.e., which are considered essential to a complete description of that activity in this context). Add a rule of the form

<ACT> → <activity>[att1=val-list1 att2=val-list2 ... ATTRIBUTE-LIST] (Where <ACT> is the nonterminal form of the activity and each required attribute appears together with the nonterminal symbol for its value-set for this activity. ATTRIBUTE-LIST is included to allow optional attributes to be added by other rules.)

## 7. ADD RULES FOR OPTIONAL ATTRIBUTES

## Discussion

Having presented our artifacts, we now consider their evaluation.

## Evaluation of the Artifacts

In evaluating the utility of the artifacts proposed in this study, we will use the following criteria:

1. The extent to which the commonly observed alternatives are generated by the method.

2. The number of generated alternatives that are not currently observed.

3. The extent to which the method helps us to understand why some alternatives are commonly observed and why others are not.

4. The usability and usefulness of the method for the task of business process alternative design.

We argue that the proposed artifacts do as well as existing methods with respect to the first three criteria because they produce a grammar that systematically generates all alternatives, including those commonly observed as well as those rarely if ever observed (at least to the extent that the lexicon for the given domain is complete). Furthermore, as demonstrated in our discussion of the sales example, the application of the methods provides the user with an understanding of how the generated alternatives are related (e.g., different actor assignments or activity sequences), which in turn can provide insight as to why some alternatives are feasible and others are not. Assessing usability and usefulness (criteria 4) raises several issues, which are discussed next.

## Usability and Usefulness of the Artifacts

The features that make a grammar-based method very effective in meeting the first three criteria also create special challenges in making it usable and useful. In particular, the two challenges discussed in the “Introduction” have to be addressed.

1. The method cannot present an exhaustive listing of all possibilities generated by the grammar since there are just too many for the user to handle.

2. Automatic filtering of these alternatives is not practical because, at least at present, automatic methods lack the capacity that human users have to judge which alternatives are organizationally feasible (often by reinterpreting the symbols or by “enrolling” the objects in ways different from originally intended).

Therefore any grammar-based method must provide support to the designer in choosing which parts of the space to search and in keeping track of interesting alternatives.

We submit that our artifacts—two methods together with the prototype—meet these challenges by providing the features discussed earlier: (1) an interactive and iterative process where the user is presented with only a small subset of possibilities (i.e., those rules applicable to the current state), (2) the ability to annotate various alternatives in the design space and to backtrack to previous alternatives, and (3) a modular approach in which additional constraints, as they are realized, can be enforced or relaxed through addition or deletion of rewrite rules. To be sure, the usability and the usefulness of the proposed methods and software can be enhanced in many ways. However, the problem of allowing the user to explore potentially useful alternatives without being overwhelmed is a fundamental problem in ensuring the utility of an alternative generation method. A key contribution of our study is to provide artifacts that address this problem.

The potential usefulness of the proposed method for practitioners and organizations is also clear. Existing methods for alternative generation in business process design or reengineering include local variations, emulating best practices, creative techniques, and deducing from first principles (Lee and Pentland 2000). These methods often suffer from a lack of systematicity (e.g., best practices, creative techniques), the limited range of alternatives generated (e.g., local variations), or poor usability and usefulness (e.g., first principles).

A notable exception to these limitations is the process recombinator (PR) (Bernstein et al. 1999). While not explicitly employing a grammar, the PR shares much with the grammar-based approach. The PR generates alternative process designs by allowing a designer to explore alternative implementations of each activity in the process as well as alternative methods for coordinating those activities. In addition, the PR allows users to combine the features of several alternative versions of a process so as to generate a new amalgam. Rather than employing a grammar to generate these alternatives the PR relies on the alternatives available in the MIT Process Handbook, a repository of process descriptions that maintains a specialization hierarchy for each activity as well as linking each process to its component activities (Malone et al, 1999).

The PR seems to satisfy most of the requirements we articulated for a grammar-based process design tool: it can generate a wide range of alternatives while ensuring that each alternative satisfies certain constraints (e.g., that each activity in a process is replaced only with specializations of that activity rather than some completely different activity). The PR also provides some support for navigating the vast array of alternatives by making use of data from the Process Handbook, including bundles, which group related alternatives into meaningful categories, and trade-off matrices, which can record the trade-offs among alternatives along dimensions of interest.

Despite these advantages, the PR has certain limitations which we are able to address in our approach.

First, the PR has no mechanism for capturing constraints which involve the interaction of multiple activities: the user is free to replace each activity independently of the others. By using a grammar, we are able to encode such context-sensitive constraints and thereby avoid generating alternatives that may entail infeasible or undesirable interactions among activities.

Second, the PR has no way to represent and manipulate individual attributes of an activity. If two Take Order activities differ in terms of where the customer order is taken, the PR will need to represent these differences by having alternative specializations of Take Order, for example, Take Order in Store and Take Order Online. In the sales example employed in this paper, there are 2,160 such alternatives for Take Order alone. The PR would thus need to either pre-select the most relevant alternatives (thereby losing some of its generative power) or present the user with a list of 2,160 alternatives (which would be impractical). While the bundles (mentioned above) can group related alternatives, in the PR this is done for the entire process (in this case, the sales process) and not for individual activities.

Finally, by using the notion of a rewrite rule, our approach offers the designer an opportunity to think in terms of design actions (i.e., which rule to apply) and to make use of existing knowledge about the context that favors the use of certain rules. This approach incorporates some of the benefits of the pattern language approach described earlier. The PR focuses on the alternatives that result from design actions rather than the actions themselves.

In these days of rapidly changing technologies and regulations, continuous redesign is often critical for the survival and success of organizations (Lampel and Mintzberg 1996). As a result, practical guides to reengineering (e.g., Kubeck 1995) emphasize the need for continuous improvement and revision of processes to adapt to changing environments. Tools, such as those proposed in this paper, which help with the redesign of existing business processes and the exploration of innovative alternatives offer capabilities that are of potential high value to practitioners and organizations.

## Limitations and Future Work

The grammatical approach presented here offers several interesting topics for information systems research. The most challenging is the development of a methodology for identifying and representing constraints in the grammatical framework. We have illustrated some ways in which constraints can be identified, categorized, added, or relaxed. For example, the generation of an alternative that is not commonly observed can provide hints at the existence of some constraint that needs to be added to the grammar. A more systematic approach to identifying these constraints may be found in the studies of grammatical inferences (Honavar and Slutzki 1998; Miclet and de la Higuera1996) and process mining (van der Aalst et al. 2005). The objective of grammatical inference is to induce grammar from a set of observations while that of process mining is to identify patterns from event logs. Both of these approaches provide ways of extracting patterns from data, thus surfacing underlying constraints.

Another topic for future research is the further refinement of the software, which is currently only a prototype developed for the purpose of exploration and proof of concept. There are many features that can be added to the software, such as the organization of the exploration history in the form of a design space rather than simple chronological order. That would not only enhance the usability of the tool but might also suggest additional topics for research.

Yet another logical next step would be a field study of the use of these artifacts in real organizations. Although there are many factors beyond the artifacts' own merits that will determine the successful generation of useful process alternatives, we will undoubtedly learn much about the strengths and limitations of the artifacts from such empirical tests. In particular, a field study will provide an assessment of the scalability of our approach. Is it practical to construct a grammar for a "life-size" business process in an organizational setting? Will such a grammar yield interesting and relevant insights about design alternatives? Any difficulties or limitations encountered when applying our methods in such a setting would provide valuable insight into how the methods might be improved to increase their utility in practical settings.

\- It develops new artifacts: methods for constructing and using a process grammar to generate and explore process alternatives, and a software prototype. Together these artifacts leverage the power of grammar while still satisfying the requirements discussed.

Finally, it is worth evaluating the contribution of this study using the seven guidelines for design science research articulated in Hevner et al. (2004). Our study produces two IT artifacts “that address an important organization problem”: methods for constructing and using a grammar for generating and exploring business process alternatives and a prototype application that supports their use (Guideline 1 or G1). These artifacts provide technology-based solutions to important and relevant business problems, namely that of (re)designing a business process to exploit rapidly changing technologies or other requirements (G2). The utility of the artifacts has been demonstrated by showing the advantages of these artifacts relative to existing alternative generation methods (G3). Hence, we submit that it provides a solid contribution in the area of design methodology (G4). This approach embodies a rigorous application of the basic research on grammar, which has been demonstrated to be itself rigorous and fruitful (G5). The proposed artifacts utilize available means (i.e., grammar) to reach desirable ends (potentially useful business process alternatives) while satisfying laws in the problem environment (i.e., constraints) (G6). We hope we have communicated these claims and arguments to both technology-oriented as well as management-oriented audiences (G7).

## Conclusion

In this paper, we have proposed methods, along with a software prototype, for constructing and using a grammar to generate and explore business process alternatives. This research makes three main contributions to the literature on process design:

\- It proposes and demonstrates the potential of grammar as a tool for business process design by making use of its ability to systematically generate and explain process design alternatives while providing support to assist designers in navigating and making sense of the design space which these alternatives populate.

\- It identifies the requirements that any method based on grammar needs to satisfy in the business process domain, namely its need to be interactive and iterative in order to accommodate a human user's insights and limitations.
