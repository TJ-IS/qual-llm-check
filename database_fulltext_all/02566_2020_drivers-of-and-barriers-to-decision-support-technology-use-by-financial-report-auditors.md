# Drivers of and barriers to decision support technology use by financial report auditors

Kirsty Meredith<sup>a,⁎</sup>, Jacqueline Blake<sup>a</sup>, Peter Baxter<sup>a</sup>, Donald Kerr<sup>a,b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> USC Business School, The University of the Sunshine Coast, Sippy Downs, Queensland, Australia

<sup>b</sup> School of Management and Enterprise, University of Southern Queensland, Toowoomba, Queensland, Australia

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Decision support Audit technology Technology acceptance Audit support systems Expert systems Financial auditing

## A B S T R A C T

Efective knowledge management and decision-making are essential for professional service firms. Consequences of poor decision-making are particularly significant in audit firms, where decisions afect the performance of financial markets. International corporate regulators and professional accounting bodies have raised serious concerns about the quality of audit decisions currently being made, suggesting the need for modification of current audit practices. Decision support technologies are underutilized in current audit practices

This study investigates the factors that support or hinder auditors' use of decision support technology. Using a systematic search process and thematic analysis technique, it provides a comprehensive qualitative synthesis of recent empirical studies in this area. Building on the work of prior researchers, it contributes to our understanding of how existing theoretical models regarding technology acceptance apply within the context of the audit profession, and responds to calls for research from the broader information system field. It highlights a need for strong collaboration between decision support system specialists and audit professionals and for the professional accounting bodies to be proactive in facilitating this collaboration.

## 1. Introduction

A financial report auditor's role is to objectively scrutinise a company's financial statements and provide an opinion as to whether the statements fairly represent the company's financial afairs [1,2]. Recent reviews by corporate regulators and professional accounting bodies reveal considerable worldwide concerns with the quality of financial report audits, including the inadequacy of quality control processes being undertaken within firms [3,4]. High-quality audits are essential for investor confidence and rational market performance. Without adequate audit quality,<sup>1</sup> risks of corporate failure can remain undi sclosed and investors cannot properly assess the value of investments. According to the Australian Parliamentary Joint Committee on Corporations and Financial Services [5], this could lead to systematic market failure, like that of the 2007–2008 global financial crisis.

The areas where auditors' performance is considered substandard include those that require them to make decisions using high levels of professional judgement and expertise. These areas include, for example, the use of estimates, forecasts, valuation assumptions, internal control assessments and accounting policy choices [3,4,6–8]. Firms are also failing to objectively evaluate significant judgements made by audit team members, as part of their quality control procedures [3].

Decision support technology provides an avenue for auditors to address these concerns, thereby improving audit quality. It is wellplaced to improve auditors' decision-making, particularly by reducing decision-making biases [9–11]. Further use of decision support tech nology could also benefit firms through improved employee performance, for example, greater productivity and transparency, as well as allow firms to better leverage their employees' knowledge [12,13]. Despite this, auditors' acceptance of this technology has been slow, and there are many areas where it is underused [13–18].

This study responds to calls for further research into practices that encourage application of knowledge-based systems in organisations, and factors of critical relevance to the quality and usefulness of information systems for decision-making and knowledge management initiatives [19,20]. Audit provides a useful context for this further research. Knowledge management is considered integral in professional service firms [21]. In audit, consequences of poor decision-making are significant [5]. Consideration of why decision support technologies are underused in the field provides an insight to addressing these issues more broadly. This paper explores two research questions:

$\mathbf { R } \mathbf { Q } _ { 1 } .$ : Which factors support auditors' use of decision support technology?

## $\mathbf { R } \mathbf { Q } _ { 2 } .$ : Which factors deter auditors' use of decision support technology?

The global financial crisis of 2007–2008 amplified the concern about auditor trust and audit quality which had been raised following the corporate collapses in the early 2000s [22,23]. Advancing audit technology signalled that improvements were being made in the profession [24] and, beginning in approximately 2007, there was a marked increase in research on decision support technologies, such as expert systems, in accounting [25]. This period signified an important change in the way the technology was viewed in the profession and a move toward mandated use of integrated decision support technology within many accounting firms [25,26]. This study provides a synthesis of empirical studies. It follows an inductive approach and, in doing so, allows for a comprehensive analysis that does not preclude influencing factors based on a pre-determined model. For example, many prior researchers approached their research from the perspective of the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) model [27–29]. Accordingly, prior studies were often limited to factors identified in that model and did not consider other factors, such as the deskilling efect.

This paper proceeds as follows. Firstly, key words are defined to clarify the focus of the study. The methodology used to identify, screen, and analyse relevant literature is then described. The qualitative synthesis of the literature based on key themes is presented and, finally, the implications of the study are discussed and recommendations are made.

## 1.1. Defining assurance engagements and financial report audits

An assurance engagement involves a practitioner expressing an opinion about whether specified subject matter adheres to set criteria, and is designed to enhance intended users' confidence in the evaluation and measurement of that subject matter [30]. There are various types of assurance engagements; for example, compliance engagements, performance engagements, and engagements providing assurance on sustainability information. However, the primary type of engagement—the type on which the profession has developed its reputation—is a financial report audit [31].

In a financial report audit, the subject matter is the client's financial statements. The set criteria come from the financial reporting framework (including the accounting standards) applicable to the client. The auditor acts as an independent party providing an opinion on whether their client has complied with the relevant framework when preparing the financial statements [32]. The audit process is lengthy and involves, among other things, assessing risks, considering what information would be material to shareholders, and scrutinising a sample of the client's financial data. The process is governed by compliance with auditing standards, as well as ethical guidelines. At the conclusion of a financial report audit, an auditor provides their opinion. For example, an unmodified opinion is provided if the financial statements present a true and fair view of the client's financial afairs, whereas a qualified or adverse opinion is provided where part of the financial report is ma terially misstated [33].

## 1.2. Defining decision support technology

For the purpose of this study, decision support technology is defined as knowledge-based systems that serve to aid decision-making. Knowledge-based systems are designed to capture knowledge and ex pertise from individuals within organisations [34]. This broad defini tion can encompass, for example, decision support systems, expert systems, knowledge management systems, intelligent decision aids, intelligent agents and data analytic tools.

Decision support technologies can assist with structured tasks but most commonly assist with semi-structured and unstructured decisions [35]. They do not make these decisions on their own; rather, they act as flexible mechanisms for retrieving and analysing data, and as tools that help decision-makers to assess decision-alternatives [36].

## 1.3. Decision support technology in financial report audits

In the audit profession, decision support technologies often take the form of sets of Excel macros for common audit tasks [37]. They can also take the form of simple checklists, or complex Audit Support Systems (ASS) [12,38,39]. ASSs serve as electronic records of audit work performed but extend beyond this role to include embedded knowledge components, such as those associated with decision support systems $[ 2 5 , 2 6 ]$ . Auditors use decision support technologies for analysing accounting data, identifying samples for testing, and making inferences from sampling results [37]. The technologies can provide a platform for senior staf to plan and review the audit, and a structure to guide the audit process where auditors are encouraged to use existing workpaper templates [24,40]. The technologies assist auditors with subjective judgements about which accounts and transactions to test, which procedures to perform, the strength of internal controls, the likelihood of fraud, going concern risk, and ultimately the overall audit opinion $[ 9 , 1 0 , 4 1 ]$

Decision support technologies are commonly deployed in auditing, however, are rarely used to their full potential [26,38]. The technologies have been shown to improve the accuracy, objectivity, eficiency, consistency and transparency of audit judgements $[ 1 0 , 1 2 , 1 6 , 2 4 , 2 6 , 3 8 - 4 8 ]$ . However, they are often misused and recommendations from them are frequently ignored by auditors [38,46].

## 2. Literature review methodology

This systematic literature review follows the PRISMA approach [49] as illustrated in Fig. 1. The identification stage involved the use of search strings in the scholarly databases Scopus and Web of Science, for the period 2007 to 2019.<sup>2</sup> The search strings used are shown in Table 1. These databases were selected based on their extensive coverage and reputation for high-quality publications in the information systems and accounting literature<sup>3</sup> and in consultation with the university's business school librarian [50,51]. The length of time is comparable with prior literature reviews [52–56]. The start date of 2007 corresponds with the global financial crisis and the previously mentioned change in the audit profession's view of decision support technology. The time period for the review coincided with the rise of big data and its associated developments in data analytics and business intelligence [24,57]. This marked a distinct change in the audit technology environment, with the audit profession moving toward a more analytical, risk-based approach [58–60].

The screening process involved reading abstracts and eliminating records not within the scope of the study, and the eligibility stage involved reading the full texts and documenting reasons for each article to be included or excluded from the final synthesis. Inclusions and exclusions were validated by a panel of three subject matter experts on a sample basis. The outcome of this stage was 38 articles included for qualitative analysis (see Appendix B).

In addition to reporting empirical results about decision support technology and financial report audits, articles needed to address factors that influence use of the technology. Articles were therefore excluded where the empirical results did not include such factors. ## 3.1. Perceptions of technology enabling improved performance

The most dominant theme identified in the literature supporting auditors' use of decision support technology is that auditors believe the technology can improve their performance. Improved performance can involve, for example, increased accuracy and objectivity of auditor judgements, improved speed and productivity, as well as more efective collaboration and transparency. Section 3.1.1 discusses this within the context of UTAUT, and Section 3.1.2 elaborates on the performancerelated benefits identified.

## 3.1.1. Performance expectancy

UTAUT presents a conceptual model of factors influencing individuals' use of technology (both intentions to use and usage beha viour). The UTAUT construct performance expectancy is posited to be the strongest predictor of technology use intentions and is defined as “the degree to which an individual believes that using the system will help him or her to attain gains in job performance” [27,p.447]. Empirical studies support the significance of this construct within the context of financial report audits. The following quote from a qualitative study [18,p.358] provides an example of this:

One of the auditors concluded that his/her team would not use the software available … because it would not provide any better evidence to what they had. The auditor believes that in the course of a traditional audit they would find any violation detected by the software.

The efect of performance expectancy has also been demonstrated in quantitative audit studies [28,29,62], and was identified as the most relevant motivator for auditors to use CAATs [42]. In addition, perceived usefulness (the Technology Acceptance Model [TAM] construct associated with performance expectancy [63]) was shown to have a significant influence on the behavioural intention of auditors [64], and “job relevance” was identified as one of nine key factors afecting the use of generalised audit software (GAS) [14].

## 3.1.2. Performance-related benefits of decision support technology

Prior literature suggests that decision support technologies are generally perceived as having a positive efect on audit quality $[ 1 6 , 2 4 , 2 6 , 4 1 , 4 2 , 6 5 ]$ , with improved accuracy of auditors' assessments [10,12,43,45,46] and greater objectivity [10,45,47]. Improvements in decision consistency are also documented [10,12,26,39,40]. As are benefits of increased compliance with firm methodology and better documentation of audit procedures [12,26,42,43].

Improvements in speed and eficiency can also result in improved audit quality. This is because achieving an acceptable standard of audit quality is more likely when auditors have suficient time available to complete their audits [66]. Many studies document that decision sup port technology is associated with increased speed of audit work, greater productivity, and eficiency gains [12,16,24,26,41–45,47,48].

In addition, decision support technology can facilitate timely col laboration and efective information sharing between auditors [11,41,59,65], as well as increased audit team mobility [24].<sup>6</sup> Related to this, decision support technology is identified as enabling better monitoring and improved transparency of audit work [12,42], and associated gains are evident in terms of confidence, credibility and re liability [24,43,45].

Some quality-related drawbacks are also noted in the literature. For example, Dowling and Leech [12] note potential detrimental efects on audit review quality due to less separation between the workpaper preparer and the reviewer.<sup>7</sup> Similarly, Vasarhelyi and Romero [18] note concerns about the independence of the data extraction process, as clients are likely to be more involved in the collection and provisioning of data when decision support technology is used. Concerns are also noted about a potential decrease in audit quality due to less use of professional judgement, overreliance on the technology, inappropriate use of the technologies due to complexity, and concerns that the resources involved in implementing and training staf on new systems can counter the economic benefits noted previously (these issues are discussed in subsequent sections). It is noteworthy, however, that the majority of these concerns are likely to be overcome through well considered implementation, training, and system design, and that some of the shortcomings only have short-term consequences [18,67].

## 3.2. Budget pressure within firms

In addition to quality-related considerations, performance expectancy and decision support technology use are afected by budget pressure within firms. Budget pressure is one of the most coercive pressures auditors face [62] and was the most dominant theme that emerged from the literature regarding auditors' reluctance to use decision support technology.

Contrary to the improvements in speed and eficiency noted in Section 3.1.2, some auditors perceive that decision support technology is not cost-efective. The investments required to train staf to use new technology are perceived to be substantial [14,18,26,42,43]. The initial financial outlays required for the acquisition of decision support technology are also perceived to be substantial, as are the ongoing maintenance costs [14,42,43]. Ahmi and Kent [14] note a perception that the initial investments may not be recovered over the life of the technology, which leads auditors to reject it. Vasarhelyi and Romero [18] note a similar view that the costs of utilising technology will not be adequately matched by improvements in audit quality.

The above views are amplified for small firms with limited resources and compounded by a lack of top management support. Note, however, that these views are not held by all auditors; Curtis and Payne [67] explain how “forward-thinking” auditors perceive decision support technology as a way to relieve long-term budget pressure. They describe how changes in budget time frames can help to alleviate negative perceptions and encourage use of the technology:

A longer budgeting and evaluation period will smooth start-up costs from the first year so they can be matched to cost savings in later years. In this situation, auditors will have less concern about the negative efects of budget overruns in the first year on their engagement and annual evaluations. [67 , p.117]

## 3.2.1. False positives and over-auditing

Another concern stemming from budget pressure is the reluctance to utilize technology that produces false positives and unnecessary audit procedures. A false positive occurs when decision support technology produces evidence suggesting an area of concern which is not a legitimate concern [24]. False positives result in “over-auditing” and are associated with unnecessary audit costs [24,68].

In a study by Salijeni et al. [24], false positives were identified as a common concern among auditors. A data analyst employed by an accounting firm recalled:

…I made a couple of audit partners very grumpy as they had to do additional procedures to just confirm that the things I had found … wouldn't adjust their audit opinion. [p.109].

In other cases, auditors describe decision support technology as onerous, control features as unnecessary, and perceived limited marginal benefits associated with their use [12,18,59]. The following quote from an auditor also illustrates this point:

I can get to the end of an engagement where I know I've looked at everything that I see as risky … but get an email from the manager saying that there's 22 screens that I haven't actually put my initials on yet. [12,p.244]

The existence of false positives and unnecessary procedures decreases the performance expectancy and perceived usefulness of decision support technology. It can cause auditors to “work around” the technology or avoid it entirely [12,18,24].

Fortunately, it is noted that the burden of false positives decreases with increasing experience with decision support technology and can also be lessened by integrating technology support staf within audit teams [12,18]. Another potential solution is to adjust audit methodology to make better use of available technology. For example, Hajek and Henriques [68] suggest focusing on identifying “green flags” (low risks) rather than “red flags” (high risks), as the “red flags” tend to include too many false positives. Identifying “green flags” is noted to be more accurate than identifying “red flags”.

## 3.3. Professional judgement

Auditor professional judgement is defined as “the application of relevant training, knowledge and experience … in making informed decisions about the courses of action that are appropriate in the circumstances …” [69,p.68]. The use of professional judgement in auditing is mandated under auditing standards [2,32] and is generally accepted as one of the fundamental principles underlying an audit [31]. The need for professional judgement in auditing is suggested as a justification for resisting the use of decision support technology, specifi cally due to the perception that use of the technology conflicts with use of professional judgement.

Many studies provide evidence of this perception [24,26,39,42,70]. For example, Dowling and Leech [26] cite concerns about mechanistic behaviour that emphasises “ticking the box” rather than using judgement. Similarly, in Axelsen [70,pp.7–8] a participant notes “… the auditor can get too caught up in the form filling process and not apply their professional thinking”. Salijeni et al. [24 , p.113] present the perception that decision support technology leads to “predominantly routinised and increasingly technique-based approaches to audit which minimize the need for professional judgement”. The conflict with professional judgement is identified as the second most common concern with regards to CAAT use [42]. The prevalence of this perception is concerning because decision support technology has the potential to improve auditor judgement significantly.

Section 3.3.1 elaborates on this issue by discussing how bias can arise with professional judgement. Section 3.3.2 continues by explaining how decision support technology can actually enhance professional judgement and presents evidence of a growing awareness of this.

## 3.3.1. Bias within professional judgement

Studies suggest that decision support technologies may not be appropriate for all audit tasks. For example, Dowling and Leech [26 , p.100] conclude that forming the audit opinion is an area where a decision aid cannot sufice but rather is “clearly an area requiring application of an auditor's professional judgement”. However, when the task of forming an audit opinion is carefully considered, it follows a systematic approach governed by the auditing standards<sup>8</sup> [33,71,72]. Professional judgement is required to some extent to appropriately classify a situation, for example in deciding whether an issue is “pervasive”, but beyond this classification there is no subjective interpretation required (or permitted). The authors posit that the role of professional judgement in this process is often overstated, and decision support technology could strengthen the formulation of audit opinions.

A study by Wahdan et al. [45] provides some interesting insight. In this study, the audit opinions recommended by an ASS were compared to the actual audit opinions given by auditors. Where the ASS disagreed with the auditor's opinion, it was investigated by the researchers. In one such case, the auditor conceded that the ASS was correct, and the auditor admitted to providing a less extreme opinion simply to retain a client.

It is acknowledged that this may not be the norm in the profession, and, certainly, failure to use decision support technology to form an audit opinion does not, of itself, indicate unethical behaviour. However, the example illustrates how reducing opportunities to apply judgement could potentially deter auditors from using the technology—particularly where practitioners may have low ethical standards and experience excessive competitive market pressures. Put simply: auditors may not wish to use decision support technology that will tell them what audit opinion to provide if it does not allow them the freedom to provide the opinion they actually wish to provide<sup>9</sup> [73,74]. Another anecdotal example of this is the avoidance of statistical audit sampling approaches when determining sample size, as statistical approaches tend to result in large sample sizes and therefore mandate a greater extent of testing [31].

## 3.3.2. Supporting professional judgement

Contrary to the inhibiting feature discussed above, many auditors are beginning to recognise that decision support technology can work harmoniously with professional judgement [10,12,24,26,46]. This is exemplified in a quote from an audit manager [26,p.96]:

the auditor has the final judgement… [the system's recommendations] are not prescriptive, at best they are a strong “you should do” … [it is the firm's policy] that the auditor must use judgement based upon all circumstances.

This quote illustrates a dominance of professional judgement over decision support technology. It is consistent with the supportive intention of decision support technology and was echoed in a study by Kuruppu et al. [10], which found that auditors “continue to exercise professional judgement when using predictions… rather than relegating professional judgement to a somewhat secondary role…” [10 , p.41].

Further to this, firms are recognising decision support technology as a means for leveraging the judgement of their experienced professionals. Salijeni et al. [24,p.106] present a perspective that by reducing the time spent on mundane tasks, decision support technology can “free up much needed time for auditors to concentrate on complex issues and concerns that require professional judgement”. In addition to increasing available time, studies also describe how decision support technology can improve the transparency of professional judgements. For example, Dowling and Leech [12] illustrate how decision support technology that requires auditors to provide rationales for their judgements, forces the auditors to consider the approach they are taking. In addition, Salijeni et al. [24,p.103] report that decision support technology can be used to “visualise the thought process behind auditor judgement”. The ultimate result is a greater understanding of the auditor's application of professional judgement, improved confidence in judgements made and im proved perceptions of audit quality [12,24,45].

## 3.4. Overreliance and deskilling

According to the theory of technology dominance [75], continued use of decision aids can lead to the gradual lessening of auditors' abil ities, as auditors become reliant on the decision aids. Further to this, broad-based, long-term use of decision aids by auditors can impede the knowledge growth and advancement of the profession. Corresponding with the afore-mentioned concerns regarding the need for professional judgement, the risk of overreliance and deskilling are suggested as a justification for resisting the use of decision support technology. These issues are discussed in Sections 3.4.1 and 3.4.2. It should be noted that learning facilitation benefits appear to outweigh the deskilling con cerns, and this perspective is discussed in Section 3.4.3.

## 3.4.1. Overreliance

Overreliance occurs when an auditor does not suficiently critique the recommendations of a decision aid before accepting them as part of subjective decision-making [75]. Recent studies suggest that auditors are aware of the detrimental efect overreliance can have on audit quality [26,42,70]. In one study, a research participant noted: “auditors… just kept using what the… decision aid said, rather than thinking and that's dangerous… $\because [ 7 0 , 9 . 8 ]$ . Other studies note auditors failing to incorporate beneficial procedures and ideas when they are not recommended by the decision support technology [12,76]. Concern about overreliance can lead to disuse of a decision support system [26]. However, Dowling and Leech [12] evidence how overreliance can be reduced through management interventions, and Seow [76] discusses how system design issues can potentially overcome the problem.

## 3.4.2. Deskilling

Associated with the risk of overreliance is the longer-term threat of deskilling. Deskilling refers to a decrease in auditors' decision-making abilities. It reflects the situation where long-term use of decision aids prevents auditors from developing experiential knowledge by limiting their involvement in audit tasks [75]. Dowling et al. [77] conducted an experiment and, consistent with the theory of technology dominance, found that auditors who were usually assisted by a low level of decision support technology outperformed auditors who were accustomed to extensive decision support. Similarly, Dowling [40] noted that use of a restrictive support system appears to decrease the extent of auditors' knowledge internalisation. Further to this, a qualitative study revealed that deskilling was indeed a significant consideration for senior auditors. The auditors were wary of the “impact on [their] knowledge of the details of the accounting standard requirements or industry risks...” $[ 7 0 , \mathsf { p . 7 } ]$ but noted the potential of training and passion for their work as a means to mitigate the deskilling efect [70].

## 3.4.3. Facilitating learning

In contrast to concerns about deskilling, many studies suggest that auditors see decision support technology as a means of up-skilling. Wahdan et al. [45,p.6] report that auditors believe decision support technology can “help educate and train novices”, while Salijeni et al. [24] illustrate a perception that the technology represents a learning process for auditors. Mathisen and Nerland [59] illustrate how auditors see the technology as an “explicit part of the workplace curriculum” that mobilises work-based learning and helps to improve awareness and understanding of audit roles and responsibilities. Similarly, Dowling and Leech [12,26] show that auditors feel decision support technology can improve their understanding of audit methodology; and Bierstaker et al. [46] evidence how decision aids can improve knowledge structures of novice auditors. These studies demonstrate how decision support technology can enhance auditors' abilities to conceptualise and internalise audit knowledge. ASSs can highlight core concepts and frames of reference, provide opportunities to understand the fit of tasks within the broader engagement and relate local work to general ad vancements within the audit field [12,59]. In the aforementioned studies, this was largely facilitated through visual summaries, graphical layouts, system structure, use of narratives, and increased opportunities for flexibility and collaboration.

## 3.5. Information technology competence and support

UTAUT proposes that facilitating conditions, such as IT support and self-eficacy,<sup>10</sup> can influence technology use [27]. Studies in auditing suggest relative consensus with this proposition [28,29].<sup>11</sup> Multiple audit studies identify a lack of IT competence as one of the most sig nificant inhibiting factors [41–43]. Further to this, auditors' IT expertise has been found to be positively correlated with their level of audit technology use [42].

In general, the level of auditors' IT competence is low [16,24,78], and many studies advocate for additional investments into IT training for auditors [41,42,44,64]. Ahmi and Kent [14] report that many auditors did not even know that GAS existed. IT training is perceived as a way to improve self-eficacy, facilitate perceptions of technology as enabling rather than coercive, mitigate the risks of deskilling discussed previously, and ultimately facilitate greater adoption and use of decision support technology [12,64,70].

Studies strongly suggest that this training should not just focus on lower-level auditors but also managers and top-level staf [14,18]. Further to this, Ariwa et al. [43] identify a perception that training auditors in IT will not be suficient. They explain: “It is believed that CAATs are too technical and complex for non-IT auditors, even if training is provided” [43,p. 855]. Section 3.5.1 discusses a solution, wherein along with increasing auditors' IT competence, IT specialists are integrated throughout audit teams.

## 3.5.1. Evidence for the integration of information technology support

Vasarhelyi and Romero [18] conducted a study involving four audit teams in a large accounting firm. They note the importance of IT competence for successful use of audit technology, but, in particular, they note the importance of integrating IT support within audit teams. Integration of IT expertise improved auditors' understanding of the available tools and enabled adaptation of the tools to meet auditors' specific needs. The following quotes from the study illustrate this. They describe an audit team with integrated IT support, during the planning and internal control review stages of an audit:

[The IT specialist] collaborated to present and facilitate access to the available tools. He seemed to understand better what the auditor needed and how to make the tools useful by facilitating access to them. [18,p.358]

… both teams [audit and IT] worked together in revising the necessary rules and analysing the violations to determine which of them were attributable to real internal control deficiencies. As such, the implementation was a success and the audit team expects to extend the usage of the software to other areas in the future. $[ 1 8 , 9 . 3 5 8 - 9 ]$

Conversely, the following quote describes a situation where the IT specialist was not integrated within the audit team:

The audit team applied the standard rules, finding hundreds of meaningless violations. Subsequently, because there was no IT involvement … the team became overwhelmed. Therefore, it eschewed use of the software and instead relied upon other evidence. [18,p.359]

Dowling and Leech [12] noted similar findings when audit system “champions” were integrated when deploying a new ASS. One research participant noted: “[the champions] helped maintain the visibility [of the new system] … and get the transition over the line” [12,p.236].

## 3.6. Management and top-level support

Another prediction of the UTAUT model is that social influence has a direct influence on intentions to use technology. Social influence is defined as “the degree to which an individual perceives that important others believe he or she should use the new system” [27 , p.451]. Consistent with the UTAUT prediction, multiple studies evidence that auditors' technology use is influenced by the extent of support provided by management and top-level staf within their firms. For example, in an experimental study, Curtis and Payne [67] identified that auditors were significantly more likely to deploy CAATs when the managing partner encouraged their use. Similarly, Ahmi and Kent [14] identified support from management as one of the most notable factors afecting auditors' decisions to use GAS. A participant from their study elaborated:

We don't use GAS as much as I believe we could, essentially because a lack of understanding at the strategic level, which (a) reduces the budget available for adequate training and (b) fails to provide strategic direction for the efective and eficient use of GAS. [14 , p.103]

Other studies also identify the influence of top-level support and/or social influence on technology adoption [18,28,40,42,79].

There are, however, studies with conflicting results, suggesting that generalizability of the previously noted studies may be limited [64]. The findings may not generalise across hierarchal levels within firms, with social influence potentially having a diminished efect on higherlevel staf [41,44]. The mixed results could also be due to the dominance and/or moderating nature of other variables. Curtis and Payne [67] identify that partner influence is moderated by budget pressure and the auditor's risk profile. Specifically, they noted that partner support had a greater influence when auditors perceived high budget pressure and were risk averse. Similarly, Vasarhelyi and Romero [18] note that audit manager influence is moderated by how integrated IT support is within audit teams.

## 3.7. Firm size

Another factor afecting adoption of decision support technology at a firm level is the size of the accounting firm. Rosli et al. [16], Abou-El-Sood et al. [41], and Pedrosa and Costa [79] all report a correlation between the use of sophisticated audit technology and firm size (with smaller accounting firms adopting less sophisticated audit technology when compared to larger firms). This is purportedly due to the limited resources smaller firms have available for investing in technology, as well as doubts about the cost-efectiveness and return-on-investment associated with using the technology for their clients [16,28]. The client base of smaller firms tends to be dominated by small and medium-sized businesses, which often have simple accounting information systems [16]. Advanced audit technology is viewed by many as unnecessary and not well-suited for audits of these clients [14,16,26,29,42]. Similarly, investments in audit technology are not considered economical for firms with only a small number of audit clients [14].

## 3.8. System design

As with any technology, the decision to use decision support technology is afected by how user-friendly it is perceived to be. This concept is considered in theories such as UTAUT and TAM [27,64]. UTAUT explains that the degree of ease associated with using a system (termed efort expectancy) is expected to be correlated with the extent of use of that system [27], and quantitative studies in audit generally support this prediction [29,41]. Similarly, audit studies also evidence the significance of perceived ease of use (the TAM construct associated with efort expectancy) [64,80].

Qualitative studies provide further support for the UTAUT predic tion, suggesting that system complexity serves to inhibit auditors adoption of technology $[ 1 4 , 2 6 , 5 9 ]$ . For example, in a study of GAS use, an auditor noted:

We recently changed our audit packs and decided to stay with a paper-based system mainly because reviews from peers indicate that the GAS are cumbersome and hard to use. The adoption process is lengthy and the learning curve would increase costs to jobs by ap proximately 100% in the first year. [14 , p. 107]

The negative influence of perceived complexity is unlikely to be a concern for all firms, as there is variation in auditors' IT competence, as well as in the technologies available to diferent firms. Notably, some studies identify that auditors do not perceive audit technology to be overly complex [16]. In addition, some studies conflict with the UTAUT proposition. Bierstaker et al. [28] reported that efort expectancy did not exhibit a significant influence on audit technology usage. They suggest this may be related to personal preferences regarding efort holding less weight within a firm-wide context, compared to an individual decisionmaking context. Curtis and Payne [62] also reported that efort expectancy did not have a significant direct efect on auditors' intentions to use technology but provide evidence that it mediates the relationship between facilitating conditions and performance expectancy, particularly when there is high budget pressure within a firm.

## 3.8.1. Integral display features

While complexity is likely to deter use of decision support technology in auditing, there is evidence that specific design features can encourage use. Specifically, auditors are recognising the benefits of integral display features,<sup>12</sup> including decision support technologies that combine multiple sources of information and present visual overviews. Mathisen and Nerland [59,p.88] describe how an ASS can “… become the site for the integration and re-location of distributed knowledge and information”, and Dowling and Leech [12] describe how visual summaries can provide snapshots of significant risks and enhance auditors understanding of their tasks' fit within the broader audit process. Auditors are recognising the benefits of these system features for facilitating learning and improving ease of monitoring [12,59]. Further to this, auditors perceive this knowledge integration and display functionality as a way to improve audit quality, through enhanced risk assessments, greater understanding of clients and better documentation of procedures [15,24]. An experimental study [11] provided evidence of this, finding that use of integral display improved auditor performance in risk assessment procedures. Other design considerations of relevance include the extent of automation, system restrictiveness, customiz ability, and intuitiveness of design [15,26,44,81,82].

## 3.9. Characteristics of the individual auditor

Individual auditors approach decision support technology difer ently, depending on their own experiences, beliefs, and other personal characteristics. For example, risk averse auditors are less likely to implement new audit technology compared with auditors who are more tolerant of risk [67]. Likewise, as described in section 3.5, auditors with low self-eficacy <sup>xiii</sup> are less likely to embrace decision support technology compared with those with high self-eficacy.

The results of prior studies are inconsistent regarding the significance of individual auditors' characteristics on decision support technology use, and the role they take (that is, as moderating variables or otherwise). Some studies suggest that individual characteristics are significant primarily in the absence of other contextual factors, such as budget pressure [67]. While other studies suggest individual char acteristics moderate the influence of other factors, and/or are moderated by other factors. For example, Becker et al. [37] evidence a significant efect of learner aptitude (akin to self-eficacy) on perceived ease of use, with perceived ease of use proposed to moderate the efect of aptitude on technology acceptance decisions. Other individual characteristics that are purported to influence auditor's use of decision support technology include an auditor's interest in technology [18], passion for auditing [70], ethical mindset (see Section 3.3.1), and an auditor's confidence in their memory [80].

## 3.10. More guidance needed from professional bodies

Another theme that emerged from the literature is the inhibiting efect of the lack of professional guidance about using decision support technology in audit. Extant research identifies confusion about how modern decision support technologies “fit” with pre-existing audit methodology and that insuficient guidance is available in the auditing standards to alleviate this confusion [24]. Kuruppu et al. [10] reported that auditors felt the auditing standards need to include more guidance about the use of corporate failure models for completing going concern assessments and analytical procedures. Similarly, Salijeni et al. [24] reported that big data and data analytics could impact the way risk factors are classified and how materiality and audit quality are defined. In their study, some auditors explained that “they would refrain from fully engaging with [big data and data analytics] unless standard setters eliminated what they see as uncertainties around [their] use” [24,p.110].

## 4. Conclusion

The audit profession can be accused of preserving the status quo and not embracing modern technologies to improve the efectiveness and transparency of their services. Changed times call for changed expectations of auditors [85] and it is not reasonable for the profession to disregard the new resources available to them in the digital age. Accordingly, this literature review identifies drivers of and barriers to decision support technology use by auditors.

The key driver identified is decision support technologies' ability to improve auditors' performance. The technologies can provide a platform for leveraging the expertise of audit staf such that more misstatements and fraud can be identified and more appropriate audit opinions provided-that is, the quality of financial reporting can be improved. Decision support technology also provides the means for producing transparent rules that can make the audit process much more accountable.

In order to recognise these benefits, this study identifies the need to overcome uncertainties regarding how the technology can work with current audit approaches and auditor's professional judgement. Barriers identified also include system design considerations, limited IT competency, and a dominating focus on meeting short-term audit budgets.

This paper contributes to our understanding of how existing theoretical models (such as UTAUT) apply within the context of the audit profession. While providing support for the predictions of the UTAUT model with relative consensus, this study builds upon eforts by prior researchers to adapt the UTAUT model for the audit profession; for example, it supports the inclusion of additional variables such as budget period [62] and firm size [28].

On a practical level, this study highlights the need for decision support system specialists and auditors to collaborate in order to develop systems that auditors have confidence in and understand how to deploy successfully in their firms. At a firm-level, accounting firms should consider integrating IT specialists within audit teams and increasing decision support technology training for staf, including managers and top-level staf. Accounting firms should also consider increasing audit budget evaluation time frames to alleviate short-term concerns about the costs of new technology. At a profession-level, the professional accounting bodies should be proactive in facilitating the required collaboration. Professional accounting bodies need to provide guidance regarding how modern decision support technologies “fit” with pre-existing audit methodology. They also need to consider changes to audit methodology that make better use of available technology. For instance, by focusing on identifying “green flags” (low risks) rather than “red flags” (high risks), decision support technology can more accurately identify areas that require audit attention. It can then prompt and document audit procedures appropriate for each area. These considerations should inform the revision of auditing standards.

The study was limited to a qualitative synthesis of publications which met the study's selection criteria. This meant that articles that did not include empirical results were excluded from the synthesis.

Further empirical research is a logical extension of this study. There have been limited empirical studies regarding decision support system use in small accounting firms, and this represents a topical area for future research. Further research is also recommended regarding the optimal extent of guidance needed about decision support technology use in auditing standards, balancing the need for prescriptive guidance with the need for flexibility.
