Abstr act : In recent years, chief information officers have begun to report exponential increases in the amounts of raw data captured and retained across the organization. Managing extreme amounts of data can be complex and challenging at a time when information is increasingly viewed as a strategic resource. Since the dominant focus of the information technology (IT) governance literature has been on how firms govern physical IT artifacts (hardware, software, networks), the goal of this study is to extend the theory of IT governance by uncovering the structures and practices used to govern information artifacts. Through detailed interviews with 37 executives in 30 organizations across 17 industries, we discover a range of structural, procedural, and relational practices used to govern information within a nomological net that includes the antecedents of these practices and their effects on firm performance. While some antecedents enable the speedy adoption of information governance, others can delay or limit the adoption of information governance practices. Once adopted, however, information governance can help to boost firm performance. By incorporating these results into an extended theory of IT governance, we note how information governance practices can unlock value from the ever-expanding mountains of data currently held within organizations.

Key wor ds and p hr ases: big data, data growth, information artifact, information governance, information life cycle management, information management, information risk, information value, IT governance.

IT [information technology] does matter, but not because of hardware or even standard commercial software. It is because the intelligent and innovative application of information solves business problems and creates customer value at high speed, low cost, and the right scale. To put it simply, it’s not about the box; it’s about what’s inside the box. (Broadbent et al. [4, p. 10] in reply to Carr’s “IT Doesn’t Matter” [8]).

Wit h t he adv ent of what is colloquially t er med t he network, computer, or information age, there is growing recognition among business and information systems (IS) executives that data, or more precisely, information, may be the only inimitable IT resource that can create a sustainable competitive advantage [27, 39, 48]. Industry surveys show that the volume of raw data stored in corporate data centers is doubling in size every other year, with some industries, most notably health care, doubling in size annually [25, 44]. As data is transformed into information for decision making, practitioners describe how an organization’s ability to realize value from its information is based, in part, on how information is governed over its life cycle [24]. Based on prior studies by Khatri and Brown [18], Kooper et al. [23], and Weber et al. [51], we define information governance as a collection of capabilities or practices for the creation, capture, valuation, storage, usage, control, access, archival, and deletion of information over its life cycle.<sup>1</sup> Exponential data growth, most notably in recent years when the pace of data growth has begun to outpace the rate of decline in hardware costs causing total storage spending to climb, has transformed information governance and its related practices into a top issue for senior business and IS management [24, 32, 42].<sup>2</sup>

The IS practitioner literature sees information governance as having two goals: (1) to maximize the value of information to the organization by ensuring that information is reliable, secure, and accessible for decision making and (2) to protect information so that its value to the organization is not diminished through technology or human error, loss of timely access, inappropriate use, or misadventure [18, 35, 44]. Achieving these goals has inspired organizations to consider a variety of different information governance practices. As shown in our literature review, much of what we know about information governance comes from a handful of case studies and framework analyses that offer insights into the role and composition of information governance. Some insights can also be gleaned from the mainstream IT governance literature, principally as to physical infrastructure or personnel skills needed to support data center management, data security, application use, and access control. However, since the mainstream IT governance literature has tended to highlight the management, use, and control of physical IT artifacts—defined by Orlikowski and Iacono [30] as bundles of properties (functions or capabilities) packaged in hardware or software, and objectified by the IT governance literature in the context of IT infrastructure, IT resource allocation, locus of IT decision making, IT planning/control, and project management—researchers may need to exercise caution in extrapolating what we know about governing physical IT artifacts to governing nonphysical or information artifacts. Hence, a goal of this study is to motivate consideration of the information artifact within the confines of IT governance but with the knowledge that a more inclusive view of IT governance—one that expressly includes information—may need to consider issues that are exclusive to information. For example, unlike physical artifacts, information can be easily and quickly replicated and shared across vast distances. Information is not a wasting asset whose value declines as a function of increased use as is the case with IT hardware. Paradoxically, the value of information can increase with greater use [44].

Orlikowski and Iacono [30] report five different views of IT artifacts: a tools view that considers IT as a computing resource, a proxy view that considers IT spending and use, an ensemble view that focuses on the link between IT and people, a computational view referring to processing needs, and a nominal view that portrays IT in purely generic terms. Information artifacts can similarly be seen through the same five views: tools for manipulating information (tools view), how information is valued and used for decision making (proxy view), how information and people interact through policies (ensemble view), storage tiers and capacity management (computational view), and in a more generic sense as an asset that drives performance (nominal view). For IT governance to include both physical and information artifacts, IT governance structure and practices must necessarily consider all five views across both sets of artifacts.

Just as research has studied the antecedents and consequences of practices relating to governance of physical IT artifacts [53, 55, 56], we argue that to fully comprehend information governance, we need to understand the factors that motivate organizations to adopt different information governance practices and the primary effects of those practices. Some progress has been made in the literature toward discovering the antecedents and content of IT governance, but few researchers have tried to explain how this applies to information governance [32, 51]. The literature is equally silent on the effects of information governance. Consequently, this study seeks to answer three questions: (1) What information governance practices are organizations adopting? (2) What factors (both exogenous and endogenous) precede or predict the adoption or use of these practices? (3) What are the performance effects of information governance?

To answer these questions, we analyzed data from semistructured interviews of 37 executives in 30 organizations across 17 industries. Our findings reveal a range of structural, procedural, and relational practices of varying maturity and sophistication that organizations consider under the guise of information governance. Our analysis finds that the antecedents of information governance subdivide into two groups—one group facilitates or enables the adoption of information governance practices while a second group, primarily driven by legacy systems and product/market complexity, hinders or limits the adoption of such practices. Lastly, our analysis finds that information governance is associated with a range of intermediate or process-level benefits that are unique to the types of organizations we interviewed and the industries in which they operate. Many of these intermediate effects could conceivably affect firm-level performance. A theory of the antecedents, content, and effects of information governance complements and extends our knowledge of IT governance at a time when data is gaining in strategic importance. Where IT governance is implemented through structures and practices that more commonly revolve around physical IT artifacts, our analysis suggests a need to extend these structures and practices to recognize data or information.

The remainder of the paper is as follows. In the next section, we synthesize the existing literature on IT governance in order to learn what it says (or omits) about information governance. We then present our methodology around interviews of top executives at 30 organizations. After discussing our results, we assess research contributions and limitations, discuss implications for research and practice, and conclude.

Our review highlights a number of factors that individually or in combination are found to affect the type or range of structural, procedural, and relational IT governance practices and the degree to which these practices have been adopted by organizations. These antecedents can be grouped into six categories: organization strategy [34]; organization structure [36]; complexity and mix of product or service offerings [36]; the type of IT investment or IT architecture [21, 54, 56]; IT knowledge and culture [36]; and market factors, including environmental uncertainty, sector concentration, regulation, and market growth [55, 56].

While our review did not find evidence to indicate that IT governance practices vary by business strategy, Raghupathi [34] declares that IT governance is directly tied to the implementation of IT strategy. Since the alignment literature indicates that the focus and content of IT strategy follows or, in some cases, leads business strategy, differences in business strategy could be important to IT governance [17, 41, 43]. Sambamurthy and Zmud [36] find that organization structure reinforces the structural practices around IT governance so a centralized organization structure, for example, is more likely to produce a centralized IT governance structure. Low IT knowledge among line managers is also more consistent with centralized IT governance [36]. In the medical domain, complex, risky, costly, or potentially valuable IT investments that span organizational or process boundaries are better served via a centralized IT decision-making structure [56]. In the case of product and service offerings, Sambamurthy and Zmud [37] argue that at low levels of market relatedness (highly complex mix of products or services), IT governance decisions are likely to be decentralized, whereas with high market relatedness (minimal mix of products or services), IT governance decisions are more likely to be centralized. Legacy IT issues or a complex IT environment with diverse IT standards and minimal IT integration point to a decentralized IT structure and to an absence of procedural practices that can help organizations to trim wasteful IT spending and to minimize bad IT choices [54]. IT knowledge and culture can also shape IT governance decisions whether in the form of IT function power [56] or the level of IT knowledge found in a culture created around the exchange of IT ideas or vision [9]. Lastly, industry factors as evidenced by the overall degree of environmental uncertainty, regulatory rules, industry concentration, and location (through regulatory implications) can affect IT governance [53, 55].

Throughout Table 1, we note that IT governance encompasses a mix of structural, procedural, and relational practices. Structural practices tend to present the locus of decision making as either centralized, decentralized, or federal (a hybrid) with decisions emanating from high-level steering committees, IT task forces, or other structures for strategy setting and policy determination [2, 9, 32, 34, 36, 46, 47, 53, 54, 55, 56]. Procedural practices identify how IT governance is executed, potentially at different levels within the organization. The controls literature shows how individual projects or tasks can be managed through self-regulation, group norms, policies, industry standards, and regulations [19]. IT controls come from policies or practices that are meant to produce mutually agreeable outcomes between the principal and agent [19]. As our review shows, these practices comprise a wide variety of actions such as ensuring that all large IT investments undergo a rigorous cost-benefit evaluation, managing costs and resource use over the life of a project, and evaluating IT business value before, during, and after an IT investment has gone live [32]. To the extent that IT governance is meant to promote accountability around IT resource usage and creation of IT business value, procedural practices promote greater accountability via IT chargebacks or service-level agreements [53, 54]. More recently, business intelligence tools and related IT dashboards modeled on the balanced scorecard collect IT performance metrics, thus allowing managers to monitor their projects [54]. With relational practices, the literature recognizes that IT governance is increasingly likely to draw on the expertise and experience of users and non-IT managers to plan, execute, and monitor IT investments. For example, Raghupathi [34] notes that boards of directors are more cognizant than ever of the risk and legal liability of failing IT investments, and so relational practices are evolving to allow shared participation by IT and non-IT decision makers in setting and enforcing appropriate policies [36]. Xue et al. [55] find that organizations are more likely to utilize a decentralized form of IT governance when business units have a different strategic focus than their corporate parent. Meanwhile, Boh and Yellin [2] find that management support of IT and mechanisms to expand stakeholder involvement in IT decision making are critical to IT governance. Lastly, De H aes and Van Grembergen [9] present knowledge management, end-user training, communication, and education as examples of relational practices that bring chief information officers (CIOs), non-IT managers, and end users closer together in their mutual understanding of the purpose of IT governance in organizations.

Evidence also shows that when consistent with the environment and other contingent antecedents, IT governance practices contribute to superior alignment or fit between IT and business strategy, which in turn leads to higher firm performance [9, 45, 47], higher return on assets [53], and enhanced IS functional performance [46]. Taken as a whole, the literature paints a theory of the antecedents and consequences of structural, procedural, and relational practices that together represent IT governance. Our review indicates that these practices focus on the physical aspects of IT—enterprise architecture [2], IT infrastructure [55], hospital applications [56], and systems development [19, 20, 21, 46]—areas that align with the tools view of IT artifacts as outlined by Orlikowski and Iacono [30]. We also note the presence of the ensemble view in the form of structural and relational practices that speak to the links between structures (people) and IT. The proxy view is also evident in studies that reveal the value of IT governance. Yet the focus throughout the literature remains on physical or tangible IT assets such as hardware and software. Information or data may be a peripheral consideration when organizations are questioning how to govern physical IT artifacts, but the reverse does not hold. Information and, by implication, information governance relies on hardware and software. Without IT governance or at least those aspects of IT governance that deal with physical IT, there would be no need for information governance. Weill and Ross [53] see data center technologies as a quintessential aspect of IT governance and storage as a necessary component of IT infrastructure, yet the focus remains on the physical box and less on the data or information inside the box. Recognizing this, a new area of study (reviewed in the lower part of Table 1) has emerged to explicitly address information governance under the general framework of IT governance using many of the same structural, procedural, and relational practices previously attributed to successful forms of IT governance. We now consider this subset of the literature to determine what practices are the same as those already known in the mainstream IT governance literature and what practices are different and, therefore, unique or specific to information.

## Information Governance Literature

Using the five decision domains of IT governance outlined in Weill and Ross [53]—IT principles, IT architecture, IT infrastructure, IT applications, and IT investments—with their prime focus on physical IT artifacts, Khatri and Brown [18] describe a parallel set of procedural practices covering data principles, data quality, metadata (data about data), data access, and data life cycle (storage, retention, and retirement practices). These parallel practices entail explicit consideration of data/information although there is still some residual and necessary consideration of the physical IT artifact. For example, the functionality, cost, reliability, complexity, capacity, scalability, and maintainability of storage hardware and software are key considerations in how information will be managed over its life cycle [44]. These factors are influenced by changes in the economic value of information to the organization such as deciding to migrate data from a less reliable (lower cost) platform to a more reliable (higher cost) platform as its value rises.<sup>3</sup> Data access may also have implications for storage infrastructure. For example, while organizations are mindful of the need to store information for defined periods, the ability to search and extract information using particular search criteria has given rise to new practices in the area of e-discovery.<sup>4</sup> Some types of storage hardware function well from a cost and reliability perspective (most notably tape), but these can complicate or delay e-discovery, forcing IT managers to make important trade-offs. Likewise, data access and quality practices are affected by the need for timely access to accurate and complete data [18, 42, 44].<sup>5</sup> Khatri and Brown [18] explain that structural practices can be positioned on a decentralized-centralized continuum. For example, data custodians or stewards (whose responsibilities can be defined at the function, user group, or application level) can decide on overarching IT principles such as strategies on how to use information for competitive advantage or how to comply with new legal requirements. In contrast, decentralized users can act as data owners since they have an ability to use local knowledge to assign a value to their information. Users can also work with IT managers on ways to protect and enable that value through retention policies.

Just as there are multiple practices underlying IT governance, Weber et al. [51] argue that there is no one-size-fits-all approach to information governance. Consistent with the three types of practices in IT governance described earlier, Weber et al. [51] portray information governance as having decision-maker roles (structural practices), decision tasks (procedural practices), and responsibilities (relational practices). They also argue that information governance practices are contingent on seven factors that map to the six antecedents of IT governance noted earlier: performance strategy and competitive strategy (jointly map to organization strategy), diversification breadth (maps to product or market complexity), degree of process IT harmonization (maps to IT investments and architecture), decision-making culture (maps to knowledge and IT culture), organizational structure (maps to organizational structure), and industry regulation (maps to environmental uncertainty). This degree of commonality between the antecedents of IT governance and information governance is important for it shows, at least in theory, that factors that are already known to IT executives and that are likely being measured already can be extrapolated from the realm of governing physical IT artifacts to governing information artifacts. Hence, if we know something about the drivers of IT governance already, we can infer that those same factors are likely relevant to information governance. In Table 2, we identify areas of overlap between the IT governance and information governance literature. A critical difference between the antecedents of each type of governance is the rate of information growth, which is a natural trigger for more effective information governance [42, 44]. IT strategy and IT structure are also said to affect information governance because of their association or alignment with organization strategy and structure, which, as noted earlier, are also shown to influence information governance [5, 36].

We note also that the three general categories of IT governance practices—structural, procedural, and relational—reappear in the information governance literature, albeit now with a focus on managing or protecting the information artifact rather than just the physical IT artifact. In practical terms, however, the Economist Intelligence Unit (EIU) reports that a mere 38 percent of businesses have an information governance strategy in place; fewer than 25 percent consider their information governance strategy to be effective on a host of outcome measures [15]. It seems, therefore, that most organizations have yet to truly deploy structural, procedural, or relational practices to maximize the value of their information assets or to protect that value from myriad risk factors that might undermine or delay the flow of organizational value from information. Theoretical arguments also point to similarities between IT governance and information governance as to their impact on firm performance. While there is as yet no empirical evidence in the literature to suggest a link between information governance and strategic alignment or IS effectiveness, the literature shows that risk mitigation is a much heralded consequence of information governance but not of IT governance [44].

In summary, what our review shows are critical links between the long-established IT governance literature and the nascent information governance literature as to the antecedents and effects of each type of governance. There is also substantial overlap in the three types of practices used to achieve governance but the focus of those practices is different in each case. We now turn to investigate the nomological links between the antecedents, content, and consequences of information governance. Beyond what can be seen in the IT literature, our goal is to create a theory to describe how information governance practices reflect different antecedents and how information governance practices, in turn, can influence firm performance.

## Methodology

Field int erv iews ar e an imp ort ant mechanism for gathering information about topics of interest in social science, particularly in the early stages when researchers are trying to understand an emergent issue [28]. Interviews can be used to build theory through grounded research where researchers have no a priori guiding theories or assumptions but they can also be used to iteratively refine theory if there is some level of basic understanding of a phenomenon or an initial theory that needs to be tested or further refined [28]. As shown in our literature review on information governance and IT governance, we have some basis for believing that certain antecedents could shape the content of information governance. We also have some basis in the literature for decomposing information governance into a range of structural, procedural, and relational practices, and for believing that these practices could affect firm performance. Field interviews, therefore, provide a way to validate, build on, and test what we already believe from the current literature. Interviews can also refute whatever associations or assumptions researchers might have held beforehand. The ability to create, test, and refine theory can be further improved by conducting multiple interviews to expand generalizability and create external validity through cross-interview comparisons [10, 12, 13, 14].

Accordingly, data for this study were gathered through interviews with senior executives at 30 large for-profit and not-for-profit organizations (see Table 3). All of the interviews were conducted as part of the How Much Information (HMI) project through the Global Information Industry Center at the University of California, San Diego.<sup>6</sup> A panel of 10 senior IS researchers was assembled to conduct the interviews; this panel included the first and third author.<sup>7</sup> Two members of the panel—not the authors—were tasked with identifying prospective interviewees in different organizations, schedul ing interviews with IT executives, transcribing interviews, postinterview follow-up, and sharing results with the interviewees. The interviewees and their organizations were chosen on the basis of past relationships with the research panel; several had participated in prior research projects with members of the research panel or were affiliated with IT research centers directed by certain members of the research panel. During the planning phase of the project (third quarter of 2008), the research pane sought to identify organizations and industries that were experiencing accelerated rates of data growth and that might therefore be more likely to have adopted some type of information governance. This approach to identifying prospective interview sites is supported by the literature as it helps to focus on sites that are experiencing the phenomenon of interest rather than randomly selecting an organization to study [14, 57]. Hence, 19 of our 30 interview sites are concentrated in 6 data-intensive industries (health care: 7; energy: 3; insurance: 3; financial services: 2; pharmaceuticals: 2; and retail: 2). Health care is particularly noted for its growing use of data as hospital adopt electronic medical records and as data-intensive scans such as MRI (magnetic resonance imaging) (> 250 MB [megabytes] per image) become more pervasive. The energy/utilities, insurance, finance, and pharmaceuticals sectors are also reported to be collecting and retaining large amounts of data as they react to governmental mandates [44]. The retail sector is included based on its interest in analytics and growing use of data to monitor in-store activities. The remaining 11 organizations in ou interview sample span a wide range of industries, but their common trait is an ability and claimed desire to capture and retain growing amounts of data from customers, their digitization of critical processes, and a trend toward selling digital products and services. When initially approached by the research panel, prospective interviewees were informed that the project was investigating the data growth phenomenon and that interviewees would be asked how much data their organization were capturing, the different practices they were using to manage information, the factors that directed them to adopt such practices, and their effects on various aspects of firm performance. Consistent with Yin, who says that to fully “explain a phenomenon is to stipulate a presumed set of causal links” [57, p. 141], the use of these lead-in questions, which were e-mailed to the interviewees before each interview and then verbally repeated during each interview, is key to finding any causal links between the practices that collectively represent information governance, the antecedents of those practices, and their consequences.

Interviews with key IT executives or others with significant data center or information governance responsibilities began in late 2008 and continued through June 2009. Six interviews involved two or more interviewees. Each interview was tape recorded and transcribed by a professional secretarial service; each transcription was later checked by the interviewers to ensure accuracy and completion. In some cases, the two researchers overseeing the project contacted the interviewees to finalize the collection of data on rates of past and present data growth and other pertinent data that were unavailable during the initial interview. Each interview followed a semistructured approach using a set of questions devised by the research team (interview questions appear in the Appendix). Questions emphasized the extent and nature of data growth; the different structural, procedural, and relational practices used to govern information; the antecedents of those practices; and the different effects of those information governance practices on firm performance. The interviewers were encouraged to follow the interview protocol carefully but to insert additional questions as appropriate based on the flow of the conversation. Follow-up questions were encouraged if this led to additional data on information governance or the factors driving the rapid growth in data collection and retention. Weekly or biweekly conference calls allowed the researchers to report on the effectiveness of the interview protocol and on trends or comments that they began to identify with each passing interview.

## Interview Coding Analysis

Two of the authors worked independent of one another to code all 30 interviews. The authors first agreed on a set of 14 nodes that mapped back to the literature review (see Table 2). Nine nodes were used to capture interview commentary on the antecedents of information governance: organization strategy, IT strategy, organization structure, IT structure, product/service complexity or market mix, IT architecture, IT culture, regulation, and information growth; three nodes were used to capture key practices (structural, procedural, and relational); and two nodes were used to capture data on performance/future opportunities and risk outcomes. Definitions and operational examples of data to possibly include under each node were also agreed in advance by the coders (see Table 4). Rather than compel the coders to strictly adhere to this 14-node structure, each coder had the flexibility to add additional nodes if he found comments that were relevant to understanding information governance but that did not fall under any of the 14 existing nodes.

Coding occurred in two phases: first on an initial set of 12 interviews, which was then followed by the final set of 18 interviews. The coding of each interview followed a three-step procedure as outlined in Strauss and Corbin [40]. Using NVivo—a software tool for content analysis—open coding was first used to assign labels to key areas of text (examples of these labels are shown in Table 4). Second, axial coding was used to assign each label (and its associated text) to one of the preexisting 14 nodes or to a new node if necessary. A third step—selective coding—involved selecting and arranging nodes to form a “causal” relationship model. Since our analysis spanned multiple interviews in two phases, this third step was first performed at the end of the first phase of coding and then again at the end of the second phase after all 30 interviews had been coded. The research model shown later in the paper is the result of that third phase.

Since coding occurred in two phases, the two coders met at the end of the first phase to compare their findings and, if necessary, to reconcile areas of disagreement on how a comment should be interpreted or if additional nodes were required beyond the initial set of 14 nodes. At the end of the first phase, the coders were in agreement on 90 percent of the content of their independent coding analysis and discussed how to code comments where there was disagreement. The use of multiple coders and this degree of consensus is key to establishing reliability of the coding process since the use of a single coder could introduce a degree of bias [33]. Both coders agreed that no additional nodes were warranted prior to beginning the second phase and that the original 14-node structure could be retained when coding the remaining 18 interviews. At the end of the second phase, the coders again met. Again, the coders agreed on 90 percent of their coding and were able to explore areas of disagreement. No nodes were added or removed during this final phase of coding.

To establish the validity of the coding results—namely, convergent and divergent validity—at the end of the second phase when the results from both phases were combined, two graduate students were independently asked to review comments that had been coded to seven nodes (organization strategy, IT strategy, product/service complexity, regulation, information growth, relational governance practices, and firm performance) that were randomly selected from the initial set of 14 nodes.<sup>8</sup> Any information that could reveal the name of the node or its definition was removed. Consistent with the notion of convergent and divergent validity where items load more on a single construct than on other constructs, both students were asked to assign a descriptive label to each node that best represented the contents of that node. Each student assigned a label to each node that largely matched those established by the authors at the outset of the research. Hence, the contents of a substantial portion of our 14 node structure can be considered valid.

## Antecedents of Information Governance

A review of the transcripts from all 30 interviews confirmed that the total volume of raw data was expanding rapidly in all but one firm where a reduction in the overall level of business activity, coincident with the 2008 global financial crisis, resulted in zero data growth. At the other 29 sites, the annual rate of data growth ranged from 18 percent to 150 percent (mean: 40 percent); as such, the amount of stored data will double every other year. Considering differences in firm size, the absolute amount of stored data ranged from hundreds of terabytes to double-digit petabytes. Excluding Intel, whose current data holding is publicly known, nondisclosure agreements prevent us from reporting each firm’s data holding and growth rates although every interviewee outlined data growth as challenging, costly, and an immediate cause for managerial concern.<sup>9</sup>

In reviewing the factors that led organizations to actively consider or use information governance practices, several common antecedents emerged. The interviewees talked about how the goals inherent in their business strategy and how they sought to compete led them not only to collect particular types of data but to design governance mechanisms to ensure that value was realized from that data. Several noted that data were now a primary component of their IT strategy and IT planning deliberations, while their strategies for using data (which is now part of their IT strategy) have also led them to adopt particular governance practices.

The push toward governance is really [a reaction to] business strategy, the nature of the product, and requirements for integration with outside partners. (CIO, Carefirst)—Organization Strategy

Dealing with the growth of data is now a central part of our IT strategy just like managing data is now a part of our IT strategy. (CIO, Direct Energy)—IT Strategy

Organization structure and IT structure were similarly identified as important factors behind the adoption and use of information governance. Centralization was seen as enabling information governance, whereas decentralization was seen as allowing disparate approaches to information management that then made it difficult to govern information in a holistic fashion.

In terms of data management and putting a governance model in place, the biggest challenge for us is our decentralized heritage and our large legacy that instantiates a variety of disparate data across the organization. (CIO, American Red Cross)—Organization Structure/IT Structure

Just as a centralized IT structure facilitates the adoption of information governance practices that can be pushed down to all users, IT standardization/IT integration also enables information governance. Several interviewees referred to standardized platforms that deliver shared services and that this type of integrated infrastructure can make it easier to build and execute a common set of information governance practices.

Chapters have historically been semi-autonomous but with our new governance structure, we can integrate how their data is serviced. We’re aiming for standardization and centralization through a shared services structure. (CIO, American Red Cross)—IT Standardization

Several interviewees repeatedly pointed to analytics programs as a key factor behind their increasing data growth, but many also mentioned a data-driven culture that promoted innovative uses of data. Their ability to gain insights from data (and to turn insights into value) was in turn mediated by their ability to devise suitable governance mechanisms with structures and policies designed to protect data or to enable its use. Hence, an information-based IT culture and knowledge of IT can affect information governance practices.

Once you get the ability to start asking questions, you get more questions that you never thought about before. It’s wonderful to watch the business areas using that information and being able to analyze data, which then leads them to ask more questions, and then to go back and analyze data again. That desire to get fresh insights from data is forcing us to put governance structures and policies in place. We have to facilitate this type of use. (CIO, Selective Insurance)—IT Culture

Regulation was mentioned as a significant driver of data growth and one of the key forces behind information governance. Industry mandates can be unique, but there was consistency in how organizations reacted to these rules by enacting information governance policies to accelerate compliance and to reduce the risk of financial or reputational penalties in cases of noncompliance. One CIO referenced a desire by legal counsel in his organization to capture and retain all data indefinitely based on what they read in case law regardless of whether current legislation actually required this extreme level of data retention or not.<sup>10</sup>

Data protection regulations require that I know where every bit of data is, so that plays directly into our data governance decisions. (CIO, Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center)—Regulation

While the above analysis uncovered specific antecedents that had a positive or enabling influence on information governance, we also noted instances where certain factors undermined or inhibited the use or adoption of information governance. For example, some interviewees alluded to silo-oriented legacy systems that limit both process integration and data standardization as interfering with the adoption of information governance practices and adding to their overall complexity when used in a highly complex environment. Product and service complexity or mix can also prove limiting when organizations operate across a wide variety of heterogeneous markets with different data requirements; a single set of information governance policies may be difficult to impose on all users. Some interviewees further noted how a pack rat–like culture can permit users to retain data regardless of its age or value or the ongoing costs of keeping the data on active storage devices. Such behavior can inhibit or undermine the adoption of information governance practices as organizations may first need to educate their users against the continued retention of unnecessary data.

There are positive and negative drivers of information governance. Positive drivers occur where companies are naturally growing. It’s where you have more data to go deeper into an analysis of your markets or customers, showing what you can do to provide better service. But there are also a lot of negative drivers. For example, the lack of a proper application platform or architecture can create a situation where information is going to grow in a negative way. Product complexity is the same. The company will try to get more information and to animate their processes but the lack of proper architecture will create silos, data duplication, and all around lack of integration or weak integration at best. It is information growth but in a negative way. This is not associated with new revenue generation, new market opportunities, or profits. In fact, all this really does is to generate costs. About a year ago, 20 percent of our information growth came from positive drivers. Now, it’s more like 60 percent positive drivers because we operate on a decision framework that gives priority to the positive and rejects the negative. (CIO, Novell)—Legacy IT/Product Complexity

Researchers, to a large extent, act independently when it comes to storage environments. So their grants and research monies allow them to set up an independent shop, hire their own people, and do their own thing when it comes to systems and storage administration. Senior management has recognized the dangers with this data being managed independently in a nonuniform fashion and with not a lot of policy put against some of the data as far as backup and disaster recovery issues. [As the researchers see it], it’s more of a cultural discussion, it’s control. . . . I think they’re just very protective and very possessive of their environment. (CTO, Johns Hopkins University)—Pack-rat culture

## Composition of Information Governance—Representative Practices

As previously noted, information governance represents a spectrum of structural, procedural, and relational practices that span all the stages of the information life cycle from the point of data creation through data destruction. Our efforts to understand the range of information governance practices used by our sample led to a bottom-up approach, whereby, as an implementation of axial coding [57], we clustered different practices under each of the three main practice nodes. This allowed us to uncover similarities between the organizations in our sample and to isolate practices that may have either been exclusive to one site or exclusive to an industry.

We have a master data map that looks at our six major areas of data: financial, HR [human resources], development, facilities, student records, and research. We have a data management committee whose members are the owners of these six areas. They set the policies and decide what is core and peripheral. It also consults with administrators from the various schools as to how information is used. We also have a policy document that sets out roles for data custodians, stewards, and owners. Even if I’m the CIO, I don’t own the institution’s data—I’m not the business owner. Our senior VP [vice president] of finance and facilities owns our financial information. Our VP of HR owns the personnel information. But being responsible for data integrity, storage, and security, I have a role as the main data steward. My job is executing policy. (CIO, University of Washington Medical Center)—Structural practice

Information management has reached a point where it’s far beyond the boundaries of the internal IS function and now has entered into the organization as a whole, which means that the user base has power and responsibility for information management that we [IS] need to understand better. It’s their data, they own it. It’s jointly governed: us and them. (CIO, Novell)—Structural practice

A review of structural practices identified by the interviewees shows an attempt to share oversight for information governance with a broad spectrum of users and other parties (data center managers, CFO [chief financial officer], and legal counsel). Shared oversight allows participants to communicate their aversion to certain policies that could create a perception of over-governance (too restrictive). The literature already states that a standard one-size-fits-all governance policy may not work, and interviewees seem to have internalized that fact [51].

In the realm of procedural practices, we discovered a plethora of activities that organizations have adopted to either maximize the value potential of information or to protect that value from various sources of risk that could undermine that value. CIOs are acutely aware of the rising costs of managing expanding amounts of information and the need to signal those costs to users in the form of differential service levels and, more importantly, chargebacks. Costs are monitored often on a tier-by-tier basis as a way to motivate users to consider moving their least valuable information to a cheaper tier. As one CIO argued, procedural practices permit a greater understanding of the changing value of information and how this value needs to be matched with the characteristics of different storage systems that will maximize and protect that value.

So the challenge now is how do I provide information life cycle management of every kind of data, not just for the [CT scan, X-ray or MRI] image but for the five terabytes of research data or the hundreds of terabytes of medical records? But what’s the value of a lab test that’s 20 years old? I have to constantly think about that. I have a finite budget for SAN [storage area networks], NAS [network attached storage], Fiber Channel [a network for storage networking], various types of RAID [redundant array of independent disks], and non-RAID systems so I have to judge reliability, security, and cost and then balance value with all those factors. (CIO, Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center)—Procedural practice

We want to motivate behaviors that motivate users to get value from their information so we have switched to consumption-based pricing. We want people to manage their consumption; storage is not cheap. In some cases, I might take a hit and not cover my costs if I want people to do certain things like data analytics but I’ll balance it somewhere else by charging more. We’ll subsidize the right behavior when it matters and vice versa. (CIO, Constellation Energy)— Procedural practice

Procedural practices are sensitive to inflection points on the information life cycle where the value of information expands (as its usefulness grows) or contracts (as its usefulness falls). Designing practices that respond to changes in value can be complex if an organization has different types of information such as e-mail or customer relationship management records but then tries to govern everything with just a single set of governance policies. An analysis of procedural practices in our sample indicates that some interviewees identify practices relating to access and security as different points in the information life cycle but they also spoke of the effectiveness of practices for migrating data between storage tiers. These practices refer to the age of information (auto-archiving policies ordinarily migrate data after it reaches a certain age), its usefulness, and recoverability.

One of our key ILM [information life cycle management] goals is to protect data. We have practices that help us decide when we need to move data from a near-term or frequently accessed server to a less frequently accessed system. We have practices to protect data as we’re doing that in the event that you have to recover from a hardware error. We put these practices in place by working with a very small group of people. If a user says, “this data is not needed or can be archived,” we move it. We don’t always wait for them to tell us—we can also track it ourselves where usage falls off. . . . We do it to manage the cost and the risk of critical data getting lost or corrupted. (CIO, Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center)—Procedural practice

The way that I would refer to tier-one would be primarily for the things that are, from a retrieval point of view, needed fairly quickly. So that would include e-mails, any reports that we’re storing, documents, presentations, and so on. Now the way that data are archived to tier-two or tier-three has more to do with the frequency of access so we monitor access. So even if there’s something in your e-mail but it’s in a folder that you haven’t accessed for a month, that’s automatically moved to a lower tier of service. Then if you haven’t accessed something for 90 days, it would be moved down to the next tier. There are some things that we’re excluding automatically, we’re not putting anything for our trading systems on tier-two or tier-three. That stuff goes into tier-one and it stays there. So we really manage tier-one since the amount of stuff on that tier is growing exponentially and it stays on tier-one because it never loses value. (CIO, T. Rowe Price)—Procedural practice

Procedural practices are described by CIOs as the mechanical arm of information governance—it is how information governance is actually applied in practice. Procedural practices are both technical and managerial. Technical practices describe how systems have automated migration of data between tiers or how additional storage resources are provisioned or how systems are used to govern access and backups. Managerial practices describe how data is classified (data classification) so that storage decisions can be made based on differential value characteristics. It also includes practices to better control storage costs.

We also reviewed our data for examples of relational practices that show how organizations build knowledge among users around the need for information governance and how they work with conflicting policies. Relationships were described in terms ranging from adversarial to cooperative. The purpose of relational practices seemed to be around directing users to reorient their perception of storage as a cheap and infinite resource to instead regarding storage as a finite and increasingly costly resource. User education can focus on costs, but it is also meant to inform users as to why governance policies are written in specific ways so that end users do not try to trivialize, circumvent, or ignore the rules, thereby exposing the organization to unwarranted levels of technical, reputational, or financial risk. As one CIO noted, establishing governance rules may necessitate some form of active negotiation with users so that all policy arguments are weighed.

There’s a real user education challenge here. You have to allocate scarce storage resources. You have to make them aware of that. This has significant impact from a cost perspective. It’s not just how much you have to invest on additional space. It’s how much you compromise on other things so you can afford storage, so education is really important. (CIO, Novell)—Relational practice

You have to have a governance framework in place that says ultimately this is where IT decision-making power resides. You need to have a skill in your IT organization that is able to engage in that dialogue as opposed to simply being the order taker. We want to be much more assertive and engaged in that dialogue early on. It’s not just a question of the storage type that we’re ultimately going to use to support your application or the choice of technology that we’re going to use. We need to know the policy trade-offs—it’s a constant negotiation. (CIO, Visa)—Relational practice

As regards senior business executives, the challenge for me and any CIO thinking about this stuff is you’ve got to explain to them how ILM works and do it in a way that they get it. These guys are not technologists but they have to understand what’s happening. You have to do it in simple terms and explain to them why you are spending so much time and money on data architecture because to them it’s plumbing; they need to see the bigger picture. (CIO, JC Penney)— Relational practice

Overall, our analysis suggests that organizations are undertaking a range of structural, procedural, and relational governance practices. Some organizations noted that their governance practices were still in the initial stages, whereas others were more mature and confident with their choice of practices. ## Consequences of Information Governance

Our analysis of interviewee comments around the consequences of information governance point to a range of intermediate-level effects that often reflected the industry in which an organization operates. For example, in the health care sector, interviewees spoke about how information provisioning (having the right information in the right place at the right time) led to reduced medical errors and associated lawsuits, fewer adverse drug interactions, higher quality of care, shorter hospital stays, and reduced operating costs. Other outcomes included reduced risk and improved decision making in the energy trading sector, whereas in the automotive industry, information governance was associated with enhanced customer satisfaction and reduced costs. In the airline industry, information governance was associated with better decision making in scheduling, market analysis, and ticket pricing. Persistent mention of intermediate performance effects rather than firm-level effects such as sales growth or profitability show that interviewees may face a more difficult task linking information governance to firm-level outcomes. Prior research, however, reveals that when IT delivers positive intermediate-level effects that these effects aggregate up to the firm level [1].

We’ve seen a spike in data mining, data analysis, and data-driven decision making. You have a lot of information but it’s not so much historical analysis but rather near-time decisioning. These are very tight decision loops. You go from data collection to a very quick near-time decisioning process to the transactionalization of that decision. It’s a very tight loop and so better information management allows us to make better and faster revenue-generating decisions. (CIO, Alaska Air)

If doctors had a comprehensive view of health records, they could deliver better care. So it really is about driving quality, safety, and lower cost. (CIO, Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center)

It’s helping us to look at different pricing hypotheses and scenarios, and to calculate how the risk would change if the pricing dynamics change. (CIO, Direct Energy)

We see it impacting . . . customer satisfaction and customer retention. We can get out in front of possible recalls. We can make potential improvements in parts for the same model and for next generation models, as well as reducing our warranty expense. (CIO, Nissan)

The belief that increased use of governance practices is good for firm performance may not seem unusual, particularly if these practices are intended to enable innovative uses of information or to suppress risky activities that might lead to an erosion of value from using information. When asked about this, one IT executive cautioned against making a sweeping more is better argument. In his opinion, governance is good up to a point, beyond which users can feel constrained. Over-governance could limit information-led innovation, motivating users to work around policies and to take unnecessary risks with their information.

When you take a lock down approach to information flow and you over-govern it, you destroy value, and if you under-govern it, you won’t maximize the value to the business. It’s an interesting balancing act and one that we constantly have to monitor and adjust. This past year, I revised our mission from safeguarding information assets to something we call “protect to enable.” If we just focus on protection, we will over-control and constrain and then we’ll generate actually more risk since people will go around the controls. The only way to manage risk is to run towards it and be at the front of it. That’s the point of opportunity rather than just trying to contain it. So we protect to enable. If we don’t, then we’re not in the sweet spot of reasonable risk and reasonable controls to maximize the value of information assets to the firm. If you constrain, you drive the business to create. You will drive [users] to go out and do it on their own, which without the right expertise, is like a kid running with scissors. Unless you can somehow manage that, you will have ungoverned data storage that will create more challenges down the road, more risk, more compliance issues, and probably a far higher total cost of ownership. (CISO, Intel Corporation)

The inflection point at which the effects of information governance start to decline and potentially become negative is an open question, but one that may become increasingly important as the strategic importance of information becomes more widely accepted. Our interviews did not reveal a sense of resentment among users toward information governance policies or practices where resentment may be an indication of over-governance, but as our interviews involved IT executives rather than users, it may be difficult to find this inflection point without speaking with users who are likely to be most disadvantaged by having to compromise their independence by following a strict set of information governance policies.

## Final Research Model

Our analysis to this point has centered on building up, in a piecemeal fashion, the three key elements of our theoretical research model: the antecedents of information governance; various structural, procedural, and relational practices used to implement information governance; and their intermediate effects on firm performance. Since efforts to identify a causal link were explicitly designed into our questioning, our final model, shown in Figure 1, identifies our assessment of the causal link between information governance, its antecedents, and consequences. The left-hand side of our model shows that some antecedents have an enabling impact on the adoption of information governance—making it easier to create and implement information governance structures or policies—whereas other antecedents create obstacles that potentially frustrate, limit, or inhibit the adoption of information governance practices. The right-hand side of our model shows the effects of information governance as indicated through various intermediate-level measures of firm performance.

## Discussion and Implications

As pr ev iously not ed, t he IT g ov er nance lit er at ur e focuses primarily on how organizations govern physical IT artifacts (hardware, software, networks). This focus is justified given the significant historical cost and importance of physical IT artifacts that routinely consume as much as 60 percent of IT budgets and the finding in the productivity literature that IT capital is a key source of value [16, 29, 52]. As hardware and software become more commoditized and with the focus now turning to data or information as a strategic resource, the question becomes: What happens to IT governance? Can existing structures and mechanisms that organizations use to govern their physical IT artifacts be adapted or extended to include consideration of information artifacts? As outlined earlier, it is challenging to think of information governance separate from technical infrastructure or physical IT artifacts (storage area networks, RAID, virtualization systems, database technologies, dashboards, or storage provisioning software) needed to store and manipulate data. Information governance will almost certainly flounder without governance of these physical IT artifacts, even if the latter are seen as commodities. Hence, it may be challenging and unwise to govern physical IT artifacts separately from information artifacts, under separate structures, separate decision rights, separate policies, and with little regard for what the other is doing or whether they are working against each other. As such, it may be preferable for organizations to link governance of their physical IT with that of data or information by placing both artifacts under one governance structure, or if they are governed separately, to link both structures through shared membership or to otherwise devise mechanisms for exchanging ideas.

Our approach to studying information governance was to begin with what we already know about IT governance—even if what we know is skewed toward physical IT artifacts. The fact that the practices that comprise IT governance—structural, procedural, and relational—apply as much to how physical IT is governed as to how information can be governed is reassuring for it reveals that both types of artifacts can be jointly governed under a common umbrella of practices. Research on IT governance by Konsynski and McFarlan [22], Weill and Ross [53], and others shown in our literature review (Table 1) describe different governance archetypes for allocating decision rights or responsibilities and the decisions they cover in the areas of IT principles, IT architecture, IT infrastructure, business applications, and spending prioritization. These areas almost certainly cover the physical side of information governance in terms of a supporting or enabling IT infrastructure. By adding information governance as a further decision area populated by the procedural and relational practices identified in our interviews and by embedding structural practices and responsibilities such as data stewardship within existing governance archetypes, all aspects of information governance could be addressed. As noted in the IT governance literature, organizations are not required to adopt a consistent governance archetype for all aspects of IT, and so by adding information governance as a separate decision area, an organization could, if required, combine varying degrees of centralization and decentralization for different elements of IT [5, 36, 53]. The net result is a richer theory of IT governance that explicitly includes governance of the information artifact without losing sight of the need for ongoing governance of physical IT artifacts or how physical IT artifacts— even if they are commodities—continue to be necessary for organizations to generate value from using information for enhanced decision making.

## Research Contributions

This study extends the nascent information-related elements of IT governance research in three respects. First, given the strategic importance of information and the emphasi in the IT governance literature on physical IT artifacts, we develop a nomological network around the antecedents, composition, and consequences of information governance. Our results can be added to what we know about IT governance to produce a more inclusive theory that addresses the governance of physical IT and information artifacts. Many of the factors that lead organizations to adopt information governance practices are equally relevant to governing physical IT artifacts. Similarly, the practices used to implement information governance can be grouped under the same general headings—structural, procedural, and relational—as used in the extant IT governance literature for governing physical IT. As such, future IT governance research could address the governance of all IT artifacts—physical and informational—without significant disruption. Second, in reviewing the antecedents of information governance, we find that while some factors enable the adoption of structural, procedural, and relational practices—practices that help organizations maximize the value of their information through higher profitability or improved decision making while minimizing risk factors that could delay or impede the real ization of value—other antecedents can delay or hinder the adoption of information governance practices. This clash of antecedents with potentially offsetting effects sets up an interesting exercise for organizations that concurrently possess both enablers and inhibitors of information governance. For example, an organization could face legislation that requires retention of data, something that our analysis shows is an enabler of information governance but they could also possess legacy IT that impedes the adoption of information governance practices. Future research could help to determine which antecedents (enablers or inhibitors) are likely to dominate. Third, while there is evidence that the adoption of information governance practices can benefit firm performance—through intermediate level effects—there is at least a suggestion in our analysis that more information governance is not always better for firm performance. To the extent that certain information governance practices breed resentment among users if such practices are considered harsh or over-reaching by disrupting work patterns or reducing productivity, a stricter application of information governance could cause costs and risk to rise in ways that reduce firm performance. Future research could help to identify this inflection point (if one exists) and could also help business managers to think about mitigating relational practices such as involving users in policy creation. Future research could also clarify the scale of the effects of information governance on firm performance.

## Limitations and Future Research

Our research has four key limitations. First, our sample of 30 organizations is small and unrepresentative of organizational groups such as the Fortune 500. This raises a concern as to the generalizability of our model. While this concern may be tempered by efforts to use for-profit and not-for-profit organizations drawn from a broad spectrum of 17 industries, our research model can really only be verified with a broader and more representative sample. Second, our interviews involved IT executives and others with data center responsibilities. It would have been useful to interview business managers with a personal stake in information governance such as legal counsel, function or process leaders, application owners, or data stewards as a way to gain a more rounded perspective of the limitations, complexities, and challenges of information governance. Third, information governance is evolving as organizations react to new internal data needs and external forces such as new legislation that may call for a shift in governance focus. Since our interviews are cross-sectional, we did not conduct more longitudinal analysis or consider how information governance might evolve over time. Future research could assess changes in information governance over time and how those changes arose. Fourth, we are limited in terms of our ability to argue that information governance has a positive influence on traditional measures of firm performance such as sales and profitability. Interviewees talked about intermediate performance outcomes that were specific to their organizations and industries. It may be difficult for executives to have visibility into the exact causal link between information governance and firm performance. Future research could offer a richer analysis of the links between intermediate and firm-level performance effects of information governance.

Our hope is that this research will spark debate among researchers in the area of IT governance in order to more fully develop a core body of knowledge around information governance. We build on prior IT governance studies by Weber et al. [51] when assessing the antecedents of information governance and Peterson [32] in terms of capturing the practices underlying information governance. Our conversants also include Sambamurthy and Zmud [36] and Xue et al. [56] in terms of detecting an appropriate structure for structural practices and Tallon and Scannell [44] as regards practices that mitigate various risk factors that can hinder organizations from realizing value from information. Our study has implications for work in the area of IT business value. That literature tends to use the stock of IT capital (the cost of physical IT artifacts, capitalized labor, and software) rather than the stock of information to predict firm performance [3, 6, 7, 29, 38]. As information begins to directly affect firm performance, it will be important to include measures of the stock of information (proxied by storage spending, network flows or raw data volumes by storage tier as a way to weight different types of information) alongside other types of physical IT capital. It may be equally important to investigate the contribution of information governance practices alongside other IT governance practices and to equally consider the coevolution of both sets of practices over time.

## Conclusion

Cur ent r at es of infor mat ion gr owt h ar e lik ely t o cont inue on an upward exponential trajectory for the foreseeable future as organizations respond to the allure of data analytics and far-reaching regulations mandating retention of raw data for extended periods. Governing this growing mountain of information is widely recognized by IS academics and practitioners as a technical and managerial challenge, particularly as information develops into a strategic resource with characteristics that make it different from governing physical IT artifacts. The extant IT governance literature has tended to focus on how organizations govern IT infrastructure, project management, and control, but the underlying focus is almost entirely on physical IT. This was appropri ate when physical IT artifacts consumed the bulk of corporate IT budgets and were a confirmed source of firm performance. While it is still critical to ask how physical IT artifacts (hardware, software, networks) may be governed, information artifacts must be uniquely considered also. Using data from interviews of 37 top IT executives at 30 large organizations across a range of industries, we found that the underlying antecedents, composition, and consequences of information governance are consistent with what the extant literature reports as the antecedents, composition, and consequences of IT governance. Hence, future IT research could include consideration of information governance without having to revise or fundamentally reorient the constructs used to assess IT governance, its antecedents, and consequences. Operationalizing this extended theory of IT governance—one that includes information governance— is a necessary next step for researchers to begin to assess the magnitude of the role of information governance in modern-day organizations and a step that we actively encourage our fellow IS researchers to undertake.

1. Information life cycle management (ILM) describes how information is managed at distinct points in its life cycle. ILM is most often shown as an inverted U  curve, showing the theoretical relationship between the extent of use of the information and the age of the information [44]. Over time, the frequency or extent of information use grows as the information becomes increasingly relevant for decision making. At some point, use starts to decline as the information becomes less relevant for decision making. As the point where the information is no longer used or is too old or irrelevant to be of future use, IT managers must decide whether to archive or delete the information.

2. Analogous to how Moore’s law describes the pace of innovation in microprocessors, Kryder’s law (named after Mark Kryder, Seagate senior vice president and chief technology officer) describes how the area density of a disk drive doubles every other year [49]. Since the introduction of magnetic drives in 1956, disk density has grown by a factor of 100 million. At the same time, in constant dollars, the cost of a one-terabyte (TB) disk drive fell from \$10 billion in 1956 to \$193 million in 1980, \$9 million in 1990, \$9,580 in 2000, and \$400 in 2011. In Q1 2014, a 1-TB SATA (Serial Advanced Technology Attachment) drive costs \$135 at Dell (see http://ns1758.ca/winch/winchest.html for historical storage pricing).

3. Storage infrastructure is most often configured as a hierarchy of storage tiers. Each tier corresponds to a different level of information value. Tier 1 storage hosts high-value information; it is the most expensive to operate. Tier 2 hosts less valuable information and is consequently less expensive to operate than tier 1 as there is less compulsion to provide real-time disk mirroring or high levels of resilience. Tier 3, or the lowest tier in the hierarchy, is associated with archived information. This may be the least expensive tier (information is frequently archived to tape) but it can be slow and less reliable than the other tiers. As such, storage tiering follows a step-function relationship between progressively higher information value and progressively higher storage costs as a way to support and protect that higher value.

4. E-discovery refers to the identification and delivery of electronic records (data files, transactions, e-mails, etc.) to opposing counsel as part of court-ordered discovery. E-discovery can be especially challenging given the amount of data that must be searched, whether that data is archived, consolidated or stored in different locations, and the difficulty associated with tagging or labeling data for later retrieval. Metadata management refers to how data can be tagged based on its contents. Many firms store data oblivious to the need to tag data in ways that facilitate search and retrieval. For example, the subject line of an e-mail may not be an appropriate way to tag its contents.

5. Return point objective (RPO) is the age of the last backup; this will indicate the possible amount of data lost and the need for reentering unsaved data. If incremental backups occur nightly, a storage failure in the late afternoon could create a significant effort to rekey transactional data that was entered earlier. Return time objective (RTO) is the length of time needed to recover or fully restore data from backups. While RPO and RTO are separate and unrelated considerations, storage hardware vendors will sell solutions on the basis of minimizing both metrics.

8. The two graduate students (one male, one female) chosen for this task were the top two students (based on grade point average) among a group of 51 students enrolled in the IS program at the lead author’s university.

Coleman Holdings Inc., SC 07-1251, Florida Supreme Court (Tallahassee)). The court had requested specific data from the defendants only to be told, falsely, that no such data existed. This case established the requirement for firms to produce data in court in a timely manner. Corporate legal counsel has reacted to these decisions by requesting that all data be retained indefinitely.

## Appendix: Generic Interview Protocol—Semistructured Interviews

(Interview sections map directly to NVivo open coding headings.)

## Interviewer Instructions

Please use the following open-ended questions as a basis for conducting your CIO interviews. You can add other questions to this list if you think it will prove useful in discovering interesting facts about data growth and information governance at each interview site or how key variables relate to one another.

## Organizational Strategy, Organization Structure, and Product Markets

What are the key business goals and challenges in your corporation? What are the key critical success factors for your business to succeed? Describe your organization structure and product mix.

Information Growth Rate, Storage Spending, Regulations, and Information Culture Is the business experiencing growth in the volume of data/information?

1. Describe the amount of data/information and the rate of growth. Include specific metrics of volumes and of capacities of equipment over recent history, if possible.

2. What is driving this growth? List, rank order, and explain internal and external factors.

3. What business functions or business processes account for most of this growth?

4. What is the size of your storage budget? How is that changing over time?

## Information Technology Strategy

Describe your IT strategy and IS organization. Have data growth and its value exploitation become an important part of your business or IT strategy and plans?

## IT Architecture, IS Organization, Information Life Cycle, and Information Governance Principles

What are the implications for the IT organization of the data/information growth you are now experiencing?

1. What policies have you enacted to manage information?

2. Who sets and monitors these policies?

3. Do you have specific data retention policies?

4. H ow do you classify data over its useful economic life?

5. Who is responsible for data migration between storage tiers?

6. Is information managed centrally or what other structures have you put in place? How are these structures changing over time?

7. What role do you currently assign to the cloud? Will this change in future?

## IT/IS Performance and Information Value Opportunities

What do you see as the major business opportunities and challenges arising from growth in data/information?

1. What new business opportunities are being pursued or is the growth seen as a problem to be contained and limited?

2. If there are opportunities, what needs to be done with the data/information?

3. H ow is the performance of the IS organization impacted by information governance?
