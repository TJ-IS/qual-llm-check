# Large Online Product Catalog Space Indicates High Store Price: Understanding Customers’ Overgeneralization and Illogical Inference

Abstract. Previous research has shown that because offline store space is costly, customers tend to associate large interstitial space among products in a bricks-and-mortar store with high price. Drawing on consumer inference and signaling theories, the present research suggests this offline association could be overgeneralized to online contexts and lead customers to illogically infer high price based on large interstitial space among products in an online product catalog (i.e., online product catalog space, OPCS). We conducted five experiments to test whether and why OPCS affects customers’ online store price perception and its downstream effect on store evaluation. Our findings indicate that (1) an online store with larger OPCS is perceived to be selling more expensive products (Study 1); (2) the effect of OPCS is due to offline–online overgeneralization rather than online learning, because either a reminder of offline–online differences (Study 2) or sufficient web design knowledge (Study 3) diminishes the effect of OPCS on store price perception, and only people who believe that large offline space is linked with high price show this effect (Study 4); and (3) customers who care more about quality evaluate a store with larger OPCS more positively, whereas customers who care more about price do the opposite (Study 5). These findings contribute to literatures on web design, price perception, consumer inference in e-commerce, and offline–online behavior transfer. We also discuss implications for practice and offer suggestions for future works

## 1. Introduction

Imagine that Anna is shopping for a coat offline. Which store will she prefer: Store A, which has exquisite decor and appealing amenities, or Store B, which has narrow aisles that look crowded and uncomfortable? The store features suggest that Store A might be upscale and expensive, while Store B might be economical and provide better value for the money. If Anna is highly price sensitive, she might not even consider walking into Store A, and if she desires luxury goods, she may ignore Store B. That is, customers’ first impression of a retailer’s average price level is likely to determine their consequent behaviors (Kuksov and Lin 2017). For this reason, it is common practice for offline stores to send signals about price to attract their target customers (Brown 1969, Smith and Burns 1996, Baker et al. 2002; also see Hamilton and Chernev 2013 for a comprehensive review). But what if Anna is shopping online? Considering that website interface is the first line of contact between customers and an online store, in this research we aim to answer the following questions: Can an online store shape its potential customers impression of the price level of the store through it web design? If yes, how?

The answers to these questions are important. Potential online customers need price signals from an online store to help them evaluate the price level (i.e., perceived expensiveness) (Hamilton and Chernev 2013), given that price level is one of the most important factors that affect online purchases (Bauboniene and˙ Guleviciˇ ut e˙ 2015). It is difficult for potential customers to judge price level based solely on label prices (Slonim and Garbarino 1999, Garbarino and Slonim 2003) because customers lack an internal scale for average price, price dispersion, and price dynamics (Hsee et al. 1999, Hsee and Zhang 2010). Although they might solve this problem by comparing prices across stores, some 12–24 million online stores exist worldwide (Retail TouchPoints 2016), which makes the cost of price comparison unaffordable. More importantly, online customers are now buying products in more personalized categories (e.g., apparel), and a large number of small and pure-play online brands offer such merchandise (Bain & Company 2015). It is difficult if not impossible for customers to judge price level through comparisons across different products from different brands given that the products carried by each of the brands are unique. To reduce customers’ effort to compare different stores and let them quickly evaluate the price level of a store through a costless method, personalizing website design to fit with their price sensitivity is important.

Price sensitivity is one of the most important customer features, and a stream of research has demonstrated that customers have it at different levels (Hoch et al. 1995). As suggested in the scenario of Anna’s shopping for a coat, customers who are highly price sensitive may not even enter an online store that they perceive to offer merchandise that is luxurious and pricy, whereas customers who care more about quality than price may be willing to do so. Fortunately, advanced information technologies enable online retailers to analyze their customers’ characteristics and individualize a website accordingly, without being noticed—that is, web personalization (Ho and Bodoff 2014). It has been demonstrated that personalized websites facilitate business-to-consumer communication and lead to positive outcomes (Tam and Ho 2005, Komiak and Benbasat 2006, Tam and Ho 2006). In this set of studies, we reason that, because it is easier to profile customers in an online environment (e.g., via customers’ purchase and browsing history), online stores should be designed to provide appropriate signals or cues for price level to attract their target customers.

However, previous research about web design is mostly about its importance for e-commerce success (Song and Zahedi 2005, Gregg and Walczak 2008, Wells et al. 2011b) and how to create a pleasant online shopping environment (Palmer 2002, Jiang and Benbasat 2007, Parboteeah et al. 2009, Deng and Poole 2010, Wells et al. 2011a, Campbell et al. 2013). Although a few exceptions examine signaling effects of web design cues, they focus on the relationship between website design quality and product quality (Gregg and Walczak 2008, Wells et al. 2011b). Little is known about the effect of web design on customers price perception.

Inspired by the previous finding that in offline contexts products presented with more interstitia space are perceived as more expensive, following the logic that retailers need high prices to afford the rental costs of such a display (Sevilla and Townsend 2016), we focus on online product catalog space (OPCS) in the current research. Here, OPCS refers to the interstitial space among products in an online catalog. It is manipulated by enlarging the white space around a product while keeping the size and number of products constant. Larger OPCS indicates a higher space-toproduct ratio in a catalog. Because OPCS offers an online visual counterpart to the interstitial space of offline product presentation, it too may affect perceived store price level. Some retailers seem already to have adopted this notion in their online channels. For example, compared with Walmart, Whole Foods, which has a relatively higher price image, tends to display its products online more loosely and with more white space. However, online retailing space is not costly and therefore retailers would not need to sell products at a higher price to afford larger OPCS. Van der Heijden and Verhagen (2004) even point out directly that although “crowdedness” is important for offline stores, it is irrelevant for online stores. Therefore, whether OPCS has an effect on price perception invites empirical examination. If it does have such an effect, it would be interesting to uncover the underlying mechanism.

To explore why OPCS might have an effect on customers’ price perception, we draw on consumer inference theory. This theory suggests that people learn to generate if–then linkages that associate information (e.g., web design quality) with conclusions (e.g., product quality) (Kardes et al. 2004, Huang and Zhang 2016). Once people learn an if–then linkage, overgeneralization is likely (Kardes et al. 2008). For example, in daily life, accurate information usually spreads widely and becomes familiar to people. Thus, people learn that if a claim feels familiar, it is likely to be true (Alter and Oppenheimer 2009). Because of overlearning, the inference process may become spontaneous, and customers may perceive online reviews to be trustworthy even when the feeling of familiarity does not come from the reviews per se but from an easy-to-read font (Huang et al. 2018). That is, customers might adopt a simpli fied strategy and use the if–then linkage to make an inference even when it is illogical in the new context.

Based on consumer inference theory and possible overgeneralization, we propose that because customers have established a link between large space and high price based on their offline experience, they might overgeneralize it to online contexts. Through one laboratory experiment and four online experiments, we show that larger OPCS leads to higher perceived price of online stores. To investigate our prediction regarding the underlying mechanism of offline–online overgeneralization, we further demonstrate that the focal effect is moderated by a reminder of offline–online differences, customers’ web design knowledge, and a belief of “large offline space = high price.” Finally, we find that whether OPCS increases or decreases customers’ store evaluation depends on the extent to which they care about quality (versus price).

The present research has both theoretical and practical significance. Theoretically, by showing that perceived price level can be altered even when customers know exact prices, we highlight the importance of investigating factors that shape price image of online stores. By showing that large OPCS increases perceived store price, this research also adds to the growing web personalization literature (e.g., Ho et al. 2011, Johar et al. 2014, Xu et al. 2014). Furthermore, the current research is the first to demonstrate that customers may make illogical inferences based on spatial cues online; it thus contributes to consumer inference theory in e-commerce. Finally, uncovering the underlying mechanism of offline–online overgeneralization helps us to understand how and when associations learned offline may affect online behaviors. Practically, the downstream effect of “large OPCS = high price” on store evaluation provides guidance for online retailers to better design and personalize their websites according to customers’ price sensitivity.

## 2. Theoretical Background 2.1. Consumer Inference and Signaling

Consumer inference theory suggests that to make judgments and decisions based on incomplete information, customers usually use their prior knowledge about relationships among people, objects, and events, and they make inferences about missing but important properties from available information (Kardes et al. 2004, Wyer 2004, Kardes et al. 2008). That is, people first learn if–then linkages (e.g., if it is expensive, then it must be high in quality) and then use the occurrence of one (e.g., high price) to infer the co-occurrence of the other (e.g., high quality) (Lalwani and Shavitt 2013, Lalwani and Forcum 2016). As a result, once retailers know about the possible inferences customers may make, they could use cues to signal product or store properties and thus influence customers’ behaviors (Ge et al. 2009, Huang and Zhang 2016).

2.1.1. Consumer Inference in Of<sup>fl</sup>ine Commerce. Previous research has provided much evidence that customers make inferences about different dimensions of product properties. For examples, they judge product quality based on price, warranty, seller reputation, advertising expenditures, and other customers’ purchase behaviors (Rao and Monroe 1989, Rao et al. 1999, Kirmani and Rao 2000, Stock and Balachander 2005); they infer that they need to pay more attention to product quality if many options are provided by a same manufacturer or brand within a product assortment, as the large amount of options indicates that customers in that market are heterogeneous (Bertini et al. 2012); they consider a product accompanied with a free gift as of low value (Kamins et al. 2009); and they also believe options placed in the center of a simultaneously presented array of products are popular (Valenzuela and Raghubir 2009).

2.1.2. Consumer Inference in E-Commerce. Given that it is difficult for customers to judge online products and sellers before purchase (Pavlou et al. 2006), previous research in e-commerce has focused primarily on consumer inference about quality. Besides cues such as price, warranty, seller reputation, advertising expenditures, and other customers’ purchase behaviors that apply to both offline and online contexts for quality signaling (Biswas and Biswas 2004, Mitra and Fay 2010, Cheung et al. 2014), there are also cues that are more commonly adopted in e-commerce. For example, customers draw on number of reviews (Amblee and Bui 2011), diagnosticity of product descriptions, and third-party product assurances (Dimoka et al. 2012) to reduce product uncertainty. As another example, employers in global online labor markets use service providers’ ratio of successfully completed projects to all projects to judge providers’ quality (Kanat et al. 2018). Likewise, sellers of online auctions adopt multiple picture postings, money-back guarantees, third-party payment methods, minimum starting bids, hidden reserve prices, buy-it-now options (Li et al. 2009), and their choice of selling in an auction (Despotakis et al. 2017) to indicate high product quality. Another commonly seen example is online retailers’ use of a third-party web assurance seal to signal institution-based trustworthiness and thus quality (Ozpolat et al.<sup>¨</sup> 2013).

Quality signaling seems to draw the most attention in the field of consumer inference in e-commerce. However, given that customers lack information about price dispersion and have difficulty in making a judgment based on label prices (Hsee et al. 1999, Biswas et al. 2006, Hsee and Zhang 2010), it is important to understand factors that affect customers’ price perception through consumer inference.

## 2.2. Consumer Inference Theory and Factors Signaling Price

Prior literature has provided evidence that retailers try to alter customers’ price perception by sending appropriate signals (Brown and Oxenfeldt 1972). One commonly used strategy is the low-price guarantee (Dutta 2012), also known as the price-matching guarantee (Kukar-Kinney and Grewal 2007) or pricematching refund policy (Jain and Srivastava 2000), in which retailers promise to match or beat competitors lower prices before purchase or to refund money to customers who find lower prices after purchase (Arbatskaya et al. 2004, Dutta 2012). Previous studies have confirmed that these low price signals do lower customers’ price perceptions for bricks-and-mortar retailers (Jain and Srivastava 2000) as well as online retailers (Kukar-Kinney and Grewal 2007). More subtly, Kuksov and Lin (2017) suggest that close competitors (retailers) usually carry overlapping assortments with competitive prices on the common products, which serves as a signal for a “competitive” price on relatively unique products and thus prevents further consumer search for a lower price on those unique products.

Going beyond marketing strategies that retailers might adopt, physical characteristics of a retailer, such as store location, ambiance, and decor, have also been found to affect customers’ price perception (Hamilton and Chernev 2013). Among them, spatial cues (O’Guinn et al. 2015) are most relevant to the current research. Previous research has shown that product display with a high space-to-product ratio in bricks-and-mortar stores leads to a perception of high price level because customers infer that stores that use large spaces to exhibit products must carry highpriced items to afford the rental costs necessary for such a display (Sevilla and Townsend 2016). Similarly, large white space in product advertisement signals high price. This assumption occurs because customers believe that white space in an advertisement is costly, and it would be worthwhile for stores to spend a large amount of money on a product only when its price is high (Kirmani 1990, Jacobs and Poillon 1992).

All of these findings suggest that price perception is malleable, even when customers know exact product prices. Considering that customers form their first impression of an online store based on what they see in the user interface of the store, which also conveys spatial cues, we seek to understand whether web design of online stores could send signals about store prices.

## 2.3. General Web Design Signals

In the present research, web design signals are defined as functionalities and/or visual cues on the user interface of a website that customers might draw on to make inferences. Previous research has shown that website quality, which includes multiple attributes such as security, download delay, navigability, and visual appeal, contributes to customers’ perception of product quality and thus influences customers’ purchase intention and actual purchase behaviors (Gregg and Walczak 2008, Wells et al. 2011b). Similarly, website informativeness, product description diagnosticity (Pavlou 2007), and usability and transparency (Benlian and Hess 2011) serve as signals of online marketers’ trustworthiness. However, most of the previous research in this area has focused on signals that help to reduce customers’ uncertainty about product quality and seller quality. The question of whether certain attributes of web design also affect customers’ store price perception still calls for an answer. More interestingly, while most valid cues (e.g., price guarantees or expensive ad page) are costly, are there any uncostly signals for price level? In the current research, we focus on OPCS.

## 3. Research Model and Hypotheses

Previous research has shown that the design of an online product catalog has a direct impact on customers’ choices and on a vendor’s sales (Janiszewsk 1998, Mandel and Johnson 2002, Lamberton and Diehl 2013). However, less is known about whether it affects customers’ store price perception. The current research proposes that OPCS affects customers’ store price perception because of an illogical inference, which is elaborated on below.

In most of the cases we reviewed above, the inferences that customers make are reasonable. For example, customers might infer high quality from high price because price is a valid signal for quality in real life. However, customers sometimes make illogical inferences when they use well-learned if–then linkages as shortcuts and overgeneralize them to situations in which they are inapplicable (Kardes et al. 2008). Prior literature has provided considerable evidence of overgeneralizations from offline to online. For example, although physical distance is one of the most important structural constraints for offline interactions, it is not for online communications (Cairncross 2001). Still, geographical distance continues to affect social ties of online social networks, reflecting that physical distance as a barrier learned offline maintains its effect despite its meaninglessness online (Spiro et al. 2016). For another example, social baseline theory suggests that because for humans social proximity and interpersonal interactions help to minimize the effort needed to survive in potentially dangerous environments, close proximity to social resources becomes the baseline assumption of the human brain (Beckes and Coan 2011). This preference for the presence of others generalizes from offline to online and leads users to perceive websites including human images as warmer and more trustworthy (Cyr et al. 2009). Likewise, people have learned a beauty–goodness association during the process of evolution, as human aesthetic sense is actually based on what has been important for biological evolution (Enquist and Arak 1994). Consistent with this premise, visual appeal of a website has been found to be highly correlated with perceived usability (Tractinsky et al. 2000) and relationship rewards (Campbell et al. 2013), even though beauty does not lead to biological advantages in the online contexts.

To this end, it is reasonable to expect that similar offline-to-online overgeneralizations would occur when customers are making inferences. Previous findings support this proposition. For example, because customers have learned from their daily life experience that familiar claims are more likely to be true, they infer e-word-of-mouth (e-WOM) as more trustworthy when it is written in an easy-to-read (versus difficult-to-read) font, which feels familiar, without carefully considering the source of familiarity (Huang et al. 2018). As another example, customers learn from traditional advertising markets that conspicuous advertising expenditures signal high quality because only high-quality firms could hope to recoup their investments (Jacobs and Poillon 1992, Linnemer 2002, Pracejus et al. 2013). Logically, this belief should be inapplicable when advertising cost is based on performance rather than exposure. However, in online sponsored search markets where advertisers pay only for received clicks, customers still believe that a seller listed higher in the search results of a search engine is of higher quality than others (iProspect 2006, Animesh et al. 2010). These overgeneralizations happen because for well-learned if–then linkages, inferences are usually formed naturally and spontaneously, without deliberation (Kardes et al. 2008).

One key difference between logical and illogical inferences is that for illogical inferences, people make corrections when they realize that the cues that they have drawn on are invalid (Schwarz and Clore 1983). That is, if external reminders trigger people to cast doubt on the rationality of the inference (Reber et al. 2004, Fang et al. 2007) or if people tend not to rely on the focal cue because of their individual characteristics (Avnet et al. 2012), they are less likely to make an illogicalinference.Forexample,customersmightdrawon the feeling of perceptual ease (i.e., the cue) coming from the font of product description rather than the product itself to infer the quality of the product. But this illogical inference diminishes when customers are reminded of the actual source (i.e., the font) of the cue (Schwarz 2012) or when they do not trust their feelings and consider the cue to be undiagnostic (Avnet et al. 2012).

Note that prior literature about rational versus irrational herding has also supported the distinction between logical and illogical inference (Simonsohn and Ariely 2008, Zhang and Liu 2012).<sup>1</sup> It has been suggested that rational herding is based on Bayesian inference about valid cues, which is logical. For example, well-funded borrowers tend to attract more funding because lenders make a logical inference about a borrower’s creditworthiness from observing others’ lending decisions (Zhang and Liu 2012). In contrast, irrational herding is based on inference about invalid cues, which is illogical. For example, winners of auctions on eBay pay more when the auctions start at a low (versus high) price because lower starting prices usually result in more bids and bidders misattribute popularity to good quality instead of to the true reason—namely, a low starting price. Moreover, experienced bidders are less likely to show such herding behaviors (Simonsohn and Ariely 2008).

In the current research, we postulate that OPCS might affect price perception through customers’ illogical inference. Specifically, customers have learned from their offline experience that large space–product ratio is usually linked with higher product price, believing that otherwise, retailers could not afford the large space, which is costly offline (Kirmani 1990, Jacobs and Poillon 1992, Sevilla and Townsend 2016). When customers come across an online store and try to form a first impression based on its appearance (Janiszewski 1998, Mandel and Johnson 2002, Lamberton and Diehl 2013), this well-learned association is likely to take effect spontaneously, and customers then use OPCS, which seemingly mirrors offline space, to judge store price level. That is, customers might overgeneralize the “large offline space = high price” association and apply it to the online context, where in actuality online retailers do not pay more for a larger visual space. In this case, customers make an illogical inference. Formally:

Hypothesis a. OPCS increases the perceived price level of an online store.

Hypothesis b. The effect of OPCS on the perceived price level of an online store is due to offline–online overgeneralization

## 4. Methodology and Results

## 4.1. Study 1: Large Online Product Catalog Space = High Price

Study 1 aimed at providing initial evidence for the “large OPCS = high price” association. To ensure that online stores differed only in space, this study also measured and controlled for helpfulness, attractiveness, product assortment, and the extent to which participants felt the website drew their attention to the design features.

4.1.1. Design and Method. One hundred twenty-one university students (59.5% female; mean age = 21.45, standard deviation [SD] = 2.57) participated in a laboratory experiment for a possible payment from ¥0 to ¥10, with additional gifts of bookmarks.<sup>2</sup> This study adopted a one-factor (space: large versus small) between-subjects design. Participants browsed the department for bookmarks in an online store selling cultural and creative products (see Online Appendix A1 for details). Across all five studies in the current research, participants were randomly assigned to different treatment conditions (please see Table 1 for the number of participants in each condition). The images of the products were the same size across different conditions, but the space surrounding each product was either large or small.

To simulate real-world shopping experience, participants received a budget of ¥11 to purchase bookmarks (all priced at ¥1). They were told that they need to purchase at least one to proceed with the experiment, and at the end of the experiment they could get the bookmark(s) they chose and any remaining money. After the purchase, participants rated perceived price level (dependent variable), crowdedness of the website (manipulation check), and other control variables (see Table 2 for the detailed measurements).

## 4.2. Study 2: Moderating Role of a Reminder of Online–Of<sup>fl</sup>ine Differences

Study 1 showed that OPCS increased perceived store price. However, it did not provide evidence for the offline–online overgeneralization that we theorized to be the underlying mechanism. One alternative explanation is that in real life, webpages that display products more spaciously do tend to have higher prices, and customers learn this association through their online purchase experience, which they draw on when judging price level. In this case, customers would be making a logical inference based on their real-life experience of online shopping, rather than making an offline–online overgeneralization as we theorized. To rule out this alternative explanation, Study 2 examined the moderating role of a reminder of online– offline differences. We expected that the effect of OPCS on store price perception would diminish when people were made aware of differences in online and offline shopping. In particular, if the effect of OPCS is due to the overgeneralization of the offline association as we propose, factors that weaken illogical inference should moderate the focal effect. Previous research has shown that illogical inference due to overgeneralization can be corrected by external reminders (Reber et al. 2004, Fang et al. 2007). Following the same logic, reminding customers of online and offline differences should lead them to step back and reconsider the applicability of the “large space = high price” association in online contexts. In this case, they would be less likely to illog ically infer higher price from larger OPCS. However, the alternative explanation of learning from actual online experience would not expect a moderating effect of a reminder about online–offline differences.

4.5. Study 5: From Price Perception to Evaluation Studies 1–4 showed that customers equate larger OPCS with higher price, stemming from offline–online overgeneralization. In Study 5, we aimed to show how the belief “large OPCS = high price” translates to customers’ store evaluation. Store evaluation refers to customers’ overall evaluation of the online store (Mattila and Wirtz 2001). The downstream effect of OPCS on store evaluation requires examination because price has both positive and negative effects (Lichtenstein et al. 1993, Chang and Wildt 1994). High price signals quality and value (Stafford and Enis 1969, Kerin et al. 1992), and thus increases purchase likelihood and perceived consumption experience (Sevilla and Townsend 2016). At the same time, price defines the amount of economic outlay that must be sacrificed to engage in a purchase transaction. A product becomes more attractive when it is perceived to have a lower price (Janiszewski and Lichtenstein 1999). Thus, for the same product, high price also tends to decrease perceived product value and thus purchase intention (Zielke 2010). Because price is a doubleedged sword, the valence of the effect of price might depend on whether customers care more about quality or price (Lichtenstein et al. 1993, Sinha and Batra 1999, Deval et al. 2012). That is, large (versus small) OPCS, which indicates high (versus low) price, should lead to higher online store evaluation among customers who weight quality more heavily as compared with price, but result in lower store evaluation among those who weight price more heavily. Combined with the moderating effect of web design knowledge dem onstrated in Study 3, the above interaction effect between OPCS and the relative weighting of price (versus quality) should occur among unknowledgeable customers but diminish among knowledgeable customers.

## 5. Discussion

Drawing on consumer inference and signaling theory, the current research examined the effect of online product catalog design (i.e., online product catalog space, OPCS) on customers’ store price perception. The findings from five experiments suggest that an online store with smaller (versus larger) product catalog space is perceived to sell cheaper products. Consequently, customers evaluate it more (versus less) favorably if they care more about low price as compared with high quality. The effect of OPCS on store evaluation reverses if customers care more about high quality. Moreover, the effect of OPCS on price perception diminishes when customers (1) receive areminder of offline–online differences, (2) are knowledgeable about web design, or (3) do not hold a belief of “large offline space = high price.” These moderators not only shed light on boundary conditions, but also support our prediction that customers overgeneralize what they learn offline (i.e., large space = high price) to the online context and illogically infer higher price based on larger OPCS. Our research has important theoretical and practical implications, as discussed in the next section.

## 5.1. Theoretical Contributions

Our research extends the literature in four ways. First, while most previous research about web design has focused on how to create pleasant online shopping environments (Palmer 2002, Jiang and Benbasat 2007, Parboteeah et al. 2009, Deng and Poole 2010, Wells et al. 2011a, Campbell et al. 2013) or how to send signals about quality so as to reduce customers perceived uncertainty (Gregg and Walczak 2008, Wells et al. 2011b), our research is the first to show that online retailers can use web design to deliver price image of their stores. Moreover, most previous research has treated web design as a result of multiple features without considering the detailed implications of each feature. Our research focuses on one specific feature, OPCS, and our findings demonstrate that even one single feature of web design may have specific implications. This finding deepens understanding about the influential effect of web design and sheds light on the importance of understanding the effect of individual design features, with possible implications for shaping consumer behaviors in e-commerce.

This finding also contributes to literature about web personalization. Most previous research has focused on offering customized web content (e.g., Tam and Ho 2005, Tam and Ho 2006, Ho et al. 2011, Johar et al. 2014). To our knowledge, no research has explored how web personalization could also involve personalizing visual features of websites. Our approach sheds light on the importance of including web design as a part of web personalization as a prospective research area.

The second contribution is our identification of a unique factor—namely, OPCS—that affects customers’ store price perception. Although previous research has shown that online retailers could deliver a low price image through low-price guarantees (Kukar-Kinney and Grewal 2007) and overlapping product assortments with competitive prices on common products (Kuksov and Lin 2017), these signals are costly. Moreover, while these previous findings show how to generate a low price image, less is known about how to generate a high price image, which might be very important for high-end or luxury stores (Bellezza et al. 2013). Our research suggests that by simply enlarging (versus shrinking) OPCS, online retailers could deliver a high (versus low) price store image in an uncostly way. This finding contributes to a more comprehensive understanding about factors influencing customers store price perception. Most importantly, given that the majority of research on e-commerce focuses on cues that signal quality, the current research highlights the importance and possibility of exploring cues that signal other dimensions of properties, thus opening up a new avenue for future research.

Third, our research adds to consumer inference and signaling theories in e-commerce by distinguishing illogical inference from logical inference in online shopping contexts. A majority of previous research in e-commerce has focused on customers’ logical inferences and the signaling effects of valid cues (Biswas and Biswas 2004, Hu et al. 2010, Mitra and Fay 2010, Ozpolat et al.<sup>¨</sup> 2013, Cheung et al. 2014). Illogical inferences in the e-commerce context have been mostly ignored. For example, although Animesh et al. (2010) point out that customers might incorrectly consider a seller listed higher in the search results of a search engine to be of higher quality than others, the differences between logical and illogical inferences do not draw their attention. Going beyond providing evidence for an illogical inference in e-commerce contexts, our research shows that illogical inferences can be corrected by external reminders and internal individual knowledge. These findings should also apply to other online illogical inferences, suggesting ways in which customers’ biases may be corrected.

Finally, this paper has interesting implications for how associations learned offline could affect online behaviors. Although most prior research has focused on online–offline differences (e.g., Danaher et al. 2003, Chu et al. 2008, Zhang and Wedel 2009), the present research emphasizes offline–online transmission. Our findings suggest that although offline shopping and online shopping are different in nature, customers are likely to rely on a seeming similarity to make judgments. The current research suggests that although offline interstitial space among products (which is costly because of rent) is essentially different from online interstitial space among products (which is mainly a result of the preference of online retailers and not costly at all), customers tend to ignore their differences and treat both as signals for store price level. This finding further suggests that, once a rule or linkage is well learned offline, people are likely to apply it online as a shortcut, especially when they fail to deliberate or lack relevant knowledge. Our finding is consistent with human beings’ knowledge development and transfer, in which children first apply rules (e.g., proportional methods) when they are not applicable but then learn to distinguish when they are applicable and when they are not as they get older and more experienced (Van Dooren et al. 2005).

## 5.2. Practical Implications

Our research also has implications for how online retailers can better design their stores. Although most research on website design seems to suggest that there is a “right” way (e.g., Liu and Arnett 2000, Palmer 2002), based on our current findings, we strongly recommend that online vendors design their websites to fit the characteristics of their target customers. That is, online store design involves more than usability and aesthetics. It is also about whether the implicit message of the design is congruent with customers’ internal needs, and these needs are different across different individuals and different situations. When the online store is price-oriented and would like to attract customers who place great importance on price, they should not present large space around their products in the product catalog. On the contrary, if the online store would like to attract customers who care more about quality, it should show a loose product catalog that is not at all costly but transmits a high-price store image. More importantly, online retailers might also consider personalizing their websites according to customers’ price sensitivity at the individual level or use different levels of OPCS in different subchannels (e.g., sales versus new arrivals).

Moreover, the distinction between logical and illogical inference indicates that it is important for online retailers to be aware of whether the cues they use for signaling are valid. When online retailers are using valid cues for signaling and to drive users to make a logical inference, such as using a low price guarantee to signal low store price (e.g., Kukar-Kinney and Grewal 2007), indicating that they are using these cues on purpose or elaborating the underlying meaning of these cues would not be a problem and might even be beneficial (Zhang and Liu 2012). Thus, online retailers could encourage or guide users to consider the meaning of those cues more deeply. In contrast, when online retailers use invalid cues for signaling and to drive users to make illogical inferences, such as using small OPCS to signal low store price, users who are knowledgeable about the irrelevance of these cues or who tend to elaborate might not make the inference that retailers expect (e.g., Simonsohn and Ariely 2008). This latter type of cue would be more effective when customers are not fully aware of the offline–online differences and are purchasing in a hurry. Thus, the characteristics of the target customers and their consumption patterns should be taken into consideration when deciding whether to use these kinds of cues.

## 5.3. Limitations and Future Research

Although we have highlighted several notable findings and contributions of our research, we acknowledge some limitations as well. First, although we theorized and empirically validated that the major force of the OPCS effect on price perception is through an illogical inference (i.e., offline–online overgeneralization), we were unable to rule out that in some cases a logical inference might also play a role (e.g., a better web design means a higher design budget and thus a higher store price). Future research should take a finegrained examination of the determinants of illogical versus logical inferences about OPCS. Second, although we tried to elicit participants’ behaviors close to those in real life, laboratory and online experiments cannot fully capture customers’ actual purchase behaviors. Field experiments that distract participants from noticing that they are participating in a study should be considered. Third, we did not examine the possible effect of brand equity of the online platform. Whether brand equity might affect our research outcomes should be further examined.

Our research suggests some future research directions. First, through the same underlying mechanism of overgeneralization, there might be other cues, such as vertical position of a product (Cian et al. 2015) and perceived physical distance from a product (Huang et al. 2017), which may influence online customer behaviors in ways similar to how they influence offline customer behaviors. Second, although we focused on offline-to-online overgeneralization, online-to-offline overgeneralization might also occur. Future research could explore these probabilities.

## 6. Conclusion

Previous research has shown that larger offline interstitial space among products is associated with high store price. Our research examined whether and when this offline association overgeneralizes to online contexts and leads customers to perceive stores with larger online interstitial space among products as more expensive. Theoretically, our research adds to the literature about antecedents of malleable price perception and implications for web design. It also extends understanding of illogical consumer inference in e-commerce and of how associations learned offline could affect online behaviors. Practically, our research illuminates ways of designing or personalizing an online product catalog according to customers’ price sensitivity.
