# Platform drifting: When work digitalization hijacks its spirit

Yasser Rahrovani

Ivey Business School, Western University, London, Ontario N6G 0N1, Canada

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Digital work Platform alignment Social media alignment Platform logic Platform drifting Digital strategy Organizational transformation Digital transformation

## A B S T R A C T

Organizations are increasingly digitalizing the work associated with information exchange by using enterprise social media. However, social media’s openness to outsider users poses significant challenges to maintaining alignment between social media logic (or platform logic that guides decisions and actions on the platform) and the dominant organizational logic. Through an in-depth, longitudinal, abductive study of three successive social media implementations in a single organization, I explore the process of maintaining (or losing) social media alignment and its long-term consequences on the nature of the work. The paper shows that digitalization exposes the work to continuous adjustment within and across three elements of digital work: digita infrastructure work (embracing new uses of the platform at the user level), digital strategy work (redesigning governance policies), and aligning work (fitting uses with the platform logic underlying digital work). The findings show that despite initial social media alignment, through continuous coevolution of these three elements, the platform logic underlying digital community work eventually drifted away from supporting the organization's original logic of cohesion to supporting an alternative logic of inclusivity. Accordingly, a process model of platform drifting has been developed. By taking a closer look at actual practices, the paper contributes to digital work research by identifying distinct elements of digital work involved in social media (mis) alignment and illustrates the profound, long-term consequences of social media on the nature of the work.

## Introduction

Organizations are increasingly adopting enterprise social media to digitalize different types of work, such as capturing emplovees interactions and their informal knowledge exchange (Jarrahi and Sawyer, 2013; Leonardi and Vaast, 2017). Enterprise social media, hereafter called social media, are “computer-mediated tools of the Web 2.0 generation. [adopted for organizational use purposes]. that make it possible for anyone to create, circulate, share, and exchange information in a variety of formats and with multiple communities” (Leonardi and Vaast, 2017, p. 150). Social media technologies are radically diferent from past enterprise systems (e.g., groupware or knowledge management) (McAfee. 2009). They are open, participatory technologies that enable extensions by allowing users to create new uses and make them visible to other users for use or adaptation (Huang et al., 2015; McAfee, 2009; Treem and Leonardi, 2013).

However, the uses made of social media are in constant flux (Denyer et al., 2011), creating challenges for managers as they seek to maintain social media alignment with the organizational logic. In the context of this research, social media alignment (or platform alignment) refers to the degree of fit between the platform logic (underlying social media use) and the dominant organizational logic. Organizations are typically associated with a high-level logic that justifies relationships between means and goals, shapes organizational actors' cognition, and guides their decision making and strategy choices (Thornton, 2004). Strategies to digitalize work by social media are shaped and guided by the dominant organizational logic in order to attain certain goals. However, the social media openness to creating new uses gives rise to tensions between maintaining social media alignment when these new uses are misaligned with the organization’s dominant logic (and its goals and values). If the social medium is too often used for purposes other than those originally intended, the platform logic underlying the digital work may eventually drift toward an alternative logic, diferent from the dominant organizational logic, i.e., platform drifting. Managing this tension is more challenging for external social media, which include users who are not members of the organization and who may hold a broader diversity of goals and values

Despite prominent work on firm-level (Chan et al., 1997), process-level (Queiroz, 2017; Tallon et al., 2016), and project-leve alignment (Rahrovani et al., 2014c), our knowledge of the platform-level alignment (e.g., social media alignment), particularly for external social media, is limited. Our knowledge of alignment is mainly formed in the context of traditional IT in which use boundaries and user autonomy are predefined. However, today’s digital technologies, particularly social media, enable the engagement of autonomous actors with diverse motivations and goals (Yoo et al., 2010). This makes social media alignment excessively volatile and unpredictable, both in its process and its outcomes. First, and with respect to the alignment process, research shows that developing organizational capabilities, such as flexibility, can help in managing misalignment (Baptista et al., 2017; Wareham et al., 2014), for example, between manager- and employee-generated content (Huang et al., 2015). However, flexibility can be a doubleedged sword in the context of external social media because of the exposure to communities that are not bound to the goals and values derived from the dominant organizational logic. Thus, we have less knowledge about the process by which diferent factors and work elements contribute to maintaining external social media alignment. Second, and with respect to alignment outcomes, work digitalization with external social media opens the gates to collective actions being carried out by outside users (O’Mahony and Lakhani, 2011) who can then use social media for purposes unrelated to the organization’s goals. This can eventually drift the socia media platform from realizing management's intended goals (Ciborra et al., 2000). The long-term consequences of social media for the nature of work, particularly on the institutional properties of the work, is understudied (Leonardi and Vaast, 2017).

With the increasing reliance of organizations on social media for diferent types of work (e.g., collaboration, ideation, or design) (West and Lakhani, 2008), there is still much to investigate regarding the process of maintaining (or losing) the alignment of externa social media and its long-term outcomes and conseguences for the work. Lack of knowledge about the process of maintaining social media alignment and the long-term outcomes for the digital work limits our knowledge about contributing factors, their interactions, and the temporal progression that maintains alignment (or results in drift) of platforms. It can also limit our ability to adequately guide managers about the organizational implications of social media. This could discourage managers from including social media in their value creation process, given the complexity of managing these open platforms and the risk of drifting.

In line with recent calls for exploring the long-term impacts of social media (Leonardi and Vaast, 2017) and its impacts on the work elements (Ali-Hassan et al., 2015), this paper takes an abductive, longitudinal approach to address these gaps by asking two questions: First, “how do organizations maintain (or lose) alignment of their social media with their dominant organizational logic when social media provides autonomy to internal and external users (RQ1).” Second, “what are the long-term consequences of social media on digital work and its institutional properties, specifically platform logic underlying digital work (RQ2).” This paper explores the digitalization of a specific type of work (i.e., community work) with social media in a not-for-profit organization called the Montreal Toheed Society (MTS). MTS is an organization with oficial members, an elected board of directors, and project leaders (volunteers appointed to lead a project). MTS has a dominant logic of cohesion, which aims to create a coherent ‘family’ environment for members. Following a top-down digital strategy, the MTS board adopted three successive social media technologies over four years to capture member interactions and, subsequently, to digitalize directors’ community work. Community work involves identifying MTS members’ needs, planning for and implementing a portfolio of projects to address those needs, and monitoring the result for feedback (see Appendix D). With the underlying logic of cohesion, community work had been conducted face-to-face by directors and project leaders before the adoption of social media.

The findings show, with respect to the (mis)alignment process (RO1). work digitalization by social media exposes the work to continuous adjustment within and across three elements of digital work, in which their coevolution forms social media (mis) alignment: digital infrastructure work (which involves embracing new uses of the platform at the user level), digital strategy work (which involves designing and redesigning governance policies for digital work), and aligning work (which involves fitting socia media uses to the platform logic and vice versa). In the case of MTS, the drifting occurred over a long-term process that was initiated by a seemingly small adjustment in governance policies (i.e., digital strategy work) that made new, useful-to-all social media uses (i.e., digital infrastructure work) accessible to external users. These changes eventually led to rapid scaling among external users who were only interested in useful-to-all uses, rather than uses exclusive to MTS users. With respect to misalignment outcomes and consequences (RQ2), the findings show that the above process transformed the nature of work, by drifting platform logic underlying digital community work, from cohesion to an inclusivity logic, which placed value on serving all platform users over only serving MTS members. This led to a drifted social media platform, which functioned based on an alternative logic (in its goals, values, and priorities), diferent from the logic dominant in the rest of the organization. As a consequence, the emerging digital community work on social media became inefective in addressing MTS members’ needs, while beneficially serving thousands of external users

The paper contributes to digital work research by identifving distinct elements of digital work that dynamically interplay to align or drift a social media platform and the work it enabled. The paper shows how work digitalization using social media can transform the platform logic underlying digital work with the result that the organization’s realized strategy difers from the intended strategy. In what follows, the paper expands on the theoretical background of the study. After a discussion of the methods, the paper elaborates on the findings in three sections: community work, within-case analysis of each social media platform, and the longitudinal cross-case analysis. It concludes with discussions of the findings, limitations, and avenues for future research

## Theoretical background

## Social media alignment

Enterprise social media can have profound positive impacts on work practices through promoting collaborative working, engagement, and management oversight (Denyer et al., 2011). Social media can include internal users (e.g., employees) or externa users, who are not oficial members or employees of a particular organization (Leonardi et al., 2013). Internal social media captures distributed internal communications and the individual’s social network (Leidner et al., 2018), thus providing organizations with the metaknowledge of “who knows what” and “who knows whom” (Leonardi, 2014, 2015), which in turn leads to improved performance (Ali-Hassan et al., 2015; Leftheriotis and Giannakos, 2014). External social media connects organizations to a larger community (Huang et al., 2017; Svahn et al., 2017), facilitating the building of relationships with external users (Kane, 2015). Relationships with external users help organizations to develop value-creating products and services (Gunarathne et al., 2018; Suseno et al., 2018), and to engage in knowledge transfer, sense-making, innovation, and problem-solving (O’Mahony and Lakhani, 2011; West and Lakhani, 2008). Because of these benefits, social media use is proliferating across organizations (Leonardi and Vaast, 2017).

More recently, organizations have included external social media to better integrate their external stakeholders in their functioning (Gallaugher and Ransbotham, 2010). For instance, social media evaluations have resulted in organizations rearranging internal practices and processes in the hospitality industry (Scott and Orlikowski, 2014). In the healthcare field, the transformationa and long-term impacts of social media have manifested themselves in benefits ranging from medical knowledge creation using input from patients (Kallinikos and Tempini, 2014) to improved medical education (Cheston et al., 2013). However, the risks associated with using open, participatory social media can be magnified when external stakeholders participate. The visibility, persistence, and editability of content (Treem and Leonardi, 2013) can create challenges to organizations using external social media (Leonardi and Vaast. 2017) as there are both risks and benefits in using social media (Gunarathne et al., 2018: Vaast and Kaganer. 2013).

Existing research shows that organizations are not in full control of social media (Ford and Mason, 2013; Vaast and Kaganer, 2013) and are unable to impose much structure on it (McAfee, 2009). They can only exert stewardship (Huang et al., 2015) rather than tight moderation and monitoring (Denyer et al., 2011). That is because social media provides generative, self-organizing mechanisms that allow work structures to emerge (e.g., likes, comments, tagging, etc.) (McAfee, 2009). While too much moderation by the organization can weaken the use and growth of social media, too much freedom and flexibility (Baptista et al., 2017; Wareham et al., 2014) can result in a drifting away from the dominant logic, thereby reducing the opportunities for organizations to realize the goals that reflect their values. Social media drifting increases tensions on these platforms, which can subsequently reduce user involvement and can lead to failure in realizing intended strategies (Denver et al.. 2011)

Despite acknowledging the dificulty in foreseeing and controlling social media use, the process by which social media influences the work elements (Ali-Hassan et al., 2015), and the long-term efects of social media on organizations remains understudied (Leonardi and Vaast, 2017). This poses major challenges for digital work and at diferent levels: in the areas of planned vs. emerging uses, planned vs. emerging work policies, and planned vs. emerging practices to align use with goals. While IS research recognizes that in order to fully exploit social media, managers need to clearly define and align social media goals with organizational goals (Denyer et al., 2011), less is known about the process of maintaining social media alignment, and the long-term consequences to the work when drifting occurs. To address these gaps, I draw on institutional logics (Thornton et al., 2012) as a relevant theoretical lens that is increasingly used to explore IT-enabled institutional change in IS research (e.g., Faik et al., forthcoming; Hansen and Baroody, 2020).

## Institutional logics

Institutional logics generally refers to the “broader cultural beliefs and rules that structure cognition and guide decision making” (Lounsbury, 2007, p. 289). In their seminal work, Friedland and Alford (1991) theorized six higher level, ideal types of institutiona logics that constitute organizing principles and cultural norms for individuals and organizations, that is, family, religion, state, market, profession, and corporation, They show how the goals. bases of attention, and sources of legitimacy are different across these ideal types of institutional logics. More recently, “community” has been recognized as a new ideal type of institutional logic (Marquis and Battilana, 2009; Marquis and Lounsbury, 2007; Thornton et al., 2012). Communities are “aggregates of people who share common activities and/or beliefs and who are bound together principally by relations of afect, loyalty, common values, and/or personal concern $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ interest in the personalities and life events of one another)” (Brint, 2001, p. 8). Whether territorial, spatial, or digital, communities afect forms of organizing and practices in organizations, as organizations live and perform “in the shadow of communities” (O’Mahony and Lakhani, 2011, p. 3).

Organizations draw on an ideal type of institutional logics to develop their own logic (Gümüsay et al., 2020), which guides their cognition, attention, and actions $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . g . } }$ , digital strategies). Within any given field or industry, diferent logics coexist, each having its own unique core principles and assumptions (Herremans et al., 2008; Lounsbury, 2005, 2007), such as community versus nationa logics in banking (Marquis and Lounsbury. 2007). aesthetics versus efficiency in architecture (Thornton et al., 2005), judicial versus social service logics in the field of alternative dispute resolution (Purdy and Gray, 2009), and open-source versus proprietary logic in software (von Krogh and Spaeth, 2007; Morgan and Finnegan, 2014).

While a variety of logics may coexist at the industry or community levels, organizations are predominantly embedded in one logic (Friedland and Alford, 1991; Marquis and Lounsbury, 2007; Purdy and Gray, 2009). When an institutional logic prevails in an organization, the dominant organizational logic shapes organizational identity, the focus of attention, values, goals, strategies, and material practices. For example, when adopting a social media platform, the dominant organizational logic justifies the reasons for adoption, forms its governance policies, and shapes social media uses. Individuals and organizational actions, at diferent levels, are embedded in and aligned with the dominant organizational logic and are constrained by it (Thornton and Ocasio, 2008, p. 104).

Regardless of the dominant organizational logic, organizations also live in larger communities that embody alternative logics, understandings, norms, rules, and resources (Besharov and Smith, 2014; Scott, 2008), which afect organizational behavior (Thornton et al., 2012, p. 70). An organization’s social media, if open to external communities, provides a gateway that empowers external users to make an impact on the organization through their use of social media. But work digitalization using external social media can create challenges for organizations if the contribution of external users is misaligned with the organizations’ original goals In this study, institutional logics literature informs the exploration of the practices that undergird the platform logic underlying an organization’s community work on social media and whether the platform logic remains aligned or drifts away from the dominant organizational logic when the work is digitalized by social media.

## Research design

The paper adopts an in-depth, longitudinal case study approach (Yin, 1994) to study digital community work enabled by social media at the Montreal Toheed Society (MTS). To build theory, I explore the two research questions with a multiple-case study approach as MTS adopted three diferent social media platforms. The social media platform is the unit of analysis.

## Context of the study

MTS. consisting of Iranian-Muslim university students or graduates, was founded in February 2012 by ten PhD students who attended universities in Montreal. According to its constitution, MTS aims to create a unified ‘family’ of Iranian scholars to promote their religious identity while respecting each other’s diferences. The founders felt a need to create an organization for immigrants, in which they could strengthen their relationships, express their concerns, support one another spiritually, emotionally, and socially, and create a local ‘family’ given how far they were from their home. MTS is based on shared membership, which provides social and cultural resources that enable individual and collective action (Marquis, 2003; Marquis et al., 2011). MTS does not own any physical property and relies on its student members to book university rooms in which to hold their events. It is self-funded and finances its events through ticket sales or internal fundraising by members. MTS also interacts with the other Iranians living in Montreal, who are not members of MTS, hereafter referred to as the external community.

At the time of data collection, MTS had about 140 members, who resided in Montreal, agreed with MTS values, and signed its constitution. Members have voting rights in annual general assemblies. The majority of the members are PhD students; a few faculty members as well as a smaller number of masters and undergraduate students are also members. The MTS board consists of five directors, two substitutes (to replace a director in case of resignation), and an inspector (to ensure the board’s compliance to constitution and address member complaints). who are elected by members in the annual general assembly. The board of directors is in charge of community work, which refers to the process of actively stewarding MTS members to employ their resources to address their problems and achieve their goals. The community work, informed by the MTS dominant logic, aims to create a coherent group of members. It involves diferent activities such as identifying members’ needs, planning projects to address those needs, implementing the projects, and obtaining feedback. Not all community work is done by board directors; it is also carried out by project leaders who are volunteers that step forward to lead projects, subprojects, or processes at MTS. Examples include volunteer leaders who administer MTS’s social media (admins), leaders who manage the newcomer workshops, or members of the newsletter team. Sometimes a board director may volunteer to become a project leader.

MTS projects can be classified in diferent groups based on their main goal: sharing knowledge (e.g., through holding workshops on job hunting, tax filing, or resume writing), socialization and networking opportunities (e.g., through implementing internal social media for member socialization, organizing weekly team sports, movie nights, and fruit-picking outings), welcoming and orientation opportunities (e.g., through information workshops for newcomers), religious and cultural discussions, and charity programs (e.g., internal fundraising for a member in need, Street Store project to support the homeless).

MTS adopted social media for two main reasons. First, the goal was to expand member interactions beyond the occasional, sporadic communication (at face-to-face events) to a more stable and continuous digital connectivity of members (to realize MTS’s vision of building a coherent ‘family’). Second, and more importantly, capturing member interactions with social media aimed to digitalize the community work of directors and project leaders. Thus, MTS initially adopted the Joomla open-source social network (JSN) as its social media platform (Oct 2013–Sep 2015). Then, MTS adopted Viber instant messenger (VIM), which limited the user group to 200 users (Feb 2015–Feb 2016). Finally, during the study period, MTS migrated to another platform, Telegram instant messenger (TIM), with no user limit (Jan 2016–present).

The choice of MTS for empirical investigation was driven by two main reasons. First, the degree of social media alignment with dominant organizational logic varied significantly in each of the three social media adoptions—that is, some were in alignment with organizational goals and enabled digital community work based on a logic of cohesion; others were misaligned and enabled digita community work based on an alternative logic. Multiple social media implementations within a single organization provide replication logic (Yin, 1994). Second, MTS resides in a broader community of Iranians with diferent values and logics who interact with MTS. This makes MTS a theoretically relevant context for exploring the research questions, as social media provide opportu nities for individuals in the external community to become users on MTS platforms and have an impact. These external users are called non-MTS users.

Following the initial conceptualization of the phenomenon of social media alignment (step 1 in Fig. 1), data were gathered from four types of sources inside and outside MTS: (a) interviews (with directors, project leaders, and members), focus groups, and observations (e.g., at genera assemblies); (b) external sources such as media coverage; (c) internal sources such as the website, internal documents (e.g., archives of newsletters), policies, meeting minutes, and annual reports; and (d) electronic logs from the digital platforms.

## Concepts

Here, I define the key concepts used in this study. Community work was conducted by directors and project leaders and refers to practices used to steward MTS members to employ their own resources to solve problems and achieve their goals. It led to needs identification and project development in diferent categories, such as knowledge sharing or newcomer welcoming. Prior to digitalization. community work was conducted face-to-face to identify members' needs, plan ways to address them. organize teams for project implementation, and monitor the results. After social media implementation, part of the community work around identifying the needs of members and addressing them became digital. Digital community work refers to the community work that was conducted on social media. Digital work involves diferent activities such as surfacing community needs on social media, regulating new uses to address the needs, and aligning uses of the platform with the platform vision. Hereafter, the terms digital work and digital community work are interchangeably used. Digital community refers to the community of users who adopted a social media platform. They may be members of MTS (MTS users) or outsider users from the Iranian community (non-MTS users). Social media logic or platform logic refers to the logic that guides organizational decisions to manage the platform. Social media or platform logic shapes and constrains digita community work conducted by project leaders and directors on social media. Platform drifting occurs when the platform logic, which guides decisions, actions, and uses on the social media, deviates from the organizational logic toward an alternative logic.

In the following, the study’s findings are presented in three main sections. First, community work at MTS is described, and its underlying logic articulated. Second, the implementation of the three social media platforms in a within-case analysis is discussed. Third, the cross-case analysis and a process model of platform drifting is presented. Finally, I discuss the implications of the study for research, practice, and teaching.

## Community work and its underlying logic

In this section, I elaborate on the nature of community work before digitalization and the underlying logic that shaped decision making and strategy choices.

## Community work

Prior to digitalization, community work involved a variety of activities conducted face-to-face by directors and project leaders. These ranged from planning for the portfolio of projects to implementing projects and monitoring their results (see Appendix D).

The planning involved identifying and prioritizing members’ needs, clustering them, and developing a portfolio of projects to address them. Continuous interaction with members informed directors (or project leaders) about members’ needs and concerns. These included work-related concerns (e.g., gathering tips on managing the challenges of being a PhD student, need for a job), social concerns (e.g., making new friends, planning for family events), and assistance to members relating to specific incidents (e.g., a friend’s mental or physical health issues, rejection of a spouse or parent’s visa application). An example is the idea of the “Hello Montreal” project in which an eighty-page guideline for newcomers to Montreal was developed. This project emerged from observing member interactions as they shared stories of the challenges they faced when initially arriving in Montreal. When implementing a project, the board actively engaged with members to find project leaders who could work with the board to build a team, envision a plan, and manage its delivery. Finding project leaders required directors to proactively engage with specific members and communicate the project vision and board priorities with the hope of securing participation. For example, to establish MTS’s monthly newsletter, a project leader was found who engaged with more than 15 members before choosing a team of five volunteers to be the newsletter editorial team. Monitoring ensured the projects were appropriately implemented in alignment with the stated goals and provided feedback to the board concerning direction and changes. Engaging with members and conducting paper surveys after events were some of the activities that provided directors and project leaders with feedback about implemented projects (positive or negative) and informed plans for revisions.

## The logic underlying community work

The findings show that MTS was predominantly embedded in what I call cohesion logic, which shaped the community work (see Table 1). This cohesion logic originated from religion, an ideal type among inter-institutional systems (Friedland and Alford, 1991; Thornton et al., 2012). Immigration to Montreal and the dificulties of living as a minority was a key motivation for most founders, whose goal was to promote their religious identity. Cohesion logic was structured around the goal of improving the quality of relationships among members in a way that would form a coherent whole around common cultural and spiritual practices. This required significant care and time to add new members as deepening and enriching relationship was a primary goal [Director #5]. Therefore, given the importance of the quality of relationships. the number of members and the pace of member growth was not a priority for cohesion logic [Director #3, transcript of the second strategic planning meeting in 2014]. Accordingly, MTS envisioned an environment in which its members would socialize weekly for a variety of reasons (from job-related to socialization to religiouscultural) and support one another through dificulties. Cohesion logic structured the board’s cognition and guided their community work through identifying issues as important (or not), developing projects to address those needs, and obtaining feedback about their success. Cohesion logic shaped actions and actors at different levels. For example, it drove the selection of newsletter volunteers, the structure through which they interacted with the board, and the team’s vision and goals for the newsletter.

Despite the roots of cohesion logic in religion as an ideal type, there was also an interest in establishing and maintaining relationships with other Iranian and Canadian communities (community as an ideal institutional logic). Cohesion logic did not promote isolation from society; indeed, MTS developed a few projects each year to “serve all” and to create bonds with the broader community in Montreal. Street Store programs<sup>2</sup> (to support the homeless) and an outreach project to thank nurses of Montreal General Hospita (who helped an MTS member during cancer treatment) are examples of projects which served all. While MTS members did not obtain an immediate benefit to internal cohesion from these outward-serving projects, these projects were aligned with the cohesion logic that advocates good citizenship in society. However, this focus was peripheral, resulting in only four outreach projects (out of 22 projects) during the first board’s term, and four outreach projects (out of 18 projects) during the second board’s term.

## Within-case analysis: digitalizing community work by social media

This section presents a detailed narrative of the three social media adoptions that were aimed at digitalizing the community work. Digitalization started with the board’s decision to adopt Joomla social network (JSN) to digitally connect members and subsequently improve the management oversight and access necessary for efective community work. Many member interactions occurred outside MTS events (e.g. over the phone or through personal conversations) and were completely hidden from the board. A universally adopted social media platform would capture more of these personal interactions, and subsequently, provide the board with better oversight of the members’ needs. However, after two years, JSN failed to reach adequate adoption by MTS members. The board then adopted VIM (with a maximum of 200 users), which was successfully adopted and used by 200 individuals. Later, due to increasing demands for membership, VIM was replaced by TIM (2200 users at the time of data collection, and 3200 at the time of publication). Joomla social network (JSN) platform

In October 2013, the board adopted an open-source social network and installed it on the MTS website. JSN was selected over Facebook because it was deemed superior in data control and user privacy; it supported the “locality of the users’ needs in Montreal” [JSN User #3] through providing a private digital platform for MTS members, without exposing a post to the global network of one’s friends. JSN provided features to extend and capture face-to-face interactions and socialization among users, for example, the ability to create a profile; add friends; post content; comment on and “like” the posts of others; run polls; and create groups. JSN’s interface and ease of use, however, were inferior to Facebook (e.g., it lacked an app version).

To implement JSN, the board appointed a passionate director and an active MTS member (with electrical engineering and computer science backgrounds) who volunteered to be JSN administrators (i.e., admins). After coordination with the board, the admins made JSN invisible to outsiders (posts and membership not visible to non-members). JSN had no limit on the number of adopters. The initial adopters consisted of board directors and their closely related friends. Following repeated advertisements in the MTS e-newsletter, other MTS members started joining, including some anonymous users. Thus, the admins “decided to have a policy of asking users to provide their real identities” [Admin #9]. This transparency was to help JSN adopters feel more comfortable on JSN because they would know who was seeing their posts and discussions. Over the next two years—when smartphone usage was not yet ubiquitous—users created a variety of uses, as many of today’s apps did not exist. An analysis of the JSN log shows that 22 groups emerged from among the JSN users, such as Good Deals (for sharing bargains they encountered when shopping), Settlement and Moving (for finding a friend to help move or a roommate with similar values), and Open Mic (for more in-depth discussions and Q&As about spirituality and religion). Despite reaching a total of 90 users, active users remained fewer than 30 (mainly directors and project leaders), which was considered inadequate usage for daily interactions.

JSN functioned in alignment with the dominant cohesion logic by creating an environment where MTS users could socialize, help one another move, learn where to buy cheap groceries, solve each other’s IT problems, collect donations for a friend in need, and have conversations about controversial topics. While JSN enabled digital community work based on cohesion logic (e.g., coordination groups for board or newsletter team), its poor adoption led the board to continue to rely on face-to-face community work.

## Viber instant messenger (VIM)

In February 2015, a member of MTS approached the board with the suggestion of creating a group on Viber, which is an instant messenger on iPhone and Android-based phones that ensures immediate circulation of news among group members. The board decided to promote VIM as a complement to the barely-diffused JSN. VIM aimed at facilitating interactions among MTS members. It was chosen because it was the dominant messenger in Iran, and most MTS members already communicated with their families through this platform. In 2015, Viber supported text, pictures, and voice messages over a shared group space visible to all users. In contrast to JSN, Viber did not allow for multiple subgroups within a group, and all messages were posted on the same timeline, visible to all group users. However, users were not able to delete or edit messages after posting. New users could be added by an existing VIM user, up to a maximum of 200 per group.

VIM was created as a group on Viber. It was informally announced by manually adding friends of directors and the VIM’s first admin (VIM champion) to the group, followed by informing these friends about the group’s raison d'etre, that is, “shar[ing] news among the MTS ‘family’” [Admin #2]. VIM admin was given full autonomy on decisions, with directors supporting him in the background. VIM’s governance policies were initially shaped by a simple, but vague, terms and conditions of use set by the admin (elements of which included being respectful of MTS’s values and norms and avoiding controversial topics like political debates). The group was private and not visible to outsiders. However, the ability to add new members was not limited to the admin; all VIM users were allowed to add new members to the group.

VIM users started to create new, unforeseen uses of the platform that they felt would be useful to all, such as finding rides from downtown to weekly event venues, and Q&As about daily life issues. These emerging uses were in line with the cohesion logic as they allowed MTS users who knew each other to help one another. As VIM difused among MTS members, its governance policies evolved to manage tensions that emerged among users. These tensions originated mainly because, unlike JSN, VIM users included non-MTS users (“about 40%” [Director #14]). These were mostly newcomers (friends of MTS users) who were added so that they could ask a few questions. However, they did not necessarily share the same values and principles as MTS users. The addition of non-MTS users created occasional tensions and led the admin to tighten the policies to ensure VIM’s efectiveness for all users. However, as a result of these additions, some uses were eventually created that were not directly aligned with the logic of cohesion. That is, non-MTS users used VIM to ask questions with no plan to know others, deepen a relationship, and integrate into MTS (i.e., no contribution to community work, not informing directors about needs of MTS members).

Overall. and with a large proportion of the 200 users being MTS members. VIM was relatively successful in creating a coherent environment that informed directors about the needs and challenges of MTS members. The resultant digital community work helped directors to better recognize members’ needs, ask questions on VIM, and search for volunteers who were willing to lead a project. Once the group reached Viber’s size cap of 200 participants, a waiting list was created.

## Telegram instant messenger (TIM)

Despite the success of VIM, three factors led the MTS board to migrate to a new instant messenger in 2016—Telegram. First, the majority of Iranians back in Iran were switching from Viber to Telegram, which made Viber less frequently used overall. Second, Telegram features were significantly superior to Viber. Telegram had no limit to the number of members that could participate. The admins decided to create a public group on Telegram with a public link where interested individuals could see the group content and then join without requiring addition by a user (i.e., TIM was fully visible). TIM was superior in terms of both user features (e.g., better privacy through the anonymity of phone numbers, improved usability, the ability to edit and delete messages after posting, and better search capability) and administrative features (e.g., polls could be posted on the timeline, better logs and backup, messages could be pinned for all users to see rather than reposting). Third, extrapolating from VIM’s relatively coherent environment, some project leaders and directors saw the migration as an opportunity to benefit a larger number of people while maintaining group cohesion.

Following the migration, TIM users shared the group link with their contacts “as a useful group for answering questions of [any] Iranian immigrants in Montreal" [Director #14]. Subsequently, TIM became visible to many outsiders and led to even more new additions to the group. While TIM started with governance policies similar to VIM, policies evolved following the increasing number of new non-MTS users and the new uses of the platform. At first, existing policies were strictly imposed to increase TIM eficiency (e.g., prohibiting messages of thanks, welcome, or congratulation). Later, policies were revised to treat MTS and non-MTS users more equally (e.g., reducing the number of business ads users can post per week for all). In contrast to VIM’s friendly environment with a majority of the active users knowing each other, the majority of TIM users were strangers who were there for transactional information exchanges (e.g., fast Q&As). Given TIM’s visibility to outsiders, policy changes (treating all users the same coupled with less cohesion-based use) led to a rapid scaling of non-MTS users (more than 97%) who were mostly newcomers with a lot of daily questions. Non-MTS users far outnumbered MTS users, and as a consequence, MTS users posted fewer messages in line with cohesion logic, as these uses required trust and deep knowledge of each other (e.g., asking for friends’ help when moving, socialization, ride sharing to a religious event, charity for MTS members in need, or asking for prayers for a friend’s sick child). The scaling resulted in TIM being chiefly used as a Q&A platform. Eventually, for the sake of eficiency and given frequent tensions (e.g., non-MTS users objecting to religious postings), several uses that were exclusively based on cohesion logic were banned (e.g., religious discussions, reminders, or socialization), and were partitioned out to other MTS platforms.

TIM became MTS’s largest and most attractive platform, used predominantly by non-MTS users ofering and seeking advice among themselves. TIM drifted from enabling community work based on cohesion logic toward functioning exclusively based on an inclusive goal of serving all Iranians living in Montreal. TIM turned into an outreach platform to serve all; and thus, many of the new non-MTS users did not even realize that TIM belonged to a religious organization given the partitioning out of such uses. TIM was rarely useful for MTS users and did not contribute to cohesion within MTS, nor did it contribute to the directors’ community work based on cohesion logic. TIM’s rapid growth did not afect the number of MTS oficial members, which remained relativity stable. However, due to its visibility and popularity, TIM required significant supervision and resource allocation (e.g., an increase in the number of admins to three and then to five).

All in all, despite the initial strategy to digitalize exchanges in order to facilitate more efective community work based on cohesion logic, after four years, MTS ended up with a drifted platform that enabled digital community work based on inclusivity logic (inclusive to all Iranians: identifying and addressing the needs of non-MTS users) and cost MTS more in terms of resources. In the following, the drifting process is explored.

## A longitudinal cross-case analysis: platform drifting process

The MTS board digitalized community work with social media to contribute to the vision of realizing a coherent, socially connected community. However, to ensure vision achievement, maintaining social media alignment with the dominant cohesion logic was crucial. The cross-case analysis reveals that social media exposed the digital community work to continuous adjustment within and across three elements of digital work (digital infrastructure work, digital strategy work, and aligning work), which eventually drifted the social media platform away from functioning based on cohesion to inclusivity logic. This section elaborates on the characteristics of the emerging inclusivity logic, followed by theorizing on the process by which platform drifting unfolded over time.

Following TIM’s drifting, the digital community work enabled by social media no longer contributed to the goal of making MTS a coherent ‘family’. The MTS directors returned to mainly using face-to-face community work for identifying and addressing the needs of MT members.

In contrast, the digital community work on TIM was conducted based on what I call inclusivity logic, which was focused on

## An emergent process of drifting

The cross-case analysis revealed three distinct elements of digital community work, which, in their coevolution, shaped socia media alignment or misalignment over time (see Fig. 4): digital infrastructure work, digital strategy work, and aligning work. In this section, first, I explain the digital infrastructure work that relates to welcoming new social media uses that difer in their fit with the dominant logic. Next, digital strategy work is presented, which involved directors’ and social leaders’ design of initial governance policies based on the dominant logic, later, the redesign of work policies to manage the tensions or seize emerging opportunities. Finally, this section theorizes directors’ and social leaders’ aligning work that involves fitting social media uses with the dominant organizational logic, and later realigning work to fit uses with the alternative inclusivity logic. The coevolution of these three elements eventually shaped social media drifting (i.e., represented by the ring sliding on the platform logic spectrum; Fig. 4).

The analysis showed that digitalizing community work started with the digital strategy work of establishing the governance policies to be in line with the dominant cohesion logic (#1 in Fig. 4), then mobilizing resources for implementation (#2), and shaping the infrastructure work to develop planned uses (#3). This positioned MTS’s social media logic initially on the cohesion side of the logic spectrum in alignment with the dominant organizational logic. However, social media logic eventually drifted away toward an alternative inclusivity logic through continuous adjustment of the three elements of digital work. Drifting started with users developing uses that were useful beyond MTS members (useful-to-all) (#4) to the realigning work to accommodate and legitimize these inclusivity uses (#5), and the revision of the governance policies (digital strategy work) (#6). This recursive, evolving cycle led to rapid scaling among non-MTS users when digital strategy rework shared useful-to-all social media uses with the outside community on a visible and open social media platform. Subsequently, the alternative inclusion logic emerged as the dominant platform logic underlying the digital community work, reshaping the digital community on social media based on the inclusion of all users, regardless of their membership in MTS.

In the long-run, this placed the platform logic on the inclusivity side of the spectrum, in contrast to MTS’s initial vision. ## Digital infrastructure work: embracing social media uses

The digital infrastructure work relates to the welcoming of new social media uses that difer in their fit with the dominant logic. The three social media varied in the extent to which they embraced new uses based on the alternative logic. According to the coding of the messages posted on social media timelines, digital infrastructure work evolved from a collection of uses that were exclusively based on the dominant logic (on JSN and initially on VIM) to a collection with some uses based on cohesion and others based on inclusivity logic (late VIM), to a collection that was exclusively based on inclusion logic (on TIM) (black bands in Fig. 5).

Fig. 5. Evolution of social media uses across social media platforms

Welcoming social media uses based on the dominant logic refers to the extent to which new social media uses were created based on the dominant organizational logic of cohesion. It includes uses that were initially planned and uses that emerged unplanned.

First, digital infrastructure work includes enabling uses initially planned by directors or project leaders to bridge an identified gap based on the dominant logic. On JSN, the Open Mic or Good Deal groups (sharing city discounts) were created to realize the uses initially discussed in the board meeting as potential uses of JSN and were immediately implemented after the roll-out. On VIM, the directors and the VIM admin initially started by sharing news of other similar centers in Montreal and posting religious and cultura calendar reminders. These uses had been previously envisioned by the VIM champion (i.e., it was to be a platform for fast sharing of community news).

Second, digital infrastructure work involved the emergence of new unforeseen uses developed by users in alignment with the dominant cohesion logic. On JSN, these new uses emerged with new groups created by users (e.g., groups for coordinating events, raising awareness about new legislation in Quebec to ban Hijab for government employees, classifieds, charity, and moving and settlement) and were used among users who knew each other intimately [User #4]. Similar uses occurred on VIM, such as finding rides to weekly event venues, fundraising for an MTS member in need, asking all users to sign up for mass praver for a terminally ill infant in the community, and eventually Q&As about daily life issues faced by users (e.g., applying for governmental scholarships, sorting out insurance problems, renewing study permits, etc.). These new uses remained within the bounds of the dominant logic because they were mainly helping MTS users who closely knew each other, deepening the relationships among them.

Digital infrastructure work also involved welcoming social media uses based on the alternative logic. This occurred in two ways. First, digital infrastructure work involved accepting new social media uses that were legitimate according to both dominant and alternative logics (useful to all). It occurred when MTS users started to ofer existing social media uses to non-MTS users. For example, on VIM, due to the usefulness of the O&As, users began to add individual friends from outside the MTS network. Thus, without changing the nature of a particular use (e.g., answering an insurance-related question), it became increasingly aligned with the alternative logic of inclusivity. That is because MTS users started serving non-MTS members more instrumentally, that is, providing answers with no potential or plan for future interaction and bonding within the MTS membership. While this social media use did not contribute to cohesion logic, it did not contradict it, as helping others is appreciated in cohesion logic as well. This type of digita infrastructure work made the inclusivity logic more prominent in the digital community work by project leaders and directors as it took more of their time and efort to handle messages that addressed the needs of non-MTS users. This mainly occurred late in the VIM use period, in which the contributors were still mostly MTS users, and non-MTS users mainly sought help.

Second, infrastructure work also extended to the creation of new uses that were exclusively based on the alternative inclusivity logic. It occurred when non-MTS users developed and engaged with transactional social media uses among themselves. With the much larger numbers, proportionally, of these non-MTS strangers, these uses were mostly developed on TIM by those who had no common values with MTS and with no plans to join the MTS family.' As both the contributors and help seekers were non-MTS users. the transactions did not represent MTS members’ needs. These transactions include both asking and answering questions, buying and selling, posting ads, and providing generic help among non-MTS users. An example of these transactional uses is:

[when someone is] searching for a volunteer to take prescribed drugs/pills to Iran for a family patient who cannot access it due to recent US sanctions. Other examples are sharing a job opportunity from one’s company with TIM users … searching for anyone who travels from Tehran to Montreal on a certain date to accompany an elderly mother who wants to visit family in Montreal but does not know the language [Interview; TIM Admin #7]

In summary, digital infrastructure work originated from the generative nature of social media that empowers users to create new uses, which vary in their extent of alignment at a micro use level with the dominant logic. In the past, face-to-face community work provided project leaders with an overview of the emerging needs of MTS members and resources available to address them (e.g., on VIM). However, the emerging digital infrastructure work enabled on social media contributed to a community work based on an alternative logic, where project leaders and directors predominantly allocated time, attention, and resources to steward a platform that identified and addressed the needs of non-MTS users, rather than MTS users. However, this adjustment with digital infrastructure work would not have emerged without changing governance policies, that is, digital strategy work.

## Digital strategy work: positioning the social media platform

Directors’ and project leaders’ community work included digital strategy work that involved the design of initial governance policies that were expected to realize a vision based on the dominant logic. Over time, they engaged in strategy rework by redesigning policies to manage emerging issues, tensions, and potentials.

Designing the governance policies to reinforce the dominant logic refers to setting the rules and regulations (e.g., for joining social media and posting content) to realize the goals and values in line with the dominant cohesion logic.

The digital strategy work included setting the initial governance policies in accordance with the dominant cohesion logic. These policies were based on the expectation of future challenges. JSN was set as invisible to outsiders and the platform link was only circulated among MTS members. Admins had no other policy for users who wanted to join nor about the content. VIM started with initia policies that were aligned with the dominant cohesion logic. The VIM admin decided to have no restriction on joining, yet created some rules for the posting of content to reinforce the dominance of cohesion logic and manage potential tensions among users: “Initially, I started with very simple terms and conditions with few lines asking for posts to be respectful of others and be generally aligned with values of MTS community”. [VIM champion, Admin #2]

Eventualy, project leaders adapted the governance policies to regulate new uses in alignment with the dominant logic. On JSN, for example, in response to new anonymous users checking out the Good Deals group, the admins imposed a policy of requiring rea identities in order to secure MTS users’ privacy when interacting and socializing with their close friends. On VIM, with the increasing number of ads, primarily from non-MTS users, the VIM admin adapted the policies to ensure the cohesion logic was maintained:

I decided to ban ads because I saw VIM as diferent from Kijiji, where users visit the site for a transaction and no one knows each other. At the same time, I decided to let Ali [an MTS member with a business] post his ad since he is a very active, contributing VIM user [who answers many questions]” … However, I decided to block ads that come from users who are not part of MTS and are here to promote their business. Otherwise, VIM will be like any other ad platform in the city. [VIM champion, Admin #2]

Project leaders also adapted and imposed policies to ensure that content that contradicted the values of the dominant logic was removed, as [Admin #7] pointed out: “On TIM, and on a daily basis, we remove a lot of posts that are against the terms and conditions such as inappropriate pictures sent by bots, disrespectful comments, debates about politics, etc.”

Redesigning governance policies to adapt to uses based on the alternative logic refers to changing governance policies to accommodate such uses. MTS reacted in two ways when revising the governance policies of the platform: either by instituting neutra policies (i.e. allowing uses that corresponded to both logics to coexist) or by instituting policies that liberated the platform from uses based on the dominant cohesion logic (e.g., by discharging uses that were exclusively based on the cohesion logic and redistributing them to other MTS platforms).

First, digital strategy rework involved implementing neutral policies to independently accommodate the alternative logic, by including a rule that made room for coexistence of diferent logics on the platform. By doing so, admins legitimized the existence of uses based on an alternative logic. On VIM, while it was initially intended for posts related to the MTS users, the first VIM admin allowed the addition of new non-MTS users and their engagement in Q&A, although many of the questions were not relevant to MTS users (e.g., a new user residing in Iran asked about the quality of schools in planning for immigration). This decision overturned JSN’s policy for the identification of users of social media. As illustrated in Fig. 6, neutralizing some policies was most evident later on VIM when the successor to the first admin gave more freedom to non-MTS users, despite their minority status in the group. He overturned the first admin’s policy of only allowing MTS users to post ads; allowed all users to post ads on VIM’s timeline, a policy which remained the same on TIM. Neutral policies contributed to transforming the nature of digital community work by increasing focus on identifying and addressing the needs of both MTS and non-MTS users equally.

Fig. 6. Evolution of governance policies across social media platforms.

Second, to handle emerging challenges, the digital strategy rework shaped new policies that partitioned out the content that was exclusively based on the dominant cohesion logic and placed it on other MTS platforms (digital or nondigital). Content partitioning refers to the assignment of a distinct platform for specific content or services (Huang et al., 2015). I call these liberating policies as they contributed to platform liberation from the dominant organizational logic. For example, reminders for the religious calendar and celebratory messages on religious occasions were banned after tensions emerged because new non-MTS users did not know about MTS, and its values. As another example, following the demise of JSN and migration to instant messaging platforms (VIM and TIM), the need for more in-depth discussions on religious or political topics was not met on any platform. This can be observed in the sporadic but frequent debates that emerged on VIM (and initially on TIM) among MTS members. To address members' unsatisfied need, and to reduce the increasing tension with non-MTS users who obiected to lengthy religious discussions. VIM's champion [Admin #2] created another Viber group to deal with this need.

I decided to create another [Viber] group with a similar name to VIM but open to political and religious debates: ‘Religious VIM’ Group … [After announcement], I forwarded such messages to Religious VIM, [asked all to continue the debate in that group], and deleted them from the main VIM. [Admin #2]

Due to rapid scaling, the workload involved in managing the large TIM platform justified the strategy rework to increasingly liberate some policies and maximize eficiency (for all). MTS needed to expand the number of TIM admins to five, including some non-MTS admins, as “an average of 90 messages [were posted] per day, excluding the deleted ones” [Admin #7]. Also, TIM’s governance policies became stricter in order to handle daily tensions. With increasing platform liberation from the once-dominant cohesion logic, the collection of TIM policies was cleared from uses that were exclusively based on cohesion logic.

Overall, the analysis shows that digital strategy work and rework led to three types of governance policies that are diferentially related to the dominant logic, leading to varying degrees of accommodation to the inclusivity logic (reinforcing, neutralizing, liberating). As summarized in Fig. 6, compared to JSN policies that reinforced cohesion logic, TIM governance policies became fully liberated from cohesion logic by treating all users equally (maximizing inclusivity). In contrast, VIM initially started with policies based on cohesion logic. However, it evolved to more neutral governance policies that favored cohesion for some uses and inclusivity for others. Neutralization and liberation of governance policies helped transform the digital community work and its underlying logic (from cohesion to inclusivity) by legitimizing serving non-MTS users while leaving MTS users’ needs unattended. Such policy evolution was achieved by the continuous aligning work of project leaders.

## Aligning work

Upon digitalizing the community work, the role of project leaders became paramount in mediating between digital infrastructure work and digital strategy work. Project leaders engaged in two distinct types of efort to fit social media uses with the platform logic.

First, the aligning work of project leaders involved actions to shape social media uses based on the dominant cohesion logic. Second, the realigning work of project leaders involved eforts to change the platform logic to fit it with emerging social media uses based on the inclusivity logic.

Aligning work of project leaders based on the dominant logic refers to the continuous eforts of project leaders to ensure that social media use fits with the dominant logic. As part of the community work, the aligning work of project leaders involved envisioning a digitally connected, coherent community based on certain uses of social media, mobilizing resources to realize the vision, and updating the board on the progress and the problems encountered in realizing cohesion-based goals

First, project leaders initially envisioned potential social media uses that could satisfy the needs of MTS users based on the dominant cohesion logic. For instance, when discussing JSN with some directors, a project leader drew on the potential of socia media’s continuous connectivity to act as a digital dashboard that members could check for a variety of reasons every day beyond checking for event information [Interview with Director #1; board approval in the meeting minutes on May 6, 2013]. Similarly, the VIM champion envisioned a connected, coherent community based on VIM’s potential for instant messaging:

[As] an integrated, connected community we should not have to [individually] call multiple community members to inform them of an incident like this [case of a member diagnosis with cancer and need for immediate support]. It can simply be achieved by relying on today’s technologies to instantly inform each other [VIM champion and its first admin]

Second, project leaders engaged in finding resources, specifically volunteers, necessary to implement social media so it would realize the vision based on the dominant logic. For instance, for JSN, the admins reached out to some active members (who supported the cohesion logic) and asked them to proactively engage with JSN use to make its vision of a digital community dashboard happen. In VIM, the admin asked active MTS members to support him in maintaining VIM in line with MTS values by backing his decisions informally on the timeline and helping him implement policies. This informal support was crucial when he was seeking policy compliance on the VIM timeline, according to an interview with the VIM admin.

Last, project leaders updated the board about the progress toward realizing the cohesion logic goals. In general, board meetings, which included directors and project leaders, were the key venue where “everyone worked on the project in order to partially or fully convince [others to agree with decisions about their project]” [Director #1]. For instance, following lengthy discussions, “engagement on JSN” became “the theme of the year” [Director #7] for the first board, leading to continuous updates and follow-ups with the board. These updates were based on the extent to which members adopted the JSN and made progress in their engagement. They became part of the board agenda in weekly meetings [meeting minutes after May 2013]. Similarly, for VIM, Director #1 mentioned that “after VIM’s [successful] initiation, … getting updates on the VIM situation became part of our agenda on the board meetings” [Interview, Director #1].

Realigning work of project leaders based on the alternative logic refers to the eforts of project leaders to fit policies with social media uses that were in line with the alternative inclusivity logic. Realigning work involved day-to-day questioning of the efectiveness of the cohesion logic as dominant on the social media, the reinterpretation of the alternative logic as independent from, rather than embedded within, the dominant logic, and convincing new project leaders more aligned with the alternative logic to join

First, project leaders increasingly questioned the efectiveness of cohesion logic’s dominance on social media from both theoretica and practical perspectives. Their questioning originated with the growing number of useful social media uses based on the alternative inclusivity logic. On a practical note, for instance, Director #1, after failing to find a successor to the admin, pointed to all available digital and nondigital MTS platforms and complained about “putting too much emphasis on finding someone who fits the ideal profile for a position …[He continued that] we are a small community, and everyone is busy at school. We should be more open to alternative people.” From a theoretical perspective, project leaders questioned the dominance of cohesion logic in all MTS platforms and advocated for the possibility of running a platform based on inclusivity logic, diferent from the rest of the organization [Director #4 in the minutes of a strategic planning meeting held in 2015].

Second, project leaders also reinterpreted the alternative logic as an independent logic rather than being embedded as a peripheral, sub-goal within the dominant logic of cohesion. While cohesion logic aligns with helping others, it also prioritizes MTS members as the focus. That means less emphasis on the outside community (non-MTS), as can be observed in the low average number of projects that “serve all” among the total MTS projects in the first two years (averaging 4 out of 20 projects per year). In the process of reinterpretation, some project leaders became increasingly vocal about inclusivity and magnified the benefits of welcoming the participation of non-MTS users on social media. To make their point, project leaders drew on achievements on digital and nondigita platforms that aligned with inclusivity logic (e.g., outreach programs, newcomer workshops, the airport pickup program, and professional workshops). For example, Director #14 stated:

We dedicated hours and hours and were not successful in getting [more than] 90 users to adopt our social network [i.e., JSN with less than 30 active users]. In contrast. in less than two vears, we reached 1900 [the number at the time of this interview] users on one of our digitaì platforms. I am not saying TIM should be the only thing we do at MTS, but it is a success in itself.

According to the minutes of a strategic planning meeting held in 2015 with more than 20 current and past directors as well as project leaders, this became the focus of the debate in the meeting. For example, Director #6 justified the dominance of inclusivity logic locally on TIM:

I understand that some of you believe an important mission of MTS is to increase the quality of the relationship among likeminded people. But, personally, it is a very important criterion to evaluate myself to see whether I can help people who are not connected [or similar] to me.

If I can serve people, I feel satisfied religiously. [Being likeminded or not], MTS has to be able to continue providing a platform for serving others, such as professional workshops or “Hello Montreal” that helps hundreds of Iranian immigrants to Montreal.

Finally, the defection of cohesion logic advocates made finding their replacement from among inclusivity advocates easier. With greater MTS focus on the useful-to-all platforms (VIM, then TIM), advocates of the cohesion logic argued that “despite being an efective organization, today’s current mission and direction of MTS is not what [I] prioritize in [my] activism” [Director #5 who defected]. In parallel, the TIM and other outreach projects (featured in media) eventually led to an image of MTS as an inclusive organization that serves all. Thus, inclusivity logic gradually “became salient in the eyes of MTS and also non-MTS members [in the outside community]” [Director #11]. Increasing saliency and reputation in inclusivity-based projects made it easier for project leaders to find successors for vacant roles for MTS platforms or projects who were more inclined toward inclusivity logic (e.g., TIM admins). For instance Director #16 mentioned that “there are some individuals who actively answer strangers’ questions on TIM. They are not part of MTS too. I approached one of them and asked her if she were willing to become another admin for TIM. She accepted. This happened twice recently” [Director #16].

Through the aligning work of project leaders, eforts were made to ensure the close fit of the social media use with goals that were formed based on cohesion logic. Initially, aligning work enabled digital community work that fit with the dominant logic, that is, identifying and addressing the needs of MTS members (on JSN and initially on VIM). However, realigning work contributed to the emergence and establishment of inclusivity logic on social media. Mediation of realigning work between digital infrastructure work and digital strategy work eventually shaped digital community work so that it functioned based on inclusivity logic to identify and address needs shared by all (later on VIM and on TIM).

## Discussion

By taking a closer look at the actual practices and a more grounded view of organizational transformation, this paper captures the process by which organizations maintain (or lose) social media alignment as well as the long-term consequences of work digitalization by social media on the nature of the digital work and its underlying logic. In the context of a not-for-profit organization, the paper demonstrates that digitalizing with social media exposes the work to continuous adjustment within and across three elements of digital work: digital infrastructure work, digital strategy work, and aligning work. The paper also develops a process model that illustrates how, through constant adjustment of these three elements, the platform logic underlying digital community work drifted away from the cohesion logic toward an emerged inclusivity logic. The analysis shows that digitalizing work by social media could have strategic implications for the institutional properties of the work, specifically its underlying logic.

Prior to digitalization, the top-down. slow-paced. face-to-face community work involved identifving needs based on cohesion planning for projects to address them and implementing them, followed by monitoring and feedback (Fig. 7; left). In contrast, digitalization by social media led to lively. fast-paced, recursive digital community work that promoted the surfacing of “all” users needs on the platform (vs. face-to-face inquiries for need identification among MTS members during pre-digitalization), let the crowd satisfy the emerging needs (vs. deliberate project planning and implementation during pre-digitalization), received instant feedback on the platform (vs. questionnaire-based or face-to-face feedback inquiries), and allowed the crowd to selectively adopt and promote specific new uses (vs. top-down decisions for change during pre-digitalization) (Fig. 7; right). Realigning work continuously synchronized the digital strategy work with the emerging uses created daily by the crowd (digital infrastructure work).

## Implications for research

The paper contributes to the literature on digital work, social media, and organizational transformation.

## Digital work as an evolving process

The paper identifies three distinct elements that shape digital work as an evolving process (digital strategy work, digital infrastructure work, and aligning work). Digitalization of work by open, participatory platforms (e.g., social media) exposes digital work elements to continuous change by users who create or adopt new uses. It empowers actors (e.g., new users or project leaders) to be change agents who can eventually transform policies and strategies associated with digital work through aligning work. While generalizing these findings to other settings and other technologies is beyond the intention of this study, the three elements of digita work can inform future research when studving digital work and the process by which it leads to organizational transformation.

With today’s increasingly open, participatory digital technologies, the continuous coevolution of these three elements makes digital work a messy, nonlinear, fluid process that can potentially have profound impacts on the nature of work. When any type of work is digitalized, particularly with today’s open, participatory social media (Leonardi, 2018; Vaast and Kaganer, 2013), it enables proactive user innovations with digital technologies to develop new work-related goals and outcomes, beyond management foresight and expectation (Rahrovani and Pinsonneault. 2020). In contrast to innovations within the bounds of existing work structure and goals, user innovations that change work goals and outcomes can empower new actors on social media platforms to mobilize resources and champion changes at the strategic levels. In the case of MTS, platform drifting started with user innovations at the grassroots level (i.e., digital infrastructure work), which led to a continuous loop of digital strategy rework (i.e., modifving platform policies). In this process, the role of project leaders and their realigning work is paramount. Project leaders exert influence over digital strategy work through continuous theorization (e.g., envisioning new uses, questioning dominant logic, reinterpreting alternative logic), resource mobilization, and action. Thus, digital work, enabled by today’s open and participatory digital platforms, is an evolving process of continuously maintaining and revising digital infrastructure work (new uses), digital strategy work (policy and strategic implications of new uses), and the aligning work (theorization and resource mobilization needed for new uses).

The findings also contribute to the literature on social media control (Ford and Mason, 2013; Vaast and Kaganer, 2013). The results illuminate the importance of digital strategy work in stewarding (Huang et al., 2015) social media alignment (vs. drifting) b decisions in three areas. First, digital strategy work includes decisions about platform openness: (1) openness to outsiders to freely join a platform (with or without mediation by a moderator), (2) openness to allowing users to create new uses, and (3) openness in having a maximum cap on platform membership. Second, digital strategy work involves decisions about platform visibility, that is, the extent to which platform outsiders can view internal social media exchanges without joining. Third, digital strategy work includes the extent to which governance policies enforce rules and regulations that support the dominant logic. The combination of these aspects of digital strategy work play a pivotal role in maintaining alignment or drifting.

The findings emphasize the importance of balancing several factors in digital strategy work for maintaining social media alignment. As illustrated in Table 3, despite high openness in JSN, the platform logic remained aligned with organizational cohesion logic when invisibility was associated with reinforcing governance policies. However, this came at the cost of poor user adoption. In contrast, in the case of TIM, when full openness and visibility of the social medium was coupled with liberating governance policies, it led to an unplanned platform drifting toward the alternative inclusivity logic—through scaling of the user base of external users. The VIM case straddled these extremes, keeping the platform logic relatively in alignment with the dominant organizational logic (cohesion) and with a decent user base size (200 users and only 40% were non-MTS users). This was achieved by having a semi-open (having a 200-user cap on membership and manual addition of new users), semi-visible (invisible, until a user was added by an existing VIM user) platform and more neutral policies (allowing uses that corresponded to both logics to coexist). VIM was more open than JSN as it allowed non-MTS member users and legitimized their use of the platform (for Q&As). However, adding new users was neither too controlled (e.g., checking for user identity when joining JSN), nor was it too open (e.g., joining by a public link in TIM) Instead, the manual addition of external users by existing MTS users became an organic, crowd-based control strategy: likeminded friends were added through slower-paced manual addition.

## Digital work as an emerging outcome

This study responds to the call for an exploration of the long-term efects of social media on work (Leonardi and Vaast, 2017). Past research suggests that, for traditional IT implementation, initial user innovations would quickly turn into routine IT uses (Ortiz de Guinea and Webster, 2013) and changes in work practices occur “in bursts and stops” after a brief window of opportunity (Tyre and Orlikowski, 1994, p. 98). However, my study shows that the outcome of digitalizing work using social media is not a complete, stable product and is continuously subject to change. This paper showed that the outcome of digital work has the capacity to be profoundly transformed over time, even in its institutional properties (e.g., its underlying logic). This originates from the exposure of the three elements of digital work to emerging innovations and changes by various actors. In MTS, work digitalization by social media was expected to improve the quality of relationships between members around common cultural and spiritual practices (cohesion logic), and to enable more efective community work (i.e., expanding the reach of directors to a larger number of MTS members and yielding them a better oversight of members’ changing needs). However, the outcome of MTS’s use of social media redirected digital community work towards identifying and addressing the needs of all users (chiefly non-MTS users) with no improvements in the quality of relationships between MTS members. Thus, digital community work, as an outcome, lost its spirit of creating a coherent family and turned into inclusivity-based digital community work. This outcome had strategic significance to MTS in resource allocation, organizational membership, and organizational image in the external community.

My study shows that, while organizations increasingly adopt open and participatory digital technologies to achieve certain outcomes (Vodanovich et al. 2010), scaling can become a mechanism by which a social media platform can be hijacked<sup>3</sup> by alternative logics (as put by Director #1). In digitalizing work, competing logics do not necessarily benefit equally from openness and visibility and when bundled with neutral governance policies, could result in a disproportionately larger benefit to one logic over the other leading to drifting. It is important to note that platform drifting unfolded from the common ground between the competing logics; and with a chain of decisions made by proiect leaders and directors who were advocates or appreciative of the cohesion logic. This extends past research on online community size that suggests misaligned uses (aka topic inconsistency) would reduce the number of users on the platform (Butler et al., 2014). In contrast, my paper shows that misalignment gradually emerges at the use level and from common ground and can lead to increased community size, yet in a misaligned direction (i.e., the drift). The paper also extends recent research on social media and the shift toward more open forms of strategizing (Baptista et al., 2017; Peppard et al., 2014; Wareham et al., 2014): too much flexibility in the governance policies of external social media platforms (e.g. in openness to outsiders joining or full visibility to outsiders) facilitates rapid recruitment from the outside community and subsequently, drifting. Thus, for some logics, limitations in openness and visibility can function in favor of maintaining alignment (as per the VIM case). This complements past research on scaling that occurred in alignment with organizational goals (e.g., Huang et al., 2017).

While the drifting occurred when the social media became open to the outside community, I argue that my study may also have implications for internal social media in large organizations. Recent IS research suggests that, within organizations, the decision to adopt digital technologies is not tightly integrated (see “symbolic IT adoption” in Rahrovani et al., 2014a), but fragmented across diferent departments (e.g., programming vs. UX design) with distinct local logics (Tumbas et al., 2015). Thus, the adoption of an internal social media can create tensions between competing logics internally (e.g., designers’ aesthetic logic vs. programmers efficiency logic) on the platform. This makes the contribution of the paper about the elements of digital work and their role in drifting relevant to internal social media, and a topic for further future research.

## Digital work and alignment

The paper also contributes to the alignment literature (Sabherwal and Chan. 2001: Tallon et al., 2016). The study showed the increasing importance and dificulty of maintaining alignment on a moving target such as open enterprise platforms. While alignment literature has been chiefly focused on firm-level or process-level alignment (Queiroz, 2017), less is known about the alignment of open, participatory platforms (e.g., social media). The findings of this study support the idea “that alignment is a moving target” (Coltman et al., 2015) and conflict with Palmer and Markus' (20o0) notion of alignment maturity that claims alignment is institutionalized and is no longer a differ entiating factor for organizational performance. This study counters that claim, suggesting that maintaining alignment becomes significantly more challenging with the proliferation of open, participatory platforms such as social media. While in the past, realignment was a challenge for organizations due to high inertia (Levallet and Chan, 2018; Yeow et al., 2018), digital technologies have resulted in faster realignment processes, distribution to more actors, and, at the same time, less control. This makes the realization of digital strategy and digital alignment more closely associated with daily activities (Henfridsson and Lind, 2014; Karpovsky and Galliers, 2015). Maintaining alignment is more challenging on open, participatory platforms that empower individuals and communities to transform organizations. Future research can explore the implications of a misaligned platform for organizations (e.g., their organizational identities).

## Digital work and organizational transformation

This paper contributes to the organizational transformation literature (e.g., Besson and Rowe, 2012). Digitalization has been associated with organizational transformation (Mignerat and Rivard, 2009) at the business level (e.g., improving work pace and productivity (Hull et al., 2007; Nambisan et al., 2017)) and at the institutional level (e.g., by changing the business model (Davidson and Vaast, 2010)). However, IS research has largely taken organizational logic for granted and has studied work digitalization mainly as a source of convergent transformation (Besson and Rowe, 2012). Convergent transformation refers to changes that occur largely within the boundary of the existing dominant logical structure (e.g., improving eficiency or efectiveness in processes by adopting digital technologies). This study shows that work digitalization using social media might not remain within the bounds of the dominant logic. Instead, it may contribute to the inclusion of new logics or changing the balance of power between competing logics, and subsequently, transformation of the existing work logic. The paper shows that work digitalization with external social media may lead to deep-structure organizational transformation (Besson and Rowe, 2012), which occurs when an organization experiences significant changes in its institutional properties (Baptista, 2009; Davidson and Vaast, 2010). The drifting model suggests convergent transformation is an initial step in the process of change in the work logic. Deep-structure digital transformation does not occur overnight and cannot be comprehensively planned (Rahrovani et al., 2014b), rather, it emerges through day-to-day practices (Peppard et al., 2014) that either intentionally or unintentionally establish an alternative work logic within the organization.

This study has implications for the literature on community organizations (Marquis and Battilana, 2009; O’Mahony and Lakhani, 2011; Thornton et al., 2012) by showing that when organizations use open digital technologies to benefit from external communities (e.g., for knowledge transfer or innovation), they face the risk of drifting by sharing control with the external community. While organizations oficially maintain control of some mechanisms (e.g., appointing admins or setting policies), they empower externa users to employ the generative, self-organizing mechanisms of social media to create new institutions on the platform by hybridizing multiple elements from distinct logics (Battilana and Lee, 2014) and advocate for their adoption at diferent levels. In line with prior research. this study shows that radical transformation emerges from an existing institution and its arrangements (Mever and Rowan 1977) (e.g., taken-for-granted usages) coupled with new digitally-enabled institutional infrastructures (Hinings et al., 2018) (e.g., social media policies that enable or constrain actions). This paper shows that social actors (e.g., project leaders) start by selectively choosing and promoting uses that are considered legitimate and valuable based on both competing logics (e.g., Q&As on VIM). Obtaining legitimacy from the dominant intuitional logic (i.e., policies revised to treat diferent logics equally) when coupled with openness (in creating new uses and number of users joining) and visibility (to outsiders) can lead to an organic, rapid scaling among external communities, and a flipping of the power balance toward a competing logic.

## Implications for practice and teaching

IS strategy research can ofer implications for both practitioners as well as university instructors (Moeini et al., 2019). For managers, this study emphasizes that more open policies are not necessarily better for governing open, participatory digital platforms. When leveraging the power of the crowd, managers should be mindful that managing open and participatory social media requires attention, time, and most likely, diferent management styles to steward the dynamic interaction between a social media platform and the organization. This research shows that social media have the power to transform organizational logics, goals, and strategies. Particularly when enlarging the user base, social media alignment with organizational goals and logics should be carefully and constantly stewarded through pulling policy levers (about platform openness, visibility, joining, and user behaviors). For university instructors, this study is an extreme case that highlights the challenge of managing open digital platforms (teaching case: Rahrovani, 2019). Mostly, students view digital platforms as an easy and cheap way to access novel ideas and develop new products and services. This paper shows the risks and costs associated with a successfully growing platform user base and the factors that contribute to the challenges of maintaining platform alignment.

## Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted in the context of a not-for-profit organization and a specific type of work (i.e., community work), which limits its generalizability. MTS was chosen as the setting because it had implemented three diferent social media platforms to achieve the same goal, yet with very diferent results. MTS, as a community organization based on volunteerism, provides more freedom to project leaders to take an active role in shaping strategies and making changes, compared to for-profit organizations. In for-profit contexts, there are formal structures that exert control over employees (e.g., through lines of reporting and reward systems). Therefore, while for-profit organizations still have challenges in managing a community of insiders and outsiders, they have more control over internal employees and can be more proactive in maintaining alignment. This makes managing social media more challenging in the context of community, not-for-profit organizations. Future research could explore how organizations, especially for-profit organizations, can leverage their resources and hierarchical structure to maintain their dominant logic on open, participatory social media platforms. Second, this research project was focused on social media that included outsiders. Due to increased levels of exposure and visibility, managing external social media that includes outsiders is more challenging and more subject to drifting. Therefore, we should be cautious in generalizing the findings to all types of social media. In addition, this study focused on the high-level question of maintaining alignment, despite diferences in the three types of social media studied. Future research could take a more micro approach by exploring the effect of the social media features of each type and the afordances they provide at the user level. Finally, while an institutional logics perspective was adopted, other theoretical lenses could provide further insight into the phenomenon. For instance, MTS’s platform drifting could be studied from the infrastructure perspective (Constantinides and Barrett, 2015; Monteiro et al., 2013; Street et al., 2018) contrasting bottom-up vs. top-down processes in infrastructure development. Another relevant lens might be the perspective of organizational identity change (Corley and Gioia, 2004; Stein et al., 2013), which is related to institutional logics (e.g., Lok, 2010; Rao et al., 2003). The efect of social media drifting on MTS’s identity and its membership body is an intriguing question worth exploring.
