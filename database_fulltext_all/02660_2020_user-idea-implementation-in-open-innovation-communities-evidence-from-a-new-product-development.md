## Abstract

In collaborative crowdsourcing communities for open innovation, users generate and submit ideas as idea co-creators. Firms then select and implement valuable ideas for new product development. Despite the popularity and success of these open innovation communities, relatively little is known about the factors that determine the implementation of the user-generated ideas. Based on research on individual creativity, we propose a conceptual model integrating users' previous experience, idea presentation characteristics and feedback valence to explain the likelihood of idea implementation. We validate our research model with a panel data analysis of 43 550 ideas submitted by 16 360 users in the MIUI new product development community hosted by Xiaomi, a large electronics manufacturing company in China. We find an inverted U-shaped relationship between users' past successful experience and idea implementation. Furthermore, the length of ideas is positively associated with the likelihood of idea implementation. There is also an inverted U-shaped relationship between supporting evidence and idea implementation. Finally, we demonstrate the negative effect of positive feedback and the positive effect of negative feedback on idea implementation. These findings offer rich insights to understand the phenomenon of open innovation better. Theoretical and practical implications are discussed.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

With the rapid development of the service economy, users have gradually shifted from passive product receivers to active value co-creators (Kornish & Hutchison-Krupat, 2017; Lusch & Vargo, 2006; Qiu & Kumar, 2017; Vargo & Lusch, 2004; Wooten & Ulrich, 2017; Zwick, Bonsu, & Darmody, 2008). They express their opinions or needs through firm-hosted online forums or communities to engage with and influence firms' product design and develop ment (Zhao & Zhu, 2014). A growing number of firms have taken advantage of this trend and established collaborative crowdsourcing communities for open innovation (Cui, Ye, & Teo, 2015; Whelan, Conboy, & Crowston, 2014) Prominent examples include Dell's IdeaStorm (Ebel, Bretschneider, & Leimeister, 2016), Starbucks' MyStarbucksIde (Sullivan, 2010) and Beijing Xiaomi Technology's (Xiaomi) MIUI new product development community. Using the ideas of users, firms outsource product innovation tasks traditionally done in-house to a large network of externa individuals in the form of an open call (Chesbrough, 2006; Osterwalder & Pigneur, 2010; Tapscott & Williams, 2007) Unlike a one-time contest (Bockstedt, Cheryl, & Mishra, 2016; Lee, Walsh, & Wang, 2015; Mo, Sarkar, & Menon, 2018) or a multi-stage tournament held in a competitive crowdsourcing community (Terwiesch & Xu, 2008), a collaborative crowdsourcing community encourages users to generate ideas repeatedly over time, in a non-competitiv manner (Sullivan, 2010; Ye & Kankanhalli, 2013). Firms adopt valuable user ideas and incorporate them into interna new product development, ultimately producing products that better meet users' demands or preferences (Eservel 2014; Schlagwein & Andersen, 2014). Through collaborative crowdsourcing, firms effectively use a large pool of peo ple and exploit their knowledge, skills and resources at a lower cost to promote effective integration of internal and external resources of firms and achieve open innovation of new product development (Brabham, 2009; Howe, 2006 Taylor & Joshi, 2019).

In a collaborative crowdsourcing community, users voluntarily respond to a company's call for idea generation tasks (Bayus, 2013) and offer a large number of ideas related to various functions and types of products (Hossain & Islam, 2015a, 2015b). However, the quality of the proposed ideas cannot be guaranteed. For example, ideas may not be detailed, constructive or innovative enough (Di Gangi & Wasko, 2009; Di Gangi, Wasko, & Hooker, 2010; Magnusson, 2009). In some cases, submitted ideas may have already been tested by internal staff or be similar to those generated by other users (Bayus, 2013). Ideas are accepted and implemented only when they are innovative (George, Jones, & Zhou, 2007) and have potential commercial value with reasonable implementation cost (Burroughs, Moreau, & Mick, 2008; George et al., 2007; Shalley, Zhou, & Oldham, 2004). The value of user ideas i not just essential for the survival of a collaborative crowdsourcing community, but it also directly affects the innova tion effects of new product development (Zhao & Zhu, 2014). Moreover, users who spend time and effort generating and submitting ideas want to know how to increase the likelihood that their ideas will be implemented by firms (Li Kankanhalli, & Kim, 2016). Therefore, how to take adequate measures to increase the likelihood of idea implementa tion has become a real concern for firms and their users.

Although current empirical studies have discussed idea implementation from the perspective of user experience and idea characteristics (egBayus, 2013, Hoornaert, Ballings, Malthouse, & Van den Poel, 2017, Li et al., 2016, Ma Lu, & Gupta, 2019, Schemmann, Herrmann, Chappin, & Heimeriks, 2016, Yan, Leidner, & Benbya, 2018), there ar several research gaps. First, the existing literature is not conclusive regarding the role of past experience on futur success of the users. Specifically, previous works suggest that users' past successful experience either positivel (Hoornaert et al., 2017; Ma et al., 2019) or negatively (Bayus, 2013) impacts the likelihood of idea implementation Besides, prior studies on idea implementation failed to consider the users' failed experiences, ideas submitted but not implemented. Second, existing studies investigate the effect of idea presentation on idea implementation by considering the cognitive overload of idea recipients (Li et al., 2016), rather than the input of the idea generators. Third, previous studies on the antecedents of idea implementation have not considered contextual factors such as feed back valence. However, feedback valence is a powerful tool for enhancing individual creativity performance (Zhou & Shalley, 2008).

In this study, we systematically examine the determinants of idea implementation for new product development in a collaborative crowdsourcing community. Based on the perspective of idea generators, we develop a theoretica framework integrating personal characteristics, process engagement, and contextual characteristics to study the like lihood of idea implementation. Idea implementation comprehensively evaluates the individual creative performance of users participating in idea tasks. Accurately, the ideas implemented by firms are generally characterized by hig quality, novelty, high commercial value or high feasibility (Franke, von Hippel, & Schreier, 2006), indicating to no small extent that users have achieved superior creative performance. To study the likelihood of idea implementation we first use the user experience factor in the personal characteristics model, disentangle user experience into suc cess experience and failure experience and explore their effects on idea implementation. Second, we introduce idea presentation characteristics into the process engagement module, use idea length and supporting evidence an explore their impacts on idea implementation. Finally, we include feedback valence as a contextual characteristic to explore the effects of positive feedback and negative feedback on idea implementation.

Overall, the theoretical contributions of this study are as follows. First, our study reconciles the mixed findings on the relationship between past success and the likelihood of idea implementation in previous studies (Bayus, 2013; Hoornaert et al., 2017; Ma et al., 2019) by demonstrating the inverted U-shaped relationship between these two factors. Moreover, we propose that past failure negatively affects the likelihood of idea implementation. Sec ond, unlike previous studies on idea implementation from the perspective of idea receivers (Li et al., 2016), ou research explores the effect of idea presentation characteristics on idea implementation from the perspective of idea generators. To this end, we show that idea length is positively correlated with idea implementation and that there is an inverted U-shaped relationship between supporting evidence and idea implementation. Third, based on the user experience and idea presentation characteristics, we further introduce feedback valence as a contextua factor to construct a theoretical model of the likelihood of idea implementation. As a result, we demonstrate the negative effect of positive feedback and the positive impact of negative feedback on the likelihood of ide implementation.

## 2 | LITERATURE REVIEW

## 2.1 | Idea implementation in an open innovation community

Open innovation refers to the process by which firms integrate internal and external knowledge to stimulate domestic innovation and look for external channels to commercialize products and ideas (Chesbrough, 2006; Kohli & Melville, 2019). The central idea behind open innovation is the crowdsourcing of knowledge, skills and plans to the public (Chesbrough, 2003, 2012; Schlagwein, Kecmanovic, & Hanckel, 2019; Taylor & Joshi, 2019). Crowdsourcing, as defined by Howe (2008) and Estellés-Arolas and González-Ladrón-de-Guevara (2012), is the act of outsourcing a task once performed by an employee to a large, undefined group of individuals outside the company in the form of an open call. We focus on collaborative crowdsourcing, which involves user groups jointly participating in creativ tasks and collaborating to generate innovative ideas (Chiu, Liang, & Turban, 2014; Ye & Kankanhalli, 2013). In collab orative crowdsourcing communities for open innovation, business experts adopt user-generated ideas and imple ment them in the process of new product R&D (Brabham, 2008; Liu, Hull, & Hung, 2017). Ideas are accepted and implemented typically when they are innovative and have potential commercial value with reasonable implementa tion costs (George et al., 2007; Shalley et al., 2004)

In collaborative crowdsourcing communities for open innovation, idea implementation reflects users' innovation performance. Apart from a few case studies (Di Gangi & Wasko, 2009; Di Gangi et al., 2010; Howe, 2008), empirica studies involving idea implementation are conducted from two perspectives: user experience and idea characteristic (Table 1). From the user experience perspective, previous studies have explored the effects of previous implementation, previous commenting activity, previous submissions, tenure, employees/product users and generalists/non generalists on the likelihood of idea implementation. Bayus (2013) found that past success harms the implementatio of ideas and that previous commenting activity has a positive effect on the implementation of ideas in Dell's IdeaStorm. Based on data from Dell's IdeaStorm and Starbucks' MyStarbucksIdea, respectively, Hossain and Islam (2015a, 2015b) showed that a higher number of ideas submitted by a member predict the probability of having more ideas implemented. Li et al. (2016) found that previous participation (commenting) and the last rate implementation are positively related to the likelihood of idea implementation in Dell's IdeaStorm and Salesforce.com's IdeaExchange. Schemmann et al. (2016) found that paying attention to the crowdsourced ideas of others (the com menting behaviour of users) positively affects whether an idea is implemented by the crowdsourcing company, an internationally active beverage producer, and a retailer. Hoornaert et al. (2017) suggested that contributor informa tion (number of previous ideas, amount of last contributor comments, number of ideas implemented in the past an tenure) is predictive of idea implementation in a crowdsourcing community for an IT product (Mendeley). Examining Salesforce.com's IdeaExchange Community, Yan et al. (2018) found that ideas contributed to and promoted by employees are more likely to be implemented than those only developed by product users. Ma et al. (2019) foun that the previous adoption experience of innovators positively influences the adoption of user innovation by the firm in an online gaming community. Hwang et al. (2019) suggested that ideas generated by generalists are more feasible and implementable than that of non-generalists.

From the perspective of idea characteristics, previous studies have discussed the effects of idea characteristics (eg, idea content, idea presentation, idea popularity and idea classification) on the likelihood of idea implementation Li et al. (2016) argued that based on message persuasion theory, popular ideas are more likely to be implemented by companies and found that idea length is negatively related to the likelihood of idea implementation. In addition, the number of reference pages and supplementary pictures has an inverted U-shaped relationship with the likelihood of idea implementation. Di Gangi and Wasko (2009) used the diffusion of innovation theory to propose that relative advantages (appropriability, market value, return on investment, development of a new capability), compatibility of ideas and changes in agents' promotion efforts (votes, comments and unique users) have a positive effect on an organization's decision to accept ideas. However, the hypotheses proposed by Di Gangi and Wasko (2009) have not been empirically validated. Hossain and Islam (2015a) divided ideas into linked ideas, which are ideas linked with other ideas, and sole ideas. They found that sole ideas are more quickly implemented by organizations than linked ideas Similarly, Hossain and Islam (2015b) divided ideas into product, experience and involvement ideas. They showe that, compared with experience ideas, product ideas have lower requirements to be implemented by organizations, whereas involvement ideas require users to have a longer period of presence in the community, a higher total num ber of submitted ideas, interval between submission and implementation. votes submitted. votes received, comments submitted, points earned, comments received and points for ideas. Based on message persuasion theory, Schemmann et al. (2016) revealed that an idea's popularity and its potential innovativeness positively influence its likelihood of implementation by the crowdsourcing company. Hoornaert et al. (2017) indicated that crowd feedback (crowd votes and comments) and idea content (relative distinctiveness) are the best predictors of idea implementa tion. Ma et al. (2019) showed that the popularity, integrity, maintenance and the rareness of the idea positively influ ence the innovation adopted by the firm. The complexity and images of the idea also have an inverted U-shape relationship with adopted innovation.

First, there are several inconclusive findings regarding the effect of users' past successful experience on idea imple mentation likelihood. Specifically, Bayus (2013) found that past success negatively influences idea implementation likelihood. However, Hoornaert et al. (2017) and Ma et al. (2019) found that users' past success (number of ideas

## 2.2 | Individual creativity performance

We propose that the likelihood of idea implementation can be explained from the perspective of individual creativit performance. In this regard, research on individual creativity provides us a suitable theoretical framework to explai the users' performance in generating ideas, that is, idea implementation.

The antecedents of individual creativity performance involve personal characteristics, process engagement and contextual characteristics. Specifically, personal characteristics involve personality, knowledge, cognitive, experience skill, career and so on. For example, Guo, Su, and Zhang (2017) showed that openness to experience and extraver sion are positively related to individual creativity, whereas neuroticism and conscientiousness are negatively associ ated with individual creativity. Salter, Wal, Criscuolo, and Alexy (2015) found that individuals' openness to externa sources of knowledge is curvilinearly related to their ideation performance. Bogilovic, Černe, and Škerlavaj (2017) revealed that personal knowledge of hiding is negatively associated with individual creativity. Zwick, Frosch, Hoisl, and Harhoff (2017) found that education level, professional skills, personality traits, career motivation, cognitive abil ities and cognitive problem-solving style are significantly related to the performance of, inventors during thei careers.

Process engagement refers to the effort, time, energy input and the cognitive effort of individuals. For example Garcia Martinez (2015) revealed that the higher the contestants' psychological involvement when performing com petitive tasks, the more creative their solutions. Catarina, Luis, and António (2015) found that self-leadership posi tively affects individual innovation through the mediating effect of work engagement. Sigala and Chalkiti (2015 suggested that employee creativity is positively related to their participation in social networks and their use of socia media for searching, storing, and reading information (internal cognitive processes) and sharing, discussing and co creating information (external cognitive processes).

Contextual characteristics involve leader-member exchange, team size, extrinsic rewards, feedback valence and so on. Audenaert, Decramer, George, Van Waeyenberg, and Verschuere (2019) revealed that individual innovation is related to consistent employee performance management and that leader-member exchange plays the role of mod erator in this relationship. Lee et al. (2015) found that increasing team size has an inverted U-shaped relationship with novelty and an ever-increasing relationship with the likelihood of producing a high-impact paper. Salter et al (2015) proposed that extrinsic rewards positively affect the intrinsic motivation of employees with an internal locu of control, thus improving their creative performance. Hoever, Zhou, and van Knippenberg (2018) suggested tha negative feedback will stimulate the team's systematic efforts and the attention of the new information of th external department. Instead, positive feedback will motivate members to use their information flexibly and contrib ute different insights to the team.

## 3 | THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK

In an open innovation community, ideas are implemented by firms as a result of the idea generation process reflecting the performance of user participation in idea tasks. The ideas implemented by firms are generally charac terized by high quality, novelty, commercial value or feasibility (Franke et al., 2006). Previous studies on idea imple mentation focused on user experience and idea characteristics, which are personal features of users (Shalley et al., 2004) and their input in the creative process (Zhang & Bartol, 2010), respectively. According to the literature on indi vidual creativity performance, the antecedents of idea implementation also include the contextual characteristics Thus, we develop a theoretical framework to explain the likelihood of idea implementation that comprises persona characteristics, process engagement and contextual characteristics (Figure 1).

In the personal characteristics model, we distinguish between users' success experience and failure experience and specifically discuss the effect of both types of experience on idea implementation. In a collaborative open inno vation community, users respond to a company's call to participate in the idea task of new products and continue to submit idea solutions. If the company implements an idea, the user accumulates a successful experience of partici pating in idea tasks (Bayus, 2013). In contrast, if the company does not implement the idea, the user gathers a failur experience. Different experiences accumulated in the early stage may have different effects on the efforts and per formance of users in subsequent tasks. Past success is defined as the number of implemented ideas submitted b users previously, whereas past failure refers to the number of non-implemented ideas submitted by users already

In the process engagement model, we adopt idea length and supporting evidence to explore the effect of use engagement in the process of idea generation on idea implementation. Zhang and Bartol (2010) defined engagement as ‘employee involvement in creativity-relevant methods or processes, including problem identification, information searching and encoding, and idea and alternative generation’ (p. 862). Correctly, the length of an idea and its supporting evidence objectively reflect the user's creative efforts to identify problems, search for information, edit and generate th idea. Idea length is defined as the total number of words in its content. Supporting evidence is defined as the number of multimedia materials used to illustrate or support an idea, such as pictures, videos or network links.

In the contextual characteristics model, we adopt positive feedback and negative feedback to explore the effect of feedback valence from community members to users on the likelihood that their subsequently generated idea will be implemented by the company (Wooten & Ulrich, 2017). Researchers on individual creativity have define feedback valence as ‘the positive or negative outcome of the comparison between an individual's creative performance and situational criteria’ (Zhou, 1998, p. 262). Our study defines positive feedback as a supportive evaluation of the user's previous ideas before submitting these ideas and negative feedback as a negative evaluation of th user's earlier ideas before submitting these ideas.

## 3.1 | Effect of user experience on idea implementation

Studies of new product design have adopted different views on the effect of examples in the design process. Some researchers have proposed that examples inspire creation (Helma, Vattam, & Goel, 2009), whereas others have suggested that they lead to fixation (Chrysikou & Weisberg, 2005; Jansson & Smith, 1991). When designing new products, individ uals typically do not create new things from scratch. Generally, they modify, combine or adjust existing design element to generate new ideas (Ward, 2007). Scholars have called this method ‘design-by-analogy’, which means using examples to stimulate idea generation (Hey, Linsey, Agogino, & Wood, 2008). Although examples may not always provide adequat solutions to complete design tasks, they can serve as cues to help individuals retrieve relevant concepts from long-term memory and generate ideas. However, there is a risk of using the sampling approach. Individuals may adopt design ele ments related to the sample, thus limiting their ability to generate original ideas (Chan et al., 2011).

Previous empirical work has shown that presentation samples can inspire creativity (Casakin & Goldschmidt, 1999; Goel, 1997). Helma et al. (2009) further found that individuals who want to solve a design problem first search for elements related to the design task in long-term memory, then elaborate and integrate these elements to generate solutions that meet the design goals. Presentation examples stimulate the creative inspirations of individuals to create higher quality idea solutions, thereby reinforcing the possibility that idea solutions can be implemented. In contrast, existing examples in design may harm the novelty of design schemes because of fixation (Chan et al., 2011). Jansson and Smith (1991) first discovered and described the phenomenon of design fixation in engineering design, which means that individuals not only reproduce the ideas of the sample when it meets the requirements of the task but also unconsciously refer to the sample when it does not meet these requirements. Even if the examples have negative characteristics, the individual will still integrate the elements or features of the examples in their design. Studies in cognitive psychology have also proposed that individuals tend to fixate on the characteristics or elements of existing examples, making their ideas unoriginal (Marsh, Landau, & Hicks, 1995; Smith, Ward, & Schumacher, 1993). Fixation is considered an unconscious behaviour of plagiarism, and thus individuals are often unaware of the characteristics or elements of their fixation and their connection with previous examples (Marsh & Landau, 1995; Marsh, Ward, & Landau, 1999). Cognitive fixation limits the heuristic application of knowledge in th innovation process (Birch & Rabinowitz, 1951), resulting in a lack of novelty and commercial value of ideas (Dahl & Moreau, 2002) and reducing the possibility that these ideas will be implemented.

In the collaborative open innovation community, users participate in new product innovation and put forward their ideas for creating new products. Every day, a large number of users submit many ideas to the community. Afte screening and evaluation by business experts, only a minimal amount of ideas are implemented by companies. Thes few ideas naturally become reference examples for users in the idea generation process. If users themselves submit the implemented ideas, these implemented ideas, as rare successful experiences, become the early examples of th subsequent creation of ideas by users. For example, an individual user's first or previous ideas implemented by a company help create a knowledge base and ideal elements for all users to engage in idea tasks. This knowledgebase or idea elements play a role of inspiration in the subsequent generation of ideas by individual users, which help improve the quality of ideas and increase the possibility that companies implement ideas. However, with the contin uous accumulation of successful experiences, individual users become more and more familiar with the idea exam ples already implemented and unconsciously tend to adopt these familiar characteristics in subsequent ideas. This fixation effect limits the ability of individuals to come up with new and commercially viable ideas, making them les likely to be implemented. Purcell and Gero (1992, 1996) found that when the sample contained features with whic the individual was already familiar, it was more likely to produce a fixation effect. In summary, we expect that ther is a positive correlation between the likelihood that users will propose ideas implemented by companies and their ini tial successful experiences. However, as users accumulate successes, their possibility of introducing implemented ideas is negatively correlated with their successful experience.

Hypothesis 1 There is an inverted U-shaped relationship between the user's past successful experience and the likelihood of idea implementation.

Jansson and Smith (1991) carried out a series of experiments to verify the fixation effect in the product desig process. They found that even if the sample had defective characteristics, people with professional design knowl edge tended to incorporate sample features into the design schemes. Additional experimental research conducte by Chrysikou and Weisberg (2005) showed that even if people did not have professional design knowledge, the fixa tion effect still appeared in the design process when they were shown examples. More importantly, all individuals participating in the experiment without exception copied the design elements of the explicitly defective examples The findings of this study suggest that fixation, which has a significant adverse effect on the design process, is a transfer effect of sample defects

In a collaborative open innovation community, when companies do not adopt user-submitted ideas, these ideas can still be used as examples to influence users' subsequent idea solutions. Ideas rejected by companies may have specifi defective characteristics, which will negatively affect users' following submissions through the negative transfer effect of the fixation effect. The more users accumulate failures, the more likely they are to fall into the paradigm of solidified thinking formed by defective features, making their creative solutions unoriginal or unnecessary. Therefore, the mor users accumulate failures, the less likely they are to propose ideas that will be implemented by companies.

Hypothesis 2 The user's past failure experience is negatively correlated with the likelihood of idea implementation.

## 3.2 | Effect of idea presentation characteristics on idea implementation

The effort is perceived as a mean of transforming an individual's unobservable psychological motivations into work achievements (Parsons, 1986). The results of the work objectively reflect the user's input into the work; that is, the efforts to complete the task (Kohli & Schneider, 2002). Many empirical studies have found a positive relationship between effort and job performance (eg, Blau, 1993; Brown & Leigh, 1996; Gardner, Dunham, Cummings, & Pierce 1989). We expect this logic to remain valid in a collaborative open innovation community. Usually, users use word to express their ideas. The longer the ideas, the more effort users make to conceive, edit and refine their ideas, th higher the possibility that companies will implement these creative ideas. Besides, the longer the idea description the more relevant and valuable the information received by business experts to determine the quality of the argu ments and decide whether to accept the idea or not (Wang, Wang, Li, Abrahams, & Fan, 2015). A similar finding can be observed in user-generated content platforms, such as Wikipedia, in which an article is considered of higher qual ity when it contains more information (Ransbotham, Kane, & Lurie, 2012). Therefore, we expect that the longer th idea, the more likely it will be implemented by companies.

Hypothesis 3 The length of the ideas is positively correlated with the likelihood of idea implementation.

Open innovation communities allow users to provide supporting evidence on their ideas in a variety of multime dia formats, such as pictures, videos or network links, in addition to text. Users' provision of information with multi ple formats reflects their efforts in the idea generation process. Different from users' efforts in the text, users efforts in supporting evidence tend to seek the support of external resources to improve the value of an idea rathe than to enhance the ideological connotation of an idea. However, individuals' attention and cognitive resources are limited so that they might fail to participate in tasks with full capacity and focus (Kahneman, 1973; Zhang, Liu, & Ho 2019). Therefore, the excess search for substantial supporting evidence may distract users' attention from the idea' creativity and functionality, which are more essential to the idea implementation likelihood.

Appropriate levels of interest, such as attention and effort, can lead to optimal performance (Gardner, 1986; Scott, 1966). Specifically, in the process of generating ideas, users make appropriate efforts to search and edit supporting evidence, improving the quality of ideas. Zhang and Bartol (2010) validated the inverted U-shaped rela tionship between creative process input and overall job performance in the context of complex tasks. Also, Li et al. (2016) analysed the inverted U-shaped relationship between supporting evidence and the likelihood of idea imple mentation from the perspective of cognitive overload. Therefore, we propose that there is an inverted U-shape relationship between supporting evidence and idea implementation and that creative ideas with appropriat supporting evidence are more likely to be implemented by companies.

Hypothesis 4 Supporting evidence contained in ideas has an inverted U-shaped relationship with the likelihood of ide implementation.

## 3.3 | Effect of feedback valence on idea implementation

The relationship between feedback valence and individual creativity performance may depend on personal or task factors. For example, Van Dijk and Kluger (2004) found that for promotion-focused people, positive feedback can better motivate them. However, for prevention-focused people, negative feedback can better motivate them. Van couver and Tischner (2004) showed that task characteristics regulate the effect of feedback valence on creative performance. Specifically, when individuals need to use cognitive resources to perform tasks, feedback valence is positively correlated with creative performance. However, when they do not need to use cognitive resources to per form tasks, positive feedback will not improve performance, as opposed to negative feedback. Van Dijk and Kluger (2011) further validated the moderating effect of task type on the relationship between positive feedback, motiva tion, and performance. Specifically, for tasks that require vigilance and compliance, negative feedback motivates an improves performance better than positive feedback. However, for tasks that require enthusiasm and creativity, pos itive feedback motivates and improves performance better than negative feedback.

Open innovation community users participate in new product creation tasks offered by companies anonymously voluntarily, and without pay. Due to the lack of long-term or close relationships or interests among community mem bers, positive feedback from community members may have a minimal stimulating effect on the creative motivation of users. Moreover, positive feedback from community members is limited to ‘support it slightly’ or ‘good’. This lac of useful information sharing cannot provide constructive knowledge support for users to engage in follow-up crea tive tasks. Besides, users who receive positive feedback may make less effort the next time they participate in a task reducing individual creativity performance. Mattern, Child, Vanhorn, and Gronewold (2013) found that individual who received positive feedback scored an average of 5 points less in subsequent creative work. In summary, we propose that receiving more positive feedback reduces users' likelihood that companies will implement their following ideas.

Hypothesis 5 Positive feedback from community members is negatively correlated with the likelihood of idea implementation.

Previous studies showed that negative feedback has a positive effect on individual creativity performance. Van Dyne, Jehn, and Cummings (2002) found that a tense or disharmonious relationship between employees and col leagues does not harm individual creativity performance. Somech, Desivilya, and Lidogoster (2009) and Troyer an Youngreen (2009) argued that intra-team conflicts could help employees generate innovative solutions and improve individual creativity performance if employees can effectively manage conflict. Mattern et al. (2013) found that nega tive feedback has a positive effect on the subsequent creativity performance of individuals. The possible reasons ar that individuals receiving negative feedback may be motivated to work harder on their next task to prove that the can achieve the same level of creativity performance as others

In the virtual open innovation community, we expect the positive effect of negative feedback to be more promi nent than in face-to-face situations. Virtual communities are loosely and anonymously organized, providing a plat form for members to speak freely. Negative feedback is often accompanied by contradictory opinions or useful information. On the one hand, the intense discussion triggered by negative feedback is likely to stimulate the intrin sic motivation of users and maintain their enthusiasm to participate in idea tasks. On the other hand, useful informa tion brought by negative feedback provides users with knowledge support to engage in subsequent idea tasks. In addition, negative feedback can motivate users to make more effort in later idea tasks than other members to prov their ability to come up with creative ideas. Therefore, we suggest that negative feedback increases users' likelihood that companies will implement their subsequent ideas

Hypothesis 6 Negative feedback from community members is positively correlated with the likelihood of idea implementation.

## 5.2 | Estimation approach

It is a cumulative distribution function of a standard logistic random variable. Logistic regression has been used to explain choice decisions of individuals or companies in various contexts (McFadden, Train, Tye, 1977). ## 6 | DISCUSSION

## 6.1 | Key findings

Our study systematically examines the determinants of idea implementation from the perspective of idea generators in collaborative crowdsourcing communities for open innovation. Based on research on individual creativity, w develop a research model to explain the effect of users' previous experience, idea presentation characteristics and feedback valence on the likelihood of idea implementation

First, we find that there is an inverted U-shaped relationship between previous success experience and the likeli hood of idea implementation. Ideas implemented by companies as an example in the initial period play a guiding rol in the subsequent generation of ideas by users. However, with the accumulation of these examples, users tend to adopt these familiar sample features unconsciously. Cognitive fixation limits the ability of users to propose innova tive and commercially valuable ideas, significantly reducing the likelihood that companies will implement their ideas Unfortunately, the results indicate that the negative relationship between past failure and the likelihood of ide implementation is not significant. One possible reason is that ideas not implemented by companies do not leave a deep impression on users. Thus, the defective characteristics of these ideas do not harm users' subsequent idea generation.

Second, the results indicate that idea length positively affects the likelihood of idea implementation. We believe that the increased length of ideas demonstrates that users have made more significant efforts to conceive, edit and refine these ideas, increasing the likelihood that companies will implement these ideas. We also find an inverted U shaped relationship between supporting evidence and the likelihood of idea implementation. Users' attention and cognitive resources are limited. Thus, if they make too much effort to search for and edit supporting evidence, they may limit their creative performance. We believe that the best quality of ideas can be achieved by devoting appropri ate attention and effort to search for and edit supporting evidence to increase the likelihood of these ideas bein implemented by companies.

Finally, the results indicate that positive feedback has a negative effect on the likelihood of idea implementation and negative feedback has a positive impact. In the virtual community, positive feedback from community members has a limited stimulating effect on users' creative motivation and may also relax them when participating in the next task. Therefore, positive feedback has a negative impact on users' innovative performance. Conversely, the intens discussion triggered by negative feedback not only helps stimulate intrinsic motivation but also provides knowledg support for subsequent user participation in creative tasks. In addition, negative feedback can encourage users to make more effort to prove their ability to come up with more creative ideas.

## 6.2 | Theoretical implications

This study has the following implications for advancing the literature on crowdsourcing and open innovation communities. First, our study provides a new theoretical explanation of the relationship between users' past success/failur and idea implementation. Previous research has focused on the effect of users' past successful experience on th likelihood of idea implementation (Bayus, 2013; Hoornaert et al., 2017; Ma et al., 2019), ignoring the effect of failur experience – that is, the number of non-implemented ideas submitted by users. Our study incorporates failure expe rience and successful experience in the research model and shows that failure experience negatively affects ide implementation. Besides, previous findings on the effect of users' past successful experience on the likelihood of idea implementation are inconclusive. Bayus (2013) found that past success negatively affects the likelihood of ide implementation using Dell's IdeaStorm data. However, Hoornaert et al. (2017) and Ma et al. (2019) found that previ ous successful experience (number of ideas implemented in the past and previous adoption experience) positivel influences the likelihood of idea implementation. These inconclusive findings indicate that the relationship betwee past success and the likelihood of idea implementation may not be linear. To reconcile these conflicting results, ou research shows that the relationship between success experience and idea implementation is a non-linear inverte U-shaped relationship. That is, the likelihood of idea implementation increases first, then decreases as users' past successful experience increases.

Second, our study is the first to explore the effect of idea presentation characteristics on idea implementation from the perspective of idea generators, providing a new theoretical perspective on their relationship. Using the the ories of message persuasion and cognitive overload, Li et al. (2016) developed a research model to explain the rela tionship between idea presentation characteristics and the likelihood of idea implementation from the perspective of idea recipients. Our research analyses the effect of idea presentation characteristics on the likelihood of idea imple mentation from the perspective of idea generators. We introduce the concept of process engagement and demon strate that the length of idea and supporting evidence show the efforts made by users to identify problems, searc for information, edit content and ultimately generate ideas.

Third, we introduce feedback valence as a contextual factor to explore the effects of positive and negative feed back on the likelihood of idea implementation, highlighting the negative impact of positive feedback and the positive effect of negative feedback. Previous studies have mainly discussed the implementation of ideas from two aspects user experience (eg, Bayus, 2013; Hoornaert et al., 2017; Ma et al., 2019) and idea characteristics (eg, Di Gangi & Wasko, 2009; Li et al., 2016; Schemmann et al., 2016), ignoring the effect of contextual factors. Our study compensates for the lack of consideration of contextual factors in previous studies by introducing feedback valence from th literature on individual creativity and explicitly analysing the different effects of positive feedback and negative feed back on idea implementation.

## 6.3 | Practical implications

The results of this study have implications for practitioners. Specifically, our findings offer several suggestions that may help business experts improve idea generation and implementation among users

First, experts should identify users who can potentially generate valuable ideas for new product development We find that there is an inverted U-shaped relationship between past success and the likelihood of idea implementa tion. This finding suggests that experts should take different measures to fully discover the creative and knowledg advantages of users who are in various phases of past successful experience. Specifically, experts should pa particular attention to users whose past success is increasing and take measures to exploit their potential for innova tion fully. For instance, firms could allow these users to access professional materials related to products, invite them to participate in internal R&D activities on ideas, or offer them the privilege of beta testing the latest version of thei products. Although the results indicate that the performance of users with successful experience graduall decreases, these users have become professionals in product evaluation because of their rich product experience and knowledge accumulated from past success. Experts should encourage them to play a role in bringing expertise answering questions and training others to help other users improve their ideas.

Second, it is advantageous for business experts to let users know that longer ideas are more likely to be selected and implemented and that adding multimedia supporting evidence to the description of an idea increases its likelihood of implementation to a certain extent. Thus, firms' internal experts should encourage users to use longer text associated with an average level of multimedia to describe their ideas in collaborative crowdsourcing communities Companies can introduce features, such as idea templates, word size limit and size limit for the number of pictures videos and links. Firms' internal experts can also assign reputation points to users who include longer text and an appropriate amount of multimedia in their ideas.

Third, the research results show that experts should adopt corresponding feedback strategies to improve the quality of users' ideas. They should advocate for the provision of different and diverging opinions among users rathe than deliberately preventing negative comments. In other words, to improve the quality of users' ideas, experts should encourage them to provide negative feedback to each other and to discuss and share product information o professional knowledge. In addition, community users should be aware that offering different and diverging opinions can stimulate internal innovation vitality. This finding will help users actively express different views or constructiv opinions and reduce meaningless support attitudes

## 6.4 | Limitations and future work

The research design of our study strictly follows the relevant specifications to explore the influence of users' previ ous experience, idea presentation characteristics and feedback valence on idea implementation. However, there ar some limitations. First, our study adopts idea length and supporting evidence to represent user engagement Although longer ideas and richer supporting evidence objectively reflect users' creative efforts in the idea generation process, the content of the ideas can present the outcome of user engagement. Future studies should furthe explore the content characteristics of ideas and examine their effect on the likelihood of idea implementation. Sec ond, the robustness tests show that the estimates of the impact of positive and negative feedback on idea implemen tation are not robust. The possible reason is that recent feedback has more effect on user creativity than long-term feedback. Future studies should validate the gradual downward trend of the feedback effect over time. Third, th MIUI new product development is an open innovation community developed by Xiaomi for the MIUI operating sys tem of Xiaomi phones, a specific subset of the IT industry. Companies and users from other sectors and countries may have different behavioural characteristics regarding participation in crowdsourcing tasks. Future research shoul test the generalizability of our findings in other industries, such as apparel, catering or household appliances

## 7 | CONCLUSIONS

Our study explains and predicts idea implementation in collaborative crowdsourcing communities for open innova tion. Based on research on individual creativity, we develop a conceptual model that integrates users' previous expe rience, idea presentation characteristics and feedback valence to explain the likelihood of idea implementation. Ou study shows that users with appropriate success experience are more likely to propose ideas implemented by com panies. Longer ideas with appropriate supporting evidence are also more often implemented by companies. We also demonstrate the negative effect of positive feedback and the positive impact of negative feedback on idea imple mentation. Overall, the results of our research contribute to the literature on crowdsourcing and open innovation fo new product development and provide actionable information for firms' open innovation practice in collaborative crowdsourcing communities.
