# Testing the accuracy of the IT stereotype: Profiling IT managers’ personality and behavioural characteristics

Lesley Willcoxson <sup>a,\*</sup>, Robina Chatham

<sup>a</sup> Faculty of Business, University of the Sunshine Coast, Maroochydore, Queensland 4558, Australia <sup>b</sup> School of Management, Cranfield University, Bedfordshire, United Kingdom

Received 17 July 2003; received in revised form 4 February 2004; accepted 25 April 2004 Available online 24 July 2006

## Abstract

Recent literature has identified many communication and leadership inadequacies amongst senior IT managers and consequent breakdowns in the IT/business relationship. This paper goes beyond the largely anecdotally based evidence presented to date; it uses psychometric testing to investigate the personal and behavioural characteristics of IT managers and compare these with reported characteristics of business managers. The results indicate significant differences related to issues of control and consequent leadership behaviour and task/relationship orientation. It is speculated that IT managers with the personality characteristics identified in this study may tend to position IT in a service rather than a strategic role and experience greater difficulties in negotiating the IT/business relationship. ## 1. Introduction

Despite the acknowledged competitive potential of IT, research indicates that in practice the IT/IS function still often acts as a support role in business rather than as a leader and strategic partner [27]. Why is this so? Attempts to answer this question have generally looked for structural, strategic and process explanations. This paper, in contrast, examines the question from the human perspective, investigating the psychological characteristics of the people working at management level within the IT profession.

This paper drew upon psychological profiles obtained for several hundred IT managers using the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI) and the Fundamental Interpersonal Relationship Orientation-Behaviour questionnaire (FIRO-B). On the basis of a comparison between these profiles and those obtained for general managers, we considered the potential impact that the personal and behavioural characteristics of IT managers may have upon their effectiveness as leaders, relationship-builders, decision-makers and strategic partners to business.

## 2. Personal characteristics of IT managers: evidence from the literature

There is now a significant body of literature dealing with issues arising in the IT/business relationship.

While some of this literature focuses on the organisational capabilities required to take full competitive advantage of IT [9,20], a significant amount also focuses on the characteristics needed by individuals to manage the IT/business interface. In the literature focusing on individual characteristics, however, many of the insights arise from accounts of the interactions of IT managers and business managers, and these insights often reflect untested assumptions about the inherent nature of IT managers, i.e. those who have leadership responsibilities for IT projects or for the IT function within an organisation. There is little empirical evidence to explain, support, or challenge the frequently negative perceptions or stereotypes held of IT managers.

Conversely, there is a bountiful supply of anecdotes and stories abound of IT professionals as poor communicators trapped in their own world. For example, Sellers [24] observed that ‘IT professionals are woefully bad at . . . working the political agenda and selling the business benefits in the language of their customers—the business’, while Bashein and Markus [1] reported a manager’s comment that ‘IT specialists would prescribe a solution without first listening to the problem’. This sort of approach to communication may go some way to explaining the observation that IT professionals are ‘stereotyped as ‘‘techies’’ . . . have stayed well within their functional bunkers’ [25] and the claim that IT has historically been inwardly focussed [13]. Similarly, Miller [17] observes that ‘IT people do not help themselves. Yes it’s unkind and unhelpful to call them anoraks or techies, but when they wear the former and act the latter at work, the perception becomes reality’.

This isolation and disapprobation often extends even to the highest levels. The perception of Chief information officers (CIOs) as ‘geeks and not business professionals may arise from their self-reported tendency to spend about 40% of their time in general meetings and sitting at their desks, yet only about 3% of their time dealing with company customers [7]. ‘The biggest battle is with themselves. Many [CIOs] are still clinging to a paternalistic view of information systems users, seeing them as something to be controlled and manipulated rather than served’ argues Carlyle. According to Keen [14], IT managers ‘can’t listen—only lecture . . . they talk at – not to – business managers’, and Milne [18] comments that:

Top programmers thrive on solving problems. They are logical, frequently prefer working alone and can be better at interacting with a computer interface than with other people. Talented managers, however, are usually described by phrases such as ‘good at communicating’ and ‘able to empathise’.

It is, however, often ‘top’ programmers who become future IT managers [19].

The validity of these types of observations is often implied even in more academic writing dealing with the characteristics or development needs of the IT/IS manager. Amongst the few examples of systematic research into the personal characteristics or attitudes of CIOs, Enns et al. [8] report mixed results from interview and survey data seeking to determine if a strong technical background is associated with the use of less effective influence behaviours, while Burn and Szeto [5] find, in an industry specific study, that senior IT managers construe their role as a service role, acting as catalysts for the strategic vision of business managers. In discussion of individual development needs, Todd et al. [26] highlighted the need for interpersonal and communications skills training, thus implicitly suggesting that these are lacking in IT managers, while Kakabadse and Korac-Kakabadse’s [12] summary of recent relevant literature leads them to emphasise the need for diplomacy, interpersonal skills, and business skills. They also note that IT professionals need to pay attention to establishing relationships outside of IT/IS. Patching and Chatham found, however, that the desire or drive to network does not appear to be particularly high on the agenda of senior IT managers, and many writers comment on the existence of a IT/business ‘culture gap’ and detail strategies that may be used by IT managers to bridge this gap [1,2,6,9,11,16]. Despite such comments, as Zmud et al. [28] suggested in 1987, empirical research into the relationships between IT and business professionals generally continues to be overlooked, and there is even less research-based analysis as to whether the gap is really, or even in part, attributable to a lack of intrapersonal and interpersonal skills in IT managers [8].

## 3. Testing for psychological characteristics in IT managers

## 3.1. Selection of instruments

For the investigation of complex phenomena such as personality and behavioural characteristics, there is a long tradition of quantitative research using psychometric testing. Such testing requires subjects to choose from a limited set of alternatives the phrase or word that best represents his/her usual behaviour or preference in a given context, and responses are categorised to yield a profile based on an underlying theory such as Jungian psychology. As a result of extensive testing involving different occupational groupings, for many psychometric instruments there is a wealth of psychological profile data enabling the type of inter-group comparison and external validation that is not possible using qualitative research methods.

For those seeking to investigate personality attributes and behaviours, many instruments are available. Most of these require people to report on specific traits they possess (such as creativity, authoritarianism, conservatism, emotional stability) and their responses are measured against a norm. Other instruments focus less on the traits that are developed over time and more on innate psychological predispositions. Of these, the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI) [3] is one of the most widely used in the world [21]; it is this instrument that we chosen for our study, because of its capacity to measure psychological characteristics relevant to the workplace.

Instruments for measuring actual behaviours in the workplace are also numerous but many only seek to examine individual behaviours in isolation. In contrast, the Fundamental Interpersonal Relationship Orientation-Behaviour (FIRO-B) [10] seeks to encapsulate individual behaviours as manifested in interactions with colleagues in the workplace. Its capacity to tap into emotional needs and responses made it uniquely suitable for our purposes, complementing the more cognitive orientation of the MBTI.

## 3.2. Description of instrument: MBTI

The Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI) is derived from Jungian concepts of personality type. It consists of an 88 item questionnaire. Each item requires a forced choice response to one of two statements designed to test for general psychological predispositions in ways of responding to life and work. As indicated in Table 1, it assesses these predispositions on four bi-polar dimensions.

When the questionnaire is scored, results in each of the four dimensions combine to produce a profile that represents one of sixteen different possibilities (e.g. ISTJ, ENFP, INTP). Of the four dimensions, two – sensing/intuition and thinking/feeling – are assumed, in the theory underpinning the instrument, to be more innate and less the result of environmental conditioning and thus are assumed to describe cognitive style more effectively. To encapsulate results relating to cognitive style, questionnaire responses can therefore be further analysed in terms of the extent to which thinking or feeling is paired with sensing or intuition. When the MBTI is used in workplace settings, the resultant cognitive styles are often used to indicate the ways in which people are likely to attempt to influence others or to interact in teams. Table 2 outlines the key characteristics of each of the four cognitive styles.

## 3.3. Description of instrument: FIRO-B

The FIRO-B is a 54 item questionnaire that requires respondents to indicate (on a five point scale: from never to usually or from nobody to most people) the extent to which a particular described behaviour reflects their needs or behaviour. It is based on the prior work of Jung relating to inclusion, Freud relating to affection, and Adler relating to power. The questionnaire asks each base question several times with each subsequent re-working of the base question using stronger language. The instrument assesses work-related behaviours and emotional needs in three areas: inclusion, control, and affection. Results are reported numerically with a score between 0 and 9 placed in each of six boxes on a three by two matrix. A high score indicates that a particular behaviour or emotional need is manifested often, irrespective of the particular work colleagues involved; a low score indicates that a particular emotional need or behaviour is not commonly manifested, but when it is, it occurs only in interaction with specific work colleagues. Motivation for completing the instruments lay in the knowledge that personal results were to be made available to each IT manager and used for development purposes during the programme they were undertaking.

The sample size of 489 for MBTI and 130 for FIRO-B comprised mostly U.K. based and born IT managers, although a small proportion (about 5%) were based or born overseas. Although the sample was not randomly selected, participants were drawn from large, private sector organisations across a wide range of industries and from government departments. They thus represented a good cross-section of industry and organisation type as well as a range of IT management responsibilities.

The results from the data collected were compared with data published in the Myers-Briggs MBTI manual [4] under the category ‘Managers and Administrators’ and with data published in the FIRO-B manual [15]. The data in the Myers-Briggs manual was obtained through testing of a U.S.A. based sample of 7463 managers and administrators; the data in the FIRO-B manual was obtained through testing of a U.K. based sample of 1165 managers. Although the MBTI comparative sample is of a different nationality to those from which the data at Cranfield School of Management was collected, the MBTI manual indicates that distribution of types is similar throughout the world. It might therefore be expected that distribution of types in similar occupational groupings would not be unduly influenced by nationality, especially in the case where the two national groupings share a similar cultural heritage.

Independent of the validity and reliability data presented in the FIRO-B and MBTI manuals, some evidence of face validity for the two instruments was obtained from this sample. Those who had completed the FIRO-B questionnaires were in each case given individual feedback and, without exception, during discussion of the results in relation to their workplace behaviour confirmed their acceptance of the results obtained from the scoring of the instrument. In the case of the MBTI, after respondents had completed the questionnaire and subsequently received an explanation of the categories but before receiving their results, they were asked to guess their own typing. In nearly 95% of cases respondents’ guesses reflected the results obtained through the instrument. Further discussion with the remainder of the respondents generally led to acceptance of their reported type, but in a few cases (where borderline scores indicated no clear psychological predispositions), results included in this study were those that reflected the respondents’ self-assessment of type. Thus, in all cases, the results reported here have been personally validated through discussion with the IT managers who comprise the sample.

## 5. Psychological characteristics of IT managers—results

Table 5 presents the results obtained on the MBTI for the current U.K. sample of IT managers.

As can be seen, the highest percentage of IT managers reported psychological predispositions associated with ISTJ (introversion, sensing, thinking and judging). The predispositions next most commonly reported were associated with ESTJ (extraversion, sensing, thinking and judging), ENTJ (extraversion, intuition, thinking and judging), ENTP (extraversion, intuition, thinking and perceiving), INTJ (introversion, intuition, thinking and judging) and INTP (introversion, intuition, thinking and perceiving). Together, these six (of 16) categories represented the psychological predispositions of 71.1% of IT managers who comprised the current sample.

When the results are examined with reference to cognitive style (or the extent to which thinking or feeling is paired with sensing or intuition), as Table 6 indicates, many IT managers demonstrated a preference for the logical analysis and detached objectivity characteristic of the thinking style.

In fact, 78.5% of the U.K. IT manager sample reported a preference for decision-making based on logic and objectivity (thinking) rather than on a personal value base and a desire to demonstrate empathy and maintain harmony (feeling). With respect to information acquisition characteristics, the sample demonstrated some tendency to focus on the big picture and intuition (57.1%), but there is also a strong tendency to want to gather detailed, concrete information (42.9%).

When the U.K. results obtained for IT managers were compared with those reported in the MBTI manual for U.S.A. managers and administrators, a slightly different picture emerged (Table 7).

Overall, these data indicate a minor difference in the information acquisition preferences of IT and generalist managers, but a 17% difference in the numbers of generalist and IT managers who express a predisposition towards focussing on issues such as personal values and empathy rather than logic and objectivity when making decisions. The results obtained for the two groups of managers are not so different as to offer a ready ‘difference in cognitive style’ explanation for the existence of an IT stereotype or the IT/business relationship difficulties frequently reported in the literature.

The data obtained for IT and generalist managers through administration of the FIRO-B was therefore examined in an attempt to obtain information about the extent to which differences in behaviour and emotional needs might underpin stereotyping or contribute to IT/ business relationship difficulties.

Persons with such scores are described as not dependent but having some doubts about their ability, preferring to share responsibility rather than have sole responsibility for an area, and fearing criticism and failure but finding reassurance and safety in sharing responsibility. According to Ryan, such persons will feel comfortable when working within relatively clear guidelines and given instruction.

Both expressed and wanted inclusion scores (ei = 5.4, wi = 4.2) obtained for the IT manager sample indicated a moderate level of social interaction and also social flexibility; i.e. such persons tend to be equally comfortable both alone and in large groups and show little concern over rejection or acceptance by others.

The expressed and wanted affection scores (ea = 3.1, wa = 4.8) are suggestive of persons who are defensive and want affection but are cautious about initiating close, personal relationships. This defensive stance may be problematic; it may communicate to others an unwillingness to become emotionally involved and thus, in turn, inhibit others from expressing the affection needed. Thus, unless others are particularly perceptive, the need for affection may remain frustrated.

## 6. Discussion

The marked contrast found in the respective leadership styles and wanted control scores for IT and general managers suggested a significantly different behavioural approach to their managerial role. The FIRO-B data support anecdotally based observations that, while IT managers tend to exhibit a task orientation, general managers are more likely to demonstrate a people orientation. The greater predisposition of IT managers towards the thinking style (logic and objectivity) over the feeling style (empathy and harmony), found through MBTI testing, lends further support to this finding. On the basis of the FIRO-B data relating to leadership, with some corroboration from the MBTI data, it seemed that at least some IT managers were less inclined to focus on relationship building than their generalist counterparts.

This observation is especially relevant to those who fit the IT manager profile typical of the majority of this sample. As a consequence of this greater focus on task than on relationship they may be less effective in a critical aspect of the managerial or leadership role, and this focus on task at the expense of relationship may give some explanation for reported difficulties in negotiating the IT/business relationship.

Of considerable salience also when making comparisons between the two groups is the greater need for wanted control found in the sample of IT managers when compared to those for general managers (4.9 for the senior IT managers versus 2.4 for the generalists). Although specific individuals may not conform to the patterns, the broad implication of this research is that senior IT managers may be perceived by their peers as people who are good at following and implementing orders but uncomfortable when placed in a position of authority requiring decision making in the face of ambiguity or uncertainty. The data available, related to the FIRO-B interpretive categories, suggest they may be unlikely to challenge accepted wisdom or question those to whom they report about their thinking or the basis of their decision-making.

This finding implicitly contrasted with the findings of Enns et al., whose survey of influence behaviours in CIOs suggested that senior IT managers had at their disposal as wide a range of influence behaviours as generalist managers. It implicitly corresponded, however, with Enns et al.’s interview findings, which indicated that some CIOs (those with a strong technical background) were less influential and had a more limited range of influence behaviours than those with a less technical background. Perceptions of influence and success were not tested in our study, but it should be noted that while surveys are effective instruments with which to examine the range and frequency of potential behaviours they are too blunt an instrument with which to dissect the contextual effectiveness of an individual’s behaviour. That is, the results which give cause for concern in this study – those that indicate a potentially inappropriate focus on task at the expense of relationship or an unwillingness to take control or deal with ambiguity in decision-making – may derive largely from the responses of those IT managers who, in the eyes of their business manager peers, exert less influence and are less successful at negotiating the IT/business relationship.

In summary, comparison of the data obtained for IT managers and for general managers indicated several similarities in cognitive style, behaviour, and emotional needs, yet some highly significant differences related to issues of control and consequent leadership behaviour and task/people orientation. Analysis of the psychological and behavioural tests indicated that a large part of the sample consisted of people whose behavioural and personality predispositions lead them to be most comfortable in a task-focussed service role rather than a strategic one.

## 7. Conclusions

Our study sought to investigate the extent to which the stereotypes of IT managers might be based on cognitive, emotional, or behavioural differences that distinguish them from their general manager colleagues. It was found that although the cognitive style profile of senior IT managers differed only slightly from that of general managers when both groups were tested using the MBTI, their emotional and behavioural characteristics differed significantly with respect to leadership and control, as measured by the FIRO-B instrument. This result indicated a greater task than people orientation and greater willingness to follow directions and provide service than to deal with the ambiguity and uncertainty inherent in a strategic decision-making role.

While such generalisations clearly do not apply to all IT managers, our research tested some of the assumptions previously advanced in the literature on the basis of largely anecdotal evidence, and it offers a measure of support for these. In drawing a profile of the personality and behavioural characteristics of IT managers, it offers insights for IT managers wishing to reflect on their own behaviour or on negotiation of the IT/business relationship. It also indicates ways in which questions relating to IT manager characteristics and consequent capabilities might be studied more systematically.
