# IN-CONSUMPTION INFORMATION CUES AND ONLINE VIDEO CONSUMPTION<sup>1</sup>

Jaeung Sim School of Business, University of Connecticut Stamford, CT, U.S.A. {jaeung.sim@uconn.edu}

Kyungmin Choi Roundin, Seoul, REPUBLIC OF KOREA {kyungminchoi92@gmail.com}

Sang Pil Han W. P. Carey School of Business, Arizona State University Tempe, AZ, U.S.A. {shan73@asu.edu}

Daegon Cho College of Business, Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology Seoul, REPUBLIC OF KOREA {daegon.cho@kaist.ac.kr}

Online video platforms such as YouTube feature visual cues on progress bars to spotlight standout segments of videos. These cues, designed to steer users toward intriguing content moments, raise questions about their overarching impact on video consumption patterns. In this study, we delve into inconsumption information cues (ICICs), indicators that depict fluctuating video quality in real time. Drawing from a natural experiment on a live streaming platform, we evaluate the effects of engagement graphs. These are visual timelines that emphasize moments favored by prior viewers in video-on-demand (VOD) content, which represent full replays of live stream sessions. Notably, these graphs are only accessible to iOS users, leaving Android users without access. Our results show that ICICs enhance the viewership of VODs and live streams. Moreover, viewers tend to spend more time immersed in live broadcasts, suggesting heightened content appeal. Yet, these engagement graphs do not prompt users to donate more virtual gifts, a vital income stream for streamers. Even with the introduction of ICICs, while there is a rise in video production, the inherent structure of live streams remained largely unchanged. We conclude by discussing the academic and managerial implications of these findings.

Keywords: In-consumption information cues, engagement graphs, video on demand, live streaming, natural experiment

## Introduction

Online video platforms have implemented various measures to facilitate viewers’ consumption of their videos. First, online video platforms have adopted pre-consumption information artifacts—such as online reviews, popularity charts, previews, and collaborative filtering. These features help users discover videos that align with their preferences and offer higher-quality content (Aguiar & Waldfogel, 2021; Choi et al., 2019; Lee & Hosanagar, 2019). Second, some video platforms have added in-consumption information cues (ICICs) that inform users of time-varying quality changes in a video during consumption. As online videos are often lengthy, YouTube and Facebook have incorporated “Most replayed” and “engagement graphs,” respectively, on recorded videos, such as video on demand (VOD). These visual representations showcase engagement levels of previous viewers at different time points in the video, to “help people easily discover the parts of the video they might find most interesting” (Constine, 2016).

Despite the increasing popularity of ICICs in the online video market, their impact on consumer and supplier behaviors has not been examined in prior studies. In fact, there are several inherent differences between ICICs and pre-consumption information artifacts, which poses challenges in applying existing theories to analyze this new phenomenon. For example, ICICs inform consumers about moment-to-moment quality variations within a video after the video has been selected and while it is being viewed. Notably, ICICs do not influence pre-consumption beliefs about video quality as a whole, hence the expected utility of viewing a video. Therefore, it is not straightforward to determine whether and how ICICs affect online video demand and whether online video platforms will derive benefits from implementing them.

Against this backdrop, we first develop a simple, two-period economic model of viewers’ time allocation for consuming a live streamed video and a video on demand—a complete replay of a live stream in this case—to provide insights into how ICICs change viewership on online video platforms. We postulate that viewers allocate their time to VOD, live streaming, and outside options in each period. In the first period, viewers can only rely on pre-consumption quality signals, such as measures of a streamer’s popularity, but cannot determine the streamer’s fit to their tastes. In the second period, viewers will discover the fit only if they consumed either VODs or live streams of that streamer in the first period. Our results indicate that by shifting the marginal utility of video viewing to an earlier consumption period, ICICs facilitate viewers’ sampling on the VOD channel, especially among streamers with low pre-consumption quality (i.e., low star ratings). In addition, such sampling behavior leads to information spillovers on the live streaming channel, where viewers discover that a streamer’s content aligns with their taste despite the streamer’s low pre-consumption quality level (i.e., the viewer liked a lot certain most-replayed segments of a video that has low star ratings).

To empirically validate these insights derived from our analytical model, we leverage a natural experiment wherein an online live streaming platform adopted ICIC graphs—a visualized timeline of moments favored by prior viewers in VOD content appearing on the progress bar—which was available to iOS users but not Android users. To mitigate potential endogeneity issues arising from the viewer’s video selection and unobserved time-specific effects, we used a difference-in-differences approach combined with coarsened exact matching. Our results reveal that, after the introduction of ICICs, viewers watched more VODs and live streamed videos and spent more time consuming live streaming content, suggesting that the overall video demand increased across both VOD and live channels. However, our investigation yielded no substantiating evidence to support the notion that ICIC graphs positively impacted viewers’ propensity to contribute more virtual gifts, which are a significant revenue source for streamers. Furthermore, our analysis shows that streamers responded to the introduction of ICICs by producing a greater volume of video content, yet the overall structure of live streams remained largely unaltered.

We make several significant contributions to the literature on information design, search costs, and product demand. First, we propose a conceptual framework for ICICs, a topic that has received little attention in the existing literature. Unlike preconsumption information cues, ICICs provide moment-tomoment quality variations within videos as they are being consumed. We develop an economic model that offers a plausible explanation of how ICICs can facilitate user sampling behaviors for videos and promote information spillovers across streamers in their video consumption. Second, we provide empirical evidence supporting the aforementioned economic impact of ICICs. Our results demonstrate that online video platforms can effectively utilize the ICIC function to enhance video consumption without altering customers’ expectations of video quality or the quality of videos themselves. Given the experiential and timeintensive nature of video viewing, providing ICICs can assist consumers in discovering more relevant video content. However, we found no evidence to suggest that increased consumption resulting from ICICs translates into an increase in virtual gifts to streamers. This implies that at least during our study period, ICICs may not substantially contribute to the platform’s revenue growth. Therefore, the platform should carefully distinguish between strategies that enhance user engagement and those that generate revenues.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. The next section reviews the related literature and discusses the characteristics of ICICs. Then, we present our analytical model, followed by a description of our data source and natural experimental setting. Next, we present our econometric model and results. Finally, we discuss the implications of our key findings.

## Related Literature

## Information and Platform Design

Information asymmetry between the demand and supply sides poses a significant challenge for online platforms seeking to contribute to market efficiency (Brynjolfsson et al., 2011; Guo et al., 2018; Pavlou & Gefen, 2004). From the demand standpoint, consumers perceive various types of uncertainties regarding online transactions—for instance, it is uncertain whether sellers are trustworthy, how satisfying products will be, and whether better options are available (Brynjolfsson et al., 2011; Pavlou & Gefen, 2004). On the other hand, suppliers may also have concerns about how trustworthy and valuable consumers are (Guo et al., 2018).

In this vein, online platforms have adopted various information artifacts to mitigate the harms of uncertainty. An information artifact is defined as “an instantiation of information, where the instantiation occurs through a human act either directly or indirectly” in order to process data, reduce entropy, form meaning, or achieve viability (Lee et al., 2015). In online platforms, various actions and transactions enacted by consumers and suppliers are instantiated as information artifacts and provided to users to help reduce uncertainties and aid efficient choices.

Our research closely aligns with the literature on demand-side information artifacts. Previous studies have demonstrated that platforms can enhance seller trustworthiness and boost product sales through feedback mechanisms such as user ratings and reviews (Huang et al., 2009; Pavlou & Gefen, 2004; Zhu & Zhang, 2010). These studies have also suggested that platforms can significantly diminish quality uncertainty and enhance product demand by offering popularity information based on prior consumer choices (Aguiar & Waldfogel, 2021; Carare, 2012; Godinho de Matos et al., 2016; Tucker & Zhang, 2011). Regarding incomplete knowledge about product availability, several information artifacts—such as search tools, collaborative filtering, and bestseller lists—have been shown to aid consumers in discovering unknown products (Brynjolfsson et al., 2011; Lee & Hosanagar, 2019). Numerous studies have found that these information artifacts are particularly influential for experience goods, where some attributes remain unknown until consumption (Hong & Pavlou, 2014; Huang et al., 2009; Mudambi & Schuff, 2010).

It is worth noting that the current body of literature has primarily predominantly focused on pre-consumption information cues, which only inform consumers about the overall quality of a product before they purchase or consume the product. In contrast, for certain types of experience goods, such as recorded videos and audio podcasts, users may often have a preference for browsing the content by skipping to specific interesting parts. In these contexts, relying solely on pre-consumption information cues is insufficient to assist consumers in effectively discovering and consuming content.

## In-Consumption Information Cues

This study introduces the term in-consumption information cues (ICICs) to refer to information cues that provide insights into quality variations across different segments within an online video during consumption. Several online video platforms have implemented ICICs as a means to assist users in effectively navigating and exploring online videos. For example, in May 2022, YouTube launched a feature called “Most Replayed” (Figure 1a), which displays the frequency of replay for different parts of the video on the progress bar (Perez, 2022). Similarly, in May 2016, Facebook Live incorporated “engagement graphs” on the progress bar of recorded videos (Figure 1b), showing the relative level of audience reaction volume during live streaming, in order to “help people easily navigate a video that was live— especially longer ones—to find the moments that drew the most engagement” (Constine, 2016). Despite this growing attention and adoption of ICICs by online video platforms, no prior work has conceptualized ICICs’ characteristics and examined their impact on online video platforms.

## Pre-Consumption vs. In-Consumption Information Cues

Pre-consumption and in-consumption information cues serve distinct purposes in the context of online videos. While preconsumption information cues aim to reduce uncertainty regarding the overall quality of product and aid in making informed product choices, ICICs strive to assist users in navigating and consuming online videos in a nonlinear manner by allowing users to skip to interesting segments. These differences stem from several key characteristics. First, ICICs are presented to users only after a consumer has chosen to consume a particular video, unlike pre-consumption information artifacts. Second, ICICs inform consumers about quality variation within a video, whereas pre-consumption cues primarily indicate variations in quality between different videos. Third, ICICs facilitate nonlinear searching behavior, allowing users to evaluate multiple segments of a video simultaneously. This approach contrasts with linear search behavior, where users evaluate options one by one in a sequential manner (Honka & Chintagunta, 2017; Zwick et al., 2003).

Table 1 summarizes these differences and their direct impacts on the expected utility of video consumption. Essentially, preconsumption information cues reveal quality differences between videos, enabling consumers to adjust their expectations of video quality and the overall expected utility before selecting a video to consume. In contrast, ICICs do not alter any pre-consumption expectations of video quality or the expected utility of consuming all segments of a video. However, they can enhance the expected marginal utility as they enable consumers to quickly and easily discover and focus on the more engaging parts within the video.

In light of this context, the impact of ICICs on consumer choices may not be immediately apparent. Does a shift in marginal utility towards more captivating parts earlier result in increased video demand? Which type of video stands to gain the most from the implementation of ICICs? In the following section, we construct a theoretical framework that integrates these characteristics and provides an economic rationale for how ICICs could alter online video consumption patterns.

## Research Model

## Institutional Background

Live streaming platforms—such as Amazon’s Twitch, YouTube, Facebook, and Twitter’s Periscope—allow content creators to live-broadcast their content and viewers to communicate with creators in real time (Cho et al., 2019; Hilvert-Bruce et al., 2018). The primary source of revenue for these platforms is commission fees charged to content creators (i.e., “streamers”) (Zhao et al., 2022). Streamers can monetize their content through various means, including video advertisements, membership fees, and virtual gift (Bland, 2017; Cho et al., 2019). Streamer memberships include access to premium content and features, while virtual gifts enable viewers to financially support their favorite streamers; virtual gifts constitute a significant portion of the streamers’ revenue and can be converted into actual currency (Cho et al., 2019).

Although live streamed videos provide unique and immersive experiences that replays of recorded live streams cannot match, viewers cannot always accommodate live schedules due to time constraints. To mitigate this concern, many live streaming platforms also run VOD channels, offering recorded replays of previously live streamed videos.

## A Simple Economic Model of Time Allocation

To elucidate how ICICs influence viewership on online video platforms, we developed an economic model for viewers’ time allocation in VOD and live streamed video consumption. We built on the economic theory of time allocation (Becker, 1965), which posits that individuals distribute their limited time across various activities in order to maximize their consumption utility. Given its comprehensive yet concise representation of consumption utility and opportunity costs, this theory has been extensively employed to explain time allocation for a range of activities, such as work, leisure, and even sleep (Aguiar & Hurst, 2007; Biddle & Hamermesh, 1990; Gronau, 1977; Juster & Stafford, 1991). For instance, Han et al. (2016) used a structural model of time allocation to analyze mobile app usage patterns.

## Second-Period Consumption

In certain scenarios, consumers can acquire knowledge about a product and consequently modify their consumption of related products, a phenomenon known as “information spillovers” (Hendricks & Sorensen, 2009; Kumar et al., 2014). In our context, if a viewer watches a streamer’s video, be it a VOD or live stream, during the first period, the viewer-streamer fit becomes apparent. This revealed fit informs the viewer’s consumption decisions in the subsequent period. If the fit is low, the viewer will not engage with the streamer’s content under any circumstances. In the case of a medium fit, the viewer’s consumption levels will remain unchanged from the first period. Consequently, we focus on the nontrivial case where the viewer identifies a high viewer-streamer fit during the initial period.

## Empirical Context

## Data Source

We partnered with a live streaming platform in Korea, hereafter referred to as LiveCo, for empirical study. According to the company report, as of 2017, LiveCo had three million monthly active viewers and tens of thousands of monthly active streamers covering a wide range of broadcast genres such as “chatting,” “gaming,” “music,” and “sports and fitness.” Viewers can join live broadcasts as a guest for free, and streamers can communicate with viewers in real time. Similar to Twitch and YouTube, viewers can give streamers virtual gifts to show their support, and the streamers and other viewers can observe these gift-giving activities. Since LiveCo provides all content for free, its revenue is derived from video advertisements and commission fees charged on virtual gifts, rather than subscription fees.

LiveCo also offers a VOD channel that provides free replays of previously live streamed content. The VOD channel caters to viewers who either missed the live program or wish to rewatch previous broadcasts. Viewers can search and consume VODs either through the platform’s VOD section, which archives all VODs, or each streamer’s page, which only displays that particular streamer’s VODs. Since LiveCo provides information on view counts, user ratings, and comments on videos, all viewers can obtain pre-consumption information cues about the overall quality of each video.

## A Quasi-Experimental Setting

LiveCo introduced an engagement graph on its VOD channel that displays viewers’ minute-by-minute engagement levels during live broadcasts. Figure 4a illustrates an example of LiveCo’s engagement graphs. The height of the graph represents the number of viewers clicking the “Jam” button during a live broadcast at oneminute intervals. The “Jam” button (an informal term for “fun” in Korean) is located at the bottom of the live streaming interface and allows viewers to express their enjoyment of a specific moment during a live broadcast (Figure 4b). Viewers can click this button once per minute and multiple times throughout a live broadcast. As depicted in Figure 4b, when a viewer clicks the button, a pop-up message displays the number of other viewers who have also clicked the button, reflecting how many viewers rated the particular moment positively. After a streamer finishes a live broadcast and uploads the recorded video as a VOD, the number of “Jam” button clicks for each minute is automatically displayed on the progress bar of the video.

The company introduced engagement graphs to its iOS mobile app—but not its Android app—on April 18, 2017. We leveraged this distinction as a quasi-experimental setting in which viewers did not have the option to adopt engagement graphs on the VOD channel. In addition, during the period from February to July 2017, no other significant marketing initiatives were implemented to differentiate the iOS and Android apps; thus, we chose this time frame for our research. Importantly, since these graphs were prominently displayed on the VOD’s progress bar immediately after tapping the video screen, we postulate that the treatment was sufficiently salient to be noticed when viewers watched VODs. This setting allowed us to use a difference-in-differences approach by comparing changes in video consumption among iOS users before and after the introduction of engagement graphs with those of Android users.

## Data Description

As engagement graphs are only accessible on iOS mobile devices, we refer to iOS app users as treatment viewers and Android app users as control viewers. For this purpose, we excluded users with other mobile operating systems and nonmobile device users. We also excluded multihoming users who accessed the platform through multiple operating systems. In addition, we focused on users who watched at least one VOD during the post-treatment period to ensure that treated viewers were exposed to engagement graphs. Control viewers also watched VODs during the posttreatment period but were not exposed to engagement graphs. This resulted in a sample of 287,536 viewers. The empirical dataset we utilized contained viewer-week-level panel data, capturing viewing behaviors of VOD, live streamed videos, and gift-giving behaviors, as well as demographic profiles from February 1st, 2017 to July 31st, 2017. ## Difference-in-Differences Model

Our primary objective was to identify the average treatment effects on the treated (ATTs) of engagement graphs on videoconsumption behaviors. Although the update of engagement graphs was automatic and targeted exclusively at iOS users, inherent differences between iOS and Android users could have led to spurious effects attributed to engagement graphs. To mitigate this concern, we estimated a difference-indifferences (DID) model, which controlled for persistent userspecific effects and common trends that may have introduced endogeneity and spurious relationships.

Equation (2) integrates viewer fixed effects $\mu _ { i }$ and week fixed effects $\tau _ { t }$ into Equation (1), allowing us to further capture potential confounders. Specifically, viewer fixed effects control for time-invariant viewer characteristics, such as the overall liking of live streaming content, and week fixed effects absorb common demand shocks on the focal platform.

The dependent variables in our study appear to exhibit skewness and include observations with 0 values, leading us to consider two functional forms for our main specifications. First, we used an inverse hyperbolic sine function, which is defined as $\operatorname { a s i n h } ( Y ) \equiv \ln \left( Y + { \sqrt { Y ^ { 2 } + 1 } } \right)$ . This function enabled us to retain observations with 0 values and addressed distortions that might have arisen from taking the logarithm after adding 1 to the variable, (Bellemare & Wichman, 2020; Burbidge et al., 1988; MacKinnon & Magee, 1990). This estimator has been widely adopted in recent economics literature (Aksoy et al., 2021; Chen, 2013; Sim et al., 2022). Second, we adopted a fixed-effects Poisson pseudo-maximum likelihood estimator (Azoulay et al., 2010; Burtch et al., 2018; Correia et al., 2020). We report the inverse hyperbolic sine functions as our main results and the Poisson estimators as a robustness check (see Appendix B2 for details). Additionally, we conducted a linear regression without transforming our dependent variables to determine whether our findings were driven by incorrect assumptions of proportional relationships between the treatment and outcomes (as detailed in Appendix B3).

## Matching Procedure

Potential imbalances between treated and control users could arise due to differences in preferences for mobile operating systems. While a user’s preference for an operating system is not expected to directly impact live stream and VOD consumption, our fixed effects accounted for any linear, additive, and time-invariant effects. Still, matching estimators bring additional benefits, such as mitigating imbalance and model dependence (Datta et al., 2018; Iacus et al., 2012). Therefore, to ensure comparability between treated and control users based on observable variables, we employed coarsened exact matching (CEM).

This approach discretizes continuous covariates at specific breakpoints and considers them as categorical variables. It then assigns each observation to a stratum with a unique combination of categories (Iacus et al., 2012). By achieving multidimensional balance, CEM can ensure a reduction in imbalance and model dependence, which is contrary to propensity score matching (King & Nielsen, 2019). Additionally, we adopted k2k matching that retained an equal number of treated and control viewers for CEM, which is equivalent to one-to-one matching in propensity score matching.

To balance observables between the treated and control groups, we applied two matching criteria. First, we considered aggregate matching, which aims to balance aggregate consumption amounts during the pre-treatment period. However, this method, while ensuring that the matched viewers had similar consumption amounts on average, could group viewers with different trajectories similarly due to the loss of dynamic information. To mitigate this concern, we also considered trend matching using flexible trend variables as matching criteria. Specifically, we divided the pre-treatment period into three-week intervals. Then, for each dependent variable, we used its consumption amounts in these intervals as our matching covariates. By doing so, we were able to ensure that matched viewers shared similar trajectories, at least for the current dependent variable. Table 5 presents the matching covariates used for aggregate matching and trend matching, respectively. We provide their balance checks in Appendix B1.

## Robustness Checks

The DID approach is grounded in the assumption of parallel trends during the pre-treatment period. For instance, the treatment group’s live stream consumption might have already been on an upward trajectory compared to the control group due to unobserved factors. If this were the case, our treatment variable could be incorrectly attributed to this preexisting effect caused by confounding factors. To assess the possibility of such pre-adoption trends, we employed a relative time (or leads-and-lags) model, which takes into account varying treatment timings across individuals (Autor, 2003; Burtch et al., 2018). The model is represented as follows:

$$
f (Y _ {i t}) = \Phi_ {i t} \cdot B _ {1} + \Phi_ {i t} \cdot \text { Treatment } _ {i} \cdot B _ {2} + \mu_ {i} + \tau_ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t},\tag{3}
$$

where $\Phi _ { i t }$ represents a vector of relative time indicator variables, $\mathtt { B } _ { 1 }$ and ${ \tt B } _ { 2 }$ are vectors of coefficients, and other variables are identically defined as in Equation (1). $B _ { 1 }$ captures the baseline trend of the control group, while $B _ { 2 }$ estimates distinct trends between the two groups. These distinct trends were our main interest in this falsification test.

Moreover, the treatment effects appear to increase gradually over time, implying that the effects of ICIC implementation are unlikely to be temporary.

Our results consistently align with alternative specification models, such as the Poisson regression model and linear regression without dependent variable transformation, instead of using the inverse hyperbolic sine function. These results are detailed in Appendices B2 and B3. Additionally, we conducted several sensitivity tests to ensure that our findings were not obtained by chance.<sup>5</sup> While there is a possibility that our results could have been influenced by specific groups—for instance, female streamers might draw more male viewers by tailoring content to male preferences—this factor is unlikely to have been the primary driver of our findings because coarsened exact matching ensures a balanced representation of demographic characteristics. To delve deeper into this influence, we adjusted our regressions to include gender/age/week fixed effects and reestimated them; the results remain consistent. Furthermore, our results remained robust even with shorter time windows or alternative matching techniques, such as propensity score matching, or when introducing different matching variables.

Lastly, we employed a triple-differences approach that incorporated the heterogeneous timing of VOD viewing among individuals into the DID specification. This method hinged on three distinctions: (1) iOS versus Android, (2) preversus post-VOD viewing, and (3) the specific moments of VOD viewing during the post-treatment period. By examining differences within each iOS-Android pairing, where both treated and control users opted to view VODs simultaneously, we circumvented the staggered DID issue. Furthermore, contrasting iOS-Android pairs based on different treatment timings enabled us to capture OS-specific time fixed effects. This directly assuages concerns about violating the common trends assumption (Antecol et al., 2018). In essence, this triple-differences model is designed to address potential treatment-specific demand shocks. For a more detailed breakdown, including specific definitions, econometric specifications, and derived results, see Appendix B4.

The results in Appendix B4 reaffirm similar insights: the estimated coefficients for VOD view counts, live stream view counts, and live stream viewing times are positive and statistically significant at the 0.001 level but are insignificant for gift-giving amounts. Furthermore, these estimates remained robust even when we incorporated viewer-specific linear time trends into our specifications.

## Additional Analysis

## Category Heterogeneity

The degree of uncertainty surrounding the viewer-streamer fit may differ across video categories, leading to heterogeneous benefits from engagement graphs. For example, the chatting category encompasses a broad range of casual and potentially unscripted topics, where streamers have different modes of delivery and viewer interaction, making the content less predictable. On the other hand, the gaming category provides more predictable content once a streamer discloses the game they are playing, alongside conventional methods for viewer interaction. Consequently, we expect that the incremental value of engagement graphs will be greater for chatting videos than for gaming or other types of videos (i.e., high ?? in our economic model).

Table 9 demonstrates, as expected, that the coefficients for the chatting category are greater than those for the gaming and other categories for both VOD and live views. We assessed the statistical significance of these differences and found that the increases in VOD and live stream views for the chatting category were significantly greater than those for the other categories, at the 0.001 level.<sup>6</sup>

## Impact on Gift-Giving Amount

We examined how ICICs impacted gift-giving behaviors, a vital revenue source for live streaming platforms. Table 10 demonstrates that the estimates for gift-giving amounts are negative and statistically significant at the 0.05 level, irrespective of the matching methods and trend controls used. However, as shown in Appendix B2, the estimates from the Poisson regression are not statistically significant. Taken together, these results, considered conservatively, suggest that increased views of VODs and live streams do not necessarily translate into higher revenues, at least within our specific empirical context.

There are various factors that could have contributed to these findings. One possibility is that viewers may not have had sufficient time to develop parasocial relationships with newly discovered streamers, which could have discouraged them from engaging in gift-giving behaviors in the short term. Furthermore, viewers are likely to explore VODs by skipping less engaging parts, which may include donation appeals. Consequently, if viewers transition from VODs to live streams, a streamer’s donation request might come across as unexpected and unpleasant.

## Estimation Results

We estimated Equation (4 )for the amount of video content and report the results in Table 11. In the first and third columns, we observe that streamers with higher shares of iOS viewers were more likely to increase the number of live and recorded videos following the introduction of ICICs. The shares of iOS viewers are positively, albeit marginally, related to video length for both live streams (p = 0.086) and VODs (p = 0.092).

We further explore the heterogeneity based on the prior numbers of live videos (see the difference-in-difference-indifferences [DDD] estimates in Table C2). Remarkably, only streamers with a higher output of live streams showed significant increases in their video supply. Considering that the viewer-streamer fit needs to be higher than expected to increase live stream consumption (Proposition 2), this result may imply that relatively experienced or established streamers can more effectively showcase their quality during VOD consumption than inexperienced streamers.

Among the strategic responses of streamers to the introduction of ICICs, streamers strive to learn from engagement graphs and adjust their live broadcasts to include content and themes that previous viewers have enjoyed. Specifically, streamers may integrate popular themes into their live broadcasts, such that Jam clicks are more likely to be concentrated on these newly incorporated popular moments. Furthermore, streamers may relocate Jam moments to a specific segment of a live stream in order to present viewers with climactic moments. We assessed these possibilities by estimating Equation (4) for several new measures, such as the standard deviation and click shares of the most popular moments.

Table 12 presents the estimated impacts on the distribution and concentration of Jam clicks. Although we found a positive and significant coefficient for the standard deviation, the other results in the second through fourth columns in the upper panel do not substantiate the notion of Jam-click concentration. Moreover, the lower panel shows that treated streamers did not demonstrate any significant shift of Jam clicks toward either earlier or later segments of their streams. To summarize, we found scant evidence that ICICs prompted streamers in our context to alter the structure of their live streams.

## Discussion and Conclusion

## Summary of Findings

We studied the impact of ICICs on the online video market by utilizing a natural experimental setting in which a prominent live streaming platform introduced engagement graphs on the VOD channel. The results show that the introduction of engagement graphs led to an increase in VOD view counts, live stream view counts, and viewing time. These results suggest that the engagement graphs enhanced viewer engagement and increased overall video consumption. However, the engagement graph did not result in an increase in the usage of premium features, such as giftgiving behaviors. In fact, we found no evidence suggesting that engagement graphs encouraged viewers to give more virtual gifts. Furthermore, streamers increased their video content in response to the ICIC introduction, but the overall structure of live streams remained largely unchanged.

## Contributions to the Literature

Our study provides valuable contributions to the literature on information systems and platform design in several key aspects. First, we present a comprehensive conceptual framework for ICICs. While numerous studies have scrutinized the effects of pre-consumption artifacts on digital content demand, scant attention has been given to the impact of ICICs on online video consumption. Our economic model offers a plausible explanation of how ICICs can bolster users’ online video consumption.

Second, we empirically evaluated the aforementioned economic impact of ICICs, leveraging a novel natural experiment. We observed a high degree of consistency between our insights driven by the model and our empirical findings, reinforcing the credibility and validity of our theoretical contributions. Furthermore, we delved into potential streamer responses to ICICs, and found that while streamers are likely to increase the volume of live and recorded videos in response to ICICs, they are less inclined to strategically alter their live content.

Finally, we found little indication that online video platforms can generate additional revenue from ICICs through virtual gifts to streamers, contrary to other research which has suggested that user engagement levels are positively related to purchasing virtual gifts on a live streaming platform (Ghose et al., 2022; Yu et al., 2018). For example, Ghose et al. (2022) showed that viewers increased their gift-giving behaviors when nonmonetary gifts were introduced on a live streaming platform. This echoes previous findings that some dimensions of engagement improve purchase intention, with certain exceptions (Animesh et al., 2011; Hamari et al., 2017), implying that user engagement and profitability should be examined separately.

## Managerial Implications

Although major online video platforms have introduced ICICs, the impact of such efforts remains unclear because ICICs do not directly affect either customers’ expectations of video quality or the video quality itself. Our findings reveal that ICICs enable online video platforms to enhance video consumption. Considering that ICICs leverage existing consumption data and require no additional call for action from either consumers or content creators, they provide a costefficient method to promote overall engagement on online video platforms. There is also a potential for online video platforms to automatically create highlight clips,<sup>7</sup> which could also incorporate various engagement measures similar to Jam clicks, such as the number of live chats.

However, our findings also suggest that this enhanced engagement does not necessarily translate into an increase in the purchase of premium features. Although heightened activity can indirectly boost revenues through advertisements, this may not be sufficient for platforms that rely heavily on direct purchases. Consequently, online video platforms should carefully differentiate between strategies that enhance engagement and those that generate revenue.

## Limitations and Future Research

Our study has several limitations that pave the way for future research. First, we investigate a specific type of inconsumption information cue—engagement graphs—and do not aim to generalize our findings to other forms of ICICs. Consequently, we cannot discount the possibility that different types of ICICs might affect consumer behaviors in unique ways. Second, our data is sourced from a single live streaming platform, suggesting that our findings may have been influenced by specific platform characteristics. Although the age and gender compositions of our sample closely mirror those of Twitch, a representative platform in this space, our sample does not reflect characteristics such as nationality or numerous other unobservable factors. Third, due to data limitations, we could not empirically assess the impact on VOD consumption time (Proposition 1b), which forms one of the most compelling insights from our economic model. Future research could address this gap by collecting viewing time data for the focal channel, which would allow for an investigation of potential adverse effects of ICICs. Fourth, our empirical analysis does not directly account for potential spillover effects between VOD and live streaming channels. Future studies estimating a multistage causal model accounting for interactions between channels could yield deeper insights into the underlying mechanisms. Finally, our findings are not derived from a completely randomized setting. While the update of engagement graphs was unexpected by viewers, the difference in mobile operating systems may have introduced unobservable differences between the treated and control groups. Future research could conduct randomized field experiments to further eliminate potential confounding factors.

## B4. Triple-Differences Approach

In our main analysis, we leverage the introduction of engagement graphs as the pivotal treatment event, where we compare the differences between iOS and Android users both before and after this introduction. To further refine and ensure the robustness of our estimations, we introduced a triple-differences approach to capture additional confounding factors.

![](/api/attachments/JAPQBV23/fulltext/images/2aa95bfdec32fa7d5e12b43bbd52ca7e949bb5466f6aaf2d39859bc48f6554dd.jpg)  
Figure B3. Illustration of Triple-Differences Framework

Figure B3 illustrates the analytical framework. Essentially, iOS users could only encounter the engagement graph when they accessed VODs after the mobile app’s update. Notably, this update did not alter the consumption interfaces of the VOD channel prior to the user’s first encounter with the engagement graph. While the decision to view a VOD is endogenous within each OS group, it remains independent of and irrelevant to the mobile app update. Therefore, iOS and Android users accessing VODs are on comparable grounds during that period.

Motivated by this, we consider pairs of iOS and Android viewers whose first post-update VOD viewing times are identical. Each pair allow us to estimate a separate DID specification with only one treatment timing, circumventing the staggered DID issue. In this manner, we can derive several difference-in-differences pairs, each with a unique timing of first VOD watching. The variation in these timings allows us to incorporate OS-specific time fixed effects, given the heterogenous treatment timings within each OS group. This approach helps control fo unobserved confounders related to live stream consumption, as viewers might watch live videos without visiting the VOD channel. Ou econometric specifications are as follows:

$$
f (Y _ {i t}) = \beta_ {1} \cdot T r e a t e d _ {i} + \beta_ {2} \cdot A f t e r _ {i t} + \beta_ {3} \cdot T r e a t e d _ {i} \cdot A f t e r _ {i t} + \mu_ {i} + \gamma_ {i t} + \varepsilon_ {i t},\tag{B1}
$$

where $A f t e r _ { i t }$ is 1 if viewer ?? has watched at least one VOD in week ?? after the mobile app update; $\gamma _ { i t }$ denotes a set of OS-specific time fixed effects; and the other variables are defined in the same way as in Equations (1) and (2). By accounting for heterogeneous non-parametric trends across different groups, we can systematically mitigate the possibility of violating the parallel pre-trend assumption, as suggested by Antecol et al. (2018).

Tables B16 and B17 present the estimated results from Equation (B1), utilizing both aggregate matching and trend matching. The coefficients for VOD view counts, live stream view counts, and live stream viewing time are consistently positive and statistically significant coefficients, aligning with our prior findings. Furthermore, there is no indication that gift-giving amounts increased due to engagement graphs. Even when include viewer-specific linear trends (as seen in Tables B18 and B19), the results largely remain the same, suggesting that any unobservable dynamic differences between the iOS and Android groups are improbable causes for our findings.
