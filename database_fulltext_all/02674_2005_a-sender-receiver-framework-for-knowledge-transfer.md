## Abstract

The shift to more distributed forms of organizations and the prevalence of interorganizational relationships have led to an increase in the transfer of knowledge between parties with asymmetric and incomplete information about each other. Because of this asymmetry and incompleteness, parties seeking knowledge may not be able to identify qualified knowledge providers, and the appropriate experts may fail to be motivated to engage in knowledge transfer.

We propose a sender-receiver framework for studying knowledge transfer under asymmetric and/or incomplete information. We outline four types of information structures for knowledge transfer, and focus on the sender-advantage asymmetric information structure and the symmetric incomplete information structure. We develop formal game-theoretical models, show how information incompleteness and asymmetry may negatively influence knowledge transfer, and propose solutions to alleviate these negative impacts. Implications for knowledge transfer research and practice are also discussed.

Keywords: Knowledge transfer, knowledge management, incomplete information, asymmetric information, sender–receiver game

## Introduction

In recent years, the shift to more distributed forms of organizations and the prevalence of inter-organizational relationships have presented new challenges to knowledge transfer (Baum and

Greve 2001; Powell et al. 1996). Knowledge is increasingly transferred between geographically dispersed individuals and subsidiaries of multiunit organizations. Strategic alliances, outsourcing, and globalization imply knowledge transfer across organizational, cultural, and national boundaries. In such knowledge transfer, participants often have insufficient background information of each other and lack a shared language and common interests, which significantly limits their ability to assess and share knowledge (Carlile 2004).

This paper studies the impact of incomplete and asymmetric information between parties on the effectiveness of knowledge transfer. We develop a sender–receiver framework for knowledge transfer, which allows for the knowledge sender and the knowledge receiver, each with distinct self-interests, to have different perceptions of the situation. The perceptions different parties possess regarding the knowledge sharing and assessing activities in which they engage form the basis of information structure, which we will define later.

We focus on two types of information structures, which are increasingly common settings of knowledge transfer in business practice and yet highly likely to lead to suboptimal knowledge transfer. One is the sender-advantage asymmetric information structure, where the sender has more information about the knowledge transfer than does the receiver. We show that information asymmetry in this case may lead to a malfunctioning or even failed knowledge market. We also describe mechanisms such as signaling that can effectively alleviate the problem. The other is the symmetric incomplete information structure, where neither the sender nor the receiver has complete information about the knowledge transfer. We show that information manipulation may exist even in such a symmetric case, which leads to a situation called signal-jamming. A knowledge receiver must be aware of signal-jamming behaviors by a knowledge sender in order to make the right decisions on whether to acquire knowledge.

Argote et al. (2003) state that extant literature of knowledge management investigates relationships between knowledge management outcomes (knowledge creation, knowledge retention, knowledge transfer), the context of knowledge management (properties of units, relationship between units), and properties of knowledge. Our paper adds to the literature by studying the relationship between knowledge transfer and properties of relationships between units. We introduce the dimension of information structure as a characteristic of the relationship between participants and explore the impact of differential properties (viz. completeness and symmetry) of this dimension on knowledge transfer.

Knowledge transfers can be analyzed at nodal (focusing on the behavior of one party), dyadic (focusing on the joint behavior of a pair), and systemic (focusing on the behavior of a system consisting of providers and seekers) levels (Gupta and Govindarajan 2000). Our research is at the dyadic level; however, through the lens of the sender–receiver framework, we are able to provide strategic implications at the nodal level and to propose directions of improvement for designing mechanisms at the systemic level.

In terms of methodology, we develop formal, game-theoretical models and derive analytical results that have implications for empirical research and business practice.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. The next section describes the knowledge market in which knowledge transfer occurs. We then present four typical information structures in knowledge transfer: symmetric complete information, sender-advantage asymmetric information, receiver-advantage asymmetric information, and symmetric incomplete information. Knowledge transfer under the sender-advantage asymmetric information structure is explored. The signal-jamming issue in knowledge transfers under the symmetric incomplete information structure is then studied. We end the paper with conclusions and a discussion of the implications of the analysis.

## Knowledge Market

In developing the sender–receiver framework, we first introduce the market view of knowledge transfer. We treat knowledge as a good that moves in a knowledge market where parties may have incomplete and asymmetric information about other participants and the knowledge itself.

## Knowledge Market Within and Across Organizations

A knowledge market exists within organizations and knowledge buyers and sellers within a firm expect to gain from a knowledge transaction (Cohen 1988; Davenport and Prusak 1998). $^{2}$ This market view is consistent with empirical studies, which show that an effective internal knowledge sharing and transfer system provides motivations to both the provider and the seeker (APQC 1996, 1997; O'Dell and Grayson 1998).

Knowledge markets also exist across organizations (Teece 1981, 1998). Consulting firms are hired to provide strategic advice; companies sign licensing agreements and technology development contracts; services and business processes are outsourced to vendors.

Our research concerns knowledge transfer that may occur in either the internal or external knowledge market. While the transparency of prices and the payment mechanisms in the two types of knowledge markets are usually different, we focus on the common characteristics of transactions in these markets.

## Components of Knowledge Market

There are two groups of participants in a knowledge transfer: senders who are knowledge sellers, and receivers who are knowledge buyers. $^{3}$ The knowledge sender as well as the knowledge receiver can be an individual, a team, a firm, or a unit (subsidiary) within a firm. We assume that each of them focuses on maximizing his/her own benefits and their interests are generally not aligned. This applies to both the external knowledge market where the sender and receiver belong to two different organizations and the internal knowledge market where they belong to the same organization but care primarily about their own interests.

Each sender is endowed with a piece of knowledge in which a receiver is interested. Specifically, the receiver hopes to derive benefits, or in other words, value, from utilizing the knowledge. Based on prior research (Cohen 1998; Glazer 1998), we assume that the value of knowledge provided by the sender is realized when the receiver has assimilated and put in use the knowledge, which has brought about outcomes attributable to the use of knowledge.

## Assessing the Value of Knowledge Before Trading

Because knowledge transfer precedes knowledge utilization, the sender and the receiver cannot measure the realized value of knowledge when deciding on the transfer. Instead, the sender and the receiver form expectations of the value of knowledge based on their information.

All of the information available to the sender and used by him to evaluate the knowledge is called the sender's information set. The receiver's information set is defined similarly. To derive the expected value of the knowledge transfer, the sender's and the receiver's information sets should contain at least the following components:

(1) The nature of the knowledge, such as the knowledge being tacit or explicit, proven or unproven

(2) The sender's knowledge capability, such as his area and level of expertise

(3) The context in which the knowledge is put to use (whether the context has idiosyncratic features)

(4) The fit between the knowledge and the receiver based on the receiver's declared usage of the knowledge

(5) The ties between the sender and receiver, such as their frequency of interactions and trust

These five factors constitute the basis of the expectations the sender and the receiver have on the value of the knowledge transfer. $^{4}$

Since we are interested in whether the sender and receiver have sufficient information to estimate the value of the knowledge to be transferred, instead of studying the individual elements of a sender's or a receiver's information set, we introduce the notion of completeness of an information set. One party's information set is considered complete if it contains sufficient information for its owner to reach the correct expected value of the knowledge transferred; otherwise, it is incomplete. Note that our definition of completeness is with respect to the task of valuing knowledge. $^{5}$

The completeness or incompleteness of the sender's and the receiver's information sets is called the information structure of knowledge transfer. The next section discusses different types of information structure, which critically impact knowledge transfer.

## Knowledge Transfer Under Different Information Structures

## Four Different Information Structures

Depending on the completeness or incompleteness of the sender's and the receiver's information sets, there are four representative types of information structure in knowledge transfer, as illustrated in Figure 1: (I) symmetric complete information, (II) sender-advantage asymmetric information, (III) symmetric incomplete information, and (IV) receiver-advantage asymmetric information. $^{6}$

## Information Structure I: Symmetric Complete Information

We call the case where both the knowledge sender's and receiver's information sets are complete the symmetric complete information (SCI) structure. Much of the knowledge management literature implicitly assumes that knowledge transfers occur under this structure. $^{7}$ Nevertheless, the symmetric complete information structure is limited in scope. It may apply to situations where parties have close connections and frequent contact. For example, in a team with a few members who work together closely, everyone is fully aware of the expertise level of other members and the situations involved.

## Information Structure II: Sender-Advantage Asymmetric Information

We call the case where the sender's information set is complete, yet the receiver's information set is incomplete the sender-advantage asymmetric information (SAAI) structure. This structure is widely observed in practice.

Example 1. Suppose a firm based in the United States is planning to expand its business to China by partnering with a local firm and expects to acquire knowledge of local markets from its partner. A potential Chinese firm knows its own capabilities, which it has acquired through business experience. Since the knowledge to be transferred from the Chinese firm to the U.S. firm is very specific and will be used in developing business in China, it is reasonable to assume that the Chinese firm is aware of the value of its knowledge. $^{8}$ However, it is very difficult for the U.S. firm to verify the claimed value of the knowledge before an alliance is formed and business is underway, due to its lack of information regarding the context and its unfamiliarity with the Chinese firm. The two firms may fail to reach an agreement because of the asymmetry of their information.

In this example, the Chinese firm, a potential knowledge sender, knows significantly more about the expected value of their knowledge than does the U.S. firm, the knowledge receiver. Similar sender-advantage information asymmetry exists not only in corporate expansion, but also in business consulting where clients frequently find themselves in an inferior position to assess the value of knowledge provided by seasoned business, legal, or political consultants.

The challenges of this structure are how a sender can credibly communicate the correct expected value of his knowledge to the receiver, and how the receiver can determine the value of a sender's knowledge. Note that simply asking a sender to report his expected value of knowledge to the receiver cannot solve the problem; since the compensation to a sender is tied to the expected value of his knowledge, every sender will claim that his knowledge is highly valuable. $^{9}$

## Information Structure III: Symmetric Incomplete Information

We call the case where both the sender's and receiver's information sets are incomplete the symmetric incomplete information (SII) structure.

Example 2. A firm based in the United States is considering outsourcing a technology development project in India. The project is unique in its combination of technology, domain knowledge, and geographically sensitive knowledge. While a potential firm is experienced in the technology, it is unsure about the fit between its knowledge and this project, thus uncertain about the expected value of its knowledge. $^{10}$ The potential Indian firm may still try to get the project, and the U.S. firm must be aware that it may be impossible to learn the expected value of this outsourcing project ex ante.

In this example, neither the potential knowledge sender (the Indian firm) nor the knowledge receiver (the U.S. firm) has complete information about the knowledge transfer. Such an information structure imposes the fewest requirements on the participants and there are numerous examples. Most offshore outsourcing projects that are non-routine can be stylized as cases of SII. Moreover, this structure is commonly encountered when companies hire experts in emerging technologies to fill knowledge gaps, where companies often lack the technical know-how, and technical experts often lack understanding of the business context.

One challenge in this structure is for the sender and the receiver to find mechanisms to alleviate information incompleteness for both of them before knowledge transfer. One surprising aspect of this structure is that, although no party holds information advantage over the other, strategic distortion in communication may still happen. We will study this structure in detail in the section on signal jamming.

## Information Structure IV: Receiver-Advantage Asymmetric Information

The receiver-advantage asymmetric information (RAAI) structure is the case where the sender's information set is incomplete while the receiver's information set is complete. In this structure, the receiver can identify the sender with the highly valuable knowledge. As a result, the receiver can always choose the best source from which to acquire knowledge. Therefore, from the receiver's perspective, the result will be similar to the one under symmetric complete information. As far as we know, markets with such an information structure have never been formally studied. However, this does not necessarily mean that this structure is trivial. $^{11}$ Since this information structure does not present a challenge to the knowledge receiver, we do not further analyze this structure in this paper.

## A Game-Theoretical Setup of the Sender-Receiver Framework

To facilitate further exposition, we present a game-theoretical setup for the sender-receiver framework as follows. An intended knowledge receiver is seeking knowledge for a pre-declared, well-defined usage. She needs to select a knowledge sender from a pool of $n$ candidates, whose knowledge varies in its expected value. $^{12}$ We simplify the setup such that the expected value of a sender's knowledge, denoted as variable $k$ , is either of a high level, $k_H$ , or a low level, $k_L$ , where $k_H > k_L > 0$ . We call a sender whose knowledge has high expected value a high type sender, and one with knowledge of low expected value a low type sender. It is common knowledge that $\text{Prob}(k = k_H) = \theta$ . $^{13}$ $\theta$ represents the receiver's belief on the overall reputation of the $n$ potential senders—the higher $\theta$ is, the more likely that a randomly picked sender from the pool is a high type. $^{14}$ It is expected that after a high type sender's knowledge is transferred to and assimilated by the receiver, it will add a value of $k_H$ to the receiver, and a low type, $k_L$ . $^{15}$

If a sender does not transfer the knowledge, he can engage in an alternative activity and earn the amount of c, the opportunity cost of the knowledge transfer. c is a function of k, and we define $c_{H} = c(k_{H})$ and $c_{L} = c(k_{L})$ , where $c_{H} = c_{L} > 0$ .¹⁶ We assume $k_{H} > c_{H}$ , so that a high type will transfer his knowledge if the value of his knowledge is recognized.

The four information structures can then be formulated as the following:

• SCI. Each potential sender has a correct expectation of the value of his knowledge, be it $k_{H}$ or $k_{L}$ , which the receiver also knows.

\- SAAI. Each potential sender has a correct expectation of the value of his own knowledge, be it $k_{H}$ or $k_{L}$ , which the receiver does not know. The senders and the receiver know $\text{Prob}(k = k_{H}) = \theta$ .

\- SII. Neither a potential sender nor the receiver has a correct expectation of the value of the knowledge.

\- RAAI. The receiver has correct expectation of the value of knowledge of each potential sender, be it $k_{H}$ or $k_{L}$ , yet the sender does not know.

## Sender-Advantage Asymmetric Information and Signaling in Knowledge Markets

We first show that the sender-advantage asymmetric information structure may lead to the adverse selection problem. We then show that one effective mechanism to solve this problem is signaling.

## Adverse Selection Under Sender-Advantage Asymmetric Information

We first assume that there is no mechanism for a sender to credibly communicate the value of his knowledge to the receiver. Since an individual sender's type is unobservable to the receiver, the receiver will offer a single compensation rate v to all potential senders, and if multiple senders agree to engage in a transfer, the receiver randomly selects one sender. Without loss of generality, we assume that the receiver offers the amount that equals the value she expects to receive from the knowledge transfer. Any sender with an opportunity cost lower than the compensation rate (i.e., c < v) will agree to transfer his knowledge.

Under SAAI, because the receiver cannot distinguish between different types of senders and has to make the same offer to all, an offer that attracts the high type will also attract the low type, while a lower offer may be accepted only by the low type. Such a phenomenon, where the decision made by the party with information advantage (the senders in this case) adversely affects the uninformed party (the receiver in this case), is called adverse selection. $^{17}$ Proposition 1 gives the condition under which adverse selection occurs.

Proposition 1: Under SAAI and without a mechanism for a potential sender to credibly communicate the value of his knowledge (his type), adverse selection will happen if the overall reputation of senders is low enough, i.e., $\theta < (c_H - k_L) / (k_H - k_L)$ . (See Appendix A for proof.)

Proposition 1 suggests that a knowledge seeker may fail to find a potential sender with high expected value of knowledge. To better understand the implications and contingencies of Proposition 1, we derive the following corollaries:

Corollary 1.1: When the overall reputation of senders is high, i.e., $\theta > (c_{H} - k_{L})/(k_{H} - k_{L})$ , all potential senders will agree to transfer knowledge.

Therefore, there is only a probability of $\theta$ that the receiver picks a high type sender.

Corollary 1.2: When the overall reputation of senders is low, i.e., $\theta < (c_{H} - k_{L})/(k_{H} - k_{L})$ , there are two possible outcomes: either only the low type senders agree to transfer knowledge, or no sender does.

The above results suggest that under SAAI structure, it is difficult for a knowledge seeker to locate and engage a potential knowledge sender with valuable knowledge, and even when such a sender agrees to transfer its knowledge, the receiver may still fail to select him due to the information asymmetry. To the receiver, a knowledgeable sender appears the same as those less knowledgeable.

The adverse selection problem identified here can partly explain the underdevelopment of knowledge markets across organizations (Teece 1981, 1998). It has also been widely accepted that multinational corporations exist because they can transfer knowledge more effectively internally than through external market mechanisms. $^{18}$ Our research suggests one more reason for a multinational corporation to be more effective in knowledge transfers than external markets: it reduces the information asymmetry between senders and receivers, and therefore is not or less subject to the adverse selection problem.

## Signals: Information of Knowledge

One way to solve the adverse selection problem under SAAI is signaling, in which potential senders find ways to signal the expected value of their knowledge, which is unobservable to the receiver, through observable actions. $^{19}$ In practice, a knowledge sender may use various kinds of signals, ranging from corporate images to certificates and endorsements issued by a third party, from revealed preferences in negotiations to various activities such as conferences and product demonstrations.

In the knowledge management literature, theorists recognize the importance of relevant information about the knowledge being transferred. The elaboration likelihood model (ELM) of information influence (Petty et al. 1981; Petty and Cacioppo 1986; Sussman and Siegal 2003) suggests that in persuasive communication, influence may occur through either the central route where recipients elaborate on the content of the message, or the peripheral route where recipients use peripheral cues as heuristics to assess the content.

The signals we discuss here are similar to peripheral cues in that neither the signals nor the cues are the content itself, but they are relevant information about the knowledge being transferred. However, there is a fundamental difference between our model and ELM. In ELM, the central route and the peripheral route are both feasible and the recipient can choose either one or a combination of the two. In our model, we assume that the central route of elaboration is infeasible for the receiver.

## Signaling in Knowledge Transfer

A signaling mechanism can successfully communicate information only under certain conditions. $^{20}$ First, one crucial condition is that there must be a cost associated with sending signals. Second, senders different in their knowledge levels must differ in their costs of sending signals. For instance, in Example 1 in the previous section, the U.S. firm may request all potential Chinese partners to provide a concise plan, as well as accessible evidence on why the plan will work, for marketing a product. In preparing for the marketing plan, the Chinese firms inevitably incur a cost. A more experienced and well-connected firm can relatively easily draft the plan with supporting evidence, while a less experienced firm would take much more effort to provide a plan of the same quality. In other words, the same signal costs a low type sender more than it does a high type.

Formally, the signal (in the above example, the quality of a marketing plan) is denoted by a nonnegative variable $a.^{21}$ . The cost for a sender to send signal a is e, which is a function of both the sender's type k and the signal a: $e = e(a, k)$ . Appendix B provides regularity conditions on $e(a, k)$ .

Now the receiver can make the payment contingent on the observed signal, i.e., $v = v(a)$ . Then a sender of type k who chooses to send signal a will get a payoff of $u(a, k) = v(a) - e(a, k)$ if he transfers his knowledge.

An equilibrium in which senders of different types pick different signals is called a separating equilibrium. Consequently, in a separating equilibrium, the receiver is able to distinguish between high and low type senders by observing the received signal. The following proposition provides the necessary and sufficient condition for the separating equilibrium and characterizes the equilibrium (see Appendix B for formal proof).

Proposition 2: Under SAAI, a separating equilibrium such that

i. Low type senders will send the lowest possible signal, 0, and

ii. High type senders will send a strong signal $\tilde{a}$ that is determined by

$$
\begin{array}{l} \boldsymbol {e} (\tilde {\boldsymbol {a}}, k _ {L}) = \\ \frac {1 - (1 - \theta) ^ {n}}{n \theta} k _ {H} - \frac {(1 - \theta) ^ {n - 1}}{n} (\max \left\{c _ {L}, k _ {L} \right\} - c _ {L}) - c _ {L} \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

exists if and only if signaling is not too costly for a high type sender, i.e.,

$$
\begin{array}{l} \boldsymbol {e} (\tilde {\boldsymbol {a}}, k _ {H}) <   \\ \frac {1 - (1 - \theta) ^ {n}}{n \theta} k _ {H} - \frac {n - 1}{n} c _ {H} - \frac {1}{n} \max \left\{\theta k _ {H} + (1 - \theta) k _ {L}, c _ {H} \right\} \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

The intuition for Proposition 2 is that for a separating equilibrium to emerge, the high type sender must choose to send a strong enough signal such that it will be too costly for the low type sender to mimic the signal. For example, the requirements to the marketing plan must be sufficiently difficult to satisfy in order to deter the low type from providing a plan of the same quality as the high type provides.

The following corollaries can be derived from Proposition 2.

Corollary 2.1: A signaling mechanism that allows the receiver to distinguish the high type from the low type has the following properties:

i. Signals are clearly verifiable in terms of quality/strength;

ii. It should be very costly for low type senders to send strong signals, yet at the same time not costly for high type senders to do the same.

In a separating equilibrium, the high type senders choose to provide a strong signal while the low type will choose to provide none. The low type senders choose not to imitate the action of the high type because it is more costly for them to do so and the expected reward will not justify their efforts. Thus, the receiver can tell high type senders from low type ones by observing the signals and will select a high type to be the knowledge source.

Corollary 2.2: Signals are costly. However, the benefits usually outweigh the costs in allowing knowledge receivers to identify senders with high value knowledge.

The only purpose of a signal is to communicate a sender's type and it does not add value to the knowledge. However, such extra costs are usually justified if the overall benefits of signaling—being able to identify a high type sender—exceed that without signaling. In fact, such costs are inevitable in order to solve the adverse selection problem due to information asymmetry and incompleteness ("no free lunch").

Corollary 2.3: When the overall reputation of all senders is not too low, the total number of senders generally decreases the strength of the signal sent by high types.

This implies that a higher total number of competing knowledge providers causes high type senders to send less costly signals.

Corollary 2.4: The knowledge level of high type senders increases the strength of the signal they send.

This result suggests that as the high type senders' knowledge becomes even more valuable, they tend to devote more resources in convincing receivers, further “raising the bar” for lower types. In other words, as the strong knowledge senders in the knowledge market become even stronger, they compete more aggressively.

Examining Corollaries 2.3 and 2.4 together reveals that while an increase in the sheer number of competitors makes high type providers reluctant to devote resources to convince a knowledge receiver, improvements in high type senders' knowledge do intensify competition. This means that a strong knowledge sender does not care about the quantity of its competitors, but does care about the quality of them. Thus, Corollaries 2.3 and 2.4 provide a theoretical foundation for developing hypotheses in empirical studies on behaviors in an increasingly competitive knowledge market.

## Discussion

Managing an organization's knowledge assets and purchasing knowledge products and services from established knowledge markets often fit the sender-advantage asymmetric information structure well. $^{22}$ In such cases, a signaling mechanism helps the receiver of knowledge select the best source of knowledge.

When building its knowledge capital, a firm may develop a signaling mechanism and an incentive scheme that can help the firm to identify and properly reward the most knowledgeable units (individuals, teams, subsidiaries, etc.). $^{23}$

Firms seeking external expertise are constantly facing the challenge of selecting the best expert. When buying a knowledge product or service from a developed knowledge market, a firm can solicit signals from potential senders in order to identify the best knowledge source. A case study of British Petroleum's (BP) IT outsourcing decisions exemplifies how a signaling mechanism works in business practice. $^{24}$ To select its IT providers, BP first sent out two rounds of “request of information,” and identified suppliers that had experience in providing similar services in similar environments. BP then invited six suppliers to a week-long interactive workshop, where the suppliers were asked to write proposals. BP then selected a team of suppliers from the finalists that provided a higher quality of proposal. The workshop is the signaling mechanism BP designed while the proposals are the signals sent by suppliers. It enabled the company to select the best suppliers to provide the IT services of critical importance to the company.

From a knowledge provider's point of view, effectively conveying the expected value of its knowledge products and services is crucial. $^{25}$ For example, a knowledge provider may offer free demonstration and free initial consultation. A sender must be aware that a signal credible to a knowledge receiver has to be costly. In addition, in order to prove the superiority of a sender's knowledge, the signal has to be prohibitively costly for the less knowledgeable senders to imitate.

## Extension: The Impact of Individual Reputation on Knowledge Transfer

The sender-advantage asymmetric information case applies well to a knowledge transfer where a receiver encounters senders for the first time and lacks background information about the senders. Nevertheless, in practice a sender can often reduce the information asymmetry in the following two scenarios. The first scenario is where the sender has a well-publicized reputation. For instance, students can often easily find out which professor in a university is good through word-of-mouth. In the second scenario, the receiver already has prior interactions with the sender, and thus has privately accumulated experience. For instance, although there might be no public information about how good a new professor is, after taking a course from this professor, students usually have enough information to decide whether to take a second one or not.

In this subsection, we use the word reputation to represent both well-publicized reputation and privately accumulated experience. There is a full spectrum of possibilities on how reputation might impact knowledge transfer. On the one extreme, if the reputation is a strong and consistent indicator of the sender's knowledge level, the information structure is effectively changed to the symmetric complete information case. For example, if a company has successfully outsourced a technology service to a provider, it is highly likely to get good service from the same provider if the service is extended to a very similar task. On the other extreme, if past reputation is not indicative of a sender's knowledge level in another area, it then has no impact on the knowledge transfer process. In the above example, if the company wants to outsource a very different service, the experience with the provider does not warrant choosing the same provider.

The general case, however, falls somewhere between these two extremes: the reputation does contain some information about the sender, but not complete information. We call this the imperfect reputation case. For example, large consulting firms usually have strong and well-publicized reputations; however, similar consulting projects done by the same firm may still vary in their value as different experts in the same firm may be assigned to different projects. $^{26}$

To study the effect of individual sender's reputation, we modify our model as follows. We define the receiver's belief that sender $i$ is of high type as $\mathrm{Prob}(k = k_H) = \theta^i$ . $\theta^i$ represents the reputation of sender $i$ to the receiver. The reputation of sender $i$ contains two components, i.e., $\theta^i = \theta + \varepsilon^i$ , where $\theta$ represents the receiver's belief on the overall/aggregate reputation of all senders, and $\varepsilon^i$ represents the receiver's belief on how much more (or less) reputable sender $i$ is comparing to the overall reputation level. We call $\theta$ the average reputation and $\varepsilon^{i}$ the individual reputation shifter. For example, $\theta = 50$ percent and $\varepsilon^{i} = 20$ percent means that the receiver believes half of all senders are of high type, while sender i is 20 percent more likely than the average level to be of high type. Note that $\varepsilon^{i}$ can be non-zero only if the receiver has some private information of sender i's reputation, such as through prior interactions or by recommendations.

A sender with a good reputation (a positive $\varepsilon^{i}$ ) will lead to a higher $\theta^{i}$ , while one with a bad reputation (a negative $\varepsilon^{i}$ ) will lead to a lower $\theta^{i}$ . The following proposition summarizes the impact of individual reputation on knowledge transfer.

Proposition 3. Under imperfect reputation and in a separating equilibrium, individual reputation does not affect the cost for a high type sender to signal his quality. However, the higher the sender's reputation is, the more beneficial the knowledge transfer can be. (Proof available upon request.)

Intuitively, as long as the individual reputation is not strong enough to completely rule out the possibility of the sender being low type, a high type sender always has to send a strong signal if he wants to distinguish himself from low type senders. Also note that although a better reputation does not directly benefit a sender in a separating equilibrium, it increases the total benefit simply because the chance of having a high type sender, thus an optimal knowledge transfer, is higher.

## Signal-Jamming Under Symmetric Incomplete Information

One may think that information manipulation is only related to an asymmetric information structure where the sender takes advantage of the fact that he has more information than the receiver. In this section, we show that even under the symmetric incomplete information structure, however, the sender may still have incentive to manipulate the knowledge transfer process. We discuss the signal-jamming behavior, where a sender does not possess any private information but can nevertheless distort, or “jam,” the signal the receiver observes.

## Signal-Jamming by Sender and Signal-Extraction by Receiver

The offshore example presented earlier shows that sometimes neither the receiver nor the senders has the correct expectation of the value of knowledge. For another example, in business consulting, a consultant may not be sure about the fit between his knowledge and a specific project, especially when the project concerns a new industry, or a geographical location with which he is unfamiliar. It is also possible that the consultant is not aware of his position relative to other potential knowledge senders. In other words, he is not clear as to how knowledgeable other consultants are (as compared to him). The case where the consultant has an incorrect perception of his own knowledge also fits this structure rather than the sender-advantage information structure. $^{27}$

The signal-jamming process is illustrated in Figure 2. We focus on the case where the sender does not know the expected value of his knowledge.

Note that not knowing the correct expectation of the value of his knowledge does not imply that the sender cannot communicate information to the receiver. For example, firms bidding for a technology outsourcing project can provide solutions to a pilot project for the receiver, and the solution serves as a signal. $^{28}$ Even though a bidding firm knows that the project requires applying its expertise in a new, unfamiliar context, it may still devote a significant amount of resources to the pilot project in an attempt to influence the outsourcer's decision.

The signal-jamming structure has a major difference from a signaling structure. Whereas in the signaling structure a sender knows the signal that he sends and the receiver gets, in the signal-jamming structure, a sender does not observe the exact signal the receiver gets—the signal depends on the sender's actual type, which is unobservable to him. Instead, what the sender does know and can control is the cost incurred (or effort exerted). In the above outsourcing example, a sender can control how many man-hours to put into the pilot project, but as long as he does not know how fit his knowledge is with respect to the project, the quality of his solution is unobservable to him. Therefore, we shift the focus from choosing a signal level to choosing a cost level.

If the sender does not put excess effort into the proposal, the quality of the solution will only be influenced by his type and noise. In this case, we call the information the receiver gets from the solution to the pilot project the signal. However, even without knowing the value of his knowledge, the sender can still try his best to distort the signal, such as working excessively on the pilot project. Once the sender adds his distortion to the signal, the receiver will receive a distorted message. Distorting the message is often referred to as jamming the signal, as the sender hopes to mislead the receiver in a way that benefits the sender. $^{29}$

Proposition 4. If the receiver is naïve (i.e., is not aware of the possibility of signal-jamming), the sender will strongly distort the message. (See the "Naïve Receiver" case in Appendix C.)

Intuitively, if the receiver were to believe that the signal from a sender is only affected by the expected value of his knowledge level and noise, it would benefit the sender if he distorts the message, such as working excessively on the pilot project, because it would mislead the receiver to believe that the sender's knowledge is more likely to be valuable.

If the receiver is not naïve (i.e., is aware of the possibility of signal-jamming), she will face a signal-extraction problem where she tries to extract the signal out of the distorted message. Can a receiver correctly retrieve the signal? If so, will this prevent the sender from jamming the signal? The following proposition shows that, although the effects of the signal-jamming behavior can be alleviated, the behavior itself will still occur.

Proposition 5. If the receiver is aware of the signal-jamming issue, she can correctly infer the distortion and thus completely extract the signal, which serves to reduce information incompleteness. Nevertheless, the sender will still distort the message. (See the "Rational Receiver" case in Appendix C.)

This proposition is surprising in the sense that, even after knowing that distortion will be completely corrected by the receiver in the signal-extraction process, the sender still distorts the message, although in vain.

To understand why this happens, let us consider what would happen in the outsourcing example if a sender decides not to work excessively on the pilot project, as he is aware that any effort beyond his true ability would be completely discounted by the receiver. In this scenario, the sender would hope that the receiver expected no distortion in the solution to the pilot project he submitted—as a result, the receiver would correctly infer the likelihood of the sender being a high type, and that he would not need to put excessive and wasteful effort into the pilot project.

However, if the receiver follows the above logic, then the receiver becomes a naïve receiver! Following Proposition 4, the sender will then work excessively and manipulate the message. Therefore, the receiver will never follow the above logic. $^{30}$ Proposition 5 also shows that in the SII structure, as long as the receiver anticipates the signal-jamming issue, fully recovering the signal is achievable.

Our results also show that the receiver's awareness of the issue not only helps her correctly extract the signal, but also discourages (although not completely) distortion by the sender, as shown in the proposition below.

Proposition 6. Distortion and its cost are less in the rational receiver case where the receiver anticipates distortion than in the naïve receiver case. (See Appendix C.)

The intuition behind Proposition 6 is that when the receiver is aware that the sender distorts the message, the marginal return of distortion to the sender is lower than when the receiver is naïve. Therefore, in equilibrium, the sender chooses to make less effort in distorting the message.

It must be noted that, under SII, the true type of an individual sender is still unobservable to both parties even after the signal is extracted by the receiver: the receiver can now better estimate the sender's type, but cannot be sure of his actual type. The reason is that under the SII structure, the senders do not know their own actual types and, therefore, choose the same level of distortion. The receiver can only update her information at the aggregate level based on the signals, but she cannot tell an individual sender's actual type. In contrast, under the SAAI structure, senders know their own types and senders of different types often choose to send different signals, allowing the receiver to learn the type of each sender. $^{31}$

Under the SII structure, signals allow the sender and the receiver to update their information. However, such gain in information comes at extra costs: the senders spend extra resources trying to distort the signals. Yet, the strategic behaviors by the sender and the receiver make such costs unavoidable. What the receiver can do is simply to be aware of the signal-jamming behavior of the sender, and such awareness alone can discourage, to some extent, the senders from overly distorting the signal. (Appendix C provides a numeric example that illustrates signal jamming under the SII structure.)

## Discussion

Knowledge transfers that involve applying existing knowledge innovatively to new contexts can be stylized as under the symmetric incomplete information structure and should be analyzed accordingly.

For example, a firm seeking better performance through business process outsourcing considers outsourcing a new process with which no providers have any previous experience. It must realize that while soliciting signals from potential senders may help it better estimate the probability of success, it cannot expect being able to choose the most suitable provider. If, however, it has become common practice for a business process to be outsourced and there are many experienced providers, a well-designed signaling mechanism will allow the firm to select the most suitable provider.

In the technology consulting market of the early 1990s, both consulting firms and companies installing new enterprise information systems had limited skills and experience and therefore limited understanding of the value of the projects. The consulting firms tried to influence a client's perception of the usefulness of the systems, which is a signal-jamming behavior typical of the symmetric incomplete information structure. Clients could update their estimates of the value of the projects based on consulting firms' demonstrations. However, trying to learn which firm provided the best system would not have been successful.

It should be noted that the landscape of technology consulting has since changed dramatically. Consulting firms have accumulated experience implementing IT and acquired capabilities to bring value to firms in different industries. It is now possible for firms to choose a consulting firm that is experienced, knowledgeable, and fits their particular needs (Head 2004). This suggests that the information structure of the technology consulting industry has largely shifted from the SII structure to the SAAI structure and a signaling mechanism may help a firm select the best IT provider.

## Concluding Remarks

The goal of this paper is to study why and how information structures affect the effectiveness of knowledge transfer, and to suggest what can be done to facilitate optimal knowledge transfer. When incomplete and/or asymmetric information exists between a knowledge sender and knowledge receiver, the sender has an incentive to influence the receiver's perceptions of the sender's knowledge level and expectations of the value to be derived from the transfer.

Our research results on the impacts of information structures on knowledge transfer have important managerial implications. First, a firm involved in knowledge transfer must decide to which type of information structure a knowledge transfer belongs. Determining the information structure of a knowledge transfer may not be straightforward. In Example 1, the U.S. firm is looking for a partner with a special set of knowledge (knowledge on local markets) that the senders can fully evaluate, making it the sender-advantage asymmetric case. If, however, a strategic alliance involves partners extending their knowledge to a new area with which neither is familiar, it should be analyzed under the symmetric incomplete information structure. The outsourcing case described in Example 2 has symmetric incomplete information structure, because the senders lack relevant information about the project in the United States and, therefore, cannot tell their own knowledge level. If, alternatively, bidders of the outsourcing project are familiar with that kind of project, through experience for example, it then becomes the sender-advantage asymmetric case, for which different strategies should be used.

Second, the effectiveness of signals in knowledge markets depends critically on the information structure. Signals help a receiver identify a knowledgeable sender under sender-advantage asymmetric information structure, while they only provide better estimates of the value of a sender's knowledge under symmetric incomplete information structure.

Third, one should also be aware that the information structure of a knowledge market changes dynamically and participants of the market must adjust their strategies accordingly. We discussed the shift of information structure in technology consulting in the previous section. Generally, the information structure of a new knowledge market can usually be best understood as a symmetric incomplete information structure. $^{32}$ As the market matures and knowledge senders gain more experience, it tends to shift to the sender-advantage symmetric information structure. As the senders gradually establish their individual reputations among all players, it approaches the symmetric complete information structure, which is the best-understood scenario of knowledge transfer.

Our study of information structures in knowledge transfer also has important implications for research. Within the sender–receiver framework, we develop models and derive propositions and corollaries that can be used as the basis for related future research.

First, our research provides insights for building constructs and their relationships in empirical research on signals in knowledge transfer. It should be noted that we define a signal in terms of its strength and the cost of generating and communicating it (born by the sender), not in its actual form. Consequently, our results on signaling and signal-jamming mechanisms may apply to any form of signals, as long as it satisfies the restrictions on the strengths and costs. Nevertheless, the form of signals may have a crucial impact on the verifiability and measurability for both signal strength and signaling cost. Therefore, the implication of our research to empirical studies is that the form of a signal affects the effectiveness of a signaling mechanism through its verifiability and measurability of strength and cost. Empirical studies on specific forms of signals and their effectiveness in signaling mechanisms will be highly valuable for practitioners and make significant contributions to the knowledge transfer literature.

Second, our research helps advance our understanding of tacit knowledge sharing, where the quality of knowledge is often unobservable or unverifiable (or, verifiable only at a prohibitively high cost). Research has shown that tacit knowledge is best transferred in a colocated, face-to-face environment (Davenport and Prusak 1998). One of the reasons is that in such an environment, parties usually have complete information of the value of the knowledge. However, “(increasing economic and business forces) are disrupting the social nature of the workplace community where tacit knowledge lives and thrives” (Holtshouse 1998, p. 277). Firms are growing more geographically dispersed, and increasingly outsource and form partnerships. Computer-mediated communication is replacing face-to-face communication. While the information about explicit knowledge may spread relatively easily, this is not the case for information about tacit knowledge. Therefore, the current business and economic trend is leading to more incomplete information about tacit knowledge, often both on the receiver’s side and the sender’s side, challenging the effective transfer of tacit knowledge. To address the issue of effective tacit knowledge transfer, further study of the impact of information structure is necessary.

Finally, yet importantly, research on information structures may provide a new and interesting angle to the study of cognition processes. We make a first attempt in discussing the connection and distinction between signals for the purpose of transfer and peripheral cues for the purpose of elaboration. The impact of different information structures on cognition processes makes an interesting direction for future research.

As companies rely more on value derived from knowledge management, including building internal knowledge capital and acquiring knowledge through outsourcing and alliances, understanding information structures in knowledge transfers and developing proper strategies under different information structures will be the key to establishing a competitive edge.

## A Numeric Example

Assume that ex ante the receiver and potential senders all believe that 30 percent of the senders are the high type and 70 percent the low type. Suppose the receiver gets a strong message $(b = 1)$ from a random sender. A naïve receiver unaware of signal-jamming behaviors (i.e., who believes $\hat{e} = 0$ ) updates her perception of the overall reputation of all senders to 60 percent. A rational receiver, however, correctly infers the sender's distortion (excess cost) of the message (i.e., $\hat{e} = e^{*}$ ) and thereby updates her perception of the reputation of senders to 40 percent.

From the sender's point of view, before he sends the message, he only knows that he is 30 percent likely to be a high type sender. Even though he is aware that a rational receiver would discount any extra effort from him, he still has to do it, because if he didn't, it would be much less likely that his message to the receiver turns out to be strong.

It should be noted that after the receiver makes an offer to the sender based on the signal retrieved and updated beliefs, the sender can also ex post update his belief on the overall reputation of all senders (i.e., the probability of him being a high type). He can also infer, from the offer and the extra effort he has put in, the message the receiver got.
