A Cross­National Analysis of eHealth in the European Union: Some Policy and Research Directions

## Abstract

This study analyses the relationship between eHealth profiles across 27 European Union Member States. It builds on prior research which uses multivariate statistical methods to provide a crosscountry analysis on two dimensions: ICT penetration and availability compared with eHealth access and usage among health professionals. Based on the quantitative indicators/metrics used in our study, our results reveal that four distinct country groupings emerge as frontrunners, followers, leapfroggers and laggards. Frontrunners combine a strong ICT infrastructure with relatively high adoption of eHealth technologies. These countries are not defined by high levels of GDP spend on healthcare, but by a commitment to develop health IT for their citizens. Other countries with less developed health infrastructures, however, may seek to ‘leapfrog’ conventional stages technology adoption by moving directly to eHealth technologies. Our study suggests that a one-size-fits-all approach to health IT is not recommended for EU Member States as policy-makers at the national level need to develop an eHealth roadmap which reflects national, regional and local conditions which go beyond the technical imperative.

## 1. Introduction

Moving towards eHealth<sup>i</sup> is a key goal within the European Union as successive health and IT policy documents emphasise the benefits (and barriers) in pursuing the digital agenda [1]. This economic region of over 500 million people faces many challenges in the coming years, including: demographic changes; increased or decreased populations within the different Member States; an increasing ageing population (living with chronic health conditions); and increased migration of people from the poorer to the richer countries placing greater demands on health and social services. Responding to these complex challenges, EU politicians have introduced various policy initiatives to enhance the use of information and communications technology (ICT) to transition healthcare into a modern service for the 21st century where eHealth becomes a practical reality rather than a distant goal. In 2004 the European Commission initiated its first eHealth Action Plan, requesting a commitment from Member States to work together on eHealth [2]. A second eHealth Action Plan was launched at the end of 2011 [3]. The new eHealth Action Plan 2012-2020 followed a public consultation with experts across the eHealth stakeholder community. This Plan included recommendations of the eHealth Task Force and the review of the evidence to date.

In the last decade, the progress towards eHealth within the 27 EU Member States has been patchy. The academic literature has largely focused on issues of adoption and diffusion of specific eHealth technologies, such as Electronic Health Records (EHRs) [4] Health Information Exchanges (HIEs), Telemedicine [75] and their various benefits and barriers [5]. Despite growing interest in eHealth internationally [74], relatively few studies have focused on cross-country comparisons of eHealth to reveal the contextual factors as to why some countries are perceived to be leaders and others, laggards. Many of the publications providing a comparative country analysis or individual country profiles of eHealth are produced by government agencies, NGOs, industry analysts, and other nonacademic sources. However, recent work has recognised the growing interest in cross-country comparisons, with studies on the digital divide across the EU [6, 7, 8]. This issue is integral to eHealth access, availability and use, particularly as the ICT adoption in healthcare lags behind other sectors, such as financial services [9].

#

The reasons for differential rates of adoption and diffusion of eHealth, however, are not adequately explained by technology in isolation of the wider socio-economic and political landscape. While many academic studies emphasise the importance of socio-technical factors [10, 11, 12], eHealth country profiles are also influenced by wider political and economic factors [13]. To address these broader issues, this paper utilizes the TEMPEST<sup>ii</sup> database underpinned by a framework of 7 themes, 21 sub-themes over 150 (coded) quantitative indicators to deliver evidence-based insights that address scientific and policy issues (50). The multi-disciplinary thrust of TEMPEST provides a snapshot of aggregated country data across 27 EU Member States. By gathering a wide selection of indicators into one tool, TEMPEST enables policy-makers, academics and other relevant stakeholders in healthcare to gain an appreciation of the relationships between specific indicators, i.e. Broadband penetration(as a % of total population) (in the technology theme) and Total health expenditure as a % of GDP (in the economic theme). In conjunction with key EU policy priorities, such as encouraging active and healthy ageing, preventive medicine and healthy lifestyles, improving citizens' health security, reducing health inequalities, and promoting and disseminating health information [14], TEMPEST enables direct comparisons across a range of socio-economic and technical indicators. For this study, we identify 13 indicators from this database which explore the relationship between ICT and eHealth. The reasons for the relatively small selection of indicators are twofold. First, it is not methodologically viable to include a diverse range of quantitative indicators for statistical rigor (43). Second, our sponsors were keen to generate a robust research study to provide cross-country comparisons on ICT infrastructure and eHealth. Sponsors were less keen to fund research which generates case study eHealth data on single or even multiple sites within the same country.

EU stakeholders, which may include policy-makers, IT firms, health providers, NGOs and citizens are increasingly calling for studies which may offer benchmarking on various aspects of health and IT. Despite the limitations of using quantitative data for country comparison (40, 43, 52) our study aimed to answer the following research questions:- 1) How do EU Member States compare on indicators/metrics for ICT infrastructure and eHealth adoption, 2) What policy/research implications can be drawn from variations across EU Member States?

This paper is structured as follows. First we discuss the literature on ICT and eHealth policy in the European Union. We concur that it is important to understand the wider political landscape for building the digital or information society. Prior work commenting on cross-country comparisons shows that a one-size-fits-all approach to health policy and IT adoption and diffusion across the European Union is inappropriate, particularly as vast socio-political, economic and technical variations exist, not just across countries, but within countries as a whole [6]. Second, we discuss some methodological issues about measuring ICT and eHealth at the pan-European, national, regional and organizational levels. Third, we present our data using principal component and cluster analysis. Next, we discuss our findings and identify potential further areas for research. Finally, we conclude the paper with an overview of our results and observations

## 2. ICT and eHealth Policy in the European Union

The body of literature on health and IT has expanded in the last decade, with academic studies covering national ICT initiatives [15,16, 17], regional policies to introduce ICTs to improve healthcare for citizens [18, 19, 20], local level adoption of applications by clinicians (i.e. EHRs) [21, 22] and technical issues around interoperability [23]. At the EU policy level, the link between health and IT is often discussed in the context of how ICT can improve healthcare for European citizens in the face of serious current and future challenges. For example, public health expenditure in the EU27 has risen from 5.9% of GDP in 1990 to 7.2% in 2010. It is projected to increase to 8.5% by 2060. This is due, in part, to the ageing population and other socio-economic and cultural factors [3, p.4]. The EU27 population is projected to increase from 501 million in 2010 to 525 million in 2035, peaking at 526 million in 2040, before declining to 517 million in 2060 [24]. Citizens of working age are expected to fall from 61% to 51% of the total population. Coupled with this, the elderly (65+) and very old (80+) population is projected to grow respectively from 17.4% in 2010 to 30.0% in 2060 and from 4.7% in 2010 to 12.1% in 2060. [3, p.4].

To address these issues, the EU developed the first eHealth Action Plan in 2004. The main thrust of this plan was to encourage all the EU Member States to work together to promote eHealth. Further, “It advocated a common interoperability approach and the development of eHealth standards in different areas” [Flora Giorgio, Policy Officer ICT for Health at the European Commission’s DG INFSO]. In the period of the first eHealth Action Plan from 2004-2010, progress is seen to have been made in several areas, notably, increased awareness of eHealth across the EU, development of technical standards for EHRs, national-level ePrescription services in three Member States, Telemedicine pilots in the Nordic countries, pilot studies for Electronic European Health Insurance Cards, an EU Public Health portal and the development of a market for eHealth products and services [25].

In the past decade, the European Commission has developed targeted policy initiatives to foster widespread adoption of eHealth throughout the EU [1, 2]. Member States have responded to the eHealth policy agenda by participating in major large scale pilot projects in Cross Border Healthcare, and Article 14 which establishes the eHealth Network. An important goal is to maximise social and economic benefits through interoperability and the implementation of eHealth systems. A new eHealth Action Plan 2012-2020 was developed following public consultation across the eHealth stakeholder community [3, p.3]. Adopting a methodology of evidence-informed policy development, the objectives of this plan are to continue to raise awareness among EU citizens about the benefits of eHealth and to empower them to become actively engaged in their healthcare. Issues of interoperability, privacy, security of patient data, and developing a competitive eHealth market in Europe are also included in the plan.

In conjunction with many academic studies which have highlighted the difficulties surrounding eHealth [19, 20] the recent plan reiterates the main barriers to deploying eHealth which include, lack of users' awareness of the benefits, low eHealth literacy, shortage of evidence of cost-effectiveness and interoperability. Recognizing that eHealth penetration, digital literacy and ICT use vary widely across EU Member States, the plan points to the differing socio-political and economic conditions and variations in health systems, health outcomes and health workforces. It therefore emphasises the need for cooperation and partnership of all Member States and stakeholders [26, p.2]. A stakeholder in this context can refer to all citizens, whether they are policy-makers, patient representatives, health professionals or patients.

Against the backdrop of political support for eHealth in the EU27, the range of ICT<sup>iii</sup> applications in healthcare is large. Among the applications, for example are, EHRs which may have the potential to reduce medical errors and increase the quality of care and outcomes for patients [21], Telemedicine, which is seen to offer both clinicians and patients many advantages for the remote monitoring and treatment of chronic health conditions [75] and other eHealth applications in the form of Electronic Patient Records (EPRs) and Personal Health Records [26].

## 3. Measuring eHealth in 27 EU Member States

## 3.1. eHealth Measurement

Measuring eHealth across the EU27 combines methodological and analytical advantages and disadvantages. First, the unit of analysis, i.e. national, regional or local level, needs to be defined (43). The use of aggregated country data, using a single indicator (i.e. Broadband penetration (as a % of total population)) provides only a snapshot in terms of a country’s ICT profile. This indicator may be compared with that of other countries, resulting in a ranking from highest to lowest. To gain a deeper understanding of ICT profile, several indicators need to be measured and compared (i.e. broadband and Internet availability and use). Caution needs to be observed, however, since a country may show differing rates of broadband penetration with Internet adoption. While it is assumed the two indicators are synonymous, the levels may be different within the same country, thus leading to potentially misleading results [6].

Second, eHealth is not just about ICT profile. The definition for eHealth encapsulates socio-political and economic factors, yet cross-country comparisons on eHealth maturity are difficult to analyse and draw conclusions from, when multiple indicators (i.e. over 20) are used within the same study. This is not to undermine potential explanatory examples using inter-disciplinary indicators (i.e. social or technical) but to recognize that indicators and variables need to be carefully selected to ensure reliable statistical analysis. Since there are over twenty Member States in the EU, the scope of this study is a greater depth of analysis of a smaller number of indicators, rather than one with fewer countries, but a larger number of indicators.

Third, there is a lack of harmonized quantitative data for multiple cross-country analysis (40). Our literature review found numerous individual country profiles on a range of eHealth topics, yet very few cross-country comparisons. Despite these methodological constraints in cross-country comparative studies, pan-European eHealth policies and its recommendations are translated into country-level eHealth roadmaps, notwithstanding the vast differences in IT capability, economic conditions, population sizes, demographics and health systems within the EU27. An OECD<sup>iv</sup> [27] study on recent trends in the adoption and use of health related ICT using a cross-country analysis discusses the issue of identifying data sources using comparable quantitative methods. It highlights the issue where multiple studies may use different definitions (of indicators), sampling methods, techniques, and survey sizes. The OECD study therefore recommends a robust measurement framework with cross-national measures collected and published on an on-going basis.

Fourth, the ever changing technological landscape is a key challenge in measuring eHealth. As a sector, healthcare has been relatively slow to adopt ICT. For example, Electronic Medical Records (EMRs) have been in existence since the 1960s, yet healthcare organisations even in industrialised countries have been slow to adopt this technology [20, 21, 22]. One reason is that ICT literacy is less developed in healthcare than in other sectors, notably financial services [29].

However, as mature health economies look towards reducing health budgets, while developing economies attempt to build their health systems, Mobile technologies are perceived to offer attractive solutions to policy-makers. The foundation for building eHealth will rapidly emerge as the total number of mobile subscriptions in use worldwide has increased from fewer than 1 billion in 2000 to currently over 6 billion, with 5 billion in developing countries [67]. For developing countries alone, it is estimated that mobile health technology could save as much as \$400 billion by 2017 [67]. Wireless communication networks comprise the world's largest platform to deliver information, including public and social services to people in rural and poor areas. So a 10 % increase in mobile phone

subscribers is associated with a 0.8 % increase in economic growth, while a 10 % increase in high speed internet connections is associated, on average, with a 1.3 % increase in economic growth [28].

As chronic diseases increase with an ageing population, post-acute care monitoring services will comprise a large proportion of revenue growth from mobile technology. Diagnostic services are expected to comprise around 15% of the mHealth market (\$3.4 billion) with the majority of revenues from diagnostic services using call centres and mobile telemedicine solutions [67].

The global telemedicine market has increased from \$9.8 billion in 2010 to \$11.6 billion in 2011, and is expected to continue to expand to \$27.3 billion in 2016, representing a compound annual growth rate of 18.6 % [3, p.4]. Studies on cross-country analysis of eHealth therefore need to track how availability in ICT infrastructure, such as broadband and Internet at the country level can facilitate the usage of the various eHealth applications mentioned here. Only then will eHealth policy fulfil its primary aim of improving health service delivery. This is not to suggest that all countries will follow the same technological pathway towards eHealth. Recent examples in developing countries show that use of mobile technologies in healthcare, or mHealth, can enable a country to leapfrog more traditional technological pathways, i.e. by developing low cost applications for mobile device technologies, while bypassing other ICTs, such as desktop computers or fixed (home) Internet connectivity.

The research for this paper began in 2009 with the aim of developing a broader understanding of the intersection between health policy and eHealth across the European Union. eHealth, as a concept, was commonly discussed by policy-makers as a technological challenge [29] despite academic work emphasising the importance of socio-economic and political factors [10,11]. Since quantitative data for cross-country comparisons was scarce, data (indicators/variables) was gathered from different sources to gain an understanding of the eHealth profiles of the EU27. From 2011, the authors built a large database containing over 150 quantitative indicators for comparative data analysis.

## 3.2. TEMPEST Database

The TEMPEST database leverages a framework of 7 themes, 21 sub-themes and over 150 (coded) quantitative indicators to deliver evidence-based insights that address scientific and policy issues. This is reinforced by an interdisciplinary, multi-dimensional model of health, ICT and eHealth indicators/variables that allows those applying it to analyse specific health technologies, or related issues, from a market, political, commercial, stakeholder or individual perspective, through a crossnational or national lens. So far, TEMPEST has been applied to 27<sup>v</sup> European Union Member States. Incorporating key EU policy priorities, such as encouraging active and healthy ageing, preventive medicine and healthy lifestyles, improving citizens' health security, reducing health inequalities, and promoting and disseminating health information [30], TEMPEST offers a rich pool of quantitative indicators which enables direct comparisons across a range of themes and sub-themes.

The TEMPEST database can be used as a health IT tool to supplement health technology assessment (HTA) studies, which use multi-disciplinary data to inform health policy-makers. As a research approach, HTA focuses on information about the medical, social, economic and ethical issues related to the use of a health technology, as well as the short- and long-term consequences of its use, in a systematic, transparent, unbiased and robust manner. However, the different academic perceptions and practices of what constitutes HTA raise concerns about the adequacy of data collection and analysis methods. The siloed approach to HTA where studies isolate economic, organisational or cultural indicators or variables of specific health technologies leads to observations that “….the striking finding from our review (of health technology) is that there has been so little solid evaluation

#

of these (EHR) applications” [31]. These authors point out that serious structural barriers” exist which are unrelated to technology. They recommend that researchers should isolated indicators or variables to explain some of the socio and cultural incentives (or impediments) of moving towards eHealth, particularly where “legal and financial incentives provide little motivation to share information across institutions, which is critical to improving patient outcomes as well as efficiency” [32].

Prior scientific research has shown the emphasis of most HTA studies have focused on clinical aspects of health technologies, rather than economic, patient-related, and organisational aspects. There are some notable exceptions. Danish HTAs, for example, have a wider scope compared with HTAs in other countries, as patient-related and organisational dimensions are included. Evaluating HTA institutions in nine countries (Australia, Canada, Denmark, Netherlands, New Zealand, Norway, Sweden, UK and USA) the authors found that Denmark and Sweden scored highest for including organisational issues, with the USA scoring lowest. [13].

The range of health technologies within eHealth is also complex. ICTs, medical devices, imaging/monitoring technology, personalised medicines, drug discovery and diagnostics, can all be included, particularly as hardware (i.e. devices), software (applications) and communications (i.e. broadband/Internet) can be merged into the same product. Since the relationship between ICT and eHealth is complex, with HTA as an evaluation process further complicated by different practices across the EU27, the TEMPEST database is a useful tool to isolate indicators/variables for crosscountry comparisons. For example, ICT infrastructure and penetration varies widely across the EU27 [33, 34]. Similarly, the application and use of health technologies also varies, with Denmark, Sweden and the Netherlands reported as leaders in eHealth [35]. Understanding the granularity of data across different socio-political, economic and technical environments offers a more useful approach to decision-making for policy-makers than simply treating the EU as a large geographical region with over 501+ million citizens.

## 3.3. ICT and eHealth indicators

From a pool of over 150 quantitative indicators obtained from reputable data sources, 13 indicators were selected covering ICT infrastructure and penetration and eHealth applications and use among health professionals (not patients) (see Table 1). Building on prior research we adopt the distinction between General Purpose Technologies (GPTs) which cover ICTs such as broadband and Internet infrastructure used by the general population [6] and eHealth applications, which are used solely for health purposes by either health professionals and/or citizens/patients [55, 56]. The European Commission recognises that eHealth as both a policy and a practice is dependent upon building a strong pan-European ICT capability where broadband and Internet is available to all citizens [3].

There are now over 15,000 health applications for mobile devices [67]. However, adoption and diffusion of these applications will only increase if large numbers citizens have access to ICT infrastructure and devices [25]. Currently, there is wide variation among citizens’ access and use of ICT both across and within EU Member States [28]. In parallel, variations also exist for eHealth usage [30]. From our list of indicators in Table 1, the first five broadly cover ICT with the remaining eight for eHealth.

The European Commission and non-government organizations (NGOs) regularly collect quantitative data which measure country population access, connectivity and usage of technology (see Appendix 1). These datasets are publicly available and provide an excellent source for conducting cross-country comparisons for a variety of areas, including health. Prior studies have looked at ICT across EU Member States on the digital divide [6, 7, 8]. This work has utilized indicators such as Broadband

penetration (as a % total population) (brdPent), % of households with a broadband connection(hoBrdBd), % of population who are regular Internet users (popIntUs),% of households with an internet connection (hoIntCon) and % of individuals using a laptop via wireless connection(lapAcInt) to provide a general picture of how citizens are accessing and using GPTs.

Our study builds on this prior work on the digital divide by addressing the important EU policy domain of eHealth. These indicators give an aggregated country view of ICT which is important for enabling policy-makers to gain a broader understanding of ICT profile at the country level, often with a view to benchmarking competitiveness [33, 34]. However, ICT indicators by themselves say little about the move towards eHealth as they can only provide a general picture of citizens’ levels of access and usage to GPTs. So to gain an understanding of eHealth, it is important to search for more specific health IT indicators [55, 56].

Currently, there is a recognized shortage of relevant and useful eHealth indicators for benchmarking within and across countries [25, 27]. Many academic studies focus on important topics such as tracking eHealth adoption at the regional or organizational level [18, 19, 20, 21 ,22]. These studies provide excellent examples of how eHealth applications, such as EHRs are introduced into healthcare organizations. A recent US study pointed to the need to increase connectivity since only 9 % of hospitals claim to have any form of EHR and only 1.5 % of US hospitals have adopted a comprehensive, hospital-wide system. Broadband penetration is imperative to increasing this figure [20]. Across the EU, EHR availability and usage differs widely, with the Scandinavian countries ahead of many other EU Member States [55].

ICT indicators such as, % of households with a broadband connection (hoBrdBd), and % of households with an internet connection (hoIntCon) point to the potential for enhancing eHealth among citizens [24]. Political support for eHealth, encouraging citizens to become actively engaged in their health, need to be supported through increased affordable access among the population to ICT infrastructure [2].

GPT indicators provide a backdrop to a country's ICT infrastructure [6]. Since cross-country statistics were not found for eHealth access and usage among the general population, we utilized data on health professionals. To gain a deeper insight into eHealth, we selected a range of indicators measuring the availability and usage of health technologies by General Practitioners (GPs). We found two main limitations in the data sources. First, eHealth is still a relatively new concept within the health profession, with many GPs using only basic ICTs within their practices. Few studies have been

carried out which provide a reliable data set. Second, data collection on eHealth within these communities of practice remains scarce, with even fewer studies on cross-national comparisons. We therefore selected indicators on ICT infrastructure within GP practices and availability and use of eHealth technologies for storing and sharing patient data.

While many GPs have access to ICTs, it is important to look at how these technologies are used by them [55, 56]. Thus, our indicators included, Use of broadband in European GP practices (brdBndGP), Use of computers in European GP practices (comtPrac) and Use of a computer during consultations (comtCons). A more recent development is how ICTs are used in front line health service delivery particularly for exchanging medical/patient data. We identified two indicators for this purpose: Electronic storage of individual medical patient data (elecStor) and Electronic recording and storage of individual adm. patient data (elecRec). A cross-country comparison of our 13 indicators is given in Table 2. Here we observe wide variations between the EU27 with Denmark, Finland, Netherlands and Sweden, all scoring above 30% for Broadband penetration (as a % total population)(brdPent) and above 80% for Use of broadband in European GP practices(brdBndGP), respectively. This contrasts with very low figures of under 12% for Bulgaria and 11.2% Romania forbrdPent, and 23% and 5.3% for brdBndGP respectively.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Multivariate Analysis

The EU region has a population of over 501 million people, with countries of vastly different population sizes and the process of data collection needs to be consistent across all EU Member States. The same indicator must be compared using the same data set, especially as definitions and measures vary across organizations, for example health expenditure as a percentage of GDP . The OECD and other reputable organizations that collect health and ICT data (i.e. World Health Organization, World Bank and The European Commission) recognize the obvious methodological difficulties of collecting country-wide data, and continue to improve this activity. It is outside the scope of this paper to enter into a methodological debate about this issue, suffice to say that the data is from the most reputable and robust sources currently available.

The data that was gathered for the TEMPEST database was used to provide comparative analysis on each country's investment and readiness for eHealth using Principal Component Analysis (PCA). The precision of a sample statistic is how closely it can approximate a population. It is necessarily an estimated value in practice, since the population value is generally unknown. While standard errors may occur in the collection of data across countries, it is assumed that any fluctuation caused by chance is similar across all sources. Another issue with any analysis on Europe is that there are only 27 subjects that can be asked each question. Much has been written about sample size for PCA analysis, resulting in many ‘rules-of-thumb’ [41, p.6]. Assumptions have to be made if any results are to be made credible, namely: large enough to yield reliable estimates of the correlations among the variables; statistical inference is improved if the variables are multivariate normal; absence of outliers among the samples; collinearity among the variables, and no extreme degree or singularity among the variables [42].

Three tests were initially run to test the validity of the data that had been collected: Bartlett; Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) and Cronbach’s alpha. In the Bartlett test (see Table A1), the null hypothesis (H<sub>0</sub>) is that the inter-correlation matrix comes from a population in which the variables are an identity matrix. Here the computed p-value (<0.0001) is lower than the significance level alpha (0.05) causing H to be rejected. The KMO test provides a measure of helping to determine whether a sample size is large enough. If close to 1, then the variables are measuring a common factor, the closer to zero and there is no common factor at all. The KMO value for this sample is 0.854 (see Table A2), meaning conducting any PCA will extract a fairly high amount of the total variance. Whilst Cronbach’s alpha is not a statistical test, it does measure the data’s coefficient of reliability (or consistency), showing how closely related a set of items are as a group [44]. Tests that have normally distributed scores are more likely to have high reliability estimates [45]. With our data we obtained a high value of 0.951, which reliably suggests that these items measure an underlying (or latent) construct.

## 5 Cluster Analysis

This process starts by calculating the dissimilarity between the N objects, and clustering together those that minimize a given agglomeration criterion, thus creating a class comprising these two objects. Then the dissimilarity between this class and the N-2 other objects is again calculated, and the next two objects or classes of objects whose clustering together minimizes the agglomeration criterion are then clustered together. This process continues until all the objects have been clustered. It is then possible to choose groups of these classes by truncating the tree at a given level, (in this paper, the most appropriate truncation suggested four cluster groups).

Agglomerative Hierarchical Clustering (AHC) was used to generate both of the dendograms that were used in this paper: the first (Figure A1 in the Appendix) used the factor scores that had been generated from the PCA, the second (Figure A2 in the Appendix) used the original 13 variables. This is a technique that group’s data into clusters when no prior information about their structure is available. It is not a statistical method; the resulting solution cannot be assigned any measures of uncertainty. This is an iterative classification method that works from the dissimilarities between the objects that are being grouped together. These successive clustering operations produce a binary clustering tree (dendogram), whose root is the class that contains all the observations and represents a hierarchy of partitions. Cluster membership is assessed by calculating the total sum of squared deviations from the mean of a cluster. By doing this, it aggregates the groups so that within-group inertia increases as little as possible, keeping the clusters homogeneous. This criterion can only be used in cases of Euclidian distance [49].

## 5.1 Analysis of the Dendogram Generated from Factor Scores

Four groups emerge from running AHC on the rotated factor scores. The first group that was generated included Denmark and the Netherlands, two countries with the most similar eHealth profiles. These countries are generally considered leaders in eHealth [55]. With Sweden, Finland and the UK, Denmark and the Netherlands, these countries form a larger grouping, which have strong ICT infrastructures which contribute to their move towards eHealth. With the exception of the UK, and to a lesser extent, the Netherlands, Denmark, Sweden and Finland all have very low population sizes, with their healthcare systems operating as regional structures, unlike their larger country counterparts with some cities the same size of these smaller countries. The United Kingdom has invested heavily in recent years on national eHealth initiatives. While it scores highly in eHealth applications and usage, it falls behind Denmark, the Netherlands and Sweden on ICT infrastructure and penetration, with a score of 28.4% for Broadband penetration (brdpent) compared with a highest country score of 36.2% (Netherlands) and 70% for % of population who are regular Internet users (popIntUs) compared with 83% for both the Netherlands and Sweden.

In this second group we can observe that Germany and Belgium have the most similar profiles. One country which at first sight seems an outlier in this grouping is Estonia, if only because of its size and geography. Positioned next to Latvia and near to Finland and Lithuania, Estonia among those countries making significant inroads into eHealth, with some of the highest scores for Use of computers in European GP practices (comPrac) at 100%, Electronic recording and storage of individual adm. patient data (elecRec) at 98% and Use of a computer during consultations (comtCons) at 94%. While ICT infrastructure and penetration were lower, the move towards eHealth by health professionals was a significant development. Increasing ICT availability and use among the general population will further improve the country's eHealth profile. The larger countries including Germany, Belgium, France and Austria, all have mature healthcare systems, although their adoption and diffusion of eHealth technologies is slower compared with Denmark, the Netherlands and

#

Sweden. Countries in this grouping with large population sizes benefit from mature health systems, yet display a relative disadvantage of conservatism and caution in rolling out eHealth initiatives. For example, France spends 11% of GDP on health [54] which is the highest across the EU27, but is an average performer across the 13 indicators, with particularly low scores in Electronic exchange of patient data for at least one purpose (elecExDa) and percentage of GP practices having websites (prWebSit).

The third group of countries comprise a diverse mix with varying ICT penetration and availability levels and eHealth access and usage. With the exception of Ireland and Slovenia, countries in this grouping demonstrate relatively low ICT scores, yet higher eHealth scores. Slovakia scored only 10.9% for broadband penetration (brdPent) which was the lowest for this indicator of the 27 EU Member States, yet scored 95% for use of computers in GP practices (compPrac) and 90% electronic recording and storage of individual adm. patient data (elecRec). Conversely, Ireland shows higher ICT scores of 20% for broadband penetration (brdPent), 57% for % of population who are regular Internet users (at least once a week) (popIntUs) and 63% % of households with an internet connection (hoIntCon), yet scores of 73% for use of computers in GP practices (compPrac) and 63% for electronic recording and storage of individual adm. patient data (elecRec).

The fourth group of countries showed similarities, despite their different population sizes, economies and health systems. Malta appears to be an outlier in the group since it is not partnered with any of these countries, unlike the close relationship between Poland and Latvia. Malta is the smallest EU Member State with under half a million inhabitants. Our cluster analysis shows Malta to score relatively well on ICT indicators, but less well on eHealth availability and use. This group shows that Greece and Cyprus have similar profiles, perhaps unsurprisingly given the political make-up of these two EU Member States. Romania is the least digitally developed country in this group. This country had low scores across the spread of indicators so its overall ICT profile needed significant development in all sectors, not just in eHealth. For example, Broadband penetration as % of total population (brdPent) was 11.2 compared with a high of 36.2% in the Netherlands. The percentage of individuals using wireless internet away from home to access the internet was only 2%, compared with a high of 31% in Luxembourg. More encouraging figures for eHealth technologies showed a score for Romania of 65.8% for Use of computers in European GP practices, compared with a high of 100% for both Estonia and Finland. The frontrunners in eHealth, Denmark, the Netherlands and Sweden, all have well developed ICT infrastructures and mature health social and information systems. Denmark has a closely integrated health and informatics community, leading in indicators such as broadband penetration, population who are frequent users of the internet, households with broadband connection, population covered by mobile cellular network, electronic storage of individual medical patient data, and electronic exchange of patient data for at least one purpose. Similarly, the Netherlands leads in indicators of percentage of households with an internet connection, percentage of households with a broadband connection, percentage of population who are regular Internet users (at least once a week) and percentage of population who use eGovernment services. The use of eGovernment services in the context of eHealth is a relevant issue, particularly as citizens and health professionals alike can search and retrieve health information both for clinical and non-clinical purposes. Sweden also leads in indicators of percentage of individuals using a mobile phone to access the Internet, fixed Internet subscribers (per 100 people) and percentage of persons employed with ICT specialist skills. Other eHealth frontrunners include Finland and the UK, two countries with vastly different population sizes. Finland has made significant inroads into eHealth with its eHealth roadmap of 2007, which followed an earlier strategy for utilising information technology in the field of health and social welfare in 1996. Since then, Finland has the highest scores for obtaining patient consent for data storage and transfer, percentage of population seeking health information on injury, disease and nutrition and computer in the consultation room. In the UK, with a population of over 60 million people, the English National Health Service (NHS) has invested heavily in ICT in healthcare. This country had fewer leading indicator scores, yet was making progress across several areas, including, use of computers in consultation rooms, percentage of persons employed with ICT user skills and electronic exchange of patient data for at least one purpose.

The followers in eHealth comprised 6 countries, with Estonia leading in eHealth access and use. This country has been active in developing its eHealth policy framework with its National Health Development Plan initiated in 2009. The Estonian Information Society Strategy 2013 and its implementation plan are directly linked to the Health Strategy [34]. Although Estonia is not grouped as a frontrunner in eHealth in our study, it is one of the leading east European countries. Luxembourg was grouped in this cluster but, unlike Estonia, with its acceleration into eHealth, it scored well on health indicators, such as, per capita total expenditure on health and per capita government expenditure on health and also on ICT indicators, percentage of persons employed with ICT user skills and percentage of persons employed with ICT specialist skills. eHealth in Luxembourg is in a phase of transition. New healthcare reform law is in preparation focusing on the legal and organisational framework of the future eHealth platform including its applications. An eHealth Agency will be in charge of the platform and the interoperability aspects. As the smallest EU Member State, issues of interoperability as patient data is shared across different EU health jurisdictions will become increasingly relevant.

The followers in this group also include Germany, France, Belgium and Austria, all closely linked by geography and all with mature health infrastructures. Germany scored well on health indicators, with the highest number of hospital beds per 10,000 population and general government expenditure on health as a % of total government expenditure, yet strict laws on privacy and security of patient data were in evidence, with the lowest ranks for GPs connecting to different types of health actors (other GPs and health authorities). France scored highest for its health spend as a percentage of GDP, yet very low for eHealth indicators of connecting to different types of health actors (notably pharmacies) and obtaining patient consent for data storage and transfer. Interestingly, France has numerous well stocked pharmacies so the impetus to move towards electronic prescription services is less of a priority than for countries with less well developed health infrastructures. Like Germany and France, Belgium has a mature health system, with the highest number of practising physicians per 100,000 inhabitants. This country has a complex political structure with three main regions, Brussels, Wallonia and Flanders. Austria was making progress with eHealth and had the highest percentages for basic public services available online to both citizens and enterprises. This group of countries, labelled followers, operates within a mature health marketplace which is conservative and bureaucratic and possibly less agile and flexible in driving through eHealth policies.

The leapfroggers form the largest group of 9 countries, with many located in central and eastern Europe. We use the term leapfrogging to describe countries where eHealth develops in parallel to their ICT infrastructure. Bulgaria, for example, has the second highest percentage of the population covered by a mobile cellular network. Similarly, Italy has one of the highest number of mobile cellular subscriptions across the EU27 Member States. Our cluster analysis shows these countries have relatively low level infrastructure and penetration of ICTs, but are making progress with eHealth. Bulgaria is an interesting example, with three indicators: GP practices receiving professional IT support (proSuprt), Use of computers in European GP practices (ComtPrac) and Electronic recording and storage of individual adm. patient data (elecRec) all scoring above 90%. This was among the highest scores for all countries. So while ICT profile may be low across the country as a whole, the eHealth policy or road-map for Bulgaria compares favourably with other EU Member States. Hungary scored 100% for Electronic recording and storage of individual adm. patient data (elecRec), which is higher than Germany at 92%, Belgium at 83% and France at 74%. Examples of mHealth suggest that rather than investing in conventional ICTs, i.e. personal computers, countries with less developed health and eHealth systems are more likely to leapfrog this stage of the ICT pathway, by moving directly towards mobile devices. Other countries, including, Spain, Slovenia, Hungary, the Czech Republic and Slovakia, also had high scores for population covered by mobile cellular networks. So even where the number of fixed telephone lines was low, mobile devices offer a fast and cheap method of transferring and receiving health data (i.e. for GP appointments, prescriptions, test results, etc). Although this study focuses mostly on more traditional ICT indicators, for which harmonized data is available, the pace at which mHealth is developing will further complicate the health technology landscape. One potential advantage of leapfroggers is that less developed health infrastructures may allow for greater opportunties for creativity and innovation, unlike in more traditional, established health systems.

The laggards comprise 7 countries with varying levels of ICT penetration and availability. Romania has very low scores for many health, social and technical indicators, including, total health expenditure as a percentage of GDP, density of physicians per 10 000 population, percentage of basic public services available online and Internet users per 100 people. In Romania the Health Reform Law 95/2006 established the re-organisation of healthcare. The law requires the Ministry of Health to create an integrated information system for public health management. Romania is developing

eHealth plans for patient summaries and EHRs, ePrescription and telemedicine. Romania has coordinated its legislation with EU requirements which appears to be a tentative step in the right direction for eHealth. However, the gap between legislation and implementation is wide. Cyprus has a high private expenditure on health compared with low general government expenditure on health as a percentage of total government expenditure. It also has low total expenditure on health as a percentage of GDP. Malta has the highest percentage of government services for citizens fully internet connection. By drilling down on specific data points at the country level, we note that asymmetries emerge where ICT infrastructure has not kept pace with government ICT. So for citizens to be able to access and use eHealth and eGovernment services, it is essential that broadband and Internet penetration is extended across the population.

## 6. Discussion

The two dimensions of ICT penetration and availability and eHealth access and usage enable us to draw some interesting insights into the technological infrastructure in healthcare across 27 EU Member States. The relationship between general purpose technologies - GPTs and IT applications used within healthcare (eHealth) allow us to gain a deeper understanding of a country’s eHealth profile. While eHealth is a multi-stakeholder phenomenon, i.e. supported by government agencies and adopted by healthcare professionals and citizens alike, this study has focused primarily on the relationship between ICT infrastructure at the country level and the adoption and utilisation of health IT by health professionals, notably primary care physicians (doctors/GPs). Our findings point to four factor clusters, frontrunner, follower, leapfrogger and laggard. Leaders are those countries which have strong profiles in both ICT and eHealth. The factor scores for Denmark, Netherlands and Sweden confirm prior academic studies that show these countries have made significant progress in moving towards eHealth [56].

While our data does not include socio-economic and political indicators, these areas also influence the adoption and diffusion of eHealth across countries. Policy-makers can draw comparisons on the varying levels of GDP spend on healthcare and eHealth profiles of countries (40). For example, recent figures show that Denmark spends around 11.1% of GDP on health, which is comparable with France and Germany at 11.6%. Sweden has a lower spend at 9.6% which is the same as the UK. However, the Netherlands has recently increased its GDP spend on health at 12%, which is now one of the highest in Europe. In contrast, Finland, which is another leader in eHealth, spends around 8.9% of GDP on health, which is less than Italy (9.1%) Greece (10.2%) Slovenia (9%) Slovakia (9%) and Portugal (10.7%) [64]. Similarly, Estonia, which is a country making steady progress in eHealth, spends only 6.3% of GDP on health. Questions can therefore be raised about economic factors, such as annual health spend, and eHealth profile, and why countries with modest or lower spends are apparently making inroads towards eHealth. Research from northern European countries, such as Sweden and Denmark demonstrates that more indicators are used, such as social and organizational aspects, when evaluating or benchmarking health IT [13]. The inclusion of additional indicators/variables in HTA is seen to provide policy-makers with a stronger evidence-base for decision-making on eHealth [69]. Further, the Scandinavian countries also have stronger links between health and social services, which create greater synergies and integration of services from both care providers [76]. While our study uses ICT and eHealth indicators, it also shows wide variation between EU Member States, which can be further explained by socio-economic differences among countries.

The countries in the follower and leapfrogger groups are an interesting mix since they each possess opposite strengths and weaknesses at varying levels. The followers which include large countries like Germany, Belgium, France and Austria all have well developed health systems, yet also demonstrate a degree of conservatism in relation to moving towards eHealth. Germany has an excellent medical technology sector and scores highly on ICT indicators. It scored less well on some eHealth indicators, notably, % GP practices having websites (prWebSit) at 21% and electronic storage of individual medical patient data (elecStor) at 3.2%. One study [65] found that, despite 99% of German GP practices claiming to use a computer, only 59% of the German GP practices were connected to the Internet. This result is considerably below the EU average of 69%. Broadband connections were found in only 40% of the GP practices, as compared to about 50% on average in the EU27. In Germany the transfer of electronic patient data via networks or the Internet is low. Only 3.2% of German GPs exchange administrative data with other care providers and only 4% exchange administrative data with reimburses. In Germany, not even 1% of the GP practices reported making use of ePrescribing. Furthermore, apart from the three frontrunners Denmark, Sweden and the Netherlands, adoption rates for ePrescribing were never higher than 5%.

Other countries in the followers group also display contrasting levels across the 13 indicators. Estonia scores above average on ICT indicators but has made considerable progress in recent years in eHealth. Table 9 shows Estonia moving from a follower to a leapfrogger with the country in the top quartile for several eHealth indicators (see Table 2). Luxembourg performs above average on ICT indicators but below average on eHealth. As one of the smallest EU Member States, Luxembourg displays some mixed results, with relatively low rates of computer and internet usage at 80% and 64% respectively, and average results for eHealth indicators. It scores relatively well for electronic storage of individual medical patient data (elecStor) at 3.7%. As a small principality, Luxembourg citizens are more likely to engage in cross-border health [57] with other EU Member States for diagnostic and treatment purposes, but IT systems for patient data exchange are yet to be developed [66].

The leapfroggers comprise a diverse group with contrasting ICT and eHealth scores. Leapfrogger countries are those where aggregated eHealth scores may be higher than aggregated ICT scores, with Hungary, for example, scoring 100% for electronic recording and storage of individual adm. patient data (elecRec) yet low ICT scores for GPTs. Another country, Slovakia, has a population of over 5.4 millions, similar to Denmark and Finland, albeit at a different stage in its eHealth journey. Recent figures show that 96% of Slovak GP practices use a computer, with an Internet connection is available in 44% of GP practices, and only 15% of practices with a broadband connection [63]. Leapfrogger countries are unlikely to follow a similar pathway towards eHealth with countries in other groupings, as their decisions to invest in the most current technological infrastructure and applications will see them by-passing more traditional routes, i.e. making significant investments in computer hardware, such as personal computers. Moving directly towards mHealth (i.e. mobile phone health applications) is more likely. This is witnessed by Portugal, which currently has the fourth highest number of the population with 3G enabled phones in the EU after Sweden, Finland and Luxembourg. Italy holds seventh place. Interestingly, Estonia is last in this list at only 9% of the population with 3G enabled phones compared with 96% for Sweden as leader [67]. Although a more detailed discussion of mHealth is outside the scope of this paper, the growth of health applications for download onto the mobile phone is fast-growing, and will provide a fruitful area for future research [70].

Our cluster analysis is useful for identifying variations in technology infrastructure [59] and for grouping countries into leaders, followers, leapfroggers and laggards. Our multivariate approach using 13 indicators positions Denmark, Netherlands and Sweden as eHealth leaders which supports academic studies on the digital divide [6, 7]. It also positions several central and eastern European

#

countries as eHealth laggards with lower scores in both factors, suggesting they need to develop their eHealth roadmap by prioritizing eHealth projects. For example, Greece has made significant progress on the storage of administrative patient data and medical patient data, but slower progress on eprescribing, the transfer of medical patient data to other carers and the use of computers during consultations. Given the current economic climate in Greece, health policy-makers will need to identify the 'low hanging fruit' (i.e. eHealth projects with quick gains) rather than continuing with eHealth projects which risk financial cutbacks without delivering measurable results. So while Electronic Patient Records are a policy objective in Greece, it is unlikely that data will be collected and stored for the entire record, i.e. administrative demographics, electronic medication record, medical history, laboratory results, radiology reports and emergency care data [68].

Despite the concept of a European Union, our study points up significant variations across the two factors, where countries even within one of the four clusters are vastly different in terms of population size, political structure, economic conditions, demographics, health system, culture and ICT infrastructure. We recognize that selecting only 13 indicators/variables which are related to ICT penetration and availability and eHealth access and use limits our discussion to a high level country analysis rather than a more in-depth regional or local interpretation. The data only reflect an aggregated country view, although significant variations are found in eHealth profile across regions. While these limitations do not allow for a regional and local analysis of eHealth, particularly at the health authority and organizational (i.e. hospital) levels, this paper contributes to the relative shortage of studies on health and IT at a country-level unit of analysis. Our results demonstrate the EU is not an homogeneous region where trans-European eHealth policy can be implemented along the same timeline in all EU Member States. Rather, each country must develop and implement its own bespoke eHealth Roadmap, which will undergo several iterations depending on socio-political and economic factors. ICT is therefore only one part of the eHealth jigsaw.

Our research contributes to growing calls from policy-makers and academics to provide more crosscountry studies on health systems (43, 52). Currently, policy-makers in the EU are seeking to identify more robust quantitative indicators for benchmarking health, IT and eHealth [40, 58]. The recognition that current data sources do not provide an adequate basis for measurement for individual and crosscountry analysis has led to initiatives to bring together key stakeholders to develop new indicators [27]. New indicators/variables need to reflect the changing landscape of healthcare and technology, particularly as economic austerity in Europe confronts policy-makers with hard choices about health priorities.

With the rapid pace of technological change, such as Cloud computing and mobile applications (47, 79) policy-makers are exploring ways to reduce health budgets while improving health service delivery (2,3). However, the expansion of eHealth will require greater EU cross-border governance and regulation in patient data privacy and security [71]. Further research may therefore consider health and IT asymmetries within and across EU Member States, to identify specific eHealth priorities for each country, region and even local area. While the authors are cautious about the transferability of ‘best practice’ examples from country to country, or region to region, the careful section of quantitative indicators for comparative analysis provides a unique tool for policy-makers seeking to learn lessons about health IT outcomes (40).

## 7. Concluding remarks

This study has sought to use multivariate statistical methods to analyse eHealth across 27 EU Member States. The TEMPEST methodology using cluster analysis delineates four groupings where ICT adoption and diffusion varies widely among countries. The leading countries appear to have

developed greater synergies between health and eHealth policy, where clinicians in Denmark and Sweden, for example, have followed a series of political reforms to embed ICT into health and social care [72, 73]. These countries also have smaller populations which suggest that have a regional advantage in national health IT policy implementation unlike the larger countries in the EU, such as France, Germany and the UK. While this study presents a snapshot of ICT and eHealth country profiles, our findings suggest that health IT is a fast-moving space with opportunities for countries to potentially leapfrog traditional technology pathways with examples such as mobile technology. This is already occurring in African countries with under-developed healthcare systems, such as Tanzania and Kenya with some success [77]. Other EU countries with mature health systems may also consider how mobile technologies can supplement existing health IT infrastructure to improve health outcomes for patients. This is likely to involve making significant changes to traditional health service delivery, with fewer visits to physicians’ surgeries and more home care [56].

Understanding issues of incentives to adopt health IT will therefore be critical, particularly if technical change involves new working practices [29]. Countries considered as eHealth laggards may prioritize health IT projects to achieve quick-wins, especially as economic austerity will seriously curtail the implementation of a more ambitious eHealth policy. This may involve investing in ICT infrastructure to increase broadband availability and usage among citizens to strengthen mobile health, or mHealth [79]. In conclusion, this study reveals apparent differences between EU Member States in their move towards eHealth. It offers a foundation for further research on national, regional and local health IT policy, focusing on specific eHealth infrastructure and applications, e.g. EHRs, Telemedicine and ePrescribing. Multi-level studies will thus provide EU policy-makers with a more solid basis for decision-making on eHealth in the $2 1 ^ { \mathrm { s t } }$ century.
