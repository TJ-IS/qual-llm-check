# Organizing technologies of vision: Making the invisible visible in media-laden observations

Alexander Styhre

Dept. of Business Administration, School of Business, Economics, and Law, University of Gothenburg Box 600, 8 40530 Gothenburg, Sweden

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 18 November 2009 Received in revised form 12 January 2010 Accepted 12 January 2010

Keywords: Media theory Vision Matter Media-laden vision

## a b s t r a c t

Sociomaterial practices produce, in many cases, opportunities for different modes of vision that further structure social practices. The very organization of the technologies of vision, or visual media, thus demands scholarly attention. Drawing on media theory, the article suggests that the materiality observed through the use of technology is not detached from these technologies but constitutes instances inextricably bound up with the particular technology. Following Barad (2003), matter is a practice, a set of doings accomplished through the mobilization of a heterogeneous body of resources. In the technosciences, species of nature (e.g. laboratory animals) are shaped to comply with operational hypotheses and underlying assumptions. Similarly, in financial trading, markets are constituted through the use of ensembles of technologies of vision. In this view, most vision is media-laden, intimately associated with the materialities organized to accomplish particular modes of seeing. Such mediated vision is a relevant object of study for organization theorists.

## 1. Introduction

A series of organization studies takes into account the structuring nature of technology in a variety of organizational practices (Arnold, 2003; Barley, 1986; Cousins & Robey, 2005; Kallinikos, 2005, 2009; Lanzara & Patriotta, 2001; Leonard-Barton, 1988; Leonardi & Barley, 2008; Martin & Kambil, 1999; Orlikowski, 1992, 2000; Orlikowski, Yates, Okamura, & Fujimoto, 1993; Pentland & Feldman, 2007;

Ross & Chiasson, 2005). Technology, and more specifically media, constitutes an umbrella term for a variety of assemblages of interrelated tools, devices, materialities, and practices. In everyday language, the term technology takes on the meaning of a free-standing artefact, which is possible to manipulate and control. However, from an analytical perspective, it is more useful to think of technology as an irreducible element of what Suchman (2007) and Orlikowski (2007) call sociomaterial practices—i.e. practices that are inextricably bound up with specific technologies, tools, and other relevant material resources. In this view, organizing is a form of sociomaterial practice, shaped and formed by the technologies used during the activities, recursively constituting the technologies through their very use and affected by the material conditions under which the work is conducted. On the one hand, technol ogies set the boundaries for what may be said and done while on the other, technology is always mal leable and permeable, open to negotiations, local interpretations, and uses (Bijker, Hughes, & Pinch 1987). Organizing qua sociomaterial practice is thus always a matter of human–machine or human–technology interactions. Practices are bound up with material resources and know-how and skills are inextricably connected with the tools used. For the computer programmer, an intimate knowledge of the functioning of the computer and the computer code is a sine qua non for skilful performance (Case & Piñeiro, 2006; Ullman, 1997); laboratory researchers do not primarily examine brute nature ‘‘in the raw” but instead examine snippets of fabricated nature that are intimately connected with the laboratory equipment and technologies in use (Pinch, 2008, p. 463).

However, as a theory of organizing, the concept of sociomaterial practice remains somewhat unarticulated. Orlikowski (2007) suggests that ‘‘[t]he social and the material are constitutively entangled in everyday life” and proposes the term constitutive entanglement to escape giving privi lege to either the social or the material and to embrace ‘‘[a] form of mutual reciprocation”; ‘‘there is no social that is not also material, and no material that is also social”, Orlikowski (2007, p. 1437) assures us. Although Orlikowki provides many examples of how this ‘‘constitutive entanglement” enfolds social and material practices—the innovative Google search engine developed by Sergey Brin and Lawrence Page being one example—the theory of sociomaterial practice is still relatively crude in terms of being based on the two ‘‘grand” concepts of the social and the material. In Leonardi and Barley’s (2008, p. 159) formulation, the line of demarcation between these two terms ‘‘[l]ooks less solid up close than it does from the distance”. These two concepts are ‘‘black boxes” that need to be subjected to more detailed analysis. Expressed differently, these are two analytical terms which are useful when sketching broader theoretical frameworks which nevertheless remain too general and too imprecise to be of any significant help in empirical studies. At the same time, Orlikowski (2007, p. 1445) suggests that ‘‘sociomaterial assemblages” are ‘‘[f]leeting, fragile, and fragmented, entailing uncertainty and risk and producing intended and unintended outcomes”. Such statements can be further operationalized, lending themselves to empirical investigations; sociomaterial practices are to be examined qua practices, practical undertakings, rather than being a set of propositions of conjecture. This paper, following Leonardi and Barley (2008), thus seeks to make a contribution to a more elaborate theory of sociomaterial practice. However, contrary to Leonardi and Barley (2008), who use a series of philosophical terms such as materialism, idealism, and constructionism when formulating a theoretical framework for the entanglement of ‘‘technology and organizing”, the paper’s aim is to examine a specific form of sociomaterial practice, namely media-laden visua observations.

Unpacking the concept of sociomaterial practice demands that the elements constituting the socio material practice be examined and related. Adhering to a media theory view of matter, there is no detached, autonomous material substratum that can be accessed, instead observations are always mediated by necessity. Hanson (1958), examining the conflicting theories of Johannes Keppler and Tycho Brahe in astronomy, speaks of ‘‘theory-laden observations” in cases where the analytical framework provides the observer with solid theories that help him or her to see specific elements of matter: in this context, the term media-laden observations may be introduced. Following Karen Barad (2003), matter is produced through the use of particular apparatuses: ‘‘Apparatuses are dynamic (re)configurings of the world, specific agential practices/intra-actions/performances through which specific exclusion ary boundaries are enacted”, claims Barad (2003, p. 816. Emphasis in the original). In this view, says Barad (2003, p. 822), matter is not a thing but ‘‘a doing, a congealing of agency”. Matter, then, does not refer to ‘‘an inherent fixed property of abstract independently existing objects” but is instead a ‘‘materiality” and, perhaps better, a ‘‘materialization of phenomena”. Barad (2003) suggests that there is no matter outside of the experimental apparatus, the technoscientific system capable of producing “instances” of matter and “material becomings”. “Reality is not composed of things-in-themselves or things-behind-phenomena but ‘things’-in-phenomena”, summarizes Barad (2003, p. 817). ‘‘Things-inthemselves” implies an untenable realist position for Barad while ‘‘things-behind-phenomena” is an unnecessary Platonist mystification which assumes that there is a surface and an inner working of matter. Things-in-phenomena is the view that matter is produced through the very use of specific technological systems and visual media. Here, matter is a question of doings, of making the invisible visible, not primarily seeking the ‘‘really real reality” but accomplishing a shared framework for further action. Barad’s (2003) analytical framework invites us to think of visualization in fresh terms, in terms of the possibilities accomplished on the basis of a visual medium rather than its accuracy in a realist epistemology. Visual media help humans to see in order to consolidate resources and politica legitimacy (as in the case of the X-ray, helping the emerging field of orthopedic medicine to advance its position, see Warwick, 2005), not to venture into the elementary components of matter. Expressed differently, all vision is media-laden, that is, shaped and formed by the technologies and tools used in the work.

In this paper, the concept of media-laden vision will be used to examine how technologies of vision are capable of providing what is originally invisible with a visible form, thereby rendering certain targeted domains or entities open to inspection. Contemporary organizations use a variety of visual media in their dav-to-day work, including a variety of medical technologies such as X-rays (Golan. 2004: Pasveer, 2006; Warwick, 2005), brain scanning technologies like positron emission tomography (PET) (Cohn, 2004; Dumit, 2004), or magnetic resonance imaging (Alac, 2008; Beaulieu, 2002; Burri, 2008). The financial markets, for instance, also use technologies that visually illustrate the flows and movements of market transactions by using a combination of inferential statistics, mathematical algorithms, and forms of visualization (Buenza & Stark, 2004). While common-sense thinking conceives of these technologies and visual media as representing the underlving material substrata or actual events or occurrences, closer examination of these tools, based on a scepticism towards naïve realism, suggests that, rather than representing the underlying actual realities, they create images that are neither wholly detached from factual conditions nor entirely pure fabrications, instead providing instantiations of reality that lie in-between the factual and the fabricated—they are ‘‘‘things’-in-phenomena”. Like the so-called biomedia which were examined by Thacker (2004), these images represent a form of hybridity, neither solely representing nor being inscribed by technological means, but simultaneously being both. The key purpose of the technologies and visual media is to provide a common ground for further actions that will potentially produce desirable outcomes. For instance, in the case of brain scanning, the images of the human brain presented on the computer screen are not produced in isolation from the patient’s body, nor are they a fully accurate and unmediated representation of the activities of the brain. They are at one and the same time both, giving the radiologist and other medical professionals a visual framework from which they can make decisions and take action that is beneficial to the patient. Thus, the brain scan, in the first instance, is not used to produce an accurate and truly correct image of the brain. Instead, its images are useful in determining what therapies to prescribe and initiate. The technoscientific apparatus is thus developed to partly comply with the underlying materialities o the biological system examined, and to partly to take into account the operative hypotheses concerning the human body (Barad, 2003; Johnson, 2008). Using Gaston Bachelard’s (1984) concept of phénoménotechniques, suggesting that the operative and theoretical assumptions of an epistemic community are always embodied already in the scientific technologies employed, the images produced are not separated from the initial assumptions concerning the nature of the obiect being depicted. Another case is the financial markets where financial traders use mathematical formulae and algorithms which they believe to be capable of predicting market activities and outcomes; however, as soon as the majority of the traders are making use of the same algorithms, the actual behaviour will be produced on the basis of the model that was initially supposed to predict allegedly stochastic changes.

This paper is structured accordingly: firstly, the concept of media is reviewed. Secondly, the uses of media in various social practices are discussed. Thirdly, some critical reflections are made on the concept of media-laden vision. Fourthly, the relationship between media-laden vision and organization is addressed, and, finally, some theoretical and practical implications are also addressed.

## 2. Media theory

The literature on technology in the social sciences and philosophy is massive thus it is beyond the scope of this paper to review this corpus. Therefore, the concept of media is used to denote ensembles of technologies and social practices that are capable of storing, circulating, and modifying information and data (Gitelman, 2006, p. 7). In what has been called media theory (Hansen, 2006), beginning perhaps with Marshall McLuhan's (1964) influential work, media are made up of a variety of tools. technologies, and practices ranging from early human accomplishments such as cave paintings, through printed books, to more recent computer-based media. Media theory suggests, however, that such media are not only tools in the hands of humans, enabling new achievements, but that media also in fact, exert a strong influence on human thinking and action. For instance, the emergence of writing enabled significantly more abstract modes of thinking (Goody, 1977; Ong, 1982) while the more recent use of multimedia technologies that combine text, image, and sound (as in the case of movies) is radically transforming not only the way humans develop and share information and know-how but also their entire cognitive capacities (Bolter, 1996; Flusser, 2002). In addition, the use of media especially recent fashionable media, is having strong signaling effects. For instance, Brian Bloomfield’s study of the use of electronic patient records suggests that what is presented in a specific, mediated form is more credible than other (especially older) media: ‘‘Just as a typewritten script is usually taken to be more impressive than a handwritten one (leaving aside the issue of content) so too a patient record mobilized, scanned and displayed on a computer screen appears more impressive than a manual record with its ‘dog-eared’ corners and illegible notes” (Bloomfield, 1991, p. 717). During Barack Oba ma’s presidential campaign in 2008, commentators praised him for being able to exploit new ‘‘social media” to reach new groups of voters and for mobilizing young and energetic supporters. Using social networking sites such as Facebook and Twitter was not only an effective way of exploiting the possi bilities of the new media, it also helped to advance the image of Obama as a credible and foresighted politician.

One of the key discussions in media theory concerns the emergence of computer-based media which are gradually displacing a tradition in the West, which began during the mediaeval period whereby the printed page has served as the medium par préférence for intellectual effort (Eisenstein, 1983; Febvre & Martin, 1997). Critics emphasize that human cognition is shaped by the capacity to think on the basis of written texts and that the gradual loss of reading (reading books and magazines is becoming less and less frequent in the Western world) is a long-term threat to certain modes of thinking: “Print emphasizes the visual to the exclusion of other senses: electronic media emphasize sound and image. Perception and its effects are not necessarily on the same plane; the senses refer to each other, to work through the body” says Rajagopal (2006, p. 285). Landow (2006) continues to ad dress what he calls ‘‘digital text”, i.e. texts produced by digital (computer-based) media:

Digital text is fluid because, taking the form of codes, it can always be reconfigured, reformatted, rewritten. Digital text is hence infinitely adaptable to different needs and uses, and since it consist of codes that other codes can search, rearrange, and otherwise manipulate, digital text is also always open, unbordered, unfinished and unfinishable, capable of infinite extension. (Landow, 2006, p. 196)

Bolter (1996), too, points to the new possibilities and concerns deriving from the use of digital media, suggesting that the very perception of the self, as a coherent and unified site of agency and preferences, is affected by new media:

Word processing, numerical and textual databases, (verbal) electronic mail and newsgroups, and hypertext constitute a new writing space. Electronic writing, particularly hypertext, challenges some of the qualities of the printed or handwritten self. The self in electronic writing space is no longer the relatively permanent and univocal figure of the printed page. The electronic self is instead unstable and polyvocal. (Bolter, 1996, p. 112)

While the short-term consequences may be complicated to predict, these accounts suggest that media is by no means additional to, or emerging in parallel with, human subjectivities, but that new media are reformulating the capacities for human conditions. Without succumbing to a conser vative attitude whereby new media are rejected because they are new, there are reasons to think about the connections between media and major socio-psychological and cultural consequences. Anthropologists and historians have demonstrated the differences between literate and illiterate societies and there are reasons to believe that a society increasingly operating on the basis of media that favour images and sound over text will gradually change the cognitive capacities of its members (Flusser, 2002; Hayles, 2002).

In summary, media theory suggests that the technologies and tools for developing, storing, and distributing data, information, and know-how play a key role in society. When examining technologies of vision and visual media, it is important to keep in mind that all forms of media are more or less advanced forms of simultaneous inscription and description processes. This double nature of media is what makes it both epistemologically complex and practically useful.

## 3. Uses of media-laden vision

## 3.1. Technoscientific media

One of the key domains using visual media is the technosciences. Being a combination of the use of advanced technologies and equipment (including ‘‘technobiological systems” such as geneticallymodified laboratory animals, see Kohler (1994) and Rader (2004) and theoretical know-how, embedded in clinical research and theoretical work, there is a somewhat problematic recursive relationship between the technologies used and the theories used to explain the output from the use of the technologies. In Roth’s view (2009, p. 213), scientists have set themselves a difficult task in terms of not having the criteria to evaluate the outcomes independently from their actions. They are placed in what Roth calls a chicken-and-egg situation (i.e. one of radical uncertainty) wherein, in order to evaluate their actions, they have to draw on the outcomes of these actions; however, to evaluate the outcomes, they have to rely on their actions. Facing this dilemma—questioning either the outcomes or the actions (including the experimental system wherein they operate)—scientists tend to choose the output as the principal source of concern:

Experienced practitioners may question their observational actions, doubting what they see, but they normally take actions for granted in the sense that they take them as aligned with the goa intentions that had brought them forth. If an action has not realized its goals, it is reproduced often with a slight modification (researchers try again, implying it will work this time). (Roth, 2009, p. 329)

For instance, in Barley and Bechky’s (1994) study of laboratory technicians, failure was perceived as deriving from mistakes, malfunctions, or enigmas. Mistakes, often derived from inadequate training or experience, could be corrected while malfunctioning technologies or equipment could be handled; however, the residual category, the enigmas, puzzled the laboratory technicians. Roth (2009) suggests that research projects may continue for a significant period of time until researchers find themselves in a situation where they no longer fully know whether the actions taken produce the outcomes they anticipate:

Tools and instruments mediate practical action. Uncertainty therefore pertains not only to the things created in scientists’ action but also to the means of production (tools, instruments) that are used in, enable, and therefore mediate individual actions and the activity as a whole. Thus, uncertainty can be ascribed to a tool or instrument of a scientific production. (Roth, 2009, pp. 331–332)

While such situations are rare, there are certain methodologies or even research programmes that evaporate as these dilemmas cannot be resolved. In most cases, where research programmes are not abandoned, the researchers choose to believe in the technologies’ capacity to produce meaningfu images of the object under investigation.

Alac (2008), studying functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), a medical visualization technology producing images of brain activity, is a good example of how some aspects of the biological system are provided with a visual form: ‘‘fMRI images are ‘visual’ and ‘visible’ versions of what previously was not visual, inasmuch as they allow the practitioners to deal with the invisible in a practica manner”, claims Alac (2008, p. 504). However, this visuality is limited to professional groups that have long-term training and experience of understanding the images and what they indicate, as well as what therapies or actions to prescribe. It is a professional domain of jurisdiction to be able to be visually literate regarding fMRI plates, namely that of radiologists. Being able to establish what is called disciplinary objectivity (Megill, 1994; Timmermans, 2008), radiologists share the capacity not only to use the advanced technoscientific instruments but also to decode the images produced and to articulate adequate therapies. The communal vision of the radiologist is thus a highly distributed capacity, acquired during significant periods of training. In Alac’s (2008, p. 503) view, seeing is

[a] process situated at the intersection between instruments and technology, practices, settings, and the practitioners’ embodied accounts. It describes how the local management of seeing involves previous dealings and cumulative practices of know-how, and how it receives its rhetorical force by reference to the usual procedures of laboratory members. These procedures are nested in larger, historically evolving, socio-technical networks whose specifications are bound to the locally instantiated assemblages of instruments, embodied techniques, and everyday discourses in the laboratory

The radiologist’s gaze is thus bound up with visual media, historical trajectories, professional projects, and so forth. Yet, the question of what the radiologist actually sees has not yet been explained Alac (2008) suggests that what the radiologist sees is based on collectively enacted procedures and standards but she says relatively little of what the images show. Burri and Dumit (2008) share thi perspective and suggest, similar to Alac (2008), that ‘‘[v]isual representations cannot be understood in isolation from the pragmatic situation in which they are used . . . it is not enough to simply describe the things images depict of the meaning they reflect. We have to focus also on the textual arrangements and discursive practices within which these representations are embedded” (Burri & Dumit 2008, p. 300). However, rather than nourishing naïve beliefs in the ability of images to depict actual natures, scientists are highly aware of the complicated epistemological status of images. Leading scientific journals like Nature demand that authors describe what kind of Photoshop filters and processes have been applied before the images are published (Burri & Dumit, 2008, p. 305). As suggested by media theorists, digital images can be endlessly manipulated and it is thus important to account for the procedures used when producing the image

Prasad (2005) is implicitly suggesting that what the radiologist using fMRI is capable of seeing is both an image of an underlying material substratum and the very machinery per se. Using the concept of cyborg vision, Prasad says that this vision is always mediated, indeed enframed by the technology while the idea of a realist depiction of the human body has not been abandoned.

In the cyborg visuality regime, images have become bits of data in cyberspace that can be, and are, manipulated by human beings. This does not mean that within this new visual regime, claims towards realism of images are disbanded. If that were so, there would be no reason to have magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) radiological analyses. Cyborg visuality produced by MRI works within dif ferent frameworks of realism that do not seek mechanical reproduction of the observed objects(s). MR images produce different reconfigurations of the body, each of which provides a partial perspective of the body and together they constitute the MR radiological gaze. (Prasad, 2005, p. 310)

The radiologist’s gaze is thus always a gaze through and with the technoscientific apparatus. This mediated and enframed vision is thus strongly entangled with the technology – without the technology, there would be no radiologist’s gaze. The radiologist can choose between cyborg vision and no vision. This cyborg vision is also deeply paradoxical inasmuch as visual images invite common-sense thinking while this mode of thinking is declared incapable of explaining what is seen on the plates and images. As long as visual media remain confined to professional communities, this condition will not need to constitute a concern; however, as soon as, for instance, technoscientific images are brought into society, their status will become implicitly subject to questioning. In Dumit’s (2004) study of Positron Emission Tomography (PET) scanning of the human brain, there is the example of the trial of John Hinckley Jr., the man who shot Ronald Reagan in 1981, where PET images were used to support Hinckley’s case. The PET images could not, naturally, ‘‘speak for themselves” but needed to be accompanied by expert comments that drew on subjective interpretations of the images of Hinckley’s brain. Since no expert can say anything particularly credible regarding the connections between the image of the brain, Hinckley’s actual brain, the event of the shooting, and the legal allegations, the whole event evolved as a situation wherein complex social processes were supposed (hoped the defence) to be reduced to, and explained on the level of, a few blots on a photographic plate. Dumit (2004) exemplified:

[E]ven though the brain images are produced by people, they are coproduced by scientific machines, and it is the machines, especially computers, that leave their mark. Scientists . . . increasingly attempt to remove their marks from the image, even though they must still provide the text . . .. At the crux of this relationship between the image that (objectively) speaks for itself and the expert who (subjectively) speaks . . . is a desire by the court and by everyone else to reduce ambiguity, to make things clear, and clearly acceptable. (Dumit, 2004, p. 119)

Consonant with Daemmerich’s (1998) study of the difficulties involved in using DNA typing in court, Dumit (2004) demonstrates that technoscientific visual media are rather complicated to make use of in legal practice because what would seem to be ‘‘objective” representations of factual conditions is always open to professional judgment and evaluation the processes of which are in themselves questionable from a legal perspective. While truth in a disciplinary objectivity perspective is a matter of professional agreement, the legal system cannot rely on such expert witness unless credible connections can be made between the image/material substratum and actual behaviours and actions. Relatively rarely are such extended claims scientifically legitimate (see, for instance, Shostak & Conrad, 2008).

Cohn (2004) addresses what kind of knowledge claims may be made on the basis of images. Rather than seeing the technological apparatus and technology-mediated materiality, Cohn suggests that the proponents of brain scanning claim they are capable of seeing life itself. Cohn (2004, p. 55) says that brain scans are not seen as presenting ‘‘bare life” but as presenting ‘‘human existence in its totality”, the very essence of what makes us conscious and what is constitutive of what Cohn (2004), drawing on Agamben (1998), calls bios. This ambition, based on a far-driven scopophilia, is exemplified with reference to a textbook:

New imaging techniques make the internal world of mind visible . . .. As we enter the twenty-first century functional brain scanning machines are opening up the territory of the mind just as the first ocean-going ships once opened up the globe. (Carter, 1998, cited in Cohn, 2004, p. 56)

In this view, the ‘‘new imaging techniques” are supposedly capable of presenting the human mind in broad daylight. This enthusiasm, however, rests on a series of epistemological assumptions that reduce the human mind to a matter of the brain activities and flows that can be provided with a visua form:

[W]hat can be witness among researchers at the forefront of the technology is a claim that the mind is being mapped onto and into the brain, subsumed by a complex materialist paradigm, and so doing away once and for all with any legacy of dualism. The assertion is clear: that ideas of life and the mind will now be contained, enclosed, within the emerging science of the brain. (Cohn, 2004, p. 59)

The mind and human life qua consciousness is confined here to the visual media of this particular research programme. Not only are neurology scientists capable of producing evocative images of the human brain, they are also, en passant, claiming to be able to capture and illustrate life. The brain scanning technology is then not delimited to certain paradigmatic procedures, instead taking a leap into a far more complicated question, namely that of life per se. The visual media are unquestionably seductive in terms of enticing scientists and other experts to draw far-reaching conclusions.

## 3.2. Financial market trading media

While much of the discussion about visual media derives from scientific settings, there are also many other fields where technology-mediated visualization plays a key role in providing the invisible with a visible form. For instance, in financial traders’ work of predicting and anticipating market changes, there is great reliance on a variety of technologies and tools (Knorr Cetina & Bruegger, 2002; Willman, Fenton O’Creevy, Nicholson, & Soane, 2001). Buenza and Stark (2004, p. 390) suggest in their study of Wall Street financial traders that the tools used, a combination of statistical models and graphic representations, mathematical algorithms, and rules of thumb, were remarkably close to the instruments used by scientists. What these ensembles of instruments and tools share is their capacity to produce images of what is invisible to the naked human eye:

Scientific instruments, whether a radio telescope, a Geiger counter or a Petri dish, display phenomena that are often not visible to the naked eye. They reveal objects in space, radiation waves or miniscule bacteria that could otherwise not be discerned. Similarly, the trader’s tools derive their strength—persuasiveness in the former, profits in the latter—from original instrumentation. (Buenza & Stark, 2004, p. 390)

The competence of the high-flying financial trader lies in being able to discern tendencies in the marketplace and anticipating changes there before the other traders do. Therefore, formulating a mathematical algorithm that is capable of predicting market changes would be like finding the philosopher’s stone. However, since market activities tend to converge on the prescribed outcomes of widely-shared models like the Black–Scholes’ option-pricing model for derivate instruments (Mackenzie & Millo, 2003), an interest exists among traders to develop individual models. Buenza and Stark (2004) thus emphasize that the trading rooms contain idiosyncratic combinations of tools and instru ments: ‘‘[T]rading rooms can be understood as places that gather diverse market instruments to gether. Seen in this light, the move from traditional to modern finance can be considered as an engagement in the number of instruments in the room, from one to several . . . the best trading rooms bring together heterogeneous value frameworks for creative recombinations” (Buenza & Stark, 2004, p. 393). The ‘‘creative recombinations” are a key to understanding how traders outperform one another. Only by rendering what is invisible visible, by inscribing calculated scenarios into a graphical form, will traders be able to see what is expected to happen. The fluid and fluxing nature of the national and international financial markets is too complex and non-linear for the human mind to cap ture, and only when they are integrated into the technological apparatus of the financial traders will they be able, to use Hacking’s (1990) phrase, to ‘‘tame chance”. Buenza and Stark (2004, p. 370) also emphasize the fact that these calculative practices are ‘‘[d]istributed across persons and instruments”. The technological apparatuses composed of ‘‘creative recombinations” of tools and techniques are, thus, what Virilio (1994) calls ‘‘vision machines” and what François Jacob refers to as ‘‘a machine for making the future” (cited in Rheinberger, 1998, p. 288), that is, apparatuses for making the invisible visible and predicting outcomes.

In many fields and professions, the importance of visual media is growing. Today, it is virtually impossible to operate in, for instance, the technosciences or financial markets without mobilizing an assemblage of technologies, tools, and practices that structure and organize visuality. In the technosciences, actor-network theorists such as Bruno Latour (1987) have advocated the term inscription to denote the practices of translating scientific materials into documents and doctrines. In the financial markets, formulae and algorithms, which are more or less standardized and form part of technological systems, are widely used. In both cases, visual media are capable of imposing an image that is, of necessity, composed of two elements, i.e. the underlying actual materiality or flows of information of data and the technological assemblage per se. This mediated vision thus sees the real through the medium of technology and, consequently, the theories underlying that specific technology. Thus, the relationship between the image and the actual conditions are more complicated than is commonly claimed. As, for instance, Roth (2003, 2009) and Roth and Bowen (2003) have suggested, a significant degree of assumption is inscribed in the very models and graphs used to visualize, for instance, research findings. Therefore, it is epistemologically and practically complicated to make claims such as the ‘‘graphs speak for themselves”, i.e. that the graphs per se are composed in a neutral form. Contrary to such beliefs, all visual media produce, more or less conspicuously, images of actual conditions that are shaped by the technologies and theories employed. As a consequence, there are no visual media that portray or depict actual conditions ‘‘as they are”, instead always shaping and forming such images and visual representations by imposing the underlying assumptions of the visual media per se. In Bachelard’s (1984) view, the phénoménotechniques are always already material manifestations of scientific hypotheses and collaborated facts and thus the scientist sees the world through the machinery that he or she has constructed for him- or herself. Therefore, the idea that brain scans are capable of seeing mind, consciousness, and indeed life per se is an utterly naïve idea; life can only be seen if it is defined on the basis of the theoretical elements of the technologies used to scan and visualize the brain. Such a technical (or even technocratic—whereby technologies, not humans, define life) definition is of far less social interest and is less evocative than the conventional theological and existentia specializations regarding the nature of life, which are more or less detached from technological possibilities and limitations. If there is an essence of life which is to be defined in ostensive terms, it is not primarily a matter of technological apparatuses.

## 4. The limitation of media-laden vision

Human vision is precarious inasmuch as vision is always susceptible to preconceived ideas and taken-for-granted beliefs (Ratey. 2002, p. 56: Elkins. 1996, pp. 11–12). The mind enacts its own world and the eye is prone to see what it has already seen; perception and memory are tightly coupled. ‘‘My experience is what I agree to attend to. Only those items which I notice shape my mind—without selective interest, experience is an utter chaos. Interest alone gives accent and emphasis, light and shade, background and foreground, in a word,” argued William James (1890/1950, p. 402. Emphasis in the original). Like no other contemporary thinker perhaps, the French social theorist Paul Virilio has criticized both the gradual loss of what he calls ‘‘ocular perception” (Virilio, 2000, p. 26) and the increased reliance on media-laden vision. ‘‘Today”, says Virilio (2005, p. 37), ‘‘we are no longer truly seers [voyants] of our world, but also merely reviewers [revoyants], the tautological repetition of the same, at work in our mode of production (i.e., individual production), is equally the work in our mode of perception”. Being ‘‘reviewers” rather than ‘‘seers” presupposes social practices being bound up with the uses of media and technologies enabling unprecedented forms of what Virilio (1989) calls ‘‘logistics of perception”, new means of speeding up the circulation of information: ‘‘We no longer have time for reflection. The power of speed is that . . .. Democracy is no longer in the hands of man, it’s in the hands of computerized instruments, answering machines, etc.” (Virilio & Lotringer, 1997, p. 61). Virilio is particularly concerned about the combination of media-laden vision and speed, a combination that increases the circulation of data and information in real time but also undermines opportunities for reflexivity and critical thinking, paving the way for ‘‘accidents”: ‘‘A society which rashly privileges the present—the real time—to the detriment of both the past and the future, also privileges the accident” (Virilio, 2004, p. 42). In Virilio’s view, the new regime of logistics of perception is inherently military in nature. The gradual colonialization of everyday life by new logistics of perception is a worrying tendency in Virilio’s view:

One of Virilio’s most significant contributions to the philosophy of technology is his insistence that Western culture acknowledge the militarization and industrialization of vision: the logistics of perception as it relates to military tactics and (supposedly) non-military applications of vision machinery and strategies. He is indeed anxious to trace the disappearance of (traditional) human subjectivity into the military machine, into command, control, and surveillance devices, and the gradual colonialization of the human body by informatic prostheses and nanotechnologies. (Crawford, 1999, p. 172)

In organizations, media-laden vision is a central element of the recent financial crisis and economic recession which began in September 2008 when the American investment bank Lehman Brothers filed for bankruptcy. The financial markets operate on the basis of media which are, in Donald Mackenzie’s (2008) apt analogy, ‘‘engines” rather than ‘‘cameras”. The far-reaching consequences of the 2008 events still cannot be overviewed and one must not believe that the use of media and technology has single-handedly created this economic crisis of significant proportions; however, the combination of media-laden vision and socially-enacted policies and guidelines which favour risk-taking and enable the sophisticated repackaging of risks in financial instruments has arguably contributed to this precarious situation dominating political and economic life at the end of the first decade of the new millennium. Operating on the basis of an ensemble of instruments which have filtered selected signals from the market and other social institutions, the financial traders, largely motivated by pecuniary incentives (McDowell, 1997; Roth, 2006), have developed their own machinery for creating wealth both for themselves and for others. Not until the systems had derailed could an adequate degree of self-criticism regarding these practices, partially media-laden, be articulated. The speed of the market prohibited the development of more sophisticated mechanisms for questioning the financial markets.

Also in the sciences, in new drug development in the pharmaceutical industry for instance, the increased use of media-laden vision has appeared during the same period of time when the traditional new drug development model of the blockbuster is running out of steam. The production of truly new and innovative drugs has decreased from 53 in 1996 to 27 in 2000, falling still further to 21 in 2003; ‘‘even as the industry had nearly doubled in spending on development over the interval (1996–2003)”, write Mirowski and van Horn (2005, p. 533). In 2000, the pharmaceutical industry’s 11 Fortune 500 companies dedicated ‘‘30% of their revenue to marketing and administrative costs” but only ‘‘12% of their revenue to research and development” (Mirowski & Van Horn, 2005, p. 533). ‘‘Big pharma likes to refer to itself as a research-based industry, but it is hardly that. It could at best be described as an idea-licencing, pharmaceutical formulating and manufacturing, clinical testing, patenting, and marketing industry”, remarks Angell (2004, p. 73). The development of new research methodologies in the domain of genomics and post-genomic life sciences has produced a situation whereby new bioengineering and bio-computational approaches such as systems biology have been proposed as meth ods potentially able to help in the structuring and examination of the vast amount of data generated (Burbeck & Jordan. 2006: Fujimura. 2005: Pisano. 2006: Thacker, 2004: Torgersen. 2009). While the decline in the output of innovative drugs is naturally the consequence of a variety of factors, experiences from a series of visualization technologies such as high throughput screening (HTS) have, by and large, been disappointing. Recent hopes for bio-computational approaches, in themselves enabling new media-laden vision on the molecular level of biological systems, may pave the way for new opportunities in the pharmaceutical industry, but these still rest on a regime of technologies and media that favour scope and speed over reflexivity and thinking.

Following Virilio, media-laden vision is both a possibility and a threat. Instituting the means and arenas for reflecting on different scenarios will be imperative as regards avoiding mistaking the means for the ends and the maps for the territories. If, in the financial trading rooms. financial instruments are engines rather than cameras, then the need will exist for mechanisms that help financial agent to drop their tools (Weick, 1996) when things go wrong. Sociomaterial practices are always already embedded in material resources and social agreements and the one element must not dominate the other. In other words, sociomaterial practices must neither be “oversocialized" nor “undersocialized' in Granovetter’s (1985) sense of the term.

## 5. Organizational practice and media-laden vision

Suchman’s (2007) concept of sociomaterial practices, advocated by Orlikowski (2007) in an organization theory context, is a fruitful development of the concept of practice recently popularized in organization theory (Schatzki, 2002; Schatzki, Knorr Cetina, & Savigny, 2001). The concept of practice has a long tradition in the social sciences and in organization theory but one of the standing concerns has been the materiality of practice—how material and social resources are combined and co-aligned in day-to-day work. Suchman’s (2007) distinction between plans and situated actions, i.e. enacted objectives and actual outcomes, underlines the fact that all practice is embedded in bounded rationalities and situations whereby actors cannot predict and anticipate all the relevant factors and condi tions. Organizations are domains wherein vision is structured and regulated. In marketing, products and services are portrayed in order to optimise demand for the product offerings; in accounting, performance indicators present the organization as rationally and morally correctly managed. One relevant argument would be that organizations are successful to the extent that they are capable of instituting a particular form of vision that renders elements of the enacted environment visible and thus intelligible and open to inspection. Organizing is the sociomaterial practice of making enacted worlds visible. The management of vision entails the active construction of means of seeing and organizing. Such means include forms of professional vision (Goodwin, 1994), i.e., collectively enacted standards regarding how to evaluate visual representations produced by the media, and other social components of visual practice. Professional vision is disciplined in every sense of the term, resting on agreements in professional communities on how to perceive and interpret specific visual representations, e.g., technoscientific representations such as photographic images or graphs. Such professional standards for seeing are playing a key role in rendering what is invisible—blind to the untrained eye— visible. It is indicative that both Elsbach (2006) and Terranova (2004)—the former in a normative and the latter in a critical perspective—use the military term perception management to denote how organizations can actively and strategically influence how social reality is enacted and perceived. In the regime of perception management, selected elements of social realities are targeted and amplified.

In addition, as shown by Bloomfield (1991) and Bloomfield and Vurdubakis (1997), information and communication technologies—eminent forms of technologies of vision—strongly influence not only vision but also organization structure and the perception of organization structure. Sociomaterial practice is, then, both that which recursively shapes and forms vision simultaneous to these forms of vision affecting the organization wherein vision is located, thereby further influencing opportunities for new regimes of visuality. Studies of technologies of vision, as an integral component of sociomaterial practices, must thus pay attention to how organization structure is both shaped by and shapes technologies of vision. Recent organization forms such as network-based organizations, virtual organizations, and project-based organizations, at times referred to as post-bureaucratic organization (e.g. Heckscher & Donnellon, 1994), relying on information and communication technologies to coordinate and integrate spatially- and temporally-dispersed parts, are particularly interesting sites for the study of sociomaterial practices. In such organizations, much of the coordination and exchange of know-how and expertise is based on various media which structure and shape communication between the actors. In many cases, the uses of media serve as the infrastructure of the activities; that is, it is largely ignored and subject to little reflection until ceasing to function as expected (Ribes & Bowker, 2009), and it is therefore a key element of the organization.

## 6. Concluding remarks on the theory of sociomaterial practice

A proper theory of sociomaterial practice would make an interesting and fruitful contribution to organization theory and managerial practice. Disentangling the elements and relationships—the edges and vortices, the points and the lines—of a sociomaterial practice requires an understanding not only of the individual components and their relations but also of the broader socio-cultural and economic setting wherein the sociomaterial practice is located. In this view, sociomaterial practices are defined not only by the constituted elements and their relations but also by their temporality, their trajectories and gradual stabilizations over time. Taking such temporalities into account requires an understanding of how technologies, media, and scientific authority accumulate over time.

Langdon Winner (1977) criticized the view that technology is an autonomous force in society, more or less outside the control of human beings. Instead, Winner suggested that technology be enacted through its use, in the actual ‘‘doings” on the basis of the technology (see also Bijker, 1995). In media theory, at least in the constructionist media theory advocated by Karen Barad and others (e.g., Johnson, 2008), matter is not autonomous from the media and technologies used. Just as technologies, similar to Wittgenstein’s view of language, are constituted through their use, so matter is constituted through the media and technologies involved. Matter per se is never known: but only through constructing and collectively enacting technologies of vision can humans acquire data and information about it. Since such matter is never produced autonomously from technologies, the organization of ensembles of technologies of vision gain great importance. In technoscience, this organization on the level of tools, techniques, resources, and so forth is largely taken-for-granted, produced by relatively anonymous technicians and laboratory workers—it is part of the infrastructure (Star & Bowker, 2002), invisible, already in place, as if by magic. Organization theorists should pay more attention to the ensembles of technologies of vision as well as how these are constructed to produce credible, technoscientifically legitimate images of matter. Mobilizing technologies of vision and visual media is not simply a matter of connecting a few machines but also of actively constructing the matter being examined. For instance, laboratory animals such as Drosophila (the fruitfly), and genetically-modified animals such as knock-out mice, are in themselves technoscientific accomplishments that transgress the culture/nature divide by being what Braidotti (2006) calls ‘‘cyber-teratological apparatuses”. Such genetically-modified animals are, in the truest sense, machines in the original etymological sense of the term, meaning ‘‘a trick against nature” (Simondon, 1980, p. xiv); being neither an instance of nature nor a mere fabrication, these laboratory animals are real monsters, teratological creatures, in the sense that they are in medias res, in the middle of things, in-between positions, in a permanent state of liminality. They help researchers to observe, for instance, the growth of cancer cells (in the case of the famous onco-mouse, the first genetically-patented animal) and are, consequently, part of the organized framework that enables ‘‘cancer vision” or what Myers (2008, p. 189) calls ‘‘molecular vision”. The onco-mouse is thus a constitutive element of a technoscientific ensemble of technologies of vision and visual media that are capable of producing credible and legitimate knowledge which helps hu mans to grapple with the issue of cancer. However, what cancer researchers are observing is certainly the matter that Barad (2003) speaks of in terms of ‘‘material becomings”. Neither the onco-mouse qua technoscientific resource and fabricated biological system nor the technoscientific apparatus examin ing the biological system are constructed in isolation from the operative hypotheses of the cancer research programme. They are, at least partially, constructed on the basis of specific assumptions ideologies, and beliefs. Consequently, what the cancer researchers are capable of observing is not ex actly unmediated matter, ‘‘nature in the raw”, but a materiality that is intimately associated with the visual media in use. In fact, there is a recursive relationship between the materiality observed and the technologies of vision being developed; one is developed on the basis of the other and, eventually, one is further modified to accomplish more accurate vision.

The idea of media-laden vision does not suggest that such a vision is impotent or corrupt. On the contrary, media-laden vision is the backbone of contemporary technoscience (Lynch & Woolgar, 1990; Pauwels, 2006), the jewel in the crown of the history of human societies. Instead, the concept of med ia-laden vision points to the complex organization of a variety of constitutive elements enabling such a contribution. As has been pointed out by numerous commentators, if there is a key to understanding the success of the technosciences, it lies in their organization:

The authority of science is not simply grounded in its texts, but rather in its organization. The important consequence is that science deserves no more and no different type of respect than other powerful organizations. People are willing to surrender to the experts because they are in awe of the privileged rationality of their science. So, we accept the experts telling us what to eat, who we are, how we should live, and how to raise our children . . .. Once we stop being too impressed by the experts and their science, and once we realize that their power is simply that of their organization, we can begin to loosen their tight grip on our lives. (Fuchs, 1992, p. 18)

The ability to wield this kind of authority over people’s lives and thinking is, claims Fuchs (1992), based on the organization of the technosciences (see also Latour, 1988). Thus, moving into the heart of the activities, into the laboratories to examine the scientific modes of vision, one may understand both how scientists are capable of accomplishing certain objectives but also how such objectives are themselves, to some extent, what Fujimura (1996, p. 11) calls ‘‘doable problems”. Technoscience is the totality of the resources at hand (theoretical, economic, political, and cul tural) and the visual media are a central component. Studying the organization of these technologies of vision qua sociomaterial practice is a highly legitimate field of research in organization theory.

In summary, a theory of sociomaterial practice must take into account synchronic elements and their relationships as well as the diachronic growth of scientific credibility along historical pathways. No sociomaterial practice emerges ex nihilo but is constantly accumulating over time as new practices routines, or technologies are added to the previous sociomaterial practice.
