## ABSTRACT:

In current business climate, a firm’s information systems security is no longer independent from the industry’s broader security environment. A question arises, then, whether stock market values reflect the interdependence of security breaches and investments. In this paper, we used the event study methodology to investigate how a firm’s security breaches and IT security investments influence its competitors. We collected and reviewed 118 information security breaches and 98 IT security investment announcements from 2010 to 2017. We found substantial evidence supporting our hypothesis that information security breaches do, indeed, have a competition effect: when one firm is breached, its competitors have opportunities to absorb market power. For the IT security investment announcements, however, we observed the positive externalities, or contagion effect, in play: market investors feel that the security investments made by one firm increase the security level of the entire network, and hence, competitors also get benefits. Additionally, we found that the competition effect was higher when the breaches occurred after the preceding security investments than when there were no preceding investments before the breaches.

Keywords: Information security, Breach, Investment, Information transfer effect, Externalities, Event study

#

## 1. Introduction

Presently, a firm’s information system security is no longer independent from the security environment of the industry as a whole. According to a Ponemon Institute (2015) study, US firms reported the highest average cost per information security breach at \$15 million. Furthermore, cyberattacks become costlier the longer they remain undetected and unaddressed. Ponemon reported an average of 46 days, at a cost of \$21,155 per day, to detect and resolve a cyberattack. Failure to implement or maintain information security mechanisms can influence a business’ bottom line owing to lost customer confidence and brand switching.

Recent high-profile information security breaches have also raised concerns for the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) and the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board (PCAOB), thus leading them to consider whether firms should be required to disclose potential threats and the consequences of information security breaches, such as reputation loss or even the continuity of business operations.1 Such operational uncertainties and/or concerns about continuity increase the firm’s risks and generate negative market consequences.

In 2013, Target was the victim of a severe information security breach involving the release of millions of customers’ credit and debit card numbers, making them vulnerable to theft. With customer information compromised, Target paid \$10 million in a settlement resulting from a class-action lawsuit. As a result of the Target data breach, the retailer had to pay an additional \$162 million to update its information security and to get the firm back on track. In fact, Target lost an estimated 2–10% in market share, and its profits plummeted following the data breach: sales dropped by 5.3% in the fourth quarter of 2013 when the breach occurred, and its profits fell by 46%. Profits continued to fall in early 2014 owing to continuing customer reluctance to shop at Target.2

The wide-ranging impacts of information security breaches on the breached firms are clear. What, then, are the impacts on their competitors? Do they benefit from the breached firm’s sufferings? If Target’s once loyal customers begin making purchases from its competitors such as Amazon and Walmart, then one might expect that Target’s security breach would affect the competitors positively. The story, however, might not be that simple. Customers may start to distrust shopping malls in general, suspecting that other firms would have similar security problems. An interconnected Internet also makes the issue more complicated. It is well known that the LinkedIn security breach in 2012 allowed Mark Zuckerberg’s Facebook account to be hacked in 2016 because, like many Internet users, he had been using the same password for different accounts.3 This incident offers a simple example to show how one firm’s security breach might be bad news for other firms as well.

Aware of this type of security threat from an interconnected network, firms are trying to find ways to minimize the potential negative side effects from a competitor’s security breach. For example, after the Target breach, Karenann Terrell, Walmart’s CIO, emphasized the need for continuous testing and enhanced security of networks because “single points of failure anywhere can have really drastic effects, and the ability for an attack to go undetected for a period of time just exponentially increases the damage that can occur.”4

#

Approximately 6 months after Target’s massive data breach became public, Gartner’s (2015) survey indicated that worldwide spending on information security reached an estimated \$71.1 billion in 2014, a 7.9% increase over 2013. In 2015, total information security spending continued to grow a further 8.2%, reaching \$76.9 billion. Firms are becoming increasingly aware of the need to invest in sound security systems capable of securing valuable firm data. For example, Home Depot is spending billions of dollars to upgrade its registers to accept chip-enabled cards5, whereas in the post attack at Sony, Sony hired the independent security contractor FireEye to assess the damage and help clean up its systems.6 Anecdotal evidence suggests that many other competitors have also been affected and motivated to action by recent information security breach incidents, leading to an overall increase in IT security investments. With today’s interwoven networks, however, more information security investments do not necessarily guarantee better security.

Our research takes up this question of the impact on competitors of an individual firm’s efforts to ensure information security in an environment of interwoven networks. We examined the following research questions: Are the security risks of a firm affected by competitors’ security-related activities (or events)? If so, would this interdependence of security events be captured in market value? To answer these questions, we used the event study methodology to investigate how security breaches and IT security investment announcements influence other competitors. We further examined firm-level characteristics such as industry and size effects that might have contributed to the security breach and IT security investments. We also compared the effect on competitors based on the timing of the breaches and security investments.

Our paper contributes to the literature in several ways. First, this study extends the literature on the economic consequences of security breach announcements or IT security investment announcements (Campbell et al. 2003; Cavusoglu et al. 2004; Ettredge and Richardson 2003; Kannan et al, 2007; Goldstein et al. 2011) by looking at the impact on the competitors. As firms compete with their industry peers in the same product market, they serve as benchmarks for each other in their relative performance evaluation and are exposed to similar technology shocks. Hence, investors are likely to pay attention to both a firm and its competitors. However, prior research papers mostly focused on the direct effects of security-related announcements or on their suppliers only (Campbell et al. 2003; Cavusoglu et al. 2004; Ishiguro et al. 2006; Gordon et al. 2010; Chai et al. 2011).

Hinz et al. (2015) were the first study to see the impact on the competitors using an event study method. In this sense, our work is in line with their study. However, Hinz et al. (2015) paid attention mostly to the information transfer effect on the competitors without looking at the negative or positive externalities. Moreover, their work was limited to the small number of data theft announcements and the target category was the electronic industry only. Thus, comparison by industry type was not possible. In contrast, our work includes other types of security breaches as well as IT security investment announcements. Therefore, our study provides more generalizable results regarding security issues on competitors. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive study that attempts to examine the market reactions not only of firms announcing security issues, but also of their competitors as well.

Second, our study extends the growing literature on information security risk management (Chen et al., 2011; Safa et al., 2016) by analyzing IT security investments in pre- and post-security breach incidents. After several high-profile breaches (e.g., Target, Yahoo!, Sony, etc.), the importance of IT security investments has been recognized more by managers. Consistent with this notion, we further investigated how IT security investment decisions by breached firms and competitors affect market value. In doing so, our study enables managers to make better decisions by providing a richer understanding of the impact of security breaches and investments.

As shown in Table 1, previous studies found that the announcements of security breaches resulted in negative abnormal return in general, although the impact is different across industries and type of breaches. Further, security investments announcements produced positive abnormal return in Chai et al. (2001).

An event study method is a straightforward and valid method to test whether specific events affect business value. However, prior studies did not fully examine whether security breaches or security investments would affect the competitors’ market values. Chen et al. (2011), for instance, set up an economic model to argue that software diversification would limit the risk of failure under the assumption that negative externalities occur in information security investment. However, their study was not an empirical one, and there have been limited empirical studies showing the impact of security activities on the market value of competitors. The theoretical background of the impact on competitors can be found in economic externalities and information transfer effects. Therefore, we look at the previous studies on these topics.

## 2.2. Externalities of Security Activities

Using the game theoretical model, Laszka et al. (2015) analyzed the externalities of a firm’s securityrelated activities on other competitors. They found that the risks or opportunities faced by system operators and users are not only determined by their own security posture but also heavily influenced by the security-related decisions of other connected systems. This is therefore called interdependent security (IDS). Under the IDS, however, both positive and negative externalities may occur.

#

There are two game theoretical models that can explain the possibilities of positive externalities. The first is the propagation model, also called the epidemic model, which explains how improvement in security can defend against the spread of malware infections. Protecting customers’ information in one firm positively affects other firms because people usually use the same ID and password in several portal sites. If one site loses its client information, it can also be a threat to others (Anderson and Moore, 2006; Lelarge, 2009; Omic et al., 2009; Theodorakopoulos et al., 2013).

The second game theoretical model was proposed by Varian (2004) and can be called as the total effort model. The basic idea here is that each player’s risk is determined not by a single firm’s effort but by the overall level of security because security investment has declining returns for every participant. Varian (2004)’s concern was a free-riding problem owing to the public good property of security. A firm may become lazy about investing in security because it knows it can indirectly increase its security level through other firms’ investment actions, which can be seen as free-rider problems.

In contrast, the weakest target model (Grossklags et al., 2010) shows that negative externalities can also occur. Because they are opportunistic and seeking out the easiest target, security attackers always hunt for the least secured firms. Therefore, a firm’s strengthened security level makes its competitors’ security position relatively weaker. If one firm invests in information security, the possibility of it being targeted decreases, whereas other firms’ risk of being targeted increases.

There have been research studies that showed the security incidents in one firm affect others. Hovav and Gray (2014) analyzed the security breach from the perspective of stakeholders by using stakeholder analysis (SA). Through the TJX case study, they showed that the impact of a security breach affects the whole ecosystem (e.g., consumers, vendors, banks, and hackers), but the actual effect depends on subsequent events. Chen et al. (2011) argued that firms should maintain the diversity of systems to escape from the negative externalities of the common or popular software’s vulnerabilities that occurred in other firms. Many information systems researchers have also dealt with the topic of externalities of information technology investment among software (Brynjolfsson and Kemerer 1996), industries (Han et al. 2011), and nations (Gholami et al. 2009).

## 2.3. Information Transfer Effect of Announcement

Changes in the market value of competitors, however, do not always stem from the externalities of security activities. According to asset pricing, stock prices play a role as information sets available to all capital market participants, not just to one enterprise (Schipper 1990). The stock prices of one firm have the information disclosure function not only for itself, but also for other companies; this is called the information transfer effect in the capital market. This effect can occur if information released by a firm has important implications for the future profitability of other nonannouncing competitors. The information transfer effect typically arises among intra-industry firms rather than among completely unrelated companies, and it exists when information released by one firm affects the performance of other nonannouncing competitors in the same industry (Szewczyk, 1992; Zafar et al. 2012; Guo, 2017).

Using the information transfer effect, prior studies empirically analyzed the effect of an incident (or event) on other firms. For example, Aharony and Swary (1983) found that the bankruptcy of a bank may lead to the loss of public confidence in the banking system as a whole. Foster (1981) showed that a firm’s earnings releases have a statistically significant contagion effect on the stock price of other firms. Ettredge and Richardson (2003) used the event study methodology to focus on Internet firms that were subject to denial-of-service (DoS) attacks. They found that there were negative mean abnormal returns among Internet firms that were not attacked: the competitors were presumed to be in a similar situation to the announcing firm owing to industry commonalities. Hinz et al. (2015) also showed that the share prices of indirectly affected but similar firms decreased together with the directly affected firms. This information transfer effect can be called as contagion effect.

However, a nonannouncing competitor’s financial performance reaction does not have to be in the same direction as that of the announcing firm. It might be in the opposite direction owing to the competition effect because market investors try to guess the relative competitiveness of firms in the future. In other words, the announcement of an event conveys to the public information regarding the industry’s competitive balance. Therefore, it is possible that a nonannouncing competitor’s financial performance reaction would be in the opposite direction. Zafar et al. (2012) studied the contagion and competition effects on matching firms through their financial statements. They found that nonbreached same-industry firms get the effect in the same way as that of the breached firms, which might be evidence of information transfer effect.

## 3. Hypotheses Development

## 3.1. Own Firm Effects

Prior event studies showed that information security breaches have significantly negative effects on the breached firm in general (Campbell et al. 2003; Ishiguro et al. 2006; Goel and Shawky, 2009; Gordon et al. 2010; Wang et al. 2013a; Hinz et al. 2015). Campbell et al. (2003) especially found that announcements of information security breaches affect stock market reaction only when the event was related to violation of confidentiality. Their further findings showed that highly significant negative market reaction only occurred when the information security breaches involved unauthorized access to confidential data. However, when they did not involve confidential information, security breaches did not affect the stock value significantly. Ishiguro et al. (2006) also found that the stock market reacted negatively when there were announcements about information security incidents. The found that firms that had a large portion of intangible assets reacted more than firms with tangible assets did. For IT security investments, Chai et al. (2011) found that the announcements of information security investment created positive, abnormal returns to firms. Wang et al. (2013a) showed that the market reaction following the security breach announcement is different depending on the nature of the preceding risk factor disclosures.

Our study aims to confirm findings in previous literature studies by hypothesizing that information security breaches or security investments result in different stock market reactions. Hence, we posit H1 as follows:

H1a: Information security breach announcements will create negative abnormal returns for breached firms.

H1b: IT security investment announcements will create positive abnormal returns for investmentmaking firms.

## 3.2. Externalities and Information Transfer Effects

As discussed above, both the externalities of security activities and the information transfer effect theoretically explain why the market value of competitors is affected when a firm experiences an information security breach. Laszka et al. (2015) argued that both positive and negative externalities of security breaches on other competitors could occur. Schipper (1990) claimed that the stock prices of a firm have an information disclosure function for other competitors as well as for itself. Through this information transfer effect, one firm’s information disclosure affects other competitors’ market value.

Only a few empirical studies, however, have examined the market value of nonbreached competitors. Cavusoglu et al. (2004) found that the announcement of a security breach decreased the market value of the breached firm, whereas the market values of non-breached security software developers increased. With annual financial statement data, Zafar et al. (2012) found that non-breached same-industry firms move in the same way as the breached firms. Ettredge and Richardson (2003) also found that DoS attacks affect the non-attacked Internet firms negatively.

However, these studies considered only a very specific set of non-breached competitors, rather than all competitors. In other words, these studies all looked at the impact on security software suppliers (Cavusoglu et al. 2004; Ettredge and Richardson 2003) or same-industry firms using similar security tools (Zafar et al. 2012). In contrast, this study sought to assess the impact on all competitors in the industry that have similar revenue, scope, and coverage. To check whether externalities or information transfer effects exist in this larger group, we propose H2, our main hypothesis, as follows:

H2a: Information security breach announcements will create abnormal returns for non-breached competitors.

H2b: IT security investment announcements will create abnormal returns for non-investment-making competitors.

## 3.3. Timing of Security Breaches and Security Investments

A firm’s security breaches and investments are not two different issues. Internal security risk assessments and security needs significantly explain the variation in organizational investments (Cavusoglu et al. 2015). It is very natural for a firm to make investments to strengthen its security systems after breaches occur. In fact, most of security professionals invest in cyber defenses after a data breach, according to Cisco’s 2017 Annual Cybersecurity Report (Cisco 2017). In this case, IT security investment is just a mustdo agenda and would not be a surprise to the public. The actual effectiveness of security investment can be different between preventive paradigm and response paradigm (Baskervillea et al. 2014). Therefore, the stock market may not fully appreciate these defensive activities (investments after breaches) compared with proactive security investments (investments without breaches).

In addition, it is notable that security investments are preventative actions that may always have less impact than actual security events due to a lack of knowledge regarding the effectiveness of the security investment in preventing breaches. If breach occurs after the investment, it gives a clear signal that the firm’s preventive efforts and actions were failure. Hence, the abnormal return on competitors would be greater than that of breaches occurring without previous security investments. Hence, the abnormal return on competitors would be different depending on the timing of the security breaches and security investments. We posit the following hypotheses as an exploration:

H3a: Security breach announcements occurring after security investments will generate more impact on competitors.

H3b: Security investment announcements occurring after security breaches will generate less impact on competitors.

## 4. Methods

## 4.1. Event Study

Using an event study approach, this study examined stock price changes corresponding to a firm’s disclosure of information security breaches. The basic idea of an event study is to identify abnormal returns or price fluctuations in the market that are attributable to the event. We estimated the abnormal excess return by using a market model based on the capital asset price model (CAPM). The estimation window was 180 days, and the gap between the estimation and the event window was 30 days. We selected several event windows (e.g., (-2,2), (-1,1), $^ { ( 0 , 1 ) }$ , and $^ { ( 0 , 2 ) ) }$ to ensure that the results were consistent with the period. The event window is the period during which specific information affects the stock market; it is 1 or 2 days before and after the announcement date. The estimation window is the time period during which the announcement does not influence the stock market. It is used to estimate the normal return compared with the abnormal return in the event window:

$$
R _ {i t} = E (R _ {i t}) + A R _ {i t} = R _ {f t} + \alpha_ {i} + \beta_ {i} (R _ {m t} - R _ {f t}) + \varepsilon_ {i t}\tag{1}
$$

First, we estimated the normal return model by using the data in the estimation window period. In formula (1), $R _ { i t }$ is the stock return of firm i at time t and $R _ { m t }$ is the market return. $R _ { i t }$ and $R _ { m t }$ are measured in change rate instead of level. We chose SP500 (Standard and Poor's index return reported by the CRSP NYSE-AMEX-Nasdaq stock database) as a market index. $\alpha _ { i }$ and $\beta _ { i }$ are coefficients estimated from regression by using the stock return data in the estimation window period. These coefficients can be substituted into the next function:

$$
A R _ {i t} = R _ {i t} - (R _ {f t} + \alpha_ {i} + \beta_ {i} (R _ {m t} - R _ {f t}))\tag{2}
$$

$A R _ { i t }$ , an abnormal return of firm i at time t, can be estimated by subtracting the constant, independent variable from the dependent variable. We included the event window data in function (2) and estimated the abnormal return. Then, we estimated the cumulative abnormal return (CAR) to analyze the overall spillover effect, while specific events such as security breaches and investments affect the stock return. The CAR can be obtained by summing all ARs during the event window.

## 4.2. Data

We identified the announcements for (1) information security breaches and (2) IT security investments separately by using the LexisNexis database (general news topics and business news topics), major newspapers, wire services, and breach related databases such as Privacy Rights Clearinghouse (privacyrights.org), DataLossDB (datalossdb.org), the Heritage Foundation (heritage.org), and Identity Theft Resource Center (idtheftcenter.org) (Higgs et al., 2016; Kwon et al., 2013; Sen and Borle, 2015). Following prior literature studies (Campbell et al. 2003; Cavusoglu et al. 2004; Wang et al. 2013b), we used a specific set of keyword search terms for information security breaches: information/computer security, attack, breach, break-in, hacker, virus/worm, network intrusion, data/identity theft, phishing, cyber fraud, and denial of service. After eliminating events that are irrelevant or duplicated, we ended up with 118 security breach events from January 2010 to December 2017. See <Appendix 1> for the list of security breach events. Regarding IT security investments, we referred to Chai et al. (2011) and used a set of keyword search terms, such as information security, information security investment, security technology, arrangement, and contract. Through searching with these keywords, and after some screening, we identified 98 IT security investment events from January 2010 to December 2017. See <Appendix 2> for the list of security investment events. During the screening process, we carefully checked for duplication of events, not only other security-related events but also news related to lawsuits, strikes, earnings, dividends, mergers, acquisitions, joint ventures, and alliances. After screening, we used WRDS to collect data, such as ticker number, NAICS codes, market value and size, and Eventus, to run the test.

To enable us to test the competitors’ market value, we identified 10 competitors for each event firm using Google Finance, because most stock investors also get their information on competitors from this site. Using Google Finance data, we selected competitors based on what they do (Chai et al. 2011; Zafar et al. 2012). We then carefully looked through the companies one by one to check whether any of these competitors have their own events that may influence the market value during the event windows. This process resulted in an unequal number of competitors for each event firm. Tables 2-a and 2-b show the available number of competitors for the information security-breached event firms and for the security investment event firms, respectively. For the security-breached data, we were able to identify 823 unbalanced competitors or 599 balanced competitors (7 competitors for 77 event firms). For the security investment data, we found 816 unbalanced competitors or 588 balanced competitors (6 competitors for 98 event firms). ## 5. Results and Discussions

We tested the data for each of the security breach and investment firms and compared the results. By comparing two completely different events, we could understand why and how the security announcements affected the market value of non-announced competitors.

## 5.1. Own Firm Effect of Security Breaches and Investments (H1a and H1b)

Table 4 shows the CARR and t-statistics for different event windows. The line graph shows the abnormal returns for each day, and one can see that the decrease in market value occurred before the announcement date. The CAAR for the 5-day event window (-2,2) was -0.423% and was statistically significant (p<0.05). For other event windows, the results are similar. Therefore, H1a was supported. Consistent with those of prior event studies (Campbell et al. 2003; Cavusoglu et al. 2004; Ishiguro et al. 2006; Goel and Shawky, 2009; Gordon et al. 2010; Wang et al. 2013a; Hinz et al. 2015), our findings suggest that the market value of breached firms significantly decreased.

However, IT security investment did not significantly affect market value. The CAARs for various event windows were all statistically insignificant. That is, H1b was not supported, contrary to the findings of Chai et al. (2011). Dos Santos et al. (1993) and Im et al. (2001) expected that a lag effect could exist in cases of investment announcement. In our case, unlike the time of the study by Chai et al. (2011), investors already started to recognize that IT security investments is a must-do thing rather than optional strategy. Hence, they would not easily increase a firm’s value. Therefore, we can say that the security investment impact is not symmetrically opposite to the security breach impact.

## 5.2. Impacts on Competitors (H2a and H2b)

As shown in the left side of Table 5-1, an information security breach leads to positive abnormal returns to non-breached competitors. Therefore, H2a was partially supported. Competitors' market value moved in an opposite direction to that of breached firms. This might have occurred owing to the competition effect because market power might have moved from the breached firms to the competitors, following the information transfer effect argument. Competitors might have been judged to become more competitive than the breached firms did, and subsequently, their market value increased.

Either the epidemic model or the total effort model could explain this result, although the result (H2b) was partially supported.

## 5.4. Additional Tests I: Firm-Level Characteristics: Different Firm Sizes and Industries

The level of information asymmetry between managers and shareholders tends to differ across different firm sizes and various types of industries, such as finance or service (Flannery 1986), IT (Cash et al. 1992), and manufacturing. Therefore, we performed the additional tests to see whether the effects are different based on firm-level characteristics.

## Finance vs. Non-finance Industry

Finance or service industries tend to provide less relevant information to shareholders via standard financial reporting systems since these systems do not collect data on or reveal many factors that affect the value of these firms (Amir and Lev 1996). The announcement of forward-looking strategic information by firms (including peers in the same industry) in a finance (and service) industry has the potential to decrease information asymmetry and allow for proper market valuation. Thus, when firms and competitors in finance industries identify information security breaches (and announce IT security investments), it is likely that there will be a negative (positive) market reaction. Moreover, previous studies assumed that the finance industry is information intensive (Flannery 1986; Triplett and Bosworth 2003; Chai et al. 2011). Firms with North American Industry Classification System (NAICS) codes equal to or greater than 52 are coded as belonging to the finance industry, whereas other firms are recorded as belonging to the non-finance industry.

Table 7-1 shows the comparison of non-breached competitors between the finance and the non-finance industry. The market value of competitors significantly increased only for non-finance industry firms with a (0, 2) event window. Please note that, unlike in the non-finance industry, the CAARs for the competitors in the finance industry were all negative, which might suggest that the finance industry has a contagion effect rather than a competition effect, although they were not statistically significant. The difference between finance and non-finance firms was statistically significant for event windows (-2,2) and (0,2). Therefore, we concluded that the competition effect was greater for non-finance industry. Contrary to the prior literature which suggested that the finance industry deals with more sensitive information (Flannery 1986), what we found here was opposite. Firms in finance industry did not get benefit from the security breaches of the competitors unlike non-finance industry firms.

## Firm Size

Previous studies showed that the amount of publicly available information varies and is often determined by the size of the firm (Watts and Zimmerman 1986). Large firms tend to have more opportunities to engage the media and market analysts than do smaller firms. In contrast, the specific technology adoption plans of small firms are not generally known by the media or market analysts until the actual announcements are made. Larger firms can more effectively disseminate financial and non-financial information than smaller firms do, and therefore, they can more easily reduce the costs of information transfer to the marketplace (Atiase 1985; Hayn 1995). The expected negative relationship between firm size and the positive market impact of public announcements has been empirically tested in the context of IT investment; the results, however, have been mixed (Im et al 2001; Chatterjee et al. 2002). Following this line of argument, we propose to examine the impact of firm size on the relationship between information security breach announcements or IT security investments and market reactions.

As shown in Tables 9-1 and 9-2, we failed to find any evidence of impact differences based on firm sizes. None of the differences was significant. In the case of security breaches and security investments, size did not matter, unlike in Atiase (1985), Hayn (1995), and Im et al. (2001).

## 5.5 Additional Tests II: Market-Adjusted Model and Regression Analysis

Hoovers provided a list of three top competitors for each company. For the robustness check, we used Hoovers data to perform the hypotheses tests with the one-competitor model and the three-competitor model. The results were generally consistent with the main results achieved using the Google Finance list and were not qualitatively different. We can provide other results upon request.

Additionally, we re-examined the hypotheses by using the market-adjusted model (Table 10) and regression (Table 11). The market-adjusted model is similar to the market model, except that it assumes the market “beta” is 1. Because the market model uses the actual market return, the beta may provide an imperfect correlation to market risks (Brown and Warner 1985). As shown in Table 10, the results of the market-adjusted analysis confirm our main results achieved using the market model, leading to even stronger results: IT security investment announcements positively affected the competitors’ market value in the market-adjusted model, whereas they were insignificant in the market model. ## 6. Implications, Limitations, and Future Studies

## 6.1. Theoretical Implications

Despite these limitations, the expected academic contributions of this research are as follows. First, to our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive event study to empirically measure the impact of securityrelated events (information security breaches and IT security investments) on competitors based on externalities and information transfer effect. Second, we compared data on both security breaches and security investments and tried to interpret what role the information security in general played in enterprises and markets. We were able to identify and compare the distinct types of externalities and information transfer effects occurring in breaches (competition effects) and investments (contagion effects). Categorizing the firms by the timing of security breaches and investments also allowed us to explore further the impacts on competitors. Third, the results of this research provide a stepping-stone to other research topics on information security. For example, Chen et al. (2011) presented their information security risk management strategies based on theoretical modeling. Our work would provide a helpful empirical basis on which to prove the basic assumptions of other theoretical papers and expand research topics in the future.

## 6.2. Managerial Implications

Our study also carries managerial implications that could potentially improve decision making in matters related to information security. First, our results give managers a better understanding of the real market value of a firm’s security activities. Because many firms have not immediately seen the expected financial loss due to security breaches or the expected gain from their information security investments, they have sometimes hesitated to make security investment decisions. Our study may persuade such managers

#

because we are dealing with market data. Second, our study may also help managers to better understand how one firm’s security activities are interconnected with its competitors, and therefore, security systems, and decisions made about them should be understood and analyzed in relation to other firms’ activities. Third, our findings may provide an insight to market investors regarding firms’ announcements of security activities. For example, an investor may consider that a firm’s security breach may be good news to competitors, while preventive security investments may not be bad news to competing firms.

## 6.3. Limitation and Future Studies

There are some limitations in this study. First, our data might explain information security investments too narrowly. Because we collected the majority of announcements data from news on Google, the data did not reflect detailed information, such as the scale of a given security breach or the amount of a security investment. Second, as with other event studies, our study is not entirely free either from the contamination of normal return estimation by the noise of other events, either. Although we did our best to exclude announcements with possible overlaps with other events, we would need to increase the sample size further to minimize the risk of this problem in the future. Third, our study measures the effect of publicly known security events on short to medium-term investment through stock exchanges. The research does not address consumer confidence or long-term effects of cybersecurity efficiency on firm value. A long horizon event study needs to be performed in the future to provide a comprehensive analysis of the results and their impact on managerial decision-making. Fourth, our data set did not include international competitors. In some cases, major rivals of the US firms examined here might be foreign firms that are not publicly traded in the US stock market. Hence, this study was not able to measure the impact on foreign competitors.

## 7. Conclusion

With an increasing number of devices connected to the Internet, an increasing number of participants in information security are also linked in a network. The interdependence of information security fundamentally creates externalities or information transfer effects. This research aimed to find an empirical evidence to prove the impact of security breaches and investment announcements on competitors’ market value. We used the event study methodology, and we identified competitors using Google Finance and Hoovers.

We first showed that security breach announcements created negative abnormal returns for announcement-making firms, whereas security investment announcements did not significantly improve the abnormal returns for announcement-making firms. Our main hypotheses were designed to measure the impact on the non-announcement-making competitors. The market value of non-announced competitors moved positively for both security breach announcements and security investment announcements. Each result could be interpreted differently, however. For the security breach announcements, we could observe the competition effect because market power may be moved from breached firms to their competitors. Competitors might have been judged by investors as being more competitive than the breached firms; hence, their market value increased. For the security investment announcements, however, we observed the positive externalities, or contagion effect, in play. Market investors feel that the security investments made by one firm increase the security level of the entire network, and hence, competitors also get benefits. Therefore, we argued that, whereas a security breach announcement has a competition effect, a security investment announcement has positive externalities. Although security breaches and security investments look like opposite security activities, their impacts are not symmetrical.

We additionally analyzed the industry effects, firm size effects, and timing effects on security-related announcements. We found that security breaches in the IT-intensive industry had significantly higher competition effect than those in the non-IT-intensive industry. The other industry effect (finance/nonfinance) and firm size effect were not significant. One interesting finding was that the market value of competitors reacted more positively when the breaches occurred after the security investments than when breaches occurred without prior security investments. In other words, security breaches after security investments had a higher competition effect than those without preceding investments. Moreover, we found that security investments after breaches had higher positive externalities. Security investments made after breaches had occurred served to increase the overall network security and, hence, helped competitors too.
