# Activity-based justification of IT investments

Eileen Peacock<sup>a,\*</sup>, Mohan Tanniru<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Oakland University, Rochester, MI 49309, USA

<sup>b</sup>University of Arizona, Tucson, AZ 85721, USA

Received 1 June 2002; received in revised form 16 January 2003; accepted 23 December 2003 Available online 14 April 2004

## Abstract

Ever since investments in IT moved from operational to decision support, academics and practitioners have been looking for ways to justify the costs. Traditional approaches, such as NPV, have been shown inadequate in capturing qualitative and quantitative benefits, and techniques developed to address the issue have proved limited in their ability to link investment justification with impact assessment. Recent literature has called for the use of business processes to assess the impact of IT and the same processes can be used to justify investments in IT. The activity-based costing (ABC) approaches in the field of accounting were developed to relate investments to product profitability by allocating the burden to those that receive the most benefit. Thus, this paper uses the ABC approach to relate investments to activities, so that it can be used to justify IT investment. The paper provides a model to determine when the approach, referred to as activity-based justification, is most appropriate and its value is illustrated using a case study. <sup>#</sup> 2004 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Activity-based costing; IT investment; IT justification; Sales force automation; Expert systems

## 1. Introduction

The impact of IT investments is often assessed on how it impacts certain performance metrics (e.g. increase in the number of units sold, increase in total dollar sales, or firm profitability). Such an approach has come under criticism when the assessment ignores the impact of investment on business processes in the value chain that directly affect these performance metrics [6,7]. Some of the observations related to the IT paradox—an investment in IT not demonstrating anticipated benefit—have been attributed to attempting to establish a relationship between investment and performance at a macro-level. Prior research proposed staged analysis (e.g. impact on production at a local level and relating this impact, in turn, to macro-level factors [3]) to address this paradox, but this type of analysis has not used processes as the intermediary between investments and performance metrics. However, activity-based costing (ABC), with its focus on using activities to relate investment burden to profitability through business processes, has been studied extensively in the accounting literature [10,21]. Therefore, can an activity-based approach be used to justify IT investment?

When IT investments are infrastructural in nature, affecting all entities across the board, firms often treat these investments as overhead. If firms use new technologies where costs and benefits cannot be accurately estimated, firms choose pilot projects to better ascertain these costs and benefits. When IT investments are used across the board by a homogenous group (office workers, sales persons, etc.), investments are justified based on average improvement in performance (reduced cycle time in operations, number of sales calls, etc.), using methods such as net present value (NPV). When these improvements cannot be effectively quantified, firms often resort to the use of less tangible, yet quantifiable, measures (reduction in document rework, customer complaints, etc.).

Many IT investments such as desktop and decision support applications are purchased to improve knowledge worker productivity. The adoption and utilization of technology varies among these knowledge workers and is not uniform and, hence, using average improvement in performance to justify IT investments makes little sense. An activity-based approach, with its ability to relate investment to utilization through various activities in which an individual might engage, can help justify such investments.

Investing in entities based on the value they provide is common when decisions are made on capital improvements (e.g. to replace a single machine) or in the automation of certain applications (e.g. some accounting functions and not others, etc.). So, making IT investment decisions based on the value an entity receives does not appear radical, assuming that the activity analysis can relate investments to output metrics. Organizationally, this has implications, as such an analysis can possibly lead to some entities getting full share of an IT investment, while others receive less than the full complement or none. In other words, the activity-based approach can be used to justify varying levels of IT investment among, for example, to aid sales people based on their propensity to adopt and use the technology. However, such an approach comes at a cost—in providing ‘‘good’’ information that can link investment appropriation to an entity and its performance through activities.

## 2. Prior research

Firms are constantly asked to justify the investments they make in IT, especially when technologies are changing rapidly. When investments are viewed as critical for a firm’s competitiveness or survival (e.g.

Y2K investment), the effort tends to be minimal. Also, when firms are faced with making investments in new technologies to take advantage of business opportunities (e.g. e-commerce, e-procurement, knowledge management, etc.), research has proposed several approaches and these recognize the insufficiency of good cost and benefit information for supporting rigorous evaluation [1,2,4,9,20]. These approaches propose that a firm explore the technology options with some seed investment, such as a pilot project, primarily to have a presence in the market place and learn from such experience before committing significant resources.

Firms are also making investments to improve productivity of their knowledge workers, reduce operational costs to support value chains, etc. using relatively well established or enhanced technologies. Techniques used vary from the traditional quantitative models, such as NPV, internal rate of return (IRR), and payback, to those that link quantitative models with qualitative criteria or intangibles [18,19]. One of the characteristics of quantitative models is that they rely on estimating the financial benefit of average improvement on performance of an entity (e.g. average improvement in a manager’s decision time or average reduction in the time a sales person takes to complete an order) and then comparing it with the overall investment cost. This type of justification is based on the premise that investments will benefit all involved, even though there may be a wide disparity in the way individuals adopt and use technology, leading to differential improvement in their performance. Recognizing this differential impact, several authors have suggested investment classification based on the types of applications they serve, goals they satisfy, or processes they support [14,15]. This is intended to be better at assessing the impact of IT on individual organizational entities and the approach is to relate the investment costs to business processes.

The accounting field has used investment activity relationships for cost management and performance measurement [17]. For example, Computer Aided Manufacturing International (CAM-I), a group formed in 1986 from some of the largest and most successful companies in the US and Europe, suggested using activity analysis to track product costs and, in turn, profitability [5]. Cooper [13] described the ABC approach and Cooper and Kaplan [11,12] further highlighted its use when there is a hierarchical nature to the activities: unit level, batch level, product level, and facility level. While research in ABC, in general, has focused on allocation of costs after an investment is made, Chen [8] proposes its use for investment justification. In fact, ABC was used for justification of EDI by Hoogeweegen et al. [16]. After comparing several investment methods, the authors determined that the ABC approach, in conjunction with simulation, is suitable for justifying EDI investments, partly due to its ability to map investment in EDI to various activities that contribute to both tangible and intangible benefits. Such a mapping does, however, call for significant information gathering and thus may not be appropriate for every investment decision.

## 3. Model development

When IT resources are used in support of focused activities of an individual entity, measuring the impact of changes in these activities on organizational performance can be complex, depending on the activity– performance relationship. In the case discussed in Appendix A, all the sales people are selling all the products, an m:n relationship, and measuring the impact of changes in activities on product performance can be complex. The significant expense in gathering the needed information to measure this impact may suggest that the ABC approach is not appropriate. It is also harder to measure impacts when changes in activities are contributed by an IT application (e.g. an office-worker productivity tool) that is far removed from the performance being observed (e.g. firm profitability).

When measurement complexity is low, then the ABC approach can easily relate changes in activities to performance. For example, a 1–n mapping in either direction between the entity that performs the activity and the entity whose performance is being measured (e.g. a sales person sells many products but each product is sold by a single sales person, or each product is sold by many sales people but a sales person sells only a single product) can make the information gathering less complex. The best scenario, of course, is when the relationship is 1–1 (e.g. a single product is sold by a single salesperson) but this is not a frequent occurrence.

## 3.1. How is the model used for investment justification?

New technologies (e.g. e-commerce, faster communication backbone, etc.) will continue to be purchased or developed by firms to support their use in improving organizational performance (agility of the firm in meeting market changes, competitiveness, etc.). It is harder to relate such investments to changes in activities of an individual user and, in turn, to corporate performance. Even if we are able to determine how each activity is changed by the system, measuring the impact of changes on corporate performance is much harder to establish unless the impact is direct. For such reasons, the cost of investment in infrastructure-supporting systems is often viewed as an overhead and either expensed or allocated to various entities using an a priori criteria.

Justifying large-scale infrastructure investment without fully understanding the complex relationship between the application of IT and the measurement complexity often contributes to an IT productivity paradox. Management often makes large investments and fails to notice any discernable improvement in financial performance of the firm. However, this should not encourage IS professionals to justify such investments using poor metrics. It may be best to use broader benefits to justify large investments. An alternative approach is to reduce measurement complexity by bringing the performance measures closer to the activity affected, or targeting a smaller investment in support of a specific activity. The firm discussed in Appendix A started in cell 1 (under scenario 1) and treated the investment initially as an overhead.

When the application of IT resource is focused and the measurement complexity is high (cell 4 in Fig. 1 and scenario 3 of Appendix A), the information gathering effort is still high. In such cases, the strategy is to gather the needed information to estimate the average change in activities, to estimate average improvement in performance, and relate this to the cost of focused investment in IT. Careful analysis in such cases may reveal, however, that not all activities are influenced by the technology in the same way (i.e. individual adoption rate of technology in not uniform), and not all activity changes affect the performance uniformly. In other words, what may appear initially to be complex to measure, upon further analysis, may lead us to establish a clearer relationship between those that use technology and the performance measures (i.e. move us from cell 4 to cell 3).

In cell 2, the application of IT for general use often makes a firm allocate the cost to activities based on connect time, storage used, etc. by an individual entity. For example, if a knowledge-based system is centrally developed and provided to help desk operators, their use of this technology to answer customers’ questions, simple or complex, is not hard to measure. It is relatively easy then to establish how the resource (connect time to KB) has improved the time an operator spends in answering a question and improve the service quality. Even here, ABC analysis may reveal specific patterns of usage—operators are using KB only to answer complex queries. Such a conclusion may lead the firm to focus its IT application on operators who answer complex queries (i.e. move us from general use of IT (cell 2) to focused use of IT (cell 3)).

Lastly, when investment in technology is focused and measurement complexity is low, then the firm is operating in cell 3 and the activity-based approach is most appropriate to justify IT investments. In fact, activity-based justification (ABJ) of IT investments can help set the stage for determining those activities and, possibly people, who should be supported with technology investment. Some examples here include providing better tools for those involved in designing complex products, and providing intricate software to underwriters who handle complex insurance products. Then treating all individuals and activities in the same way is not only expensive (as the investment is not fully utilized by all), but also in-effective (user may not use it because the training needed and system complexity is far beyond what is seen as relevant).

As the model shows, ABC analysis may be expensive to implement due to information gathering complexity in cell 1 and is most appropriate for situations in cell 3. But, as one starts to invest IT in focused applications (cell 4) or use IT in support of activities, whose performance is not difficult to measure (cell 2), ABC analysis can help a firm focus on its information gathering efforts. It can also reveal patterns of IT usage by individual adopters, which may lead to situations often observed in cell 3. This happened in the case of Appendix A.

## 5. Discussion

We have seen how the activity-based approach has provided a way of allocating both the costs and benefits accurately, so that the investment burden is shared properly by those products that have the maximum benefit from the investments. As noted earlier, the firm introduced complex products to take advantage of one new technology (expert systems) and moved to a new technology (notebook and remote distribution) to address some of the complexities associated with implementing the first technology from a central location.

If the firm had done the activity analysis before making the investment decision, would it have introduced a large and complex product that created a negative marginal contribution to profit? Even if it had to introduce the product for competitive reasons, could it have relied on a centralized system to configure these products as opposed to using relatively more expensive notebooks? As it turns out, in the late 1990s, the firm discontinued the production of complex products and shifted much of the configuration back to a centralized environment. Yet, the notebooks still remain in the hands of the sales people. In hindsight, the cost of the notebook could have been reduced significantly had it been used for configuring only smaller units and placed in the hands of those that sell these extensively (if that fact can be established), or put in a few large regional outlets where most of these units are sold. At the same time, the larger units could have been configured at the customer site using remote access to the centralized site.

While this case illustrated the use of ABC for a manufacturing application where there is a movement towards supporting individual sales person using technology that is distributed and somewhat decentralized, the same approach can be used in many applications that seem to cater to knowledge worker productivity.

The performance metric can be related to the investment through the activities performed (i.e. measurement complexity is low). Such situations are most suitable for the use of ABC approach and they allow management to make IT justification decisions based on the value derived by an individual knowledge worker. In other words, not all consultants may need the software for sharing knowledge.

This type of activity-based analysis can also be useful for evaluating the effectiveness of multiple technologies.

The exact performance measure to be chosen for evaluating an investment is case dependent and it can be a single metric such as product profitability or a multidimensional metric, such as a combination of productivity improvement and service level improvement. When a firm has to use multiple metrics to evaluate an investment, the application of ABC approach is not any different. The relationship of the investment is mapped to each activity, and the associated changes in these activities is mapped on to multiple performance metrics. A weighted average may be employed to determine the net value of such an investment.

A drawback in using the ABC approach for investment justification is that it may be viewed negatively by some in the organization, if this approach is perceived either as a reward or punishment. If the firm cannot effectively articulate its selective investment in IT in support of a few, then the social costs of such implementation may far outweigh any cost savings. Rarely are technologies introduced today adopted uniformly by all involved and many of these technologies can be custom configured. So, a technology with multiple versions or features may be provided to user groups based on the value each derives. As users mature in their use of technology or their tasks vary in scope, additional features may be provided to support these activities.

## 6. Conclusions

There are two reasons for the inherent difficulty in justifying IT investments. First, it is often difficult to arrive at the true costs of some, as many approaches have difficulty in assessing all costs (economic and social) along a common metric. Secondly, there is increasing skepticism regarding the value they provide, primarily due to the fact that many investments are impacting the bottom-line measures only indir ectly, and firms have not been effective in relating the investments to profitability through appropriate allocation mechanisms. One can always argue that investments, once justified using some tangible and intangible factors, are sunk costs and not spend much effort in allocating this cost. But, an ABC analysis, even if the investment is expensed, can provide valuable insight into its utilization and impact on profitability, unless it explicitly fall in cell 1.

There are several reasons for taking a new look at IT investment justification. First, any effective evaluation of an investment can provide useful knowledge to be used in making future investment decisions. Second, an activity-based approach can provide opportunities for a firm to evaluate how best to bring a new technology incrementally into the organization, especially when it does not have a uniform impact on the organization’s efficiencies. In the past, an incremental implementation was used merely to support adoption of new technology, but an activity-based approach can support incremental implementation based on the bottom-line measures, and this may even lead a firm not to provide the same technology to everyone. Third, it is essential that a firm understands how various entities consume costs. Using this approach gives the firm insight. Fourth, using the insight gained, we may decide when or how much of an investment should be treated as a period cost (an overhead) and how much will be allocated to various entities, based on how it affects them.

Appendix A. Three sales force automation scenarios

## A.1. Scenario 1

In this scenario a firm decides to invest in a large mainframe computer and software that will provide a variety of services to various divisions, including sales. The system will allow the sales people to send the order by phone at the end of each day, have it filled in by the order entry clerk at the corporate head office, edit the order for errors, and input the order into the system. The software will analyze the order, configure the product price, and prepare the order that night on the mainframe computer. The next day, the order (with the expected date of shipment and value) is sent to the customer/sales person. In this case, the mainframe cost is US\$ 5M dollars and software/support used for running the sales force activity, along with other business operations, is about US\$ 4M.

## A.2. Scenario 2

The firm, on the other hand, can invest in a large software package (\$500,000) that will do product configuration and ordering for the sales personnel, among others. This software is put on a mini-computer (\$500,000) and is networked via leased lines/modems to various sales offices, so that sales people can access it at a total cost of US\$ 1.5M. The cost of training, systems conversion, etc. adds to US\$ 1M. Note that the total cost of the investment is US\$ 3.5M, of which only US\$ 1.5M is directly attributable to sales force activity. The organizational intent remains the same: provide access to expert advice for product configuration and process orders on-line, so a sales person can order the product correctly and provide the customer the needed service.

## A.3. Scenario 3

A firm invests in 400 PC notebooks and software for sales personnel so that they can use them to configure products for customers and prepare business orders at the customer site. Each piece of this hardware, software and training costs the firm about US\$ 8000 per sales person. This investment, totaling about US\$ 3M, is intended to improve the productivity of sales professionals, i.e. enable them to correctly order the product and service the customer effectively.
