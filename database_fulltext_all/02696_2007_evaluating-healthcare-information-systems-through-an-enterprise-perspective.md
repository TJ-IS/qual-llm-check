# Evaluating healthcare information systems through an ‘‘enterprise’’ perspective

N.A.D. Connell <sup>a,\*</sup>, T.P. Young b

<sup>a</sup> School of Management, University of Southampton, Highfield, Southampton SO17 1BJ, United Kingdom <sup>b</sup> Department of Information Systems and Computing, School of Information Systems, Computing and Mathematics, University of Brunel, St John’s Building, Kingston Lane, Uxbridge, Middlesex UB8 3PH, United Kingdom Received 6 March 2006; received in revised form 29 November 2006; accepted 21 April 2007 Available online 15 June 2007

## Abstract

Here we reflect upon the design and implementation of large-scale healthcare IS, noting the tendency of such systems to gravitate from a delivery (clinical) to a control (managerial) emphasis. Drawing on the concept of the ‘enterprise model’, we examined how adopting different perspectives of the enterprise might inform both design and evaluative criteria.

Three areas of tension were identified; these prompted us to consider some elements that contribute to a framework that can guide the implementation and evaluation of healthcare applications. We proposed an illustrative conceptual model which, while not claiming to identify causal factors for success or failure, nevertheless gives indications of the interplay between the areas of tension. <sup>#</sup> 2007 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved

Keywords: Healthcare IS; Evaluation; Enterprise; Design

## 1. Introduction

Over the next few years, the UK government intends to spend in excess of £6bn on IT systems in the National Health Service (NHS), through its National Programme for IT (NPfIT), also known as NHS Connecting for Health (CfH) [26]. This level of investment exceeds anything experienced to date, especially in terms of capital commitment at a national level. Given the track record of large-scale government-led IT projects, it is worth clarifying what we have learned about IS healthcare implementation.

In a heavily resource-constrained domain such as healthcare, the opportunity cost (as well as the real cost)

of investment is massive. It is not uncommon for political and social pundits to describe any public expenditure as ‘‘equivalent to n new hospitals’’. Such descriptions are not always sweeping and ill-considered [22]: for decades, UK computer-based information systems have been perceived as expensive and poorly managed and motivated [3].

In terms of evaluation, Kaplan [21] considered the efficiency of medical computing in the 1980s, and identified a ‘lag’ in its implementation compared to other sectors. Others have focused on dependent ‘‘success’’ variables [7,8], whilst more recent approaches attempted more holistic or systemic evaluations [20,11]. Within healthcare, the recognition of multi-stakeholder perspectives adds further complications, raising issues such as power asymmetry, political-driven changes, technology-led changes and conflicting objectives. Others authors have drawn on a socio-technical perspective [12] to consider ways in which the success of such systems might be evaluated. This paper was constructed to consider the field and reflect on whether healthcare IS requirements are sufficiently different to encourage us to re-think existing evaluative models, and if so, whether healthcare systems must be treated as a ‘‘special case.’

## 2. Historical context of IS healthcare application

It was not our intent here to summarise the many applications of IS to healthcare (see, for example [9,18]) but to note the wide range of such applications, including hospital infrastructure, Electronic Patient Records, decision support, and others. Evaluation of such systems does not always reflect or engage with this heterogeneity in ascribing ‘‘success’’ or ‘‘failure’’ to such systems. We have, however, seen four possible indicators – level of investment, size, conception-reality gap issues, and subjective perceptions of success – that the literature acknowledges as making it difficult to define success factors for healthcare IS.

In the UK, the level of IT investment has been perceived as a major shortcoming. A study of sectors [28] showed that, based on annual IT expenditure per employee, healthcare comes last (just under £1000), with general government funding at three times that level, and the financial sector investing more than £8000. For many, the lack of effectiveness of healthcare IT is simply a question of too little spread too thin. Whilst this raises interesting questions as to what healthcare IS might be if there were funding levels nearer £4000, it ignores the questions:

\- Would such systems be effective?

\- Would the money be well spent?

We note this disparity in investment, but consider other ways in which healthcare may be different from many other public and private sector application areas.

A further factor for failure may be the question of size, a relative term associated with scale. There are few good definitions of what constitutes a ‘‘large’’ system (see, for example [4]). In our paper, the extent (or spread rather than volume) of a system’s use seems a reasonable measure, but it is one we have not found effectively applied in the literature. Does the scale of most healthcare systems pre-dispose them to failure? Does this mean that all large systems are likely to fail, with healthcare systems being one example? Examination of ERP implementations [32] has indicated that size alone, whilst presenting special challenges and difficulties, is not per se an inhibiting factor to success.

However, there may be a scale dimension in healthcare which requires special consideration.

There has been a recent suggestion that there is a lack of fit between application and work practices – the conception – reality gap – and the environment or culture that it is expected to support. This occurs in different ways. For instance, in the UK in the 1980s the government-led resource management initiative (RMI) [16] created an internal market between healthcare suppliers and deliverers – including the implementation of IT systems – in almost all UK hospitals. This was a large initiative, both in its vision and budget, with a principal focus on resource efficiency. Despite a massive budget, its vision was never fulfilled. The cultural problems of importing a US system – (emphasising cost recovery and specific clinical protocols) – into the NHS proved insurmountable – fewer ‘than 30% of hospitals ever got their nursing information fully operational, and many of these were never fully used.’

Another UK example was the Regional Information Systems Plan (RISP). Conceived in the mid-1980s as a large, multi-agency system, it was intended to link every ward, surgery and district nurse in its Region, but it was considered a costly mistake. The loss of support of crucial stakeholders proved critical, demonstrating, ‘there is some evidence that the administrative focus of the RISP project was not supported by key clinicians who wanted a more clinical focus’ [3]. The failure of healthcare IS to gain clinical support is a recurring theme, both in the popular press, where strong dissatisfaction has been voiced over ‘‘choose and book’’ systems and in professional publications, where anxieties have been recorded.

Over the last two decades, the literature has changed from a positivisticly-driven search for success and failure variables to a recognition of the subjective nature of success, casting failure or success as ‘‘social constructions,’’ in which technology is not neutral but one of the factors that is shaped by different stakeholder groups [36]. This ‘collaborative’ [27] view shows implementation as open to a number of interpretations that are legitimised within social and political contexts. Where clinicians are involved, there are interesting applications of theories of user acceptance [6] within telemedicine [5] – where the study investigated 400 clinicians’ acceptance of IT systems based on the theory that beliefs shape attitudes and consequently guide action – and medical record use [23], where willingness to use medical record systems based on ‘perceived service level’ was investigated using a sample of 80 young, predominantly female healthcare workers. Thus, a set of clinical, managerial, and political constructions are required by different stakeholders, in the measurement of a system’s success.

A review of IT projects [30], based on interviews with IT managers, evaluated their performance in meeting three criteria: budget, timescale, and functionality (a fourth category of ‘abandoned’ ignored the other measures). The authors concluded that only 16% of all IT projects met their targets in all three dimensions. However there was a lack of robust, widely accepted evaluation methods, particularly with respect to the issue of cost. Smithson and Hirschheim, for example, suggested that different criteria were applied according to the level at which the evaluation was performed. They suggested five levels – macro, sector, firm, application, and stakeholder – pointing out that different criteria would inevitably apply to each.

In multi-stakeholder environments, notions of ‘‘success’’ and ‘‘failure’’ become increasingly subjective. Studies of senior IS professionals highlighted the distinction between technical and organisational issues, noting that the latter were perceived by their respondents as more important. These results appeared independent of organisation type.

The rich, and diverse, set of actors in healthcare IS offers serious challenges to system developers. Even system failures such as that suffered by the London Ambulance Service [2] (where the failure of a computer-aided despatch system directly contributed to the deaths of patients), might be viewed differently through a hermeneutic lens: the complex interactions of agendas and desired outcomes becomes a tangled web that reductionist approaches (however well-intentioned) cannot always address successfully [25].

Finally, we should ask whether there is something unique about the UK context where, over recent years, the NHS has been moving away from its apparent ‘‘care for all irrespective of cost’’ philosophy towards a costconscious, metric-driven environment. IT systems, whose benefits are notoriously difficult to quantify compared with, say, changes in care patterns relative to patient outcomes, appear to have ignored any value-formoney evaluation. There appears to be little evidence that the UK’s NHS is any worse at IT implementations than other countries. Indeed, primary care IT in the UK is considered one of the best in the world [10].

In summary, while healthcare exhibits a number of factors which make evaluation difficult, there is a widely stated feeling that healthcare IS have failed to fulfil their potential, and a catalogue of high profile major programme failures in the UK appear to support this. The most recent UK government initiatives address many of the more obvious criticisms of this type of programme, but continue to receive criticism in the press, in academic journals, and most surprisingly, in an open letter of criticism from 23 professors of IT and computer science.

It is possible to view this discussion in terms of two contrasting perspectives: social process (how the need for such systems is articulated, how they are then conceived, specified, designed implemented and ultimately used or abused) and architecture (the way in which the elements of the system are partitioned conceptually and physically and structured to meet user needs).

## 2.1. Clinical versus control

We see a need for healthcare systems to draw on other sectors for their basic implementation models, developing a set of application models targeted at their specific needs. In particular, the successes of IT in manufacturing has led to an ‘enterprise’ view managed and empowered through IS [33]. In healthcare applications, the ‘enterprise model’ may provide those features that aid in managing the enterprise.

Despite references to clinical applications and user needs, in some respects healthcare IS has adopted this enterprise-type model, in which the system outcome is an ability to share information and communicate through formal organisational infrastructure, rather than person-to-person. This might lead to information services that are offered to users as a reflection of what works in other sectors, rather than what is needed.

Thus there is risk that, while clinical goals are articulated, the applications tend to gravitate towards management and administration.

Returning to the example of the RMI, how well were such systems set up to support the clinical side of care? Despite the mandate of enabling the NHS to provide ‘a better service to its patients’, only one of the six RMI pilot sites built a patient-oriented aim into its objective: higher throughput. The rest sought to improve the service to managers. One might even argue that the RMI was not a failure; rather, it subverted a patient-focused vision to a set of more enterprise-oriented goals. As far as ‘‘helping managers manage’’ was concerned, they liked it. Clinicians rated it well as a management tool: 68% (doctors) and 76% (nurses) approved of the proposition that it encouraged good working practice (‘strongly agree’ and ‘agree’). Reflecting on how this would convert into better care, the response was more cautious, especially for doctors—50% felt that it would improve patient care, while only 33% believed it would do so at lower cost.

There was some evidence, also, of the dominance of the ‘management’ agenda. This type of data is not usually found in the academic literature, and we are not aware of any systematic work to classify the healthcare IS markets, beyond that performed in company marketing departments. However, an examination of the US market in 1997, for instance [15] indicated that Hospital Information Systems (finance, administration, charging), at \$4.6bn accounted for nearly four times the total (\$1.2bn) spent on departmental systems (ICU, obstetrics, ER, etc.), clinical laboratory systems and picture archiving and communication systems (PACS) combined. (Equipment, such as MR or CT scanners, will clearly have a software element, but is not normally thought of as PACS.)

However, such a market-analysis approach has its own methodological problems, not least the question of whether marketing reports were ever meant to be compared, as well as raising the question of whether information about a single year is indicative of the wider picture. The scale of the imbalance in favour of management IS suggests a clear application of enterprise-type thinking and some reluctance to address the clinical benefits of systems.

## 2.2. Local versus national

Having noted a tendency for the enterprise concept to affect the sort of information services available to healthcare, the second strand of our ‘‘distinctiveness of healthcare’’ argument relates to the scale of the IS. In many non-healthcare applications, the factory, the office, or the headquarters might represent an appropriate scale for the IS focus. Whilst IS infrastructure addresses interenterprise networks, the scale of the building block is often the enterprise itself. However healthcare in Britain is fundamentally a national-scale issue, with integrated care pathways, which apply to both primary and secondary care, as well as clinical specialties. One of the interesting developments in the UK has been that the infrastructure is being driven at a national level. Thus, building solutions around smaller units (the hospital, the surgery, the clinic), and then connecting them following the inter-enterprise model will inevitably lead to suboptimal usage and communication barriers.

In a sense, therefore, the enterprise scale, useful in many other sectors, may be both too large and too small for healthcare. It is too large for personal interaction but too small to capture the distributed nature of care with geographically separated teams of clinicians and care givers and shared clinical services at the national or local level.

## 2.3. Interactive versus interpersonal

We have noted that healthcare demands person-toperson interaction for collaborative diagnosis, treatment assessment, planning and decision-making, and that these elements need to be supported to a greater degree in healthcare than other sectors. This should lead to operational and developmental models that accentuate the need for interpersonal communication. Such a ‘partnership model’ of information use is not peculiar to healthcare—it is seen in the customer relationship models (CRM) and is similar to negotiating patient care, or applying policy to prescribing drugs according to budgetary constraints [19]. Such exchanges might take place in structured or unstructured settings (for example, information exchange with patients suffering from similar conditions). However, a key difference in the healthcare setting arises because of its inherent challenge in the face of an operationally heterogeneous user population.

In highlighting the three tensions, we recognise that many of the issues which are evident in healthcare are increasingly making their presence felt in other domains. For example, the idea that users of systems have changed more dramatically than the organisations of which they are a part [38] – that the context not only shapes, but is shaped by, the interactions of the system’s elements [35] – adds further stress to the dynamic and unpredictable nature of these tensions.

## 3. A conceptual framework for healthcare IS

The complexity and uniqueness of healthcare IS demands a re-conceptualisation of existing design or evaluation frameworks. We suggest that there is a substantial need for a taxonomy to promote the interpersonal requirements of healthcare, to draw out some themes and classify the issues. Such a taxonomy might indicate how and why healthcare needs IS models that differ from those in other sectors.

First, we have noted the heterogeneity of healthcare systems; this might impact on their being perceived as ‘‘failures’’ by different stakeholders. The distinction surrounding the purpose of the system, which might loosely be characterised as either ‘‘clinical’’ or ‘‘managerial’’, is less marked in other domains, where the managerial application arises as a by-product (for example, exception-reporting or data-mining) of a primarily business-led application.

Second, a healthcare application draws data from a variety of different sources, both internal and external. For example, a patient record might include data from a

GP, physiotherapist, acute care provider (hospital), and the patient. Each data element might take a variety of forms, some well-structured and some ill-structured.

In developing a model framework, we recognised that any social processes involving healthcare delivery would involve access to, or demand for, information.

To reflect on the relationships between the main thrust of an application and its data source, we propose four classes, which are not intended to be mutually exclusive, but which indicate the principle motivation behind the development of an IS and also help us to focus more precisely on the differences (and similarities) between health IS applications.

Internal clinical (IC) information in Table 1 might include radiology reports or the original images or notes on patients treated in the hospital. By contrast, internal managerial (IM) information is more likely to take the form of bed-management statistics or financial data. Clinical data or information from outside the enterprise (external clinical, EC) might include pathology results from an external lab or guidelines and information services to brief, train, and update clinicians. Finally, management data originates from outside the enterprise (external managerial, EM)’ this may include national statistics or management guidelines.

There is evidence of a strong preference to apply IS to managerial, rather than clinical, activities. Also, the barrier between ‘within’ and ‘outside’ will often slow the delivery process, and introduce a qualitative difference between the clinical and managerial responses. In the clinical domain, patients or samples often move to a different location, while distance may be more easily managed in the managerial domain.

From a significant background paper, a model [1] was adapted and developed for our discussion; it identifies critical information sources and demand flows (Fig. 1).

Imagine a generic, ‘healthcare information-seeker starting on some healthcare task. This ‘seeker’ may be a potential patient in search of advice or a health professional. Clearly there are several information sources. The information-seeker may decide to go online in search of data (pathway A) or access a library in search of guidance (pathway B).

Another source of data might be an application (pathway C)—such as a booking system for an appointment. Finally, people seek information in making decisions and in following processes through conversation—specifically face-to-face (pathway D). Most decisions rely heavily upon this path.

We note that information-seeking behaviour along pathways A, B and C, has similarities in other sectors, where people access a range of data repositories and use a wide range of applications. In the top half of Fig. 1, the data is available to many users as required and as appropriate. We drew a line underneath it: the ‘enterprise line.’ Much of the literature on healthcare IS failure has indicated a difficulty in doing what would be considered good practice in any other sector, including understanding the culture, capturing operational procedures, gaining and retaining buy-in of stakeholders, specifying changes in process through the introduction of new infrastructures, and designing and managing. Safran et al. [29] observed: ‘One of the challenges in the design of computer systems to assist health care providers is how to support collaboration while not requiring that people meet face-to-face.’ It is this element of healthcare delivery that appears to be missing from much of the analysis; a face-to-face approach is deeply embedded within medicine and nursing.

Below the enterprise line there is a requirement for strong interpersonal communication if people are to obtain and then secure the information through conversation. While other sectors require interpersonal communication at specific times, a great deal of healthcare information is embedded in the experience of others. This partitioning recognises that there are few, if any, really effective ICT tools to support information seeking behaviour.

We note a number of interesting possible themes, including a system to provide patients with a narrative on healthcare experience [17]; classifying stakeholder needs and developing solutions by adopting a much more cross-disciplinary approach; embracing sociological techniques [14]; and examining the nature of interpersonal communication in healthcare. More recently, ethnographic approaches have been used to define patients’ needs [34], together with exploration of ‘information seeking behaviour’ among a group of oncologists [24], all of which look extremely promising in structuring the debate as to what is required.

Thus, for each information source, our framework recognises two tensions: between clinical and managerial functions and between local and national needs.

This addresses some of the contextual issues raised by Yi et al. [37], who articulated, although they did not explore, the possibility of ‘‘exploiting a social network to enhance the subjective norm and augmenting the control perceptions’’.

Privacy needs create a tension that telemedicine must address when sending the patient’s results to a remote clinician, or bringing a remote clinician into a local discussion or decision. There is local or national control as well as problems in the scale of the IS. The tensions are not the same for each of the sources. The clinicalcontrol tension may, for instance, be weighted heavily in favour of management as far applications and data storage are concerned, whereas interactive access to library information probably favours clinicians over managers.

We contend that more services will be required if healthcare delivery is to benefit from IS. An enterprise view of healthcare may not be sufficient and a comprehensive framework has still to be developed. The enterprise model covers only a subset of the needs of the healthcare community. While we do not propose what our framework could be, we have tried to partition the problem to identify what taxonomic agenda might lie outside the enterprise model. The work of others who have started to explore this interpersonal agenda reinforces our belief that a more rigorous taxonomy is required.

## 4. Validation of the model

The main aim of this model was to mediate a set of discussions about healthcare IS that have been difficult to identify and shed light on critical failures. The model we proposed identified the criteria and factors that influenced them, rather than proving causality. However, it is easy to identify tensions in the model, and this at the very least has indicated aspects of external validity.

The historical imbalance between management and clinical development can crystallise into legacy problems in which the primacy of management over clinical systems becomes formally embedded in development schedules, giving rise to examples of new administrative systems needing to be up and running before additional clinical systems can be installed.

Finally, this model predicts that a better balance between systems – interactive versus interpersonal – would greatly enhance the impact of IS in healthcare. There is little opportunity to validate this prediction since such systems do not yet exist. However, there is some evidence that doctors strongly prefer ‘natural interfaces if not interpersonal contact. Ford [13] made the case that standard interaction through a keyboard and sequential access to files did not meet clinical needs and did not address most clinicians’ inability to type. This bore out results in earlier work [31], in which attempts to reinvent the clinical record as menu-driven proved both time consuming and a failure. However, we have highlighted the need for more consideration to be given to interpersonal systems, but until more such systems are available for comparison, it is difficult to propose precisely how the tensions can be validated.

## 5. Conclusions

We have looked at the difficulties of evaluating healthcare systems and considered some reasons for their apparent failure. We noted the clinical–managerial distinction, and that there appears to be a natural tendency for ‘clinical’ elements of healthcare to migrate towards the managerial—or perhaps even be subverted to their needs.

Secondly, we noted that it is worth asking whether an ‘enterprise’ is too small or too big a building block. Its remoteness inevitably distances ‘‘the system’’ from the communities it is supposed to serve. Without resolving the question, we point out that this highlights an innate tension between the local and the national. Finally, we describe a tension between the interpersonal and the interactive, arguing that healthcare requires more person-to-person knowledge transfer than a traditional enterprise might.

Through our use of the three tensions found especially in healthcare, we constructed an explanatory conceptual model. This allowed us to explore the role of IS in the healthcare sector in terms of an ‘enterprise-type’ model, and concluded that it will often meet healthcare needs in terms of people and resource management, but falls short of meeting healthcare’s clinical agenda and a need for interpersonal communication.

Finally, we argue that better person-to-person models are needed to understand how the collegial and interpersonal elements of care delivery could better be embodied in the systems used for care delivery, and to achieve a better balance between the interactive and the interpersonal in healthcare.

Can this analysis shed any light on the question of IS delivering better value-for-money to healthcare? Even if the failure rate were lower, healthcare IS could still markedly improve by making a strong move from the managerial to the clinical. However, the interpersonal agenda, so important in satisfying the demand for healthcare information and at the heart of our model, appears to be largely ignored. Until tools and services are available to better align healthcare requirements, it will be difficult for healthcare IS to be successful.

He leads the IS group at the University of Southampton, UK, as well as being Director of the Centre for Narrative Studies, a multi-disciplinary initiative whose work includes reflections on informal information system developments such as organisational storytelling and communities of practice.
