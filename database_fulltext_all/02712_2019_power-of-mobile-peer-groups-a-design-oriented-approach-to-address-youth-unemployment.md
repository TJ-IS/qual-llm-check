ABSTRACT: To investigate the societal value of digital peer groups in the context of youth unemployment, one of society’s most pressing problems, we developed a novel mobile peer-group-based career counseling approach. Following a designoriented methodology, we constructed an intervention in which we supplement offline one-on-one counseling sessions between youths and career counselors with mobile peer groups where youths can support one another in a trusting environment, independent of time and place. We evaluated the career counseling intervention, implemented on a WhatsApp platform, in a randomized field experiment conducted at the German Federal Employment Agency. Results suggest that the mobile peer-group-based career counseling approach offers substantial added value compared with traditional career counseling. It significantly increases a youth’s chances of finding employment while improving his or her attitude towards career choice, career maturity, and career search intensity. Our study contributes to literature on the societal impacts of information and communication technologies and to research in design science. The proposed mobile intervention has important policy implications for addressing youth unemployment.

KEY WORDS: AND PHRASES: societal benefits of ICT, mobile peer group, online peer group, youth unemployment, unemployment mitigation, field experiment.

## Introduction

Information and communications technology (ICT) is often heralded as a platform for the generation of economic and societal value. This view of ICT is evoked in the Global e-Sustainability Initiative’s [52] comment: “As ICT has become faster, cheaper and more accessible globally, [it has the] potential to generate powerful environmental, economic and social benefits beyond what we envisioned.”

One important pathway through which ICT can create economic and societal value is as a driver of “connectedness” among geographically dispersed individuals. The enormous growth of online communities and online peer groups has attracted the attention of researchers [52]. Indeed, online peer groups have proven strikingly successful in supporting people facing personal and social challenges in healthcare [52, 53], education [36, 111], parenting [41, 90], and job performance [12, 147]. Collectively, these successes affirm the oft-made claim that the characteristics of online and mobile communication, such as accessibility, disinhibition, and written interaction mode, reinforce peer group mechanisms seen offline, including informational and emotional support, social identity, and social comparison [18, 29, 32, 47, 67, 111].

Despite online peer groups’ striking value to society, research and practice have neglected one promising context: youth unemployment. Around the world, more than 70 million people between the ages of 15 and 24 suffer from unemployment [72]. In some nations, such as Spain and Greece, nearly half of youths are unemployed [45]. The consequences are severe—and not just for the people out of work. Their societies also pay a high price, since people without work experience or skills tend to have fewer prospects even when economic conditions improve [55, 103]. Millions may contribute less and impose more costs on their communities and governments well into adulthood. Their frustration can even lead to political radicalization and crime [1, 24, 44, 80, 115].

A widely used, traditional method for addressing youth unemployment is career counseling that is typically delivered face-to-face. We expect youth career counseling to benefit from online peer groups for two reasons. First, despite its demonstrated value in reducing unemployment—and the high cost of failure—career counseling for young people faces increasing criticism: it can be costly and may be insufficient [106, 114], as young people get more information and advice from social media and mobile applications [13, 71]. Indeed, many researchers believe that to “remain vital, career counseling must be willing to metamorphose into new forms as the times dictate” [114, p. 58]. Online peer groups might answer this call. Second, evidence suggests that offline peer groups can significantly increase the probability of participants’ obtaining jobs and reducing the time to find employment [8], which is encouraging evidence for a positive effect of online peer groups.

In this paper, we propose and develop a novel mobile peer-group-based career counseling intervention as the IT artifact. Following a design science approach [61], we supplement offline one-on-one counseling sessions between youths and career counselors with mobile peer groups, where the design of the mobile peer groups is informed by research on peer groups and mobile IT effects. We hypothesize that peer group mechanisms, reinforced by the characteristics and technical features of mobile interactions, will improve participants’ attitude toward career choice, career maturity, career search intensity, and employment.

We evaluate the proposed design in a randomized field experiment conducted in cooperation with the German Federal Employment Agency. We purposely selected the site for its strong connection to youths at the transition from school to job, thereby enabling an evaluation of the approach in a real-world setting. Results suggest that the mobile peer-group-based career counseling approach offers substantial added value compared to traditional career counseling alone, that is, the competing artifact. It significantly increases a youth’s chances of finding employment while improving his or her attitude towards career choice, career maturity, and career search intensity.

Our contribution to research and practice is twofold. One, this study is the first to establish the societal benefits of ICT, in particular of online peer groups, in the context of youth unemployment, one of society’s most pressing problems. Second, following design science methodology, we conceptualize and implement a mobile peer-group-based career counseling approach as a novel IT artifact based on peer group mechanisms observed offline and documented in prior literature. This approach constitutes an innovative intervention that labor organizations around the world could use to combat the scourge of youth unemployment.

## Theoretical Background

The design of our mobile peer-group-based career counseling intervention is motivated by research on peer groups as well as mobile IT effects. We briefly review the relevant literature on peer groups in both offline and online contexts.

## Peer Groups and Peer Group Effects

Interest in peer groups has generated a rich literature in diverse disciplines ranging from psychology, economics, and communication to research on IT adoption and use [2]. In this body of work, peer groups are defined as networks of people sharing certain social characteristics including age, class, education and location, and interacting on “a level of equality” [63, p. 406; 2, 27, 94]. In recent years, online and mobile peer groups have received more attention due to the proliferation of digital media [30, 31, 49, 66, 67, 111, 126, 130]. We define online and mobile peer groups as those interacting and communicating through computers or mobile communication networks [66].

Interactions in both offline and online peer groups are found to affect members in various ways (e.g., [7, 31]). Based on Erchul and Raven’s [43] definition of social influence, a peer group effect can be defined as a “change in the belief, attitude or behavior of a person […] which results from the action or presence [of a peer] or group of [peers]” [43, p. 138]. Peer group effects, analogously to social influence, often go beyond one-to-one interactions, demanding a network-centric view [57]. Indeed, peer group effects can spread virally through an entire peer network, affecting not only people directly but also influencing those indirectly connected to the source [65, 105].

## Mobile IT Artifacts and Mobile IT Effects

The past decade has witnessed such an explosive growth of mobile technology that mobile devices now outnumber people. Mobile devices have become “the preferred mechanism for many individuals to interact with friends, families, and colleagues, transact business and access the Internet, social media, news and entertainment” [100, p. 503]. The services they afford are part of everyday life [146]. Prior research demonstrates that information systems (IS) can offer new or extended functionality when experienced in mobile context. In contrast to the early days of mobile devices, when people used them mostly as phones and web browsers, mobile devices are now multipurpose, allowing people to connect and communicate with different social peer groups based on mobile app services (e.g., Facebook, Twitter, WhatsApp) [100].

The most important advantage of the mobile IT artifact is captured in the leading adjective—mobile—that is, the convenience afforded by accessibility [122], the anytime and anywhere access of mobile services [11]. By eliminating time and space constraints, mobile devices improve information access, communication, coordination, collaboration, and knowledge exchange [34]. Another benefit of mobile technology use is flexibility [104], especially in the mobile work force [134]. Thus, mobile technology can raise efficiency and productivity as people make better use of their time [75, 122]. Mobile technology can even improve the quality of life. Choi et al. [25] for instance found that mobile services positively affect quality of life in 11 life domains with self (self-presentation), friends (improving friendships), and social (improving relationship with community members) as domains with the strongest experience satisfaction.

## Peer Group Mechanisms in Offline and Online Contexts

Peer group mechanisms [121] are processes facilitating or—in some cases—inhibiting successful outcomes in peer groups [66, 91]. While a comprehensive review of the extensive literature on peer groups is beyond the scope of this paper, based on recent representative literature (see online Appendix A), we isolate four key peer group mechanisms that represent the pathways through which peer effects are exerted.

First, informational support, also referred to as advice [130], knowledge [30], or social learning [73], can induce peer effects [20, 31, 49, 78, 85], that can change beliefs, attitudes, and behaviors. Information conveyed through “hard facts,” knowledge, personal experience, opinion, or advice, typically either increases the awareness of and interest in a topic, such as an action or a product [31, 73, 126], or induces “social learning” [2, p. 279]. For example, in the case of peer groups organized around health, information regarding procedures, costs, and benefits of actions (such as treatment options of diseases) reduces search efforts and uncertainty and, therefore, increases the successful adoption of these actions [30, 31, 52,

73, 89]. Some research has suggested that in addition to these beneficial effects of informational support, information about the risks of an action or a condition (such as a health condition) may also increase the anxiety of peers [30, 66].

Second, emotional support, also known as empathy [47] and esteem support [31, 49], may induce behavior changes in a peer group setting. To illustrate, empathy, recognition, and the compliments of peers can increase a person’s self-esteem and his or her positive outlook and motivation to act [31, 49, 52, 66, 67, 74, 111]. Furthermore, expressing feelings, revealing personal challenges, or telling peers about traumatic events can provide catharsis, thereby increasing emotional stability [47].

A third mechanism is the development of a social identity, that is, belonging to and feeling part of a group. In the literature, this mechanism is also referred to as cohesion [21, 47] and social support [20, 49, 97, 130]. Peer group members realize that they are not alone and have peers in the same situation who understand them and can provide support [21, 30, 66, 130]. This can reduce feelings of isolation and self-blame for individual problems [97]. Beyond empowering people, social dynamics may also have negative effects on those who do not feel integrated in their peer group [66].

Furthermore, behavior in groups is often governed by social comparison [18], also referred to as group pressure [4] and individual reappraisal [21]. Normative pressure to fulfill the expectations of others or conform to the behavior of the group can induce peer effects [2, 4, 74], that may be positive or negative. Comparing themselves with peers facing similar challenges can help group members reappraise their own situations. This reappraisal can help members become more optimistic, motivated, and energetic or see their situation in a more negative light, recognize its severity and become demoralized and less engaged in acting to solve problems [30].

The relative salience of peer group mechanisms has been found to be affected by characteristics of peer groups such as their size and whether they are in person or facilitated by technology. The “effect of group size [i.e., whether small or large peer groups are favorable for outcomes] depends on the task set for the group” [145, p. 436]. Network externalities, also known as network effects [73, 79], imply that with every additional member, the value of participating in the group and behaving like other members increases [60, 79]. But when peers discuss sensitive issues and trustbased interactions are needed, large groups might mitigate peer group effects. In fact, peers in “small groups receive more individualized assistance, more positive feedback [and emotional support]” [14, p. 45] and might be more involved in discussions [145, p. 436].

## Mobile Characteristics and Peer Group Mechanisms

Theoretically, it is likely that online or mobile characteristics influence peer group mechanisms. Indeed, while all four peer group mechanisms apply to both offline and online peer groups, the inherent characteristics of mobile peer groups— accessibility [29, 31, 39, 41, 144], disinhibition through anonymity [29, 144] and written interaction mode [29, 31]—have been found to affect the magnitude of peer effects. Some studies indicate that uncertain quality and credibility of information [30], the risk of the digital divide [143], a shallowness of relationships in comparison to face-to-face peer groups with in-person interaction [30, 41], and the risk of mobbing [143] in online peer groups might weaken their positive effects. Most research finds, however, that the capabilities provided by digital technologies reinforce the peer mechanisms observed in offline contexts.

Accessibility, for instance, reinforces peer group mechanisms in two ways. First, support in peer groups is not limited by time or geography but can be accessed anytime [29, 31, 39, 41, 143], and anyplace, from a tethered computer or, in the case of mobile peer groups, on the move [39]. Thus, group members are not constrained by working hours, distance, or private commitments [29, 31, 143] that typically reduce participation and attendance [58]; they do not have to postpone questions or appeals for help until the next meeting of a peer group. Accessibility also reinforces informational and emotional support for people with specific questions, such as about rare diseases, who might find it difficult to connect with local specialists or local fellow-disease sufferers [53, 92]. Also, the fact that online peer groups are ‘communities of choice’ compared to groups of geographically proximate people [128], means that potential members are more likely to find peers they can truly identify with [94]. Overall, studies indicate that online groups reinforce social comparison, as group members may be exposed to people from different social strata with diverse perspectives, experiences, and opinions [3].

Likewise, disinhibition reinforces emotional support. Because interactions in online or mobile groups are mediated by software and members have no direct means of observing each other’s physical looks or behavior, they “create […] a layer of anonymity” [94, p. 94] and provide a “sense of freedom […] to express themselves online without embarrassment or fear of judgment […]” [29, p. 101]. Participants can therefore discuss sensitive issues more easily or share opinions with less risk than in traditional face-to-face groups [31, 41, 96]. This is particularly true for youths, the target audience in our setting. Such anonymity also offers “silent support” [26, p. 3] to people who prefer to been unseen and facilitates informational support and advice [15]. The anonymity provided increases the possibility for self-disclosure and encourages honesty and intimacy. This selfexpression with a minimum of embarrassment or self-consciousness can promote self-esteem [143] and reinforce a sense of belonging and identity.

The written interaction mode of online or mobile peer groups likely also reinforces peer group mechanisms such as informational and emotional support. For instance, online peer groups can provide informational support not only to active participants but also to people who “lurk,” reading messages without contributing [31]. Members can re-read messages, allowing “greater cognitive processing [of information] than [oral] communication” [29, p. 101]. Communicating in writing can also facilitate emotional support, since some participants find it easier to express thoughts and feelings through writing [29]. The asynchronous, written communication mode provides group members with opportunities to spend time reflecting on their feelings and asynchronous interaction may mitigate the pressure inherent in real-time communication as members can take their time to compose written messages with care [32].

## Problem Context and Outcome Measures

The problem context of this study is youth unemployment, which is one of today’s most pressing problems with severe consequences for individuals, societies, and economies [1, 24, 44, 55, 80, 115]. The relevance of this context<sup>1</sup> is vividly underscored by two facts. First, despite extensive research and practices to define and evaluate interventions intended to reduce youth unemployment, it persists a major problem [45, 72]. Second, many stakeholders are calling for the modernization of career counseling for young people. Indeed, many see counseling as costly and insufficient [105, 106], as young people obtain an increasing amount of information and advice from social media and mobile applications [13, 71].

To evaluate career counseling in the context of youth unemployment, research typically explores three different kinds of outcomes. First, immediate outcomes include changes in attitudes toward career choice, such as optimism, motivation, or anxiety [10, 46, 70, 83, 84], as well as career maturity: the readiness to make “appropriate career decisions and cope with career development tasks” [112, p. 336; 64, 70, 83, 112, 123, 141, 142]. Second, it measures intermediate outcomes, such as career, job, or career search activities, particularly improvements in search strategies and intensity [73, 138]. Finally, a longer-term or ultimate outcome is employability or “employment assurance,” measured by the subject’s application status or formal acceptance to a training program, a higher secondary school or voluntary social year [48, 70, 81, 87, 98, 109]. Many observers consider employment assurance as the most important outcome, as it has direct impacts on youth unemployment rates. To the extent that immediate and intermediate outcomes have been found to predict ultimate outcomes, they are highly relevant as well [28, 76, 138].

Extensive evidence supports the positive effects of career counseling on these outcomes (cf. [70] for an overview). Research on employment assurance has even established positive effects of offline peer groups; specifically as part of “job clubs,” an offline group-based job-search program devised by Azrin et al. [8].<sup>2</sup> Job clubs were shown to help job-seekers (1) increase the probability of obtaining a job (after two months, 90% of counseled clients had obtained a job compared with 55% of those not counseled); and (2) obtain a job more quickly (for job clubs in a median of 14 days compared to 53 days). These results were supported by followup studies for job-seekers who were executives [88], receiving public assistance [17, 129], older than 50 [54, 116], and physically or mentally handicapped [7, 9, 16].

Although these studies of offline peer groups in the context of unemployment did not pay explicit attention to underlying dynamics, their findings suggest that peer group mechanisms were affected. For instance, in those offline peer groups, informational support appeared to be important, such as advice about “sources of job leads” [16, p. 21], “manner of dress and […] interview conduct” [7, p. 26], “acceptable wages” [118, p. 1], and “résumé preparation” [8, p. 26]). Emotional support also seemed to play a focal role, such as encouraging job-seekers after they have been turned down by employers, as did social comparisons such as “peer pressure” [129, p. 73] and “normative pressures” [101, p. 659; 8, 9, 16, 17, 101, 116, 118, 129].

## Research Gap

In sum, our review of the literature reveals robust evidence that online or mobile peer groups can have profound effects on members through a variety of peer group mechanisms, including informational and emotional support, social identity, and social comparison. Researchers have found that mobile characteristics can reinforce the impact of these mechanisms, and evidence is accumulating for positive peer effects in technology-mediated settings. In the context of unemployment, research has documented the positive effects of career counseling and offline peer groups on job-seekers, such as improving employment rates and salaries and decreasing the duration of unemployment. We juxtapose these two sets of findings to conjecture that mobile peer groups in career counseling can have a positive effect on (youth) unemployment. To validate our conjecture, we design and evaluate mobile peergroup-based career counseling based on prior findings of peer group mechanisms and mobile characteristics. By doing so, we are able to contribute to IS literature that examines the power of mobile peer groups.

## Design Artifact: Mobile Peer-Group-Based Career Counseling Approach

In conceptualizing the approach to supporting career counseling with online peer groups, we begin by examining the characteristics of traditional career counseling and those of online peer groups that might substantially enhance career counseling, thereby improving career searches and raising the probability of employment. Traditional career counseling is characterized by one-on-one offline conversations between a career counselor and a youth. Core elements include profiling, particularly assessing the youth’s skills and interests based on a résumé, a career orientation assessment that could include motivation, knowledge of professions and career decision status, and job counseling, which might include discussions of specific jobs or vocational training.

The mobile peer-group-based career counseling approach supplements career counseling with a mobile peer group component (see Figure 1). In addition to one-on-one offline counseling between a counselor and young person (1:1), mobile peer groups allow a group of people sharing similar social characteristics (n:n) to join an online group discussion of career decisions and applications between offline interactions. We refer to this mobile peer group as a “Career Community.”

Figure 1. Design Artifact “Mobile Peer-Group-Based Career Counseling Approach”

The literature on online peer effects yielded four major design decisions for the approach and associated technical features. First, we decided to develop Career Communities based on a mobile messaging application, driven by three primary motivations. One, millions of young adults today use mobile technologies on a daily basis, perceiving it as their natural form of communication. Two, a mobile application maximizes users’ access to support anywhere and anytime [29, 31]. Thus, youths can ask for peer advice at exactly the time they need it (e.g., when preparing for a job interview) and do not have to await scheduled (offline peer group) meetings. Finally, the mobile application creates a layer of anonymity, facilitating the discussion of sensitive issues such as fears of professional failure [31, 96]; youths in our Career Communities were able to use nicknames that could not be traced back their real identity. We selected the features of this mobile messaging application to enable the key peer group mechanisms discussed in the literature (see online Appendix A for an overview of these mechanisms). To provide informational support [31, 73], we ensured that the mobile application allows users to paste links, attach documents like CVs, pictures, videos and other forms of media, and share written communication [77]. Note that in our Career Communities, messages and attachments can be seen by the whole group; to address specific individuals, youths can add an “@Name” to the message. To enable emotional support [66, 111], the mobile application offers emoticons as a “new language for expressing human emotions” [68, p. 467]. Based on previous research, we expect emoticons to encourage a caring environment [38, 69]. In addition, we included a feature that that enabled groups to form by “invitation only,” where each community had a name and created a mutual picture of its identity. For instance, one of the Career Communities in our experiment was named “A Plan for Our Career .” We expected these features to support the social identity of the peer group [82]. To encourage social comparison, we allowed each user to create a visible profile [136, 145], including a (nick)name, photo, and short personal statement. To demonstrate how the mobile application supports theoretical online peer group mechanisms, Figure $2 ^ { 3 }$ illustrates the application’s design and features and how youths use it for conversation.

Second, we chose to base our Career Communities on asynchronous technology. The differentiation of technology as asynchronous and synchronous is well known from the context of e-learning (e.g.,). Asynchronous tools support “work relations among learners and with teachers, even when participants cannot be online at the same time,” while synchronous technology such as videoconferencing enables participants to communicate in real time [68, p. 51]. While synchronous technology would have several advantages in our context, in particular its ability to foster group identity and motivation though continued contact [37, 68, 117]; our rationale for asynchronous technology was based three primary reasons. First, it provides more time flexibility by allowing users to seek advice exactly when they need it rather than awaiting a scheduled time when all peers use the mobile device at the same time [103]. Second, research has established that asynchronous communication is “more suitable for reflection” [68, p. 500], an important element in career counseling, and facilitates the sharing and processing of information better than synchronous technology [37, 68]. And third, based on research on youths’ usage of instant messaging applications, we expect Career Communities to be used “nearsynchronously” as youth continuously check their messages and interact in real time [68, p. 52]. In this way, we sought to combine advantages of both synchronous and asynchronous technology.

Third, consistent with the spirit of the job clubs [7], we designed Career Communities as groups of no more than ten to 15 to help members build trust and provide emotional support, for instance through empathy, recognition, and compliments [14]. Each group is formed of youths of roughly the same age facing comparable career decisions to encourage the development of a group identity [21, 126] and social comparison processes [18].

To improve the quality and credibility of information [30] and prevent “mobbing” (the harassment of group members such as teasing or insulting peers because of their school grades under the cloak of anonymity [143]), we assigned a career counselor to facilitate each Career Community (Figure 3). The career counselor is responsible for the youths in his or her Career Community (note that each youth was assigned to exactly one Career Community and that these communities have no sub-community structures). Based on Azrin et al. [8, 9], career counselors participate in a Career Community in several ways. For example, they provide information and advice, such as about job fairs, applications and interviews, thereby initiating information exchange and communication among members. They also answer peer-group members’ questions, for instance about application deadlines, if other youths do not know the answers. By regularly inquiring about youth’s employment status, career counselors can also reinforce the social comparison processes. Finally, they intervene if a peer-group member is bullied.

We found clear evidence in our qualitative data from app usage supporting the presence of peer group mechanisms in our proposed career counseling approach. online Appendix B shows sample transcripts of conversations of study participants within our mobile application for all peer mechanisms, including emotional and informational support, social identity, and social comparison. These transcripts also illustrate accessibility as a key mobile IT effect in the Career Communities: users ask for peer advice at exactly the time they need it and get answers immediately.

## Evaluation Methodology and Results

## Evaluation Strategy

An essential part of the design science research process is the demonstration and evaluation of the IT artifact [56, 61, 113]. According to Hevner et al. [64], “the utility, quality, and efficacy of a design artifact must be rigorously demonstrated via wellexecuted evaluation methods” [61, p. 83] such as analytics, case studies, experiments, or simulations. We conducted a randomized field experiment to evaluate the mobile peer-group-based career counseling approach. The objective of our evaluation was to prove the “value added, […] acceptance and use” [33, p. 18], that is, the “utility, quality, and efficacy” of the novel IT artifact compared to the “competing artifact” [61, p. 83] of traditional offline career counseling.

A field experiment offers many advantages. The experimental design enhances the ability to make causal claims, for example. Indeed, experimental research is “one of the few research strategies [that] enables researchers to study cause and effect directly” [5, p. 34]. Randomizing subjects helps control for extraneous variables, which is particularly relevant in the context of unemployment where a range of individual, economic, and social factors may affect outcomes.

## Experimental Design

We used a pre-test/post-test with a control group, which is a research design known to provide satisfactory internal and external validity [95]. We conducted the experiment in three phases (Figure 4). In phase I, we selected subjects, asked them to complete a pre-survey and randomly assigned them to the treatment group, who would receive mobile peer-group-based career counseling, and the control group, who would receive traditional one-on-one career counseling only. We selected subjects as follows: career counselors approached approximately 1,400 pupils in the 9th and 10th grades of 14 randomly selected middle schools, main schools, and

## Setting

The experiment was conducted at the German Federal Employment Agency (Bundesagentur für Arbeit), the largest provider of labor market services in Germany, with more than 95,000 employees, a nationwide network of 154 employment agencies, and about 600 branch offices. Its core tasks include placing the unemployed in training and employment, paying unemployment benefits, and career counseling. In 2014, the Agency began developing a strategy to modernize career counseling using new media and other methods. Our mobile peer-groupbased career counseling approach attracted wide interest in this context, and seven career counselors in three employment agencies in Baden-Wuerttemberg volunteered to participate in our experiment to facilitate mobile peer-group-based career counseling. Each subject was assigned to exactly one of these career counselors. To avoid potential bias, all seven career counselors were responsible for subjects in the control group as well as for one Career Community and the respective subjects of the treatment group. We were thus able to ensure that the approach in the control group was not changed compared to the prior process in the agencies.

## Subjects

We sampled subjects for the field experiment from among pupils in the 9th and 10th grades of middle schools, main schools, and comprehensive schools in rural districts of the three employment agencies. We focused our experiment on pupils aiming to acquire O levels or secondary modern school qualification, as these imply similar paths for pupils after school has ended. Indeed, with these qualifications, pupils are obliged to either obtain an apprentice position and start vocational school or attend higher secondary school for an additional three years to take the A levels to qualify for university entrance [42, 137]<sup>4</sup> (see online Appendix D for a more detailed overview of the German school system).

## Data Collection and Measurement

During the experiment, we collected two datasets. First, to gather information on dependent variables, we conducted surveys before (pre-survey) and after (postsurvey) the treatment phase. Such self-reports have been found suitable for research on career counseling [108]. Career counselors provided the participants with the pre-surveys on paper after explaining the project. Career counselors sent the subjects links to the online post-survey, which used SoSei-survey software and repeated the questions from the pre-survey. We revised the instrument for measuring the dependent variables of career counseling based on feedback from the Federal Employment Agency and an analysis of the psychometric properties of the scales. We designed both surveys in German and based on literature (see online Appendix F for a list of measurement items and scales). On average, subjects took 6.4 minutes to complete the survey. Those who completed the post-survey earned a chance to win an Amazon voucher worth 25 EUR. For our second dataset, we obtained information on demographics and education from the Federal Employment Agency’s system, which allowed us to control for individual differences between subjects.

## Dependent Variables

We measured immediate, intermediate, and ultimate outcomes, i.e., dependent variables of career counseling within this study [48, 70, 108] (see “Problem Context and Outcome Measures” for an overview). For immediate outcomes, we selected attitude towards career choice [70, 83, 98, 138] and career maturity [112, 123]. For intermediate outcomes, we used career search intensity [70, 138]. These variables have been found to predict ultimate outcomes such as employment and career success [28, 76, 138]. We also queried subjects about their employment assurance—whether they were accepted for a training program, a higher secondary school, or a voluntary social year—as an ultimate outcome [8, 48, 70, 98, 109].<sup>5</sup>

Except for employment assurance, we measured all variables using multiple items on a six-point Likert-type scale (see online Appendix F). Taking account of our target group, we selected an even-numbered Likert-type scale without a mid-point to reduce the social desirability bias [50]. We calculated each variable as the average of its items’ scores.<sup>6</sup> We determined the validity of all multi-item variables on the post-survey using confirmatory factor analysis. We analyzed reliability and internal consistency using composite reliability (CR) values [140] as well as Cronbach’s alpha. Table 2 shows that all CR values and Cronbach’s alpha clearly exceed the recommended threshold of 0.7 [107, 125]. We analyzed convergent validity based on factor loadings and average variance extracted (AVE). As shown in online Appendix G, all factor loadings surpass 0.6 and can therefore be seen as acceptable [51]. Furthermore, all variables meet the requirement of AVE surpassing 0.5, as evident in Table 2 [107, 129]. We assessed discriminant validity by factor cross-loadings. online Appendix G shows that except for realization activities, loading of each item on its respective variable exceeds its loadings on all other variables. Furthermore, correlations among the variables are below 0.85, while the square root of the AVE of each variable exceeds its correlations with other variables (online Appendix H). This further confirms discriminant validity [86, 125]. In summary, the reliability tests and confirmatory factor analysis indicate that the scales have acceptable psychometric properties.

Attitude towards Career Choice. We measured attitude toward career choice using two items [138]. We derived the first, assessing the cognitive component of attitude toward career choice, based on Bagozzi and Yi [10]: “How would you best describe your current attitude toward your job search?” measured on a six-point Likert-scale ranging from 1 (very negative) to 6 (very positive). The second item, assessing the affective component, is based on Feather [46]: “When I think about my job search, I feel very good”; the scale ranged from 1 (not correct at all) to 6 (applies completely). Cronbach’s alpha for this scale was 0.71 (Table 2).

Career Maturity. Making appropriate career choices [112] typically depends on a range of skills and insights. We considered five within this study: problem awareness, that is, the subjects’ willingness and motivation to engage in their professional decisions [83, 98, 120]; self-assessment, that is, their ability to analyze their professional interests and skills (cf. [48, 83, 109, 120]); level of information, that is, the how much knowledge and information subjects have about relevant professions (cf. [48, 108, 109, 120]); decision behavior, that is., their willingness and ability to deal with career decisions (cf. [48, 83, 109, 120]); and realization activities, that is, the extent to which subjects know how to execute their career plans [120].

These dimensions are consistent with the German Federal Employment Agency’s internal career counseling objectives. We assessed career maturity using the Agency’s measure [64].<sup>7</sup> The overall scale for career maturity, averaging all 15 items, and four of its five dimensions, except for realization activities, exhibited adequate reliability with a Cronbach’s alpha of greater than 0.7. Following Höft et al. [64], we also decided to retain realization activities, although its estimated reliability was very modestly below 0.7.

Career Search Intensity. To assess career search intensity, we adopted the measure of career search intensity used by Caplan and Vinokur [23] and Vinokur and Caplan [135]. We asked respondents to rate how often in the past month they had engaged in each of seven activities: checked with career counseling at employment agencies; talked to relatives about career choice; talked to friends about career choice; used the internet, newspapers, or books to obtain information about career choice; contacted potential employers; applied for vocational training (e.g., send a résumé); and went on job interviews [23, 135]. We made minor changes to adapt items to the context of career choice. For example, we changed “How many times have you checked with employment agencies” to “How many times have checked with career counseling at employment agencies?” Since peers and friends play such a central role in this context, we disaggregated the question “How many times have you talked to friends, family, or people you know to get information about jobs?” into two separate items for friends and family. In three other cases, we combined two items, consistent with Wanberg et al. [138]. Taking account of the target group’s age, we used a six-point Likert-type scale from 1 (never) to 6 (very often), instead of asking subject to indicate the specific number of times they performed each career search activity. From all seven items, we constructed an overall index for career search intensity, which obtained a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.84.

Employment Assurance. Employment assurance is defined as a subject’s application status (assurance for apprentice position, higher secondary school or voluntary social year versus no assurance) at the time of the post-survey. The score is determined based on three items: “I have a binding assurance for an apprentice position,” “I have a binding assurance of a higher secondary school,” and “I have a binding assurance of a voluntary social year or the like,” all using a “yes/no”-scale. For all respondents affirming at least one of the three items, we coded employment assurance as 1 (existing) or 0 (not existing).<sup>8</sup> This is consistent with the Federal Employment Agency’s objectives of career counseling.

## Independent Variables

Our primary independent variable of interest is participation in mobile peer-groupbased career counseling. This corresponds to the experimental manipulation of this study. As discussed in our research design, participation was determined randomly at the beginning of the experiment.

## Verification of Random Assignment

Thus, any significant difference between them can be attributed to the experimental manipulation, that is, the participation in mobile peer-group-based career counseling. To confirm equivalence between treatment and control group, we tested for individual differences that have been found to affect youth employment and career search. These include age and gender [6, 19, 119, 124]; size of place of residence [6]; migration background [93]; nationality [119]; type of school and average grade [6, 119, 132]; and professional experience, such as internships [40, 133]. Chi-square analyses for all variables (see Table 3 for results) indicated no significant differences.

## Analysis and Results

To compare the efficacy of our mobile peer-group-based career counseling approach with traditional career counseling, we conducted Mann Whitney U-tests for independent samples on the variables attitude toward career choice, career maturity, and career search intensity (see Table 5 and Figure 5 for results) as well as employment assurance (Figure 6). We selected the Mann Whitney U-test as a non-parametric alternative to the regular t-test because several variables did not conform to a normal distribution. In addition, research has established it as an appropriate method for “the comparison of a control group with a group receiving treatment” [99, p. 60]. Except for the confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), we performed all analyses using IBM SPSS Statistics 22. For the CFA, we employed Partial Least Squares (PLS) using the software package SmartPLS v.3.2.4.

We note that random assignment of subjects, the inclusion of a control group, and equivalence between treatment and control groups supports causal inference. As expected, an individual’s attitude toward career choice is affected by mobile peergroup-based career counseling. Participants in the treatment group have significantly more positive attitudes toward career choice than youths in the control group who attended traditional career counseling (control group mean = 3.89, treatment group mean = 4.39, difference = 0.50, p = 0.016). This represents a 13% improvement in the treatment group’s attitudes over those of the control group.

## Discussion

## Theoretical and Practical Implications

This research was motivated by the need to help young people become more thoughtful about their careers, improve their ability to find work, and stay motivated throughout what is often a challenging and even disheartening job search process. It sought to exploit IT that allows people to build and maintain social connections.

Building on the theoretical foundation of mobile IT effects and peer groups mechanisms, and drawing on the principles of design science, we conceptualized and developed a novel approach, an IT artifact, to fight youth unemployment. Mobile peer-group-based career counseling can supplement one-on-one counseling by enabling youths to support one another. We demonstrated and evaluated our mobile peer-group-based career counseling approach (Career Communities) in a unique field experiment with 119 youths at the German Federal Labor Agency. The results are promising.

Our study provides strong evidence for the claim that for youths at the transition from school to job, a mobile peer-group-based career counseling approach has significant added value as compared to traditional career counseling alone. The approach helps youths obtain employment more effectively: employment assurance for an apprentice position, higher secondary school or voluntary social year improved by a remarkable 98% (p-value = 0.05) compared to traditional career counseling.<sup>9</sup> We also find significant effects for mobile peer-group-based career counseling on intermediate employment outcomes such as attitude, career maturity, and career search intensity.

Our findings contribute to IS research by demonstrating the societal benefits of ICT in the novel context of youth unemployment, one of society’s most pressing problems. We link mobile technology to a new outcome, theoretically, extending extant understanding of IT impacts. Furthermore, to the degree that our artifact leverages peer group mechanisms in a technology-mediated setting, we demonstrate that such interactions dominate traditional offline one-on-one career counseling. Thus, we add to findings on online peer groups in other contexts, for instance healthcare [29, 31, 52, 143], education [35, 36, 111], and parenting [41]. Furthermore, our findings demonstrate that the benefits of offline peer effects in the context of unemployment, established in previous research [7, 17, 88, 128], extend to mobile peer groups.

Based on our observations of interactions involving peer support (see online Appendix B), we expect that both peer mechanisms and mobile characteristics, such as access to peer support anywhere and anytime [29, 31] enhance the value of our proposed mobile peer-group-based career counseling approach. We note, however, that differentiating between peer mechanisms and mobile technology effects is not feasible within the scope of our study, which focused on the overall advantages of our approach. Nevertheless, this work offers three useful theoretical insights into how our mobile peer-group-based career counseling approach works.

First, our findings clearly indicate that the characteristics and technical features of mobile interactions, accessibility in particular, positively impinge upon the efficacy of our mobile peer-group-based career counseling approach through reinforcement of peer group mechanisms. Indeed, by expanding career counseling from offline meetings to mobile media, the mobile peer-group-based counseling approach increases the accessibility of career counseling (cf. [29, 94, 143]). Users can ask for peer advice at exactly the time they need it and get answers promptly instead of having to await scheduled offline peer group meetings. The transcripts of conversations presented in online Appendix B support this assertion, which is further reinforced by feedback from career counselors involved in our study:

Young people transitioning from school to training have questions almost every day about choosing a career or want to share their experiences. The mobile peer-group-based Career Communities add a lot of value here: they inform and encourage at these moments and not just weeks later. (Career Counselor 1, translated from German).

Second, findings strongly suggest the presence of three of the four peer group mechanisms: emotional support, social identity, and social comparison. For example, significant improvements in the youths’ attitude toward career search might constitute evidence of emotional support, such as encouragement through empathy and recognition, as well as social identity or feeling part of the group. Indeed, empathy or recognition of success in the wake of negative replies to job applications might encourage youths and improve their attitudes (cf. [31, 49, 111]). Also, the sense of community and belonging engendered by being part of a peer group of other youths facing career decisions at the transition from school to job might alleviate feelings of isolation [66, 97]. Career counselors involved in our study support this interpretation:

By giving youths the chance to interact with their peers and discuss career issues with them, the novel mobile peer-group based career counseling gives youths the feeling of not being alone. In addition, peers can act as role models. The basis for this is a trusting environment within those communities. (Counselor 2, translated from German).

The increase in problem awareness might in turn reflect social comparison processes [4, 18], complementing previous research. Simply being asked about employment status induces social pressure to fulfill the expectations of peers [2, 4, 74], and hearing about career search activities or the successes of peers helps youth become more optimistic and therefore more motivated and energetic [30].

Somewhat surprisingly, we found no significant effect of the mobile peer-groupbased career counseling approach on the youths’ level of information. This suggests that informational support—providing peers with information and raising their awareness and knowledge about career search—plays only a minor role in our setting. Although this result contradicts previous research [20, 31, 49, 78, 85], a plausible explanation is that given the proliferation of comprehensive information online, young people may no longer depend on peer groups for information about relevant professions. Conversely, they benefit from learning how to interpret and use information, for instance by relating it to individual interests and skills or by learning how to make decisions based on this information, as suggested by the sharp improvement in decision behavior. For IS research, these findings suggest that instead of providing information or encouraging information exchange between peers, mobile support groups should help users understand, interpret, and use information. An implication that follows is that teams designing mobile applications in the context of support groups should create technical features that initiate and encourage emotional support, social identity, and social comparison processes.

Our third theoretical insight into how our mobile peer-group-based career counseling approach works concerns synergies among peer effects. Results show that the treatment’s effect on employment assurance is consistently stronger than its effect on immediate outcomes such as attitude towards career choice and career maturity, as well as intermediate outcomes such as career search intensity. Synergies among peer effects may help explain these findings. It is plausible that peer effects on immediate and intermediate outcomes complement and mutually reinforce each other, thereby enlarging the size of the effect on employment assurance as the ultimate outcome. For instance, a more positive attitude towards career choice along with enhanced career maturity might increase a youth’s career search intensity. Likewise, the efficacy of a youth’s career search activities might increase along with his or her career maturity. Consequently, the young adult’s probability of finding employment increases considerably. This is consistent with previous research [28, 76, 138].

Aside from these theoretical implications, the mobile peer-group-based career counseling approach proposed here has the potential to change career counseling in practice. Our research is an important step towards modernizing career counseling and addresses the call for effective and innovative forms of career counseling that are more responsive to the needs of today’s youth [114].<sup>10</sup> Indeed, based on our approach, labor agencies might adopt a more network-oriented working mode initiating interactions and discussions between peers (n:n) in addition to one-onone counseling (1:1). Furthermore, Career Communities can help practitioners create an environment where emotional support of youths is facilitated based on mobile characteristics such as accessibility, disinhibition, and written in addition to oral interaction [29, 31].

Our findings also suggest a change in the direction of labor market institutions in general and career counseling in particular. Today, much of the youth unemployment discourse among policymakers focuses on initiatives designed to place young people in jobs. But the promising results of our career counseling approach suggest that to increase youth employment, institutions should also do more to help young people improve their career maturity and attitudes towards career choice. In the long run, this will increase youths’ placement in employment. Furthermore, our results indicate that labor market institutions should reduce emphasis on the provision of information for career choice and job search. They might instead focus more on providing emotional support, creating a social identity, and initiating social comparison processes in career counseling (cf. [18, 30, 31, 47, 49]). This is true for both online and offline career counseling as well as one-on-one counseling sessions and peer groups.

## Limitations

We acknowledge the limitations of this study. First, the strengths of the field experiment notwithstanding, we studied a single implementation of the mobile peer-group-based career counseling approach at the German Federal Employment Agency. The findings may therefore not be generalizable to labor market institutions in other countries.

Second, as in many experimental and empirical studies, our initial sample (i.e., those who agreed to participate from the total population of eligible youths) may be afflicted with self-selection bias: those who chose to engage in our study might have already taken proactive approaches to career choices and been motivated to seek and give support [66]. Expectancy bias [59] could also arise if career counselors participating in mobile peer groups consciously or unconsciously influenced behavior, for instance by encouraging youths to participate or by communicating optimism about the benefits of the mobile peer group approach. To address the first bias, we assigned subjects who chose to participate randomly into treatment and control groups, and provided limited standardized information about the study. To mitigate the potential for the second bias, we coached the career counselors on how and how not to engage with youths.

Third, our study did not empirically investigate peer group mechanisms and the mobile IT effects on youth unemployment. Instead, following the approach of design science research, we evaluated the value added of the comprehensive approach compared to traditional career counseling alone. While we were able to demonstrate that the approach added substantial value, for instance by significantly increasing a youth’s chances of finding employment, we do not observe the exact cause of this outcome. As a result, we are unable to isolate how much of this effect is explained by peer group mechanisms (or which are most relevant) or mobile IT effects, such as increased accessibility (and therefore would also be achieved by one-on-one mobile career counseling) and how much is a result of the increased availability of career counselors and higher intensity of offline counseling. In addition, we did not theorize or empirically evaluate the relationship among attitude, career maturity, career search intensity, and employment assurance. Finally, our experimental design did not include a no-treatment control group. Our findings should therefore not be interpreted as a demonstration of traditional career counseling’s ineffectiveness (cf. [7]).

## Future Research

We suggest that future research could address the limitations of our study and represent broader areas where deeper investigations into mobile peer groups would be illuminating. Online Appendix C proposes questions relevant for future research.

We invite researchers to work on the further generalization of our results. They might, for instance, evaluate our approach in other developed and developing countries. To address selection, expectancy and other potential biases in more detail, future research might compare the demographics of subjects to the overall population and place controls on the study.

Analyzing mobile IT effects in the context of peer-group-based career counseling in detail would be a valuable area for empirical research. In particular, researchers might evaluate how much of the effects of our mobile peer-group-based career counseling approach is explained by mobile characteristics as opposed to peer group mechanisms. We recommend two starting points for this analysis.

1. We compared our mobile peer-group-based career counseling approach with traditional career counseling. Researchers might in addition compare the approach developed here to offline peer groups to further illuminate the underlying mechanisms of offline and mobile peer effects in more detail.

2. Future research could compare our mobile peer-group-based career counseling approach with bilateral mobile career counseling (1:1) between career counselor and youths.

In addition to differentiating mobile IT effects and peer group mechanisms, researchers might also consider investigating the potential advantages of mobile peer-group-based career counseling in comparison to offline peer-group-based career counseling, such as improved accessibility, disinhibition, or written interaction mode. Manipulating technical features of the mobile application, for instance the ability to use emoticons or send attachments, applying synchronous technology instead of asynchronous technology, or building a computer-based peer-groupbased career counseling approach would also be worthwhile in future research.

Furthermore, our investigation of our mobile peer-group-based career counseling approach is only a first step in examining how peer group mechanisms work in the context of youth unemployment. Indeed, we call for research to investigate the mechanisms underlying our approach in more detail. Future studies might, for instance, analyze the content of messages exchanged within mobile peer groups or survey subjects and career counselors about peer mechanisms to measure them quantitatively. This would allow for a more fine-grained and explicit assessment of the relative relevance of peer mechanisms. Research might consider manipulating group size and composition, or the effect of the career counselor as a moderator. Researchers might also compare the availability of career counselors to the increased intensity of offline counseling for the control group to control for spillover effects.

Another opportunity for future research is the investigation of the proposed approach on labor market outcomes of attitude, career maturity, search, and employment assurance in detail. With a structural model, researchers could test the relationships among these elements and temporal sequence. Finally, a costbenefit analysis of mobile peer-group-based career counseling could help labor market institutions think about whether and how to adopt our approach. In this context, it would be relevant to compare traditional counseling and our mobile peergroup-based career counseling approach regarding counseling intensity—counseling hours per youth—and administrative efforts.

We note that that the opportunities identified above are applicable to contexts beyond youth unemployment. In particular, deeper understanding of the relationships between peer mechanisms and specific mobile IT characteristics and how they manifest in different contexts where peer groups are prevalent—such as health and education—would support the development of broader theory related to mobile IT artifacts. An interesting nuance to examine here could be the negative as well as the positive effects of mobile IT features. For example, does anonymity induce behaviors that are competitive rather than supportive? Do cliques or other types of structures inhibit the free flow of information? Research that examines both positive and negative outcomes would be essential to support the development of policy interventions based on mobile technologies that rely on social features.

## Conclusion

The enormous growth of online communities and support groups has sparked interest in their potential societal benefits. Despite abundant evidence demonstrating the effects of online peer groups in supporting people facing societal challenges in the contexts of health and education [29, 30, 31, 62], research has not yet considered or evaluated online peer groups in context of youth unemployment, one of today’s most pressing problems for individuals, the economy, and society as a whole.

Against this backdrop, this study proposed and developed a promising mobile peer-group-based career counseling approach to fight youth unemployment. Based on design science, we supplemented traditional career counseling, limited to offline one-on-one sessions, with mobile Career Communities that enable youth to support one another in a trusting environment, independent from time and place, together with peers facing comparable career decisions. Using a unique field experiment at the German Federal Employment Agency, we established that our novel approach has a substantial added value compared to traditional career counseling alone.

Our findings suggest that the proposed approach not only significantly increases a youth’s chances of finding employment but also his or her attitude towards career choice, career maturity, and career search intensity. Thus, this research is the first to establish the societal benefits of mobile peer groups in the new and promising context of youth unemployment. Moreover, it develops a novel peer-group-based career counseling approach that builds on mobile application features to strengthen peer groups mechanisms, thus providing practitioners with an effective and innovative alternative or supplement to traditional career counseling, answering their call for modernization.

From a broader perspective, we note that our mobile peer-group-based career counseling approach might be effective with other target groups. For example, considering the European refugee crisis and the inevitable challenge of integrating thousands of new citizens into society and the labor market, we believe that new approaches to career counseling, such as our mobile peer-group-based method, are worthy of further investigation.
