## a b s t r a c t

This paper investigates the effect of director interlock on corporate research & development (R&D) investment from the perspective of inter-organizational imitation. We argue that managers will imitate the R&D investment intensity of their interlocked-<sup>fi</sup>rms when deciding how much to spend in R&D for their own <sup>fi</sup>rm. Following prior literature, we further argue that under different types of interlocking director and industry characteristics, the impact of director interlock on corporate R&D spending is different. Using a sample of public firms listed in Chinese Shanghai and Shenzhen Stock Exchanges, our empirical results show that managers imitate the prior R&D investment intensity of their interlock partners, and the impact of director interlock on corporate R&D intensity is stronger when the interlocking director is an inside director in the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm or when the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm and interlock <sup>fi</sup>rm belong to the same industry. Our results still hold when we account for the potential sample selection bias, <sup>fi</sup>rm similarity, and the confounding factors that can contribute through unobserved industry characteristics.

## 1. Introduction

As the primary source of <sup>fi</sup>rm innovation, investment in research & development (R&D) enables <sup>fi</sup>rms to maintain sustainable competitiveness and development [6,27,44]. Investment in R&D is the driving force of future competitive capacity and pro<sup>fi</sup>tability [16,42], and it is expected to enhance shareholder wealth in the long run. However, it also adversely impacts short-term performance owing to its high uncertainty and high cost [1,12]. Therefore, how much to spend on R&D is one of the most sophisticated investment decisions faced by <sup>fi</sup>rm managers [2,14]. In fact, to a certain extent, it represents a trade-off between short-term performance and long-term performance [14]. Given the key role of R&D investment in <sup>fi</sup>rm development and its complexity in decision-making, scholars often investigate what factors affect corporate investment in R&D [2]. Minnick and Noga [33] suggest that board of directors play integral roles in choosing <sup>fi</sup>rm strategy as they have the <sup>fi</sup>duciary duty to participate in all major <sup>fi</sup>rm decisions and increase <sup>fi</sup>rm performance and shareholder wealth. According to the ‘upperechelons’ theory <sup>fi</sup>rms' economic outcomes are a re<sup>fl</sup>ection of their top managers' cognition and values [19]. Differences in managers' characteristics, which are composed of human capital and relational capital such as director interlock [11], lead to differences in the estimation of decision-making environments and eventually result in different <sup>fi</sup>rm decisions [19,40].

From the perspective of ‘upper-echelons’ theory, directors' human capital characteristics, such as age, tenure, education background, affect <sup>fi</sup>rms' investment in R&D [2,10,27]. From the perspective of resource dependency theory, directors' relational capital can serve as a key conduit for linked <sup>fi</sup>rms to important information and essential resources that are crucial to <sup>fi</sup>rm decisions [39]. According to social embeddedness theory [17] <sup>fi</sup>rms' economic action is closely embedded in networks of social relations. As one of the most important social relations of <sup>fi</sup>rms, director interlock acts as a mechanism for the diffusion of information about major practices and structures among linked <sup>fi</sup>rms [17,46], and plays a direct and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in <sup>fi</sup>rms' economic decisions and outcomes.

Prior studies document that imitation does occur through director interlocks. For example, Davis [13] shows that interlocking with other <sup>fi</sup>rms that have already adopted a poison pill will increase the possibility of a focal <sup>fi</sup>rm<sup>1</sup> also to adopt poison pill. Haunschild [21] suggests that managers are exposed to other <sup>fi</sup>rms' acquisition activities through director interlocks, and such acquisition activities become the role model for the managers to imitate. Regarding the relationship between interlock and R&D, previous research (e.g., [6,11]) focuses on studying whether the interlock to other <sup>fi</sup>rms in<sup>fl</sup>uences its own R&D spending.

For example, Dalziel et al. [11] <sup>fi</sup>nd that <sup>fi</sup>rms with the interlock ties to high-technology <sup>fi</sup>rms tend to have a higher R&D spending than <sup>fi</sup>rms with the interlock ties to low-technology <sup>fi</sup>rms. Chen et al. [6] <sup>fi</sup>nd that director's relational capital, measured by the total number of board directorships the board members hold at other <sup>fi</sup>rms, has a moderate effect on the relationship between CEO tenure/education level and R&D investment. In this study, we investigate whether the R&D spending of <sup>fi</sup>rm pairs connected with interlock is more similar than that of <sup>fi</sup>rm pairs without such a connection.

China provides us an ideal environment setting to study this research question. Firstly, as an institutional transition economy, the decision cost and risk for <sup>fi</sup>rms in China are higher than those in Western countries due to the unstable and imperfect institutions [38]. The poor economic environment leads to more sophisticated R&D investment decisions for <sup>fi</sup>rms. As argued by Lieberman and Asaba [30], imitation is a matter of frequent occurrence for <sup>fi</sup>rms, and imitation is more likely to occur in the environments characterized by uncertainty or ambiguity. Secondly, prior literature suggests that director interlock is an alternative mechanism to weak institution and may play an essential role for <sup>fi</sup>rms in developing countries [38]. As a result, managers are very likely to imitate other <sup>fi</sup>rms' R&D investments when they decide their own R&D intensity.

Our results indicate that R&D investment decision does diffuse across the corporate interlock network and the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's R&D intensity is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly and positively related to its interlock <sup>fi</sup>rm's previous R&D intensity. Additionally, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that this relationship can be moderated by industry homogeneity and the types of interlocking directors (inside director or independent director) that are present in focal <sup>fi</sup>rms.

The rest of the paper proceeds as follows. In Section 2, we review the related literature and then develop our hypotheses. In Section 3 we introduce our research design. This is followed by the discussion of our empirical results in Section 4. Section 5 concludes our paper with some directions for future research.

## 2. Literature review and hypotheses development

## 2.1. Imitation based on director interlock

Haunschild and Miner [23] have de<sup>fi</sup>ned inter-organizational imitation as “when one or more organizations' use of a practice increases the likelihood of that practice being used by other organizations”. Imitation is a common form of behavior in various business practices. Imitation occurs among <sup>fi</sup>rms in the introduction of new products or new technologies, in the adoption of organizational structures and managerial styles, and in the timing of corporate investments [30]. Additionally, imitation plays an essential role in several organizational theories. Strategic choice theory, for example, argues that imitation is a strategic response to the actions of competitors because second-movers realize that the risk related to product development has been absorbed by the <sup>fi</sup>rst-movers [31]. Organizational learning theory speci<sup>fi</sup>es that organizations observe and copy other organizations' practices especially when facing major environmental changes [28]. Institutional isomorphism theory suggests that organizations are very similar and they copy each others' practices, structures and routines in an effort to keep legitimacy [15]. Hence, given the importance of imitation in organizational theories and practices, researchers often investigate the conditions necessary for the occurrence of inter-organizational imitation. One of the important prerequisites for imitation is information conduit, which ensures that certain practices of the model <sup>fi</sup>rm can be diffused to the imitating <sup>fi</sup>rm.

Prior literature suggests that director interlock is a crucial information transmission channel, and it has a direct effect on the diffusion of practices among organizations [4,22]. For example, Davis [13] argues that when a <sup>fi</sup>rm adopts a poison pill, the likelihood of its directorinterlocked <sup>fi</sup>rm adopting the same poison pill will increase. Palmer et al. [36] specify that <sup>fi</sup>rms are more likely to adopt the multidivisional structure if their interlock partners have also adopted it. Haunschild [20,21] <sup>fi</sup>nds that the acquisition activities of the interlock <sup>fi</sup>rms serve as models to be imitated by the focal <sup>fi</sup>rms. And Chen and Lu [7] <sup>fi</sup>nd that managerial ties lead to imitation in corporate acquisition premium decision, and such a decision follows the laws of imitation put forward by Tarde [43], especially ‘the logical laws’ and ‘from the inner to the outer laws’. Finally interlock also in<sup>fl</sup>uences the stock option policy [4] as well as corporate disclosure [5] of a focal <sup>fi</sup>rm. To summarize, as the conduits of private information among <sup>fi</sup>rms, board interlocks can impact <sup>fi</sup>rms' strategic decision choice through inter-organizational imitation. ## 2.2. Director interlock and R&D investment

The occurrence of inter-organizational imitation relies on imitation motivation and information conduit. Decision-making theory suggests that environmental uncertainty or ambiguity is the driving force behind inter-organizational imitation, and imitating others is an ef<sup>fi</sup>cient way to economize on search costs [9]. Any decisions made by managers are based on the estimation of expected returns under possible future states of the environment. To begin with, the majority of decisions are made under risks, and it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to predict future states of the environment. The environmental changes are relevant to macro-economic policies, political issues and institutional reforms. Managers may lack adequate information to precisely predict the probabilities of various future states of environment. In addition, even if the probability of future environment states can be estimated, the actual bene<sup>fi</sup>t is still uncertain [26]. Hence, environmental uncertainty makes it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for managers to estimate the results of a speci<sup>fi</sup>c decision (e.g., R&D investment). In order to reduce risks due to environmental ambiguity, managers are more inclined to imitate other <sup>fi</sup>rms' similar decisions [30,32]. As an information conduit, director interlock can ensure the interlock <sup>fi</sup>rms' essential information relevant to decision-making be transferred to the focal <sup>fi</sup>rms.

R&D investment<sup>2</sup> is essential for <sup>fi</sup>rms to acquire innovation and competitive capacity [35]. However, R&D investment is associated with great risk and complexity owing to its uncertain outcomes [6], and deciding how much to invest in an R&D project is a dif<sup>fi</sup>cult decision for managers. R&D investment needs to sacri<sup>fi</sup>ce short-term pro<sup>fi</sup>ts to acquire long-term gains. In addition, owing to its great risk, its future outcomes are usually unclear. R&D investment may fail because of their fast market cycles or tedious processes. Furthermore, as an institutional transition economy, China's imperfect and immature institutions, such as its weak legal system, and poor information disclosure system [38], may lead to an even more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult decision-making environment for <sup>fi</sup>rms especially for high-risk projects. Therefore, the probability of future environmental states due to R&D investment is much more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to estimate for Chinese companies.

Given the condition of high uncertainty, one way for managers to acquire information and reduce uncertainty regarding R&D investment decision is to turn to external references, such as inter-organizational partners. Managers can imitate other <sup>fi</sup>rms' similar R&D investments to reduce uncertainty. When focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's director sits on other <sup>fi</sup>rm's board of directors, focal <sup>fi</sup>rms' director is exposed to the R&D activities of other <sup>fi</sup>rms. Normally, it is necessary for <sup>fi</sup>rms to discuss the R&D investment strategy during the board meetings, and how much to spend for R&D investment and how it is determined are usually included in the discussion as well [45]. Exposure to the determination process of R&D spending provides directors with concrete, vivid and integrated information, and such information is more impressionable than abstract statistics to directors [34]. Sitting on the board of the interlock <sup>fi</sup>rm and getting acquainted with the interlock <sup>fi</sup>rm's R&D investment decision process will provide directors with valuable information that will in<sup>fl</sup>uence their R&D investment decisions for the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm. It will increase the possibility that the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm imitates its interlock partner's previous R&D investment intensity because the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm can acquire relevant information about its interlock partner's R&D decision processes through its interlocked director. Thus we hypothesize that there is a positive relationship between the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's R&D investment intensity and the prior R&D intensity of its interlock partner <sup>fi</sup>rms.

Hypothesis 1. Focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's R&D intensity is positively related to its interlock partner's prior R&D intensity.

Interlock can be formed based on inside director or independent director. The role of inside directors is different from that of independent directors. Kang [24] argues that independent directors are more likely to protect investors' interests, and inside directors are more likely to be yielding to CEO's interests and decisions. So the relationship between focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's R&D spending and its interlock partner's R&D spending may change when the interlocking director is an inside director or an independent director.

Compared with independent directors, inside directors tend to be de<sup>fi</sup>ned as directors currently serving as <sup>fi</sup>rm of<sup>fi</sup>cers [37]. They are responsible for the day-to-day operations of corporations and they know more about the <sup>fi</sup>rm's operational activities than independent directors. Furthermore, in China independent directors play very limited role in shaping corporate decision due to the following reasons. Firstly, even though China has adopted the American independent director system, the functional effect of independent director is weak. The Chinese law requires that only one-third of a <sup>fi</sup>rm's board members be independent directors and in most cases, publicly listed <sup>fi</sup>rms are reluctant to hire more independent directors in the board [47]. Thus, the minority of independent directors on the board limits their decision power and the board is dominated by inside directors. Secondly, in China independent directors mostly come from the academic community [47] who do not have the experience of running a company and lack suf<sup>fi</sup>cient knowledge about corporate operations, therefore, their suggestions may be easily overlooked by the board.

To conclude, inside directors not only have more essential discourse power in corporate decision-making, e.g., R&D investment, than independent directors, but also have the incentive to improve the R&D investment ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency [11]. Hence, R&D investment imitation is more likely to have an effect when the interlocking directors are inside directors in focal <sup>fi</sup>rms because inside directors are more likely to in<sup>fl</sup>uence their own <sup>fi</sup>rms' decisions than outside directors [21].

Therefore, when the interlocking director belongs to the category of inside director in the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm, the positive relationship between focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's R&D intensity and its interlock partner's prior R&D intensity is stronger than that in a <sup>fi</sup>rm whose interlocking director belongs to the category of independent director.

Hypothesis 2. When the interlocking director is an inside director in the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm, the positive relationship between the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's R&D intensity and its interlock partner's prior R&D intensity is stronger relative to the situation when the interlocking director is an independent director.

Tarde [43] proposes that the imitation behavior follows the law ‘from the inner to the outer’. Firms have an innate tendency to imitate others with whom they share the same organizational culture. If two <sup>fi</sup>rms share similar culture, to a large extent, they will face similar institutional environments and share many of the same customers. Due to these similarities they will heartily acknowledge and trust each other, and eventually promote the occurrence of inter-organizational imitation [15]. Thus, when <sup>fi</sup>rms choose which <sup>fi</sup>rm they should imitate, they usually consider the ones that are similar to them in some ways. Prior literature has shown that when two <sup>fi</sup>rms belong to the same industry, the imitation behavior is more likely to occur [7]. Therefore, we predict that in R&D spending decisions, <sup>fi</sup>rms are more likely to imitate the <sup>fi</sup>rm which belongs to the same industry.

Hypothesis 3. When the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm and its interlock partner <sup>fi</sup>rm are in the same industry, the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's R&D intensity will be more positively related to the prior R&D intensity of its interlock partner <sup>fi</sup>rm.

## 3. Research design

## 3.1. Sample and data

The sample is composed of <sup>fi</sup>rms listed in Chinese A-share Shanghai and Shenzhen Stock Exchanges that disclose R&D expenditures during the period 2007–2012. We choose 2007 as the starting year because China started to adopt the new accounting standards for business enterprises from that year.

We then compare directors' background for one <sup>fi</sup>rm with that of all the other <sup>fi</sup>rms in the same year. To be more speci<sup>fi</sup>c, we use director's name, age, gender, education background and working experience as the matching criteria. If the information about directors from two different <sup>fi</sup>rms on these <sup>fi</sup>ve characteristics is identical, then these two <sup>fi</sup>rms are identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as having a common director. In addition, we only retain those <sup>fi</sup>rm-pairs where R&D investment <sup>fi</sup>gures of the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm in the current year and the same for the interlock <sup>fi</sup>rm in the prior year are available. This is because prior literature [20,21] shows that the behavior of the interlock <sup>fi</sup>rm occurs earlier than the same behavior of the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm. The sample selection process results in 370 distinct focal <sup>fi</sup>rms that are tied to 662 interlock <sup>fi</sup>rms. These <sup>fi</sup>rms lead to 1014 <sup>fi</sup>rm-year observations. As the next step, those observations that satisfy one or more of the following conditions are excluded: 1) the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm or the interlock <sup>fi</sup>rm belongs to ST <sup>fi</sup>rm<sup>3</sup>; 2) the ratio of liabilities to assets of the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm or interlock <sup>fi</sup>rm is greater than 1; 3) the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm or the interlock <sup>fi</sup>rm is in the <sup>fi</sup>nancial services industry; and 4) focal <sup>fi</sup>rms or interlock <sup>fi</sup>rms have missing values for other control variables. After such considerations, the <sup>fi</sup>nal sample consists of 764 <sup>fi</sup>rm-year observations where 316 distinct focal <sup>fi</sup>rms are tied to 502 distinct interlock <sup>fi</sup>rms.

We manually collect the R&D expenditure data, which is disclosed in the item ‘other cash payment related to operating activities’ in notes to the <sup>fi</sup>nancial statement. Other data is obtained from the China Security Market & Accounting Research (CSMAR) database. To account for potential outliers, all continuous variables are winsorized at both top 99% and bottom 1%.

## 3.2. Definition of variables

## 3.2.1. Dependent variable

The dependent variable is the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's R&D intensity in year t. Following Kor [27], we use R&D/assets to measure R&D intensity.

## 3.2.2. Independent variable

The independent variable is the interlock <sup>fi</sup>rm's R&D intensity in year t-1. According to Haunschild [20,21], the time period of imitation should be controlled. The behavior of the interlock <sup>fi</sup>rm should occur earlier than the behavior of the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm. Thus, the R&D intensity of the interlock <sup>fi</sup>rm in year t-1 (one year earlier) is used to link to the R&D intensity of the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm in year t. For robustness check, we also link the average R&D intensity of the interlock <sup>fi</sup>rm from year t-3 to year t-1 to the R&D intensity of the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm in year t. Qualitatively this does not change our results.

## 3.2.3. Moderating variables

The type of the interlocking director is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a dummy variable to test Hypothesis 2. If the interlocking director is an inside director in the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm, it equals one; otherwise, it equals zero.

Similarly, industry homogeneity is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a dummy variable as well to test Hypothesis 3. If the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm and its interlock partner are from the same industry, it equals one; otherwise, it equals zero.

## 3.2.4. Control variables

Prior studies show that a <sup>fi</sup>rm's R&D investment intensity may be in-<sup>fl</sup>uenced by <sup>fi</sup>rm age [6,11,27], past performance [2,6,27], growth [8], ownership characteristics [6,27], and corporate governance characteristics [11], among others. To control for these potential confounding factors, we include these variables into our regression analysis as well. ## 4.4. Robustness test

## 4.4.1. Sample selection bias

In our samples, <sup>fi</sup>rms without director interlock are excluded, so a potential issue that we need to deal with is sample selection bias. In order to control for such a bias each focal <sup>fi</sup>rm is matched with all other publicly listed <sup>fi</sup>rms which disclose R&D expenditures, regardless of whether they have interlock relationship with the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm. We create a new dummy variable called ‘Interlock’, which equals to one when two <sup>fi</sup>rms are interlocked, and zero, otherwise. We focus on the coef<sup>fi</sup>- cient of R&D intensity in year t-1 and the interaction term of interlock and R&D intensity in year t-1. The coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of R&D intensity implies the relationship of focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's R&D intensity with another <sup>fi</sup>rm's prior R&D intensity, when the other <sup>fi</sup>rm has no interlocking relationship with the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm. However, the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of the interaction term re<sup>fl</sup>ects the incremental impact of the other <sup>fi</sup>rm's prior R&D intensity on focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's R&D intensity when the other <sup>fi</sup>rm and the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm are connected through an interlock. The results of the analysis are shown in Table 6. For Model (1) the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of R&D intensity (Para = 0.0002, t = 0.54) is positive but insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant, however the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of the interaction term of R&D intensity and interlock (Para = 0.1371, t-value = 2.54) is positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at the 5% level. Thus, the R&D intensities of two <sup>fi</sup>rms are signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly and positively associated with each other when there is an interlock relationship between them. In addition, in Model (2) the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of the interaction term of R&D intensity, interlock, and inside director $( \mathtt { P a r a } = 0 . 1 6 8 1 , \mathtt { t - v a l u e } = 1 . 6 7 )$ is positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at the 10% level. In Model (3) the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient on the interaction term of R&D intensity, interlock and same industry (Para = 0.1293, t-value = 1.73) is positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at the 10% level. In Model (4) we include all the main variables as well as the interaction terms. Qualitatively our results are still valid. Overall, our results indicate that the positive association between R&D spending of two <sup>fi</sup>rms is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and positive when these two <sup>fi</sup>rms share a director. Furthermore, such an association is even stronger when the interlock director is an inside director in the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm or when these two interlock-<sup>fi</sup>rms come from the same industry.

## 4.4.2. Change mode

Another potential endogeneity concern is the possibility that unobserved industry characteristics [3] may lead to the observed relationship between the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's R&D intensity and the R&D intensity in its interlock partner. This approach controls for potential year-industry characteristics and re<sup>fl</sup>ects the relative measurement of variables for samples. Because the R&D intensity change variable requires that observations should have two consecutive years' R&D expenditures, this results in 473 <sup>fi</sup>rm-years observations. ## 4.4.3. Average R&D model

In previous analyses, we use interlock <sup>fi</sup>rm's R&D intensity in year t-1 to measure interlock <sup>fi</sup>rm's prior R&D intensity. For robustness check, we validate our results by linking the average R&D intensity of the interlock <sup>fi</sup>rm in the past three years to the current R&D intensity of the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm. We used the average of three years because according to Haunschild [20,21], a three-year period is reasonable for the focal <sup>fi</sup>rms to be affected by their interlock <sup>fi</sup>rms. We also try other time intervals and our results are very robust. If R&D data in any of the prior three years is missing, we use the average of whatever years of data are available as average R&D intensity for interlock <sup>fi</sup>rm. The results are reported in Model (1) to Model (4) of Table 8. Again, our hypotheses are supported with this new measure of R&D intensity.

## 4.4.4. Firm similarity

Larcker et al. [29] as well as Rousseau and Stroup [41] suggest that <sup>fi</sup>rms with similar characteristics are more likely to hire the same directors. Thus from this perspective, interlocking relationship might be the result of <sup>fi</sup>rm similarity. Furthermore, similar <sup>fi</sup>rms are more likely to have similar R&D investment. Hence, the imitation behavior of focal <sup>fi</sup>rm and its interlock partner may be an artifact of focal-interlock similarity. Put it in other words, the omitted <sup>fi</sup>rm similarity variables may drive both the imitation behavior and interlocking relationship.

To control for the possible impact of <sup>fi</sup>rm similarity on our result, we adopt the method proposed by Haunschild and Beckman [22] by investigating whether the current R&D intensity of the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm is related to its interlock partner's current R&D intensity. Haunschild and Beckman [22] argue that if the omitted variables that are related to <sup>fi</sup>rm similarity lead to the existence of interlocking directors and the imitation behavior, they will operate simultaneously in the focal <sup>fi</sup>rms and their interlock partners. In other words, if the <sup>fi</sup>rm similarity does work, the focal <sup>fi</sup>rms' current R&D intensity will be related to the interlock <sup>fi</sup>rms' current R&D intensity. We acknowledge that <sup>fi</sup>rms can be similar in various ways. We are not able to control for the various conditions under which the <sup>fi</sup>rms may be similar to each other. Hence, our approach only controls for <sup>fi</sup>rm similarity to a certain extent.

Model (5) of Table 8 presents our new regression results. The coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of interlock <sup>fi</sup>rm's current R&D intensity (Para = 0.0467, t-value = 0.79) is positive but insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant, which suggests that the current R&D intensity of the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm is not related to its interlock <sup>fi</sup>rm's current R&D intensity. This indicates that the alternative explanation that it is the <sup>fi</sup>rm similarity that drives our hypothesis is unlikely to be true.

## 5. Conclusion

This study investigates the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of director interlock on <sup>fi</sup>rm R&D spending from the perspective of inter-organizational imitation. The results show that <sup>fi</sup>rms' director interlocking relationship impacts R&D spending of the two <sup>fi</sup>rms through inter-organizational imitation and the R&D spending of two <sup>fi</sup>rms are more similar to each other when there is an interlock relationship between them. The results also show that the types of interlocking director and industry characteristics can moderate the relationship between director interlock and R&D spending. When the interlocking director is an inside director in the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm or the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm and the interlock <sup>fi</sup>rm belong to the same industry, the positive relationship of the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's R&D spending and the interlock <sup>fi</sup>rm's R&D spending is stronger.

Our results provide further support to the notion that director interlock is an important channel for information transmission [22]. The results of our study document that social embeddedness, such as director interlock relationship, has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect on <sup>fi</sup>rm's economic decisions and outcomes. In other words, social context factor is important in the process of determination of R&D spending. Our research illustrates that director interlock relationships add signi<sup>fi</sup>cant explanatory power to R&D spending of focal <sup>fi</sup>rms.

This paper contributes to the current literature in two ways. Firstly, this study expands our understanding about how director interlock affects <sup>fi</sup>rm R&D investment. This study <sup>fi</sup>nds that as an important conduit for information transmission, director interlock enhances the imitation of <sup>fi</sup>rms' R&D spending, and makes the R&D spending of two interlocked <sup>fi</sup>rms similar. Second, our study also provides additional evidence to role that is played by director interlock in the adoption of <sup>fi</sup>rm practices. Our results are consistent with other studies which suggest a positive relationship between director interlocks and the adoption of <sup>fi</sup>rm practices, such as the adoption of poison pills [13], adoption of disclosure policy [5], adoption of acquisitions [7,20,21] etc.

This paper has some limitations. To begin with, we only investigate director interlock's imitation role in <sup>fi</sup>rm R&D spending. In fact, director interlock can provide access to information, knowledge and resources, which may affect other innovation capabilities of the <sup>fi</sup>rm, e.g., IT spending, capital investment, etc. In addition, Rousseau and Stroup [41] de<sup>fi</sup>ne historical interlocks in the following manner: “if a given <sup>fi</sup>rm has a current director who formerly sat on the board of another <sup>fi</sup>rm but who no longer serves there, we say that the two <sup>fi</sup>rms have a ‘historical interlock’”. And they <sup>fi</sup>nd that historical interlock can confer information about the <sup>fi</sup>rm where the current director once served to the <sup>fi</sup>rm the current director is sitting on. Hence, whether historical director interlock can affect R&D investment through information transmission is another important issue that is beyond the scope of this paper but is worthy of investigation in future research.
