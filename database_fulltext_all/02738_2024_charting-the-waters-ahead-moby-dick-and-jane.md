# Charting the Waters Ahead: Moby, Dick, and Jane

Dorothy E. Leidner<sup>1</sup>

<sup>1</sup>University of Virginia, USA, dorothy@virginia.edu

In this final editorial during my term as editor-in-chief, I would like to begin by expressing my deep and sincere gratitude to the many individuals whose dedication to the Journal of the Association for Information Systems (JAIS) has immensely shaped and guided the journal’s progression these past five years: the senior editors, associate editors, and editorial review board members, all of whom have helped guide papers to higher levels of quality, the many authors who have pushed the quality of their work to meet the demands of the JAIS review teams, and the managing editors working tirelessly in the background to ensure smooth operations. I could not have asked for a more visionary, supportive, and reliable group of individuals with whom to work. I am also extremely grateful to Matt Nelson, the executive director of the Association for Information Systems, whose ongoing support of and responsiveness to the needs of JAIS made my term as EIC immeasurably smoother.

It was with deep appreciation for the importance of JAIS to the AIS community and to the IS discipline that I began my role as editor-in-chief and it is with an even deeper appreciation of this importance that I now transition out of this role. This issue will mark my last and the first for my successor, the highly imaginative, highly capable, and highly dedicated Monideepa Tarafdar. I look forward to seeing JAIS grow and thrive under her leadership.

In the remainder of this editorial, my intention is to provide a perspective of our field’s top journals and JAIS’s position therein, present some data on JAIS’s growth over the past five years, and share my thoughts on how important JAIS is to our field. The perspective draws from two keynotes I have given, one many years ago at a Big XII+ MIS Research Symposium at the University of Oklahoma and one more recent at the

ECIS 2024 doctoral consortium in Larnaca, Cyprus. The perspective is best narrated as a metaphor interwoven with some real facts and figures about JAIS over the past five years. Let’s begin.

Herman Melville’s epic novel Moby Dick begins with the now iconic three words: Call me Ishmael. The story’s narrator, a young former school teacher in the midst of an identity crisis, imagines a new name for himself and decides to embark on a ship about to set sail off the Nantucket coast in search of whales. He insists that he must journey, else he might resort to “the pistol and ball,” a sinister reference to reflections on the absence of meaning in his life. He boards a ship named the Pequod less in search of adventure as in search of evasion from his life and from a society—the Quaker society of the early 1800s New England—that has left him disillusioned. He gladly accepts a small pittance of a salary as compensation for his work aboard the ship and willingly boards without having had so much as a single word with the Pequod’s captain, an at times inspiring and at times frightening, man named Captain Ahab. Not long after leaving the cold Nantucket shore on a December day, Ishmael and his shipmates discover that Captain Ahab’s plan varies considerably from the plans of the ship’s Quaker owners who expect the crew to hunt as many whales as possible for their oil, which can then be sold for profit. But Captain Ahab is only interested in the capture of one whale, a singularly large and legendary great white whale who has maimed or killed many a seaman and cost the captain one of his own legs in a previous encounter at sea, a whale the captain refers to as Moby Dick.

What begins as a journey toward revenge emerges into a monolithic mission driven by an intransigent captain urged on by the unrelenting desire to master the whale. As irrational as Ahab’s personal mission is, he successfully convinces his crew to follow. Ishmael, not knowing what to make of his captain and, as a low-level inexperienced helper on board the ship, relegates himself to observing and following orders, never daring to openly question his captain’s fixation on a single whale. The journey is not always exciting. In fact, there are many days at sea with little activity and only the cumbersome daily chores to keep the men busy. During these times, the captain tries to keep the crew diverted with long soliloquies intended to inspire their further pursuit.

Ultimately, the Pequod is doomed, doomed by the singular fixation of its captain and by the very object of his fixation—Moby Dick—who, possibly out of playful abandon or possibly out of defensive aggression, destroys the Pequod and all aboard, save Ishmael, who takes refuge on floating wood until his rescue by a passing ship.

By now, you might be wondering why I am talking about Melville’s epic novel featuring a whale named Moby Dick, a story that may be interpreted either as a horrible tragedy driven by the vices of a delusional man (Captain Ahab) or as the triumphant struggle and survival of a young man in search of his life’s meaning (Ishmael) who, facing no choice but to acquiesce to the vice of his superior, does not succumb to it but triumphs through it and goes on to write a legendary tale about it. I am choosing this story as a metaphor because most of us who are active scholars in the field of IS begin our scholarly careers as an Ishmael. Our journey toward a PhD sets us on board a Pequod. Our advisors, whom many of us might not have known in advance of embarking, become to us as Ahab was to Ishmael. In defense of our advisors in this metaphor, whether Ahab is as bad as Ishmael recounts is anyone’s guess. We don’t hear Ahab’s perspective of Ishmael, only Ishmael’s of Ahab. And to his credit, Ahab had, in fact, mastered the art of motivation, convincing 30 seamen to follow his vision of reality and devote the essence of their being to his vision.

As for the object of Ahab’s pursuit—Moby Dick—in our field, we don’t just have Moby Dick. We have Moby and Dick, aka MIS Quarterly (MISQ) and Information Systems Research (ISR).

For several decades now, Moby and Dick have been exerting tremendous influence over the lives of information systems PhD students and faculty. Moby and Dick influence our PhD coursework, the design of our research, the journey our research takes, our future job prospects, our future promotion decisions—in short, our lives as academics. Unlike Ahab’s obsession with Moby Dick, it would be remiss to not recognize that in some ways, our field’s obsession with Moby and Dick was initially advantageous. In the early days of our field, the problem was that we did not have any prestigious journal. At ICIS 1980,<sup>1</sup> Peter Keen bemoaned the fact that there “is no major MIS journal, so that we are always trying to force-fit our work to suit some other field’s style, axioms and themes” (p. 10). He went on to say that the “outlets receptive to MIS themes ... lack prestige, and, often, readership (MIS Quarterly is not even in the library of major MIS universities)” (p. 6) and that “MIS Quarterly has minimal prestige and circulation” (p. 10). Fortunately, this is not a problem we have today. In fact, we are so removed from this problem that we face the opposite problem: So much esteem is ascribed to these two journals that many remained trapped in a fixation with Mody and Dick, much like Captain Ahab and all the men he convinced to follow his obsession with Moby Dick.

In Moby Dick, the Pequod encounters other ships at sea that are searching for whales. One ship, in particular, is the Bachelor. The Bachelor seems happy with its mission to capture whales in general and has no animosity or particular curiosity about Moby Dick. To the Bachelor, Moby Dick is just another whale, no more or less valuable than any other whale. The Bachelor is staffed by highly experienced seamen and whale hunters who have collected so much whale oil that their voyage has been declared a huge success and they are now enjoying an extended party as their ship sails home. The attitude aboard the Bachelor is happy and light. Ahab dismisses this as folly and foolishness. None of the men from the Pequod attempt to stow away on the Bachelor and ride home in abundance; instead, they choose to stay aboard the Pequod even though it was still empty of the prized whale oil and seemed to be on an impossible mission. I compare the Bachelor to the AIS Basket of 6, then 8, and now 11.

Many in our field, myself included, enthusiastically welcomed the establishment of the original “AIS Basket” of journals, relieved that an entity—the AIS Senior Scholars—with supposed expert power, if not structural power, would officially endorse anything outside of MISQ and ISR and would go so far as to not rank order the 6, then 8, then 11. This seemed to liberate us from having to obsess about Moby and Dick, allowing us to instead pursue other very attractive outlets that did not provide the same resistance as Moby and Dick. Yet behind closed doors in tenure evaluation meetings and external letter writing, as well as hiring and promotion decision meetings, the two still have frequently remained supreme above all others. Formal, explicit approvals are less powerful than hidden, secret ones. Like the men on the Bachelor, many IS scholars have been resistant to jump ship. In fact, many IS scholars are Ahabs—each of us who has written a tenure letter and pronounced how privileged a publication in MISQ or ISR is or pushed to hire one candidate over another because the former had an MISQ or ISR already accepted, each of us who has turned down writing a tenure letter because the candidate had no MISQ or ISR publications, each of us who has judged our own projects based on whether or not they eventually land in MISQ or ISR. Many in the field have been Captain Ahab at some point in their career. And, as stated, this obsession has, in fact, helped propel the field to where it is today. That said, obsessive fixation can create an existential crisis, as the men of the Pequod tragically experienced.

While in no case do I wish to insinuate that individual scholars should stop pursuing Moby and Dick, whose steadfast pursuit builds reputations and contributes significant advances to our knowledge, I would like to advance the case that as a community, we need to focus equal if not more attention on Jane, aka JAIS. All of us at JAIS—senior editors, associate editors, editorial review board members, and authors—have worked hard to make JAIS worthy of an elite reputation. We have striven to make Jane as worthy of pursuit as Moby and Dick. Not only is Jane equally worthy of pursuit in terms of the quality she offers, but her health is crucial to the health, vitality, and longevity of the larger system in which she is immersed. Whereas pursuing Moby and Dick is as much to capture personal reward and advancement as community advancement, to pursue Jane is less to capture as to nourish: in nourishing Jane, one nourishes an entire ecosystem of community identity and belonging. We are a community existing within a scholarly ecosystem, and our community is stronger when our major association binding us together (AIS) is stronger; AIS is stronger when JAIS is at its strongest.

One question often posed to me during my term as editor-in-chief is some variation of: “Why should I submit to JAIS when it’s going to take just as much effort to get into JAIS as it does to MISQ or ISR but will not count for as much.” My answer to this is that while I would not recommend that any author submit all of their papers to JAIS (or to any journal for that matter) and while each scholar also needs to respect the requirements and preferences of their own department for publication outlets, I nevertheless strongly encourage all scholars in the AIS community to submit at least one of their best papers to JAIS. As an AIS journal, JAIS is the only of our “Basket” journals that is controlled exclusively by the AIS community. It is the only of the AIS journals that has attained elite rankings, such as the 4\* rating of the CABS. <sup>2</sup> One reason I consider JAIS so crucial to our discipline is that if our major association’s most prestigious journal is not considered prestigious by the very scholars within, then the strength of our discipline itself comes into question from scholars without. This then threatens the standing of the discipline relative to others in our schools.

I sincerely urge the AIS community to rally behind the nourishment of Jane. To nourish Jane is to regularly browse JAIS content and make a routine of reading one or more papers from each issue. To nourish Jane is to submit one’s highest caliber of work to JAIS, to persevere through difficult review demands, to conduct high-quality reviews, to advocate for her inclusion on departmental journal lists, to recognize her in annual evaluations, to consult and cite her. An academic community needs a certain shared knowledge base and we can only get that if, as a community, we are regularly browsing and drawing from the same content. We are spread across regions, attend different conferences, teach different courses, and publish in many different journals. We each have our own reality of what the IS discipline means to us. Having a journal that brings disparate research communities together is one means of creating a certain shared knowledge of where we are and where we are heading as a discipline. I do believe that JAIS is well positioned to serve as such a central point of knowledge that can simultaneously represent and shape our perspective of our discipline.

I faced no delusion five years ago when taking on the role of EIC at JAIS, a journal poised to be among the most elite in the field yet up against the tide of the unwavering pursuit of Moby and Dick. I set my sights on raising the attractiveness of JAIS as an outlet for scholars’ top research, on increasing the quality of papers published in JAIS, and on increasing attention to JAIS. At the same time, it was crucial for JAIS to be associated with a multitude of research topics and methods so that the journal represented the highest quality of scholarship in the field across topics and methods rather than being viewed as predominantly focused on papers of a certain methodological orientation or papers in a particular domain of topical interest. In fact, in any issue of JAIS, one typically finds a mix of quantitative, qualitative, conceptual, and design science papers in a single issue. With these goals in focus, I felt that the journal’s readership and impact would increase.

The impact factor’s slow but steady rise has not been by design but rather as a result of the efforts by all of those involved with JAIS to push the quality, visibility, and readability of the journal’s publications. It is also rewarding to see the authorship of the journal wellrepresented across the three AIS regions. In the past three years, there have been 1288 new submissions, of which 33% have been from a submitting author in Region 1, 35% from Region 2, and 32% from Region 3.

Let us return, in closing, to our metaphor. As individual scholars, we are all at once Ishmael and Ahab. We search for our place and pursue the goals whose achievement, we believe, will provide meaning to our work. As young academics, we begin our journey with the mentality of Ishmael but transform to the mentality of Ahab as we learn to persevere against a sea of challenge in search of new knowledge. The greatest publications are those born out of passion and the greatest passion, that which invokes the deepest struggle. Publication may be the reward, but triumph lies in the pursuit. For those who pursue publishing in JAIS, I encourage you to persevere and not get discouraged by rejection; rather, continue the journey until you succeed. You will grow as a scholar and you will leave a mark on the field. And for each publication, you will have a tale to tell.

Our choices guide the pursuits our community values: the choices we make about where to submit our best work, which journals to regularly consult, which journals to include in our literature searches, which journals to count as worthy of strenuous revision efforts, which journals to admire. Our community transcends us as individuals and will hopefully thrive for decades to come even as information and digital technologies become increasingly intertwined with every other discipline, making our own community’s distinction harder to demarcate. Both AIS and JAIS play an important role in helping make this demarcation clear. I hope you will join me in rallying behind Professor Tarafdar and the strong editorial team at JAIS as they navigate the waters ahead, continuing to nurture and strengthen the journal. Although not among the earliest behemoths of our field’s past, JAIS plays a pivotal role in ensuring the health, vitality, and longevity of its future.
