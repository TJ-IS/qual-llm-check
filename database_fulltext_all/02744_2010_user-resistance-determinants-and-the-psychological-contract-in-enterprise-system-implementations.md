# User resistance determinants and the psychological contract in enterprise system implementations

Tim Klaus<sup>1</sup>, J. Ellis Blanton<sup>2</sup>

## Abstract

User resistance is an important issue in the implementation of enterprise systems (ES). However, despite a large body of user acceptance literature, there is far less literature addressing user resistance. This study seeks to better understand the development of user resistance. Information is obtained and triangulated in this study from a focus group and semi-structured user interviews from three organizations. Through the lens of the psychological contract employees have with their organization, user resistance is investigated. Twelve determinants were found that upset the psychological contract and affect the level of user resistance. These determinants have been classified into four key categories which expand on previous literature: individual, system, organizational, and process issues. The relationships among these concepts and both the theoretical and practical contributions of this study are discussed. ## Introduction

‘y most organizations must change, and change profoundly, if they’re to stay alive’ (Hirschhorn, 2002, p. 98). Similar comments have been repeated in many journal publications that focus on organizational change. One way that organizations choose to make major changes is through enterprise systems (ES). ES refer to commercial software packages that enable the integration of business processes and transaction-oriented data throughout an organization (Markus et al., 2003). ES include organizational-wide software such as Enterprise Resource Planning, Customer Relationship Management, product configuration, and sales force automation (Markus and Tanis, 2000). Typically, ES have a suite of software modules available for business functions, such as inventory management, accounting, scheduling, and forecasting. Increasing numbers of organizations are installing or upgrading full ES or ES modules with one estimate suggesting that the ERP market is expected to reach \$29.6 billion in 2012 (Pang, 2008).

An ES research stream is developing to explore several key differences between ES implementation and other types of system implementations: (1) ES implementation often occurs because of management’s desire to increase efficiencies through process reengineering, which may alter organizational structures, tasks of the job, skills, rewards, and even beliefs and values; (2) ES require mandatory usage, which is necessary for the system to integrate the data and produce useful reports; (3) in order to minimize cost and time of future upgrades, standardized modules are only partially customized as opposed to a full customization that may be performed for software produced in-house (Klaus et al., 2000). The rigidity of the ES may leave users feeling like they have lost organizational power (Wang and Chen, 2006). Furthermore, there are clear differences that exist between an organizational change facilitated by technology and redesigning organizational structures without technology (Markus, 2004), such as target outcomes, solutions, role of managers, and key success factors that are different. These differences can upset the status quo, lead to a greater burden on employees, and can be catalysts for user resistance. The context of an ES implementation is likely to be a fruitful area to examine user resistance as the system itself is more complex than other types of software, its use is mandatory, and users must adapt to new processes as well as use standardized systems to enter and retrieve information.

Although generally viewed as a negative characteristic of implementations, user resistance can be beneficial since it can draw attention to problems in the change so that unresolved issues can be addressed (Fiorelli and Margolis, 1993). Whether or not a specific instance is beneficial, user resistance must be further understood since it has been found to be ‘at the root of many enterprise software project failures’ (Hill, 2003, p. 1). Furthermore, resistance to change is important to consider since minor resistance can reduce the speed of change and major resistance can ultimately cause management to abandon its plans (Davidson, 1994). In organizational change, resistance is a normal phenomenon and ignoring resistance can cause many future problems; alternatively, recognizing resistance and dealing with it appropriately can reduce enduring problems (Doppler, 2004). Gravenhorst and in ’t Veld (2004) points out that change and resistance go hand in hand; accordingly, change suggests resistance and resistance implies change.

Successful ES implementations remain challenging as numerous articles report implementation catastrophes and implementations that have failed to provide projected benefits (i.e., Bingi et al., 1999; Robey et al., 2002). User resistance has been reported as an important reason for implementation failures (Krasner, 2000; Wah, 2000; Robey et al., 2002; Umble and Umble, 2002; Barker and Frolick, 2003). Reports of ES implementation failures and the importance of reducing user resistance (i.e., Krasner, 2000; Callahan, 2002; Maurer, 2002; Hill, 2003) indicate that a better understanding of user resistance is needed. For example, Maurer (2002) finds user resistance as the reason for low ES return on investments and Callahan (2002) finds that a significant amount of user resistance can exist even after nine months of ERP integration testing. In addition, a study of 186 ES implementations finds that resistance is the second most important contributor to time and budget overruns and is the fourth most important barrier to overall implementation (Cooke and Peterson, 1998). User resistance must be minimized in order to reap efficiency benefits from ES and reduce the risk of failure.

Despite the importance of minimizing user resistance, existing IS theories do not explain the psychology of user resistance well. This study seeks to better understand user resistance in the ES context, using psychological contract theory as a lens to investigate user resistance and users’ views towards the implementation. The psychological contract has been studied primarily in the psychology and management literature, although one IT study used the psychological contract to explain how a psychological contract breach in virtual teams affects trust (Piccoli and Ives, 2003) and another used the psychological contract in explaining outsourcing (Koh et al., 2004). The psychological contract has not previously been applied to IT-enabled change. However, psychological contract theory helps to explain the responses of employees dealing with change and organizational change can cause employees to perceive a psychological contract breach (Rousseau, 1996). In ES implementations, employees are often asked to perform different tasks than they are accustomed to, work in a different environment than accustomed, and perform more tasks than those performed in the past (Cummings and Worley, 2005). These changes may result in the breach of their psychological contracts. Since psychological contracts are not legally enforceable, users may often feel justified in responding negatively towards the company because of the psychological contract breach or violation caused by the ES implementation.

For this study, a focus group was conducted and semistructured user interviews from three organizations were conducted, in order to provide insight into user resistance. The perspectives obtained through the focus group and semi-structured interviews were triangulated to better understand the development of user resistance. This analysis, through the lens of psychological contract theory, led to the development of a comprehensive framework that explains why user resistance occurs during ES implementations. In addition, determinants for user resistance are described and categorized into distinct groups that are dependent on the type of change, which validates and expands upon previous user resistance literature. Furthermore, psychological contract theory is further developed as its role is analyzed and explained through its application in ES implementations.

## Psychological contract

The term psychological contract appears to have first been coined by Argyris (1960) and is traced back to organizational psychology literature (Schein, 1980). Although recent literature has defined psychological contract in various ways, one largely accepted view defines it as ‘beliefs that individuals hold regarding promises made, accepted, and relied on between themselves and another’ (Rousseau, 1995, p. 9). An employee’s psychological contract is subjective and continually changing based on the employee’s changing expectations. As opposed to a legal contract which can be enforced through a court system, a psychological contract is not legally enforceable since it is the subjective understanding of what an employee believes his employer is obligated to provide.

There are numerous information sources from which an employee accumulates thoughts about the psychological contract – during the initial interview with the company, from managers, from co-workers, news articles about the company, the company mission statement, the employee manual, training, job reviews, gossip, and seeing the job tracks of other workers. Employees perceive a psychological contract in areas such as feedback, job security, appropriate training/development, promotion, nature of job, management of change, job responsibilities, and appropriate compensation (Rousseau, 1995, p. 116). Rousseau (1989) further contends that these perceptions that form the psychological contract take the form of a durable and stable mental model, which influences employee beliefs and behaviors in the workplace.

A psychological contract breach occurs when employees do not perceive that their employer is meeting their obligations, whether or not the employer knowingly failed to fulfill the perceived promises (Morrison and Robinson, 1997). A psychological contract breach was found to have a significant impact on almost all workrelated outcomes (Zhao et al., 2007). For example, employees that have experienced a psychological contract breach have been shown to have lower organizational commitment, in-role and extra-role performance (Coyle-Shapiro, 2002), job performance (Robinson, 1996), job satisfaction and employee trust (Robinson and Rousseau, 1994; Coyle-Shapiro, 2002), organizational citizenship behavior (Robinson and Morrison, 1995), an increased level of turnover intentions (Robinson, 1996; Coyle-Shapiro, 2002), withdrawal behaviors (Conway and Briner, 2002), feelings of deception, anger, injustice, or betrayal (Morrison and Robinson, 1997), and overall negative attitudes and actions (Porter et al., 1998; McDonald and Makin, 2000).

A considerable psychological contract breach has been termed ‘violation’, and may cause the employee to have ‘an affective and emotional experience of disappointment, frustration, anger, and resentment’ (Morrison and Robinson, 1997, p. 242). Figure 1 shows how a perceived unmet promise can lead to a psychological contract breach or violation. If there is a perceived unmet promise, an employee compares his or her own contributions to the organization to the level of contributions that have been reciprocated by the organization. For example, an employee may compare his or her efforts at work to the rewards received (bonuses, promotions, etc.) in light of what is normally received by other employees in similar positions. This is based on the implied promises that an employee has developed from a variety of sources. If not, a perceived breach of contract occurs. Then the employee may interpret the breach of contract in light of fairness judgments, outcome assessments, and other considerations to determine whether a severe perceived breach of contract, or violation, has occurred (Morrison and Robinson, 1997). For example, an employee who was passed by on a promotion may not consider the action a perceived violation if at least several other employees were passed by. However, this same employee may consider the lack of promotion as a perceived violation if someone else who is considered lazy got a promotion. Eagly and Chaiken (1995) is a psychology study that discusses Attitude Strength, Attitude Structure, and Resistance to Change, pointing out that the strength of an attitude towards something affects the resistance behavior. Since a psychological contract violation is a considerable psychological contract breach, a user experiencing a violation would likely have strong negative perceptions towards the ES and the change. However, if the attitude is not strong enough, even though users may have negative perceptions, resistant behaviors will most likely not be exhibited.

## User resistance

The management literature has defined resistance to change as ‘the forces against change in work organizations’ (Mullins, 1999, p. 824). Resistance to change is a well-studied area in the organizational change literature. Conner (1993) describes it as the natural reaction employees have to anything that upsets the status quo. User resistance is more specific than overall resistance to change since it involves employees interacting with a system. To clarify this term, user resistance is defined in this paper as ‘the behavioral expression of a user’s opposition to a system implementation during the implementation’. An attitude that opposes the implementation plan can develop prior to, during, and after an implementation and may be expressed at one or multiple times. However, this study is focusing on the behavioral resistance that occurs during the implementation. Some IT studies have examined specific areas of resistance and these studies can partly explain user resistance in ES implementations. Resistance determinants that this literature has discussed include job insecurity (De Jager, 1994), users’ values and work habits (Alvarez and Urla, 2002), loss of power (Keen, 1981), lack of communication channel (Marakas and Hornik. 1996). misalignment of the system with the organization (Gosain, 2004), current habits and perceived risk of adoption (Sheth, 1981), the interface and process changes (O’Leary, 2000), lack of understanding (Ainsworth, 1977), and many other issues (Hussain and Hussain, 1984; Hirschheim and Newman, 1988; Shang and Su, 2004).

The IT literature has focused on user acceptance rather than on user resistance. An extensive body of research has focused on system acceptance in voluntary settings. For example, the Theory of Reasoned Action (Ajzen and Fishbein, 1980), the Technology Acceptance Model (Davis, 1989; Mathieson, 1991; Taylor and Todd, 1995), and the more recent Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology Model (Venkatesh et al., 2003) have their roots in the context of voluntary adoption. These studies have consistently found relationships between beliefs, attitudes, behavioral intentions, and usage behavior and focused on the initial decision on whether to use a system. An important distinction between the context of these studies and the context of an ES implementation is that voluntary adoption is not an option for ES users. Since the theories noted above were developed in the context of voluntary adoption to explain the acceptance of an innovation, they are not advantageous to use in studying user resistance in a mandatory context.

Markus (1983) identifies three perspectives of examining user resistance: (1) system-oriented; (2) people-oriented; and (3) interaction-oriented. The system-oriented approach suggests that resistance occurs because of technology-related factors such as the user interface, performance, security, ease of use, and degree of centralization (Markus, 1983; Jiang et al., 2000). The people-oriented approach suggests user resistance occurs because of individual or group factors such as backgrounds, traits, and attitude towards the technology (Markus, 1983; Jiang et al., 2000). The interaction-oriented approach suggests that perceived social losses caused by interaction between people and the technology affects resistance, such as changing power relationships, social structure, and job structure (Markus, 1983; Jiang et al., 2000). Although a variety of IT studies address user resistance (i.e., Jiang et al., 2000; Shang and Su, 2004; Lapointe and Rivard, 2005) and some mention potential reasons for user resistance (Ainsworth, 1977; Keen, 1981; Hussain and Hussain, 1984; Hirschheim and Newman, 1988; De Jager, 1994; Marakas and Hornik, 1996; O’Leary, 2000; Gosain, 2004), no study has used psychological contract theory to examine in a data-driven method ‘why users resist’. Using a data-driven, bottom-up process, results are obtained which are analyzed through the lens of psychological contract theory. This study develops a framework of user resistance that expands on Markus’ (1983) framework.

## Methodology

Based on the literature review that was conducted, the authors had ideas of some of the resistant determinants that may exist as well as the process by which an employee traverses prior to a perceived psychological contract breach or violation. However, since no study was found that examines the psychological contract in ES implementations or in user resistance, the authors knowledge was limited. Since the authors sought to explore and expand on what was learned through the literature review, some initial questions were created (shown in Appendix) on a variety of relevant topics to get an understanding of the interviewee and ES implementation experiences. Then, for every interview, many followup questions were asked to each interviewee to further probe into his/her responses to the initial questions. This enabled the authors to gain a more complete understanding of the psychological contract and resistance in ES implementations.

A three-phase multi-method qualitative approach was used for data collection and analysis, using the interview guide in Appendix. Phase 1 involved a focus group of IT professionals from a variety of organizations and an interview with a CIO. This phase provided a broad overview of user resistance in ES implementations from an IT perspective. Phase 2 used semi-structured interviews to examine an ES implementation at a large university. This phase sought to investigate the underlying user resistance determinants in more depth through interviewing employees from a variety of backgrounds and job positions. Phase 3 involved interviews with multiple employees in two organizations which have implemented ES. The purpose of this phase was to examine the underlying user resistant determinants in more detail as well as to obtain the perspectives of employees from multiple companies and industries. For all of these phases, the level of analysis was the individual. All interviews were recorded, transcribed, and analyzed according to Patton (2002). Table 1 describes the number and positions of interviewees from each phase and provides a description of the interviews. Although the Phase 1 interviewees consisted of IT professionals, both Phase 2 and 3 included employees from various positions.

The objective of Phase 1 was to identify the major issues related to user resistance and the changing psychological contract during ES implementation. A focus group of seven IT professionals ranging from heavy involvement in ES implementation to occasional usage was used to provide a perspective of people familiar with the process of rolling out systems to users. To assist in clarifying and interpreting the results from the focus group, the focus group was followed by a separate interview with a CIO who led the rollout of several ES in a Fortune 500 firm. The implementation overviews and description of specific stories obtained through Phase 1 helped to guide the interviews in Phase 2 and 3, leading to specific questions which were asked to users regarding many different dimensions of ES implementation.

Phase 2 was a study in a large public university in the southeastern U.S. consisting of semi-structured interviews from 22 users. With approximately 40,000 students and 10,000 employees, this location was ideal for this phase of the study: (1) the rollout of ES created a major change, affecting many employees; (2) user resistance existed in many departments; and (3) being a state institution with stable employment, employees may be more forthright in describing their resistance experiences. The ES that was implemented at the university was an ERP system that contained nine modules: Purchasing/Procure to Pay/Order to Cash, Grants, Accounts Payable, Asset Management, Accounts Receivable/Billing, Budgets, General Ledger, Project Costing, and Record to Report. User interviewees for this phase were identified by three criteria: (1) they represent different departments; (2) they represent different positions; and (3) they use the system regularly.

To further refine the results of Phase 1 and 2, Phase 3 involved interviews with users heavily involved in ES implementations at two other organizations. One organization was an airline located in Asia and the second organization was a cellular phone company located in the U.S. Phone interviews were conducted with seven users from the airline company and with four users from the cellular phone company. All of the informants were heavily involved in the rollout of ES and represented a number of different areas within each organization.

The transcripts from all the interviews were analyzed to extract themes based on recommended steps in identifying themes and developing coding schemes (Strauss and Corbin, 1990). First, comments which potentially imply user resistance determinants were identified and analyzed in order to discover themes, or commonalities, among the comments. Second, through multiple iterative rounds of analyzing the themes, the themes were revised. For example, during the first round several comments were initially classified under the theme ‘Lack of perceived value of the new system’ because some users indicated that they did not see the value of the ES to the organization. However, upon further analysis of this theme’s comments and their context, it was determined that some of this theme’s comments fit better under the theme ‘poor communication’ (since the users discussed how they did not understand the benefits of the system) while the rest of this theme’s comments fit better under the theme ‘additional workload’ (in the mind of these users, the value of the new system did not justify the additional workload required). Third, a coding scheme was developed to name and describe each theme. This coding scheme was checked for reliability and definitional clarity (Miles and Huberman, 1994). Fourth, two graduate research assistants taking part only in the coding and unfamiliar with the research coded transcripts from all three phases using the coding scheme. Each paragraph in the transcripts was assigned zero, one, or multiple codes based on the coding scheme. The research assistants were also instructed to identify any other potential user resistance determinant that was not on the coding scheme. After coding one interview transcript, the research assistants discussed discrepancies and then continued to code a sample of the remaining transcripts.

Reliability of this transcript analysis is shown in two ways. For the coding, Cohen’s Kappa statistic was used to analyze the level of correspondence between the coders. Cohen’s Kappa was 88.7% for the first two phases, and 83.1% for phase 3, well above the 61% level that is suggested to have ‘substantial strength of agreement’ (Landis and Koch, 1977, p. 165). Reliability is also shown through the suitable use of and adherence to the case study protocol (Yin, 2003). The interviews were semistructured based on the general questions shown in Appendix with follow-up questions to further probe into the underlying issues.

Validity was established in several ways. First, the coders were asked to identify any themes they saw in the interview transcripts that were not in the coding scheme. In particular, for Phase 3 coding, the coders also examined the transcripts to see if any additional themes emerged that were not found in Phase 1 or 2. Neither additional themes nor discrepancies were found by the coders, which adds support for the validity of the themes originally identified. Second, external validity was established through: (1) using an expert panel comprised of IT professionals who have been involved with ES within various industries; (2) detailed examination of user resistance through interviews with users and IT professionals from organizations representing three different industries; and (3) following the theoretical sampling techniques suggested by Strauss and Corbin (1990), selecting the research sites because of the similar (i.e., have implemented an ERP system) yet varied (i.e., different environments) conditions. All the organizations were involved with ES implementations, but were from different industries, used software from different vendors, and used different modules of the software. Third, triangulating the data from Phase 2 by comparing and contrasting statements made from the various interviewees with other interviewees in order to determine if the statements about the implementation plan were consistent. Furthermore, interviewee statements were compared to the information obtained from training manuals, emails, memos, and other written documentation concerning the implementation. Although interviewees had different perspectives about the implementation, there were no statements found about the system implementation plan description that contradicted each other.

## Discussion

The following paragraphs address each resistance category and their respective resistance determinants. These paragraphs integrate information gleaned from the interviews with the psychological contract literature and user resistance literature as each of the determinants that emerged from the transcript analysis are addressed and management strategies are suggested to mitigate each of these determinants.

## Individual issues

There were four determinants that best fit under the category of ‘Individual Issue’ because they all are individual psychological determinants that are intrinsic: Uncertainty, Input, Control/Power, and Self-Efficacy. Each employee has a desired level for these psychological determinants that must be met. For example, one employee may have his desired level of uncertainty met as long as his job is not on the line while another employee may have her desired level of uncertainty met only if the daily tasks are predictable.

Uncertainty arises since users often are unclear of the future and view the ES as a potential threat to their job and/or work life. One user commented, ‘People did perceive [the ES] as a threat to their job. “Oh, they’re not going to need me quite as much” or “I’m not going to be able to use this system and they’re going to fire me if I can’t use it”’. Comments such as these are indicative of a psychological contract breach. Management can help facilitate a new psychological contract by addressing this determinant through top-down communication and clear, consistent plans, since users will better understand why their psychological contract is changing.

Input is another individual issue since users often develop a negative attitude when the system is forced upon them without being able to contribute to the change process. It is upsetting to some users for their opinions not to be considered during an organizational change. One user stated, ‘You may not want my input, but it’s important to me y you don’t ask my opinion then fine I don’t want to be involved with it’. Many organizations convey to their employees that they are valued, thus a lack of input is considered a psychological contract breach by some employees. Furthermore, the lack of input can make users move from psychological contract breach to psychological contract violation, which as mentioned earlier is an extreme form of psychological contract breach. In this case there is likely to be a greater level of user resistance.

Control/Power is another individual issue since ES implementation causes some users to lose control or recognition as an expert by leveling the playing field. One user stated, ‘Some people liked the old system a lot better, who were the “experts” – they would resist more, because with the changes, everybody starts at square one – you don’t have that advantage or comfort zone’. Employees who have established control or power within the organization may feel that the ES implementation takes away their control/power and consider this a psychological contract breach. If a newly hired person is likely to have comparable control or power to someone employed for many years, people who have been with the employer longer sometimes resist the system. This user resistance determinant is difficult for management to mitigate since bringing in a new system often requires the loss of expertise on the old system. However, through recruiting the ‘expert’ users of the old system to help in the system change, they have the opportunity to continue as experts in the new system.

Self-efficacy is another individual issue and may exist because of a lack of confidence in the skill set needed for the new system. A user who was on the implementation team mentioned, ‘There’s a large population that are not computer savvy. Whatever computer use they have is here at work. They do very little at home y “I’m going to retire in a couple of years”. “I don’t need to learn this”. “I just don’t like computers” is what one lady told us – she wasn’t going to use the system’. For an organization that has not required computer skills for most employees, the sudden change could be considered a psychological contract breach. A secretary mentioned she now had to use a limited amount of SQL for her job, which was something she did not feel confident in nor did it fit her job expectations. Prior to the ES implementation most employees have confidence in their ability to do their job, and the system eroding this confidence may be considered a psychological contract breach.

## System issues

The determinants Technical Problems and Complexity were both put into the category of ‘System Issue’ because they were primarily related to system usage. In an organizational change that is not technology-enabled, these determinants would not be contributing factors. However, the implementation of an ES which requires usage often leads to users experiencing frustration because of technical problems or the complexity of the system.

Technical Problems is a system issue since employees who used a system that worked correctly and are suddenly required to use a system with bugs and features that do not work as planned may experience a psychological contract breach. The psychological contract of many users includes an expectation to use systems relatively free of errors. A user mentioned, ‘If you get to a certain point, you can print it, but then if you do one of two things and then you go to print, it won’t print – it’s been a nightmare. I hate it. I absolutely hate it’. This is a common problem for ES implementations since these systems have many components and employees have not figured ‘work-arounds’ to the problems that occur. Through effectively providing support, technical problems can be dealt with promptly, which should lead to users not perceiving a breach and thus this may also mitigate the level of user resistance.

Complexity is another system issue since many users experience confusion because of the complexity of ES, which usually consists of multiple modules that are not nearly as simple as a home-grown system. One user mentioned, ‘In terms of how we derive the information, how we get the numbers that we need, it’s much more complex y It’s much more difficult, much more frustrating and I have many more people driving me crazy with questions’. Users who have been employed by the organization for multiple years may assume that they have been at the job long enough to figure out how things are done and thus low complexity is part of their psychological contract. Prior to system selection, the analysis of various ES and selection of a less complex system may be useful in minimizing potential psychological contract breaches. However, once a system is selected, useful training should minimize the impact of the complexity on user resistance.

## Organizational issues

The determinants Facilitating Environment, Communication, and Training were all put into the category of ‘Organizational Issue’ since they revolve around organizational aspects necessary to meet the needs of users. Whether or not an organizational change results from ES implementation, employees’ attitudes are affected by these determinants because they revolve around general organizational change issues.

Facilitating Environment is an organizational issue since a bureaucratic organization not conducive to change will likely have user resistance. One user commented, ‘For some reason it’s a struggle to make that change here and I think it goes back to there was no one ever in place empowered to do it before, so any kind of change to try and give that person that power is met with resistance’. For these organizations, the old psychological contract will include an assumption of only minimal change and thus a large-scale change is usually considered a psychological contract breach.

Communication is another organizational issue since a common part of the psychological contract is an expectation that management will keep employees informed (Guest and Conway, 2002). However, in an ES implementation a lack of communication often exists, not conveying to users the benefits of the system and the ‘whys’ of the change (Baronas and Louis, 1988; Oliver and Romm, 2002). One user mentioned, ‘I know at our organization with the system, they didn’t communicate. There really wasn’t much communication as far as the goals or benefits or anything like that y there was no discussion of “We know it sucks now, but it’s going to be great in 6 months” – there’s not even that type of communication’. However, by communicating a clear vision, the psychological contract of users becomes more aligned with the reality of the organization. In addition, frequent communication leads to the psychological contract changing incrementally, which is less likely to lead to cause psychological contract breach.

Training is a third organizational issue and can be problematic when users perceive training to be a waste of time, that trainers are incompetent, the timing of training is inappropriate, or if there is a lack of training. Part of an employee’s psychological contract may be an expectation that suitable training will accompany new job requirements while problematic training may be considered a psychological contract breach. One user commented, ‘I witnessed some people getting just exasperated because the people who were training them were not that knowledgeable in the subject matter y I’ve got to tell you some of those trainings were terrible and when you walked out of there you didn’t know much more than when you walked in’. Training is an important bridge between the old and new psychological contract and can be used as a tool by which to help employees adjust to a new psychological contract.

## Process issues

The determinants Job/Job Skills Change, Workload, and Lack of Fit were placed into the category ‘Process Issue’ because they all are problems faced by users resulting from the changed processes synonymous with ES implementations. IT-enabled change requires new processes that change the jobs of employees, often requiring new skills. These changed processes influence the psychological contract and if the change is not what is expected by the users, employees may consider the process change a psychological contract breach.

Job/Job Skills Change is a process issue as ES often require that users’ job descriptions are revised or that users perform different job tasks or develop new skills and new ways of thinking for the job. Since employees have certain expectations of their jobs, a considerable job or job skills change is likely to be considered a psychological contract breach. One user mentioned, ‘We used to use reams and reams of paper with stuff on it. You get a hard copy – somebody else did the programming for you – now you do it yourself y Everybody here had to unlearn and look at it from a different perspective and that’s not always easy’.

Workload is another process issue as ES often causes users to exert additional effort to perform the same task or to take work home in order to complete it on time. One user mentioned, ‘it’s definitely made our jobs more time consuming. More frustrating also y I’ve had to stay late plenty and do things at home y what I used to be able to do in a short amount of time takes much, much longer y you still have the same amount of work to do, it’s just taking more time to do it’. An increase in workload often is considered a psychological contract breach, particularly if users do not feel compensated for the workload change.

Lack of Fit is another process issue as there may be problematic changes to existing processes and new processes not working as planned. A user mentioned, ‘[With the old system], the money is basically in the administrative units and they get the responsibility of handling the details. The [new] system’s not made for that. That’s one of the reasons we were resistant’. ES may fit some organizations well and not others, forcing other organizations to change their organizational structure to fit the technology. ES not working well with processes can cause a psychological contract breach if employees consider that the employer should be providing a system that works with the established processes.

This study identifies a new category of issues not addressed in Markus (2002), a well-cited article describing categories of issues. Furthermore, it provides a more comprehensive view of three categories of issues identified in Markus (2002), First, the ‘Process Issues’ category was not addressed in Markus (2002), although the changing processes in an ES implementation can greatly affect a user’s response to a system. Second, the ‘Organizational Issues’ category is somewhat similar to the ‘power/ sociotechnical issues’ in Markus (2002), but the ‘Organizational Issues’ category also includes communication and training as determinants which are not included in Markus (2002). Third, although the ‘Individual Issues’ and the ‘System Issues’ are somewhat similar to what are included in Markus (2002), determinants are not included in Markus (2002). Thus Table 2 in this study provides more detail on issues than what are addressed in Markus (2002). In summary, the categories found in this study provide a more comprehensive perspective on issues affecting organizational change than previous research.

## User resistance behaviors development model

In an effort to portray the process by which a psychological contract affects users’ resistant behaviors, a post-hoc analysis was conducted on the interview transcripts of this study. Figure 2 was then created, which adapted Figure 1 (from the psychological contract literature) in light of the interview transcript analysis. Since Figure 2 addresses ES implementation resistance as an organization moves from an old system to a new one, perceived unmet promises are likely to be from the four categories of user resistance determinants that were found in this study (Table 2). For example, a user may consider that a promise is unmet if his/her employer requires tasks that an employee does not feel competent at completing (self-efficacy), if the new system does not have an intuitive interface and is difficult to use (system complexity), or if the company does not provide effective training (training).

Figure 2 User Resistance Behaviors Development Model.

The left-hand side of Figure 2 identifies the perceived unmet promises which are the first step towards a psychological contract breach. As this figure indicates, these perceived unmet promises may lead to a perceived psychological contract breach. From the user interviews, however, there was a distinction between users – some users placed blame on the implementation for the perceived unmet promises and considered issues with the implementation to be a breach of the psychological contract. Other users, despite unmet promises with the implementation, did not place blame on the implementation, but rather justified the unmet promises. For example, some users justified this by considering the equity of their job, justifying the perceived unmet promise by determining that the outputs received from the company (salary, bonuses, promotions, etc.) was equitable based on the inputs the employee provided the company (efforts, workload). In addition, the equity was considered reasonable by these employees based on a comparison with other employees in similar positions. Through an equity comparison, some users made conclusions such as realizing that everyone has parts of their job they do not like or everyone experiences frustration with change, but that this is justifiable because of the level of equity with their job. This second group which justified the perceived unmet promises did not make comments that would imply a psychological contract breach. Thus, equity comparison is proposed in Figure 2 as a moderator of perceived unmet promises on perceived breach of contract.

The right half of Figure 2 addresses the relationship between perceived psychological contract breach and resistant behaviors. The user interviews indicate that even if a user perceived a psychological contract breach, that user did not necessarily exhibit resistant behaviors. In addition, remarks from some users indicate that there first tends to be a process through which users traverse regarding whether or not the expression of user resistance is justified. This process is where the user interprets the perceived breach of contract to determine whether it is significant enough to be considered a violation, or a considerable breach of contract. For example, if a psychological contract change was not addressed to the satisfaction of a user, if the implementation plan blatantly disregards some users, or if the change is too severe, the user appears to be more likely to rationalize user resistance. On one hand, this interpretation may cause a user to determine that the psychological contract breach is not severe enough to justify resistant behaviors. On the other hand, another user may think that the severity of the breach justifies the resistant behaviors. Thus, breach of contract interpretation is proposed as moderating the relationship between perceived breach of contract and resistant behavior.

There are some future directions for this research. First, the user resistance determinants could be further examined to investigate which determinants are the most important in affecting the psychological contract. It is likely that certain determinants are the key drivers and there are likely a wide variety of management strategies to address these determinants. In addition, a longitudinal study could examine the changing psychological contract throughout the implementation as well as possible covariance among the resistant determinants. Second, future research could test or expand on the proposed User Resistance Behaviors Development Model, which was created based on the psychological contract literature and the results of this study, but has not been validated. In addition, the proposed moderating forces of Equity Comparison and Breach of Contract Interpretation could be examined in order to propose management strategies which address these proposed moderating forces effectively.

## Conclusions

Through the lens of psychological contract theory, a framework is developed and presented that explains why user resistance occurs during ES implementations. As managers transition users from the old psychological contract to the new one, it is important to address each of the user resistance determinants in the framework. Since each determinant can lead to a psychological contract breach, a failure to address one or more determinants increases the likelihood that users will perceive a psychological contract breach and possibly a psychological contract violation. Managers striving to minimize user resistance should think through each determinant in the framework to develop strategies that address each determinant to reduce the chance of a psychological contract breach. In the context of ES implementations, employees will likely use the system (system acceptance), yet a psychological contract breach may lead to the user exhibiting resistance at the same time. Non-use of a mandated system, such as an ES, suggests blatant disregard for management policies and will likely result in consequences to the user. Thus, users who perceive a psychological contract breach may more likely resist through covert actions. Previous research points out some of the covert actions users take are procrastination, ‘forgetting’ certain tasks, entering information incorrectly, or slow performance, appearing to accept the system while masking their resistance (Marakas and Hornik, 1996; Lapointe and Rivard, 2005). The framework created through this study is useful for managers to consider in strategy development as well as for researchers to consider in examining user resistance. This is theoretically important, because the use of psychological contract theory as a lens to understand user resistance is new and is useful for shedding new light on the old concept of user resistance. The use of psychological contract theory brings about a new perspective regarding what can cause user resistance. Rather than focusing on identifying specific antecedents to user resistance, the use of psychological contract theory focuses the researcher on the overarching psychological contract which affects more than only user resistance. For example, users’ longterm attitudes and behaviors can be influenced through the altered psychological contract and may impact the organization in the long term through ways other than only user resistance.

The determinants for user resistance are categorized into distinct groups that are dependent on the type of change, which validates and expands upon previous user resistance literature. These categories are important to consider and are useful in examining changes other than only ES implementations. Although all the categories are likely to exist in an ES implementation, only some of the categories will be applicable in other organizational changes. For example, in a major organizational change not facilitated by technology, the system issue determinants will not be present because a system is not being implemented. However, the individual, organizational, and process issues are likely to be present because a major organizational change is likely to affect the individual, the organization, and the processes. These categories also expand on previous research.

Psychological contract theory has been integrated into the system implementation setting, leading to the development of the User Resistance Behaviors Development Model. This model both builds on psychological contract theory and extends its application to ES research. Not only does this model propose the impact psychological contract theory can have on ES implementations, but it also proposes thought processes through which a user traverses prior to user resistance. This model contributes to psychological contract theory by expanding it to explain user resistance. Although this model is only the initial step towards bringing psychological contract theory into understanding user resistance, future research could shed new light on user resistance through further investigating the cognitive state through which users traverse during the ES implementation. Although psychological contract theory has not been used in ES research, the idea has been proposed that when users have realistic expectations, an implementation is far more likely to succeed (Ginzberg, 1981; Ives and Olson, 1984). This is likely because user resistance is far less likely to occur if a new psychological contract is developed prior to the implementation. For example, if effective communication and training takes place in the early stages of the ES implementation, employees’ psychological contract could incrementally be changed in a manner that does not produce a perceived psychological contract breach or violation. If management has incrementally changed to a new psychological contract by addressing concerns, there is much less likelihood of users considering the issues to be a psychological contract breach. Management implementing appropriate measures early on can help facilitate the new psychological contract, which can cause employees to be more supportive of the change. Researchers can also test and expand upon the User Resistance Behaviors Development model to better understand the development of user resistance.

## Interview guide

Because of the exploratory nature of the data collection, many follow-up questions were asked. The questions shown below provided only the initial structure of the interview, followed by many additional questions to follow up based on interviewee responses.

## Background of interviewee

\- Please describe your involvement in the Enterprise System implementation, the amount of time you were involved in the project, and the name/type of system

## Change

\- What degree of change has the Enterprise system had on your job?

\- To what extent were employees of your organization affected by changing jobs and responsibilities because of the system implementation?

\- For you, what were the advantages and disadvantages of the project?

\- What did you gain and lose because of the system implementation?

## Resistance

\- Describe the type or types of resistance that occurred during the implementation.

\- Why do you think this resistance occurred?

\- Do you think anything could have been done differently to reduce the level of resistance?

\- What types of things, if any, attracted users to embrace the system and change?
