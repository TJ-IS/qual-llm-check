# Member use of social networking sites — an empirical examination

Rui Chen

Ball State University, Department of Information Systems and Operations Management, WB240, Muncie, IN 47306, United States

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Keywords: Social networking Social exchange theory Site use

## a b s t r a c t

In this research the authors examine members' voluntary use of social networking sites. Site use leads to the growth of social relationships, increased volume of site visits and traf<sup>fi</sup>c, and an accumulation of user generated contents; hence it is imperative to the success of social networking sites. Drawing upon Social Exchange Theory (SET), we develop a research model that examines the major determinants of member site use behavior through a cost-bene<sup>fi</sup>t framework. This model also investigates the key antecedents to site use that stem from website designs, personal traits, and personal beliefs in the environment. The research model has been validated through survey data collected from 222 social networking site users, and the analysis results provide strong support to the hypothesized relationships. The current study generates new knowledge on the literature of SET and social networking sites; it also sheds lights on site management for networking service providers.

## 1. Introduction

Social networking sites have thrived since the inception of Web 2.0 technologies [11]. Examples of networking sites are Facebook, Myspace, and Google+. Boyd and Ellison de<sup>fi</sup>ned social networking sites as web-based services that allow individuals to “(1) construct a public or semi-public profile within a bounded system, (2) articulate a list of other users with whom they share a connection, and (3) view and traverse their list of connections and those made by others within the system” [7]. Social networking sites offer a platform for online users to interact with one another and to maintain interpersonal relationships. Millions of internet users are now members of one or more networking sites. Facebook, for example, attracts over 500 million active users who spend over 700 billion minutes per month on the site [1]. The success of social networking sites has tremendous business implications. In 2007, for example, 48% of brand marketers deployed marketing on social networking sites. Educators nationwide have also tapped into the potential of social networking sites as schools are increasingly using these sites in order to “push learning beyond the borders of the classroom” [23]. Government agencies across federal, state, county and municipal levels have also invested in social networking sites to interact with the public. A recent report by the Human Capital Institute found that 66% of government agencies adopted social networking sites as a preferred channel [48].

Academia has recognized the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of social networking sites as well. Several research streams of social networking sites exist, yet less is known as to what in<sup>fl</sup>uences online users to use social networking sites. Findings of prior studies are limited in addressing this research question and new research is warranted [85,88,103]. New knowledge in this regard is imperative to the long-term sustainability of social networking sites. First, site use results in engagement in social interactions such as pro<sup>fi</sup>le browsing, comment exchange, and the reciprocation of mutual favors, which ultimately leads to the growth and expansion of cross site social networks [56]. Second, site use boosts the volume of site visits and traf<sup>fi</sup>c, allowing service providers to successfully attract advertisers for higher ad revenues. Third, members who engage in site use are likely to create user-generated-contents (UGCs) such as photos, videos, and blogs. UGCs contribute to the wealth of an online community and subsequently attract new members. Increasing network externalities for economies of scale allows social networking sites to propagate their business model for greater success.

The current study answers the following research questions: “What are the key impetuses of members' use of social networking sites?”, “What are the key impediments to members' use of social networking sites?”, and “How do the impetuses and impediments interact together in determining members' site use?” Drawing upon Social Exchange Theory (SET), we develop and validate a research model that provides a preliminary answer to these questions. This research makes a threefold contribution. First, it validates the value of SET in explaining member use of social networking sites. Theoretical bases such as Technology Adoption Model do not directly account for the bene<sup>fi</sup>t and cost factors experienced by online users that may affect their usage of collective technologies such as social networking. However, the bene<sup>fi</sup>t and cost factors in using networking services can be accounted for by SET [4]. SET is one of the most in<sup>fl</sup>uential theories that investigate the dynamics in social interactions and it has been widely used in information systems discipline [39,57,90]. Despite its potential for explaining user behavior of social platforms, SET has received little attention so far. Recent reviews have emphasized problems facing SET [16,18]. First, SET constructs have not been fully identi<sup>fi</sup>ed and critical theoretical variables were left out in many SET tests; Second, many formulations of SET are ambiguous, lending themselves to multiple interpretations [19]. Unfortunately, these problems are present in early studies that attempted to utilize SET in explaining user behavior within social networking context. Some studies considered SET but failed to employ SET in its entirety: cost and bene<sup>fi</sup>t factors were either not explicated or missing from the theoretical model [29,54,82]. Other studies misinterpreted the cost and bene<sup>fi</sup>t factors in social exchange. Bene<sup>fi</sup>t, for example, was often wrongly operationalized as trust, which pertains to reciprocal bene<sup>fi</sup>ts in social exchange but is not a direct assessment of the actual bene<sup>fi</sup>ts [29,82]. Still there are other papers that incorrectly treated the assumptions (e.g., reciprocity) of SET as focal theoretical variables and tested them along with other SET variables [54]. The current study has faithfully applied SET and evidenced its value in predicting site use behavior. Through the lens of SET, we have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed perceived enjoyment and risk as the key bene<sup>fi</sup>t and cost that jointly determine individual use of a networking site. By analyzing the empirical data that was collected from 222 social network users, we con<sup>fi</sup>rm the importance of cost and bene<sup>fi</sup>t factors in explaining site use. Thus, this study makes a noticeable contribution to the SET literature as it answers the general calls to address SET problems by studies such as ref. [19]. Meanwhile, the current study contributes to social networking literature as it reveals a valuable theoretical framework that helps analyze this emerging phenomenon.

Second, this study uncovers the interaction effect between cost and bene<sup>fi</sup>t in affecting end user behavior. To date, SET literature has mostly ignored the potential interplays between cost and bene<sup>fi</sup>t factors, and our study is the <sup>fi</sup>rst to <sup>fi</sup>ll this gap. Within the social networking context, we have theorized the intricate dynamics between these two theoretical constructs. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we contend a negative moderating effect of cost on the relationship between bene<sup>fi</sup>t and site use. Through two statistical approaches, we analyze the empirical data and present strong evidence to support the postulated interaction effect. This has important rami<sup>fi</sup>cations for the social networking literature. Studies of hedonic information systems have advocated the dominant role of use experience (e.g., fun and entertainment) in predicting hedonic system use [2,100]. Within the context of networking sites, our results con<sup>fi</sup>rm that performance consequences of user interactions with a hedonic system also play an essential role in shaping individual behavior. Due to the ever-growing threats of information assurance, cost (e.g. risk) has become imperative to the prediction of online system use: not only does it directly shape the use extension but it also curtails the impact of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts on member site use. The paper therefore makes a meaningful contribution to both SET and social networking literature.

Third, the current study contributes to the literature by identifying key antecedents to the cost and bene<sup>fi</sup>t factors. To facilitate the analysis, we explore the precursors through a systematic multi-dimensional framework that considers imperative sources such as website designs, personal traits, and the computing environment [5]. By surveying users of networking sites such as Facebook, MySpace, and Friendster, we con<sup>fi</sup>rm that social presence, ease of use, extroversion, internet risk perception, and privacy abuse concern are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in affecting the cost and bene<sup>fi</sup>t associated with site use. This set of antecedents recognizes factors (e.g. extroversion and internet risk perception) that have so far been understudied in social networking research as well as important factors that are consolidated from variables of models that earlier research developed.

Research <sup>fi</sup>ndings in social networking sites are still scanty; Decision Support Systems is one example. Beginning in 2008, a total of 3 social networking papers have been published [11,55,110], constituting approximately 1% of the total volume of publications since 2008. Thus, research <sup>fi</sup>ndings are highly limited and this is disproportionate to the far-reaching and transformational role of social networking sites in modern societies. New <sup>fi</sup>ndings that are reported in this paper help build the body of knowledge in this important research area.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows: the subsequent section reviews the literature in social networking sites as well as the theoretical underpinning of the current study. Next we present the research model along with the research hypotheses. This is followed by research methodology and data analysis. We conclude the paper by discussing its theoretical contributions, practical implications, limitations, and venues for future research.

## 2. Literature review and theoretical development

## 2.1. Prior studies in social networking site

To date, a number of research streams exist in the domain of socia networking sites [7]. One stream of research has examined site user self-presentation, impression management, and reputation management Boyd conducted an ethnographic <sup>fi</sup>eldwork on Friendster to study indi viduals' presentation of self and the status quo of presentation accuracy [6]. Marwick summarized the typical strategies that individuals adopt in constructing creative pro<sup>fi</sup>les, along with the impacts of these strategies on the collection of business intelligence [73]. Others have explored the typology of social networks and their development. Through an examination of 362 million messages by 4.2 million Facebook users, Golde et al. analyzed the network structure of social relationships as well as the temporal rhythm of relationship expansion [42]. Based on 30,773 user pro<sup>fi</sup>les, Lampe et al. investigated the associations between mem ber pro<sup>fi</sup>le attributes and the development of friendships on Facebook [63]. In addition, studies have explored social networking sites through a global perspective. Krasnova and Veltri studied the differences be tween German and American users of social networks and found dis tinct variability in their cognitive patterns [59]. Veltri and Elgarah investigated the differences between users from Morocco and the U.S with respect to their perceptions and adoption of online social net working sites [101]. Studies have also been conducted to understand self-disclosure behavior at social networking sites. In the application of Facebook news feed controversy, Xu et al. conceptualized the potentia impact of information control and trust on information disclosure behav ior [108]. By focusing on the modes of entry boundary and ingroup outgroup boundary, Pike et al. analyzed the impacts of perceived public ness on online self-disclosure. Further researches have explored system use of networking sites. A number of issues have been studied and they include social capitals [31], threat coping [61], self-esteem [93], self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy [103], big-<sup>fi</sup>ve personality [85], critical mass [89], trust [82], and utilitarian values [107]. While the existing literature generates interesting <sup>fi</sup>ndings, they are also limited in several ways which gives room for further improvement. First, several of these studies have exam ined use intention but not the actual use behavior. The extant literature in information systems has evidenced that use intention may not predict use behavior due to reasons such as behavioral control [30.67.68.97] Second, theoretical underpinnings that are used in the existing studies are limited since many of the studies are based upon the Technolog Adoption Model, which is limited in explaining the use of leisure and he donic information systems such as social networking sites. Third, prior studies have mostly explored either positive or negative impetus of site use but failed to explain use phenomenon by considering both positive and negative stimulus, leading to an incomplete understanding of site use. New research is therefore needed to further the investigation of net working site use through alternative theoretical foundations and through comprehensive research frameworks.

## 2.2. Theoretical background

Social Exchange Theory (SET) is one of the most in<sup>fl</sup>uential theories that investigate the dynamics in social interactions [4]. SET assumes that individuals are goal-oriented and that their choice of social interaction is a subjective calculus of the social or material resources being exchanged [99]. These resources may be tangible or intangible and they may include time, effort, reputation, enjoyment, honor, and friendship, and they are the currency of social exchanges. According to SET, individ uals proactively seek to maximize their bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and minimize their costs when exchanging resources with others [74]. Bene<sup>fi</sup>ts refer to those resources that one receives as a result of social exchange or positive outcomes of an exchange, whereas cost refers to those resources that one gives away during a social exchange or any adverse outcomes of an exchange [57]. Individuals engage in interpersonal relationships only when the expected gain is greater than the expected cost. The bene<sup>fi</sup>ts may not be achieved at the onset of social interactions since individuals tend to engage in exchanges with an expectation of reciprocity from the others [43]. SET has been used recently in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of information systems at both individual and organization levels to investigate phenomenon such as knowledge sharing [57,66], software development [3], and system evaluation and use [40,90].

The tenets of SET have an important implication for the current study. Use in a networking site implies active participation in social interactions with the online community. Therefore, SET is relevant to the studies of social networking site use. When applied, SET posits that individuals' site use may result in an exchange process in which the members maximize gains while minimizing losses. Among others, the existing literature of social networking sites has highlighted enjoyment as a key bene<sup>fi</sup>t for online users who join networking sites [50]. Prior studies have also underlined the major source of cost in site use as risks, which may be due to information security and privacy threats [10,60,84]. In this paper, we analyze an individual's voluntary use of social networking sites through the theoretical lens of SET.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

Drawing upon SET, we develop a research model that explains member use of a social networking site. Fig. 1 suggests that individuals enjoy the use of a networking site at the cost of risks. Moreover, risk in site use may attenuate the relationship between enjoyment and site use. The research model also advocates that website designs such as social presence, ease of use, and personality traits such as extroversion contribute to enjoyment. Further, this model postulates that personal beliefs of the computing environment such as Internet risk perception and privacy abuse concern will impact one's perceived risk in using a social networking site.

A key bene<sup>fi</sup>t in social networking site use is the perceived enjoyment. In this paper, perceived enjoyment is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the extent to which the activity of using a social networking site is perceived to be enjoyable in its own right, apart from any associated performance consequences [24]. The de<sup>fi</sup>nition of perceived enjoyment speci<sup>fi</sup>es the extent to which fun can be derived from using a networking site and it should not be mistaken as the net bene<sup>fi</sup>t (e.g. bene<sup>fi</sup>t minus cost) in system use [100]. It is often referred to as the hedonic component of an information system [51,100]. When a networking site is concerned, this platform allows individuals to interact with friends and family, exchange messages, pictures, and videos, and to participate in groups and events. The use of these sites is therefore hedonic as users experience fun and entertainment [103]. Moon and Kim found that individuals who enjoy a web system will view their interactions with the system more positive and form a high behavior intention to use it [75]. And thus we expect that members are more inclined to use a networking site when he or she <sup>fi</sup>nds it enjoyable.

## H1. Enjoyment will positively relate to site use.

Literature suggests that system design such as social presence is instrumental in shaping user experience [64]. Social presence is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the extent to which a computer medium allows a user to experience the others as being psychologically present [38]. Prior studies have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed website designs that facilitate the establishment of social presence, including emotive text and pictures [47], personalized greetings [41], human audio [70], and human video [62]. Lombard and Ditton suggested that one of the most prominent psychological impacts of social presence is enjoyment [70]. Yoo and Alavi also noted that social presence fosters a psychological connection between users and a web system, and renders the latter more fond [109]. Prior studies in e-commerce context have presented preliminary evidence on the impact of social presence on enjoyment [20,49]. We expect the similar to hold in the current context:

## H2. Social presence will positively relate to enjoyment.

Meanwhile, user perceived enjoyment in networking site use may be enhanced by the ease of system use [58]. Ease of use is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the degree to which a person believes that using a particular system would be free of effort [21]. Ease of use is neither a direct bene<sup>fi</sup>t nor a cost in site use while its opposite, “effort of use,” may be considered as a part of the cost of using information systems from the users' perspective [22]. Van der Heijden suggested that ease of use enhances user's hedonic experience (e.g. enjoyment in using social networking sites) [100]. Teo et al. suggested that “systems that are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to use are less likely to be perceived as enjoyable” (pp. 27) [98]. Following Deci's Motivational Theory [25] and motivational model of technology acceptance [24], literature has accumulated preliminary evidence on the potential relationship between ease of use and enjoyment [24,52,53,98,100]. We therefore posit:

## H3. Ease of use will positively relate to enjoyment.

In addition, perceived enjoyment may be cultivated by personal traits such as extroversion [85]. Extroversion is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as positive emotion, urgency, and tendency to seek out stimulation and the company of others [15]. This personality is discerned by manifest engagement with the outside world as extroverts enjoy being with people. Social

networking sites offer rich communication features such as texting, video sharing, email, and blog. These designs render a network site an ideal platform for individuals to express themselves, enjoy social events, af<sup>fi</sup>rm relationships, and extend personal networks. When presented with these opportunities, an extrovert member will take advantage of them to better meet his or her social needs. Rosen and Kluemper suggested that social networking sites are instrumental and useful for extroverts to assert themselves [85]. It is therefore argued that members who are extroverts will be more likely to appreciate the value of a social networking site and enjoy their engagement. And thus we posit:

## H4. Extroversion will positively relate to enjoyment.

Using social networking sites comes with a cost, as members may consider it risky [50]. Perceived risk is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the extent to which there is an uncertainty in signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and disappointing outcomes that may be realized [87]. Prior studies have pointed out that users of networking sites are subject to malware, spam and phishing, data theft, and content alternation. Consequently, they may experience emotional discomfort, monetary loss, or reputational damage [28,83]. Numerous accounts of social networking site members being attacked were reported in the past. In 2010, for example, several thousand Facebook users were attacked by a click jacking scam [86]. Therefore, risk is high in using these websites and it is deemed as a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant cost that may discourage users from continuous participation. MySpace, for example, was reported to lose members after spam artists started using pro<sup>fi</sup>les as bait for illicit websites [96]. We postulate:

## H5. Perceived risk will negatively relate to site use.

We expect that risks stemming from the use of a social networking site may moderate the relationship between enjoyment and site use. That is, the relationship between enjoyment and site use is a function of perceived risk. When risk is perceived to be low, online users' use behavior may be largely motivated by fun and enjoyment that is experienced during their interactions. When risk is perceived to be high, however, one may become hesitant to use a social networking site regardless of their own assessment of the enjoyment. Even with high perceived enjoyment, for example, users may decline site participation in case the likelihood of adverse consequences is substantial. That is, the predicting power of enjoyment on site use may weaken when perceived risks grow. Therefore we expect:

H6. Perceived risk in site use will negatively moderate the relationship between enjoyment and site use.

Perception of risk in using a social networking site may be shaped by individuals' belief of the computing environment. Internet risk perception measures one's uneasiness about using Internet [104]. Internet is known for the parlance of cyber threats which signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly jeopardize online users and businesses [44,79]. Internet risk perception may lead decision makers to exaggerate uncertainties in networking sites, exhibit unwarranted con<sup>fi</sup>dence in their judgment, and subsequently overestimate the risks in using social networking sites [104]. Members of social networking sites have varying levels of Internet risk perception as a result of their prior Internet use (e.g. online shopping) and outcome history. It is common for one to become reluctant to use a networking site because the sense of Internet risk may be overwhelming. Therefore we expect:

## H7. Internet risk perception will positively relate to perceived risk in site use.

Perceived risk may also stem from concern about privacy abuse [92]. Privacy abuse concern re<sup>fl</sup>ects one's uneasiness about the potential opportunistic behavior related to his or her personal information [27]. The concern of privacy abuse is conceptually different from perceived risk [27,80] as it does not assess uncertainty as its objective. Users' privacy abuse concern may range from being unconcerned (indifferent)

to highly concerned (fretful). It is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by a wide range of factors that do not relate to the contextual uncertainties. An example of an in-<sup>fl</sup>uential factor is an individual's value of privacy, ranging from low to high, which refers to the importance that an individual places on personal privacy [33]. Interactions on networking sites give rise to privacy release as one publicly discloses identity, private interest, and personal opinions [10]. Unfortunately, privacy protection has been unsatisfactory due to the lack of control features and sound policy design. Facebook, for example, employed a transitive relation Friend of a Friend design that left member information <sup>fl</sup>ow to unintended parties without awareness and consensus. Divulged privacy may be subsequently used against one's will for purposes such as stalking, embarrassment, and extortion. Online users who are concerned about their privacy tend to overrate the uncertainty in using networking sites and consider a rather small probability of privacy abuse as signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. Therefore we expect:

H8. Privacy abuse concern will positively relate to perceived risk in site use.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Measurement development

Measurement items for the principal constructs in this study were borrowed directly from existing measures to ensure validity [95]. The detailed measurements and their sources of the principal constructs are shown in Appendix A. The survey instruments were pretested with 60 users of a social networking site to check the psychometric properties of the measurement scales [14]. Following these pretests, the measurement instruments were shortened, re<sup>fi</sup>ned, and validated for their statistical properties.

## 4.2. Survey administration

The research model was tested using survey data. We collected survey responses from 222 undergraduate students who took undergraduate courses at a large institution in the Midwestern United States. College students are a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant segment of social networking site users. A recent Pew survey shows that college students represent the largest portion of Facebook users [65]. Also, 85% of U.S. college students use Facebook [32]. Thus, the student sample is appropriate for the current research. The respondents were recruited from four undergraduate courses that were offered in a business college. The samples consist of business and non-business undergraduate majors, the samples are therefore heterogeneous. Respondents were guaranteed both con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality and access to the aggregated survey results. Participation in the survey was voluntary. ## 5. Analysis and results

We tested the research model using structural equation modeling analysis. We used partial least squares (PLS), which employ a componentbased approach for estimation. PLS has an advantage as it “involves no assumptions about the population or scale of measurement” [36]. And thus it works without distributional assumptions and works with nominal, ordinal, and interval scaled variables [45]. Cassel et al. con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that PLS is robust against inadequacies (e.g. skewness and multicollinearity) and that latent variable scores conform to true values [9]. In addition, PLS is best suited for examining complex relationships (e.g. large number of variables and an interaction effect in current research model) by avoiding inadmissible solutions and factor indeterminacy [12].

Convergent and discriminant validity are inferred when (1) the AVE square root of each construct is larger than its correlations with the other constructs (i.e., the AVE shared between the construct and its indicators is larger than the AVE shared between the construct and the other items); (2) all AVEs are greater than .50; and (3) the PLS indicators load much higher on their hypothesized construct than on other constructs (i.e., own loadings are higher than cross loadings) [12]. Because the research data was collected from a single survey, we checked the extent of common method bias. First, the Harman's one-factor test was performed by including all the variables in a principal components factor analysis [81]. Common method bias exists when one single factor emerges or when one factor accounts for the majority of the covariance among the variables. The results showed that none of the emergent factors explained the majority of the covariance. Second, the partial correlation method was performed by adding the highest factor that was produced in the principal component factor analysis into the PLS model as a control variable. This factor did not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly increase the variance explained by the research model, indicating no common method bias. Third, the correlation matrix was examined for highly correlated factors. The common method bias exists when these are extremely high correlations (r>.9). Table 2 does not reveal such evidence.

## 6. Discussion and Conclusion

Social networking sites provide an open platform to accommodate online users and enable them to develop interpersonal networks and enjoy friendship [102]. Social networking sites continue increasing in popularity. The latest Pew worldwide survey showed that 47% of online adults used social networking sites and 73% of teens and young adults were a member of at least one social network [65]. A 2010 Nielsen report found that the world spent over 110 billion minutes on social networks and the numbers of people visiting these sites increased by 24% over last year [76]. To sustain the success of a social networking site, it is important that members continuously and constantly access and use the site to maintain its vitality. Site use leads to members' active engagement in communal activities, growth and expansion of cross-site social relationships, increased volume of site visits and traf<sup>fi</sup>c, and an accumulation of user generated digital content. Despite its strategic importance, site use may be limited as the members constantly weigh the potential bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and costs embedded in their participation. Recent studies con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that members had started reducing their use and even abandoned social networking sites [26]. Drawing upon SET, the current study explores member voluntary use of a networking site through a cost-bene<sup>fi</sup>t framework. Thus, the research model developed highlights enjoyment as the key bene<sup>fi</sup>t that motivates site use and stresses risk as the major inhibitor. It also suggests that the presence of risk may attenuate the relationship between enjoyment and site use. In addition, the research model posits that website designs (e.g. social presence and ease of use) and personal traits (e.g. extroversion) jointly affect enjoyment whereas personal belief of the environment (e.g. Internet risk perception and privacy abuse concern) determines the degree of perceived risk in site use. We collected survey data from 222 social networking site users to validate this research model and found strong support on all the research hypotheses.

This study makes a threefold contribution. First, it proves the value of SET in explaining member use of social networking sites. Prior studies on social networks have largely ignored the theoretical value of SET in explaining individual decision making and behavior [50]. Findings that are reported in this study have evidenced the importance of social exchange in shaping members' site use. The research con<sup>fi</sup>rms that perceived enjoyment and risk are the two sources that affect one's usage of a networking site. Second, this study uncovers the interaction effect between cost (i.e., risk) and bene<sup>fi</sup>t (i.e., enjoyment) in affecting end user behavioral outcome. The existing literature on SET has mostly focused on the direct impacts of cost and bene<sup>fi</sup>t, yet seldom investigated their interplay. The current study <sup>fi</sup>lls this gap by capturing the intricate dynamics between the two theoretical constructs. Third, this study contributes to the literature on social networking sites by identifying the key antecedents to members' use of a site. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that website design features, personal traits, and beliefs of the computing environment all in<sup>fl</sup>uence one's use choice. New knowledge augments the prior literature to provide an improved understanding of why one will choose to access online social networks at the era of Web 2.0.

The current study also informs practice. Site use adds vital support to the sustainable success of networking sites and social network service providers may stimulate the use extent by cultivating perceived member enjoyment. To increase users' enjoyment, service providers may increase website social presence through the adoption of emotive text, human pictures, personalized greetings, and human audio video [41,47,62]. Meanwhile, service providers may consider website design choices that boost the ease of use. Facebook, for example, recently altered the top menu and left column on its homepage to offer improved site navigation and ease of information access [77]. Other designs such as simpli<sup>fi</sup>ed page views, inline <sup>fi</sup>ltering, customized services, and one-click actions will allow members to perform their tasks at higher ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. Further, networking site providers may encourage member extroversion to foster increased enjoyment. Psychology literature pointed out that personality is malleable and may be reformed given proper motivations from the task environment [72]. To this end, site wide campaigns that promote an open, social, interactive, and responsive culture is likely to inspire members to turn more extroverted and subsequently better appreciate the fun in using networking sites.

Meanwhile, it is imperative that networking service providers effectively alleviate member perceived risks in using the systems. Our research notes that an individual's Internet risk perception affects his or her site use risk perception. To this end, public awareness and educational campaigns may be exercised. Educational campaigns are suggested to improve end user knowledge and skills in combating Internet threats and consequently reduce the perceived risks of being attacked [71,91]. Awareness programs help remove the cognitive bias pertaining to Internet risks and cultivate a healthy and accurate risk assessment. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings also suggest that members' perception of site use risk grows out of concerns for privacy abuse. Prior studies suggested that appropriate policy designs may reduce the likelihood of privacy breach and avoid subsequent abuse [69,94]. Vendors may also protect member privacy through technical solutions by using privacy-aware system architecture and software features that offer users the ability to search and query information, reasoning about intelligent actions with respect to privacy preservation [10].

Our study has certain limitations that should be noted. First, the current study utilizes subjective survey data to assess Internet users' self-disclosure behavior at a networking site. In his study of social networks, Whinston noted that self-reports “reveal information that the user is willing to provide which might be biased or even inconsistent with her actual opinion or behavior” [106]. Future research may employ objective measurements to assess the actual site use behavior of the research samples. Second, new research may employ samples

of a wider diversity. While college students present the largest portion of Facebook users, teenagers and adults over age 22 are all actively engaged in Facebook. It is important to validate the research model among these populations to identify whether the impetus of self-disclosure is uniform across different age groups. Third, future studies may consider recruiting users of other social networking sites. Survey respondents in the current study are predominantly Facebook users and hence the empirical data mostly describes user behavior of Facebook population. Thus, the results must be treated with caution in terms of its generalizability to the entire population of social networking sites. To understand social networking sites as a whole, it is imperative that new studies employ research samples from other social networking sites that are underrepresented in the current study. Fourth, the current sample consists of 60% males and 40% females. A recent Pew report suggested a higher proportion of female users (56%) and a lower proportion of male users (44%) of social networking sites [46]. The observed difference in gender distribution may be caused by the unique school environment that was used in data collection. And thus the results must be treated with caution in terms of its generalizability to the entire population of social networking sites. Future studies should consider gender distributions in designing sample collecting schemes. New research may also study how site usage may be correlated with the quality and quantity of whatever joy a user can get from a social network site. New knowledge will help practitioners understand the key resources that are likely to be exchanged at social networking sites and subsequently develop strategies and designs to facilitate these exchanges. While a full examination of this research issue is beyond the scope of the current paper, as we are primarily concerned about the outcomes (i.e., bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and costs) of the resource exchange process and their contributions to the use behavior, we provide a preliminary data analysis on the relationship between usage and examples of the enjoyable such as relevant information that is retrieved from using a networking site. Foa and Foa's Resource Theory considered information as a major type of resources in an exchange [34,35]. The empirical results reveal that the quality of information retrieved (b=.36, pb.001), measured by (1) “I can obtain information of the others” and (2) “I can collect information that I am interested of” using a 7-point Likert scale, is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly associated with site use. Findings as such provide some initial evidence and directions for future research to systematically validate the relationship between usage and the key enjoyable. Lastly, future studies may consider other bene<sup>fi</sup>t factors that members may enjoy during their interaction with social networking sites. Users may use social networking sites for a variety of reasons besides obtaining the hedonic utility. However, a systematic survey of those bene<sup>fi</sup>t factors requires the application of alternative theoretical frameworks and is therefore beyond the scope of the current study.
