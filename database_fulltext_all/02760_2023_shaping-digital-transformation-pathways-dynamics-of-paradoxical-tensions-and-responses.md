<sup>1</sup>Nanyang Technological University, Singapore, acsoh@ntu.edu.sg <sup>2</sup>Singapore University of Social Sciences, Singapore, adrianyeowyk@suss.edu.sg <sup>3</sup>Nanyang Technological University, Singapore, qiwei001@e.ntu.edu.sg

## Abstract

Although the pace of digital transformation (DT) has been accelerating in more organizations, there is still much uncertainty about the ways organizations can achieve the intended outcomes from such efforts. Rather than just focusing on the transformation outcomes, our paper suggests that a more fruitful approach would be to conceptualize DT as a journey that often encounters contradictory tensions. We adopt paradox theory to explain the dynamics through which organizations can manage these tensions while driving their DT efforts. Drawing on extensive review of the DT literature and preexisting research cases of three organizations’ experiences with the DT process, we developed the concept of DT pathways. DT pathways are the varied journeys experienced by organizations as they make strategic shifts - leveraging digital technology and evolving the organization’s business model. We show how DT pathways emerge as organizations adopt different DT strategic intent, actions, and responses to a range of paradoxical tensions. We outline three potential pathways—virtuous, moderate virtuous, and vicious DT pathways—and their attending predictive propositions, which can serve as a guide for managers and researchers involved in DT efforts and research.

Keywords: Digital Transformation, Paradox Theory, Paradoxical Tensions, IT-Enabled Organizational Transformation

Frantz Rowe and M. Lynne Markus were the accepting senior editors. This research article was submitted on August 30, 2021 and underwent two revisions. This paper is part of the Special Issue on Envisioning Digital Transformation: Advancing Theoretical Diversity.

## 1 Introduction

Many organizations have embarked on digital transformation (DT) as a necessity for competitiveness. As the explosion of DT-related articles in both academia and practice shows, DT is a key concern (if not the concern) for many researchers and practitioners alike (Kappelman et al., 2019). Further, the COVID-19 pandemic and the resultant abrupt shift to digitally enabled processes and work-from-home arrangements have accelerated the pace of many organizations’ DT efforts.

Despite this inexorable push toward DT, there is still much that is uncertain about DT. Organizations vary in their scope of DT and outcomes (Bonnet & Westerman,

2021; Davenport & Westerman, 2018) and many have sought to define what DT is (Bloomberg, 2018; Fitzgerald, 2014; Hess et al., 2016; Vial, 2019; Wessel et al., 2021). We note that some DT may bring about significant changes (Guinan et al., 2019), even as other DTs flounder after promising beginnings (e.g., Davenport & Westerman, 2018). These different DT experiences suggest that it may be fruitful to go beyond focusing on DT as some specific combination of data and technologies for transformation to focusing on understanding DT as an ongoing journey.

The DT journey is considered by some as an evolution of IT-enabled organizational transformation (ITOT) (Vial, 2019). Like ITOT, it involves change to the deep structure of the organization (Besson & Rowe, 2012), and this process of changes to organizational structure and behaviors reflects the organization’s strategic shifts (vom Brocke et al., 2021). These strategic shifts are often accompanied by transformed organizational value propositions as well as changes in organizational identity (Wessel et al., 2021). Vial (2019) argues that due to the pervasive adoption of digital technologies in society, DT occurs at greater scale, scope, and speed.

In conceptualizing the DT journey, we draw on the perspective of process theorists (Langley et al., 2013; Langley & Tsoukas, 2016). Specifically, we consider how the varying forms of organizational DT are but “momentary instantiations” of the DT process (Cloutier & Langley, 2020, p. 3). Following this process view, our analytical focus is on the “events, activities, and trajectory” (Cloutier & Langley, 2020, p. 4) that make up an organization’s DT process. By drawing on prior research in ITOT, DT, paradox theory, and rich case studies of three organizations’ DT processes, we carefully trace the order of DT events, activities, contingencies, and emergent interactions to propose the concept of DT pathways as well as a set of propositions that explain how and why each organization’s DT pathways may differ as it moves from one phase to another.

## 2 Prior Research and Theory

## 2.1 DT Process

We begin our review of the DT process by considering earlier work on ITOT. We found Besson and Rowe’s (2012) conceptualization of the ITOT transformation process as well as the main theoretical lenses that they used instructive. Besson and Rowe (2012) noted that the transformation process comprises key phases of uprooting, construction, and routinization/ optimization. They noted that punctuated equilibrium theory emphasizes the uprooting phase, with transformation being conceptualized as a revolutionary process. Evolutionary theory focuses more on construction as it tends to see transformation as gradual change involving exploration. Institutional theory focuses on the routinization that occurs from the development of institutional norms.

A recent review of the ITOT literature (vom Brocke et al., 2021) considered the teleological, dialectic, evolutionary, and life-cycle perspectives (Van de Ven & Poole, 1995) of the transformation change process. The review found that the teleological view dominated, followed by the dialectic and evolutionary model, with a handful viewing the transformation process from a life-cycle perspective. This is consistent with the earlier finding by Besson and Rowe (2012) that many ITOT studies take a teleological view of change, resulting in a gap in understanding how and why the transformation moves from one phase to another. Furthermore, the construction and routinizing aspects of transformation remain relatively understudied.

The need to understand how and why DT unfolds beyond the initial phase is important, as DT continues to be challenging, with many organizations not experiencing the desired outcomes (Al-Ali & Phaal, 2019; Bonnet & Westerman, 2021; Tucto-Mechán & Bazán-Martínez, 2019). Prior research highlighting unintended consequences (Boudreau & Robey, 2005) and inertia (Besson & Rowe, 2012; Gregory et al., 2015) suggests that DT is an organizational journey that can unfold along different pathways. To theorize how and why DT pathways unfold, we need to identify the key elements of a DT pathway. Here, we find Vial’s (2019) work helpful, as he identifies “building blocks” (p. 5) that make up DT processes. We incorporate these below as we review and organize the literature on DT processes by phases.

The initiation of DT is often triggered by disruptions arising from the use of digital among competitors and customers, thus triggering strategic shifts in business strategy (Vial, 2019). Much of DT research focuses on the initiation or uprooting phase where DT actions include developing new strategic directions (Matt et al., 2015; Nadeem et al., 2018; Vukšić et al., 2018), analyzing firms’ existing situations, and considering how technology can be leveraged to change the organization’s business model (BM) (Fitzgerald, 2014; Kutnjak et al., 2019; Reis et al., 2018; Vukšić et al., 2018; Warner & Wäger, 2019). Significant transformation requires resourcing. Initial resourcingrelated steps include establishing a planning team to coordinate the transformation (Barann et al., 2019), identifying new capabilities required (Al-Ali & Phaal, 2019; Dang & Vartiainen, 2020), and allocating resources required to get the DT process started (Pelletier & Raymond, 2020).

The initiation of DT is followed by the construction phase (Besson & Rowe, 2012). DT studies note that to deliver the new strategy and value proposition, changes will need to be made to the BM through the reconfiguration of resources and capabilities (Vial, 2019; Warner & Wäger, 2018). Actions in this phase most often include implementing new systems and technologies (Nwankpa & Roumani, 2016) such as operational backbone and digital services platforms (Sebastian et al., 2017), integrating and streamlining operational processes (Demirkan et al., 2016; Horlacher et al., 2016; Kutnjak et al., 2019; Morakanyane et al., 2017; Nwankpa & Roumani, 2016; Paavola et al., 2017; Reis et al., 2018, Singh & Hess, 2017), changing organizational structures (Clohessy et al., 2017; Hess et al., 2016; Vuksic et al., 2018, Vial 2019) like creating digital hubs staffed with cross-functional teams (Chaniot, 2019), and developing new competencies (Demirkan et al., 2016; Dremel et al., 2017; Pelletier &

Raymond, 2020; Weingarth et al., 2019). As these new capabilities are developed, DT prototypes will be executed to deliver preliminary outcomes (Dang & Vartiainen, 2020).

In the next phase of the DT journey, the organization will often seek to leverage, integrate, and optimize the DT capabilities that have been developed to help the organization move toward its new strategic directions (Chaniot, 2019; Pelletier & Raymond, 2020). With increasing integration and routinization, DT becomes part of the organizational culture (Chaniot, 2019; Dang & Vartiainen, 2020, Vial, 2019). Many studies have noted the importance of fostering a new culture that embraces DT (Loonam et al., 2018; Morakanyane et al., 2017; Pihir et al., 2019; Warner & Wäger, 2019). However, the required shifts in culture are often significant and are, as such, difficult (Lucas & Goh, 2009). This is especially the case because organizational culture is reflected in the mindsets of members in their organizational identity, values and norms, and is embedded in processes and structures (Baiyere et al., 2020; Chanias & Hess, 2016). Past research has also prescribed investing in existing employees to develop new perspectives and norms as well as hiring new talent with mindsets that are more aligned with the organization’s new DT directions.

Given this broad mapping of phases, why do some organizations’ DT journeys proceed relatively smoothly while others falter? The challenges encountered and the responses to these challenges influence the DT pathways that unfold (Agarwal et al., 2022; Chanias et al., 2019; Fortmann et al., 2019; Soh et al., 2019, Wessel et al., 2021; Wimelius et al., 2021; Yeow et al., 2018). Inertia and resistance to change have been identified as key challenges to ITOT and DT (Besson & Rowe, 2012; Vial, 2019). Inertia and resistance can be viewed as manifestations of the underlying tension between the current and the new direction that the organization is moving toward in its DT journey.

Several empirical studies have examined these tensions arising from competing demands and noted that they arise from long-standing, deeply rooted practices, structures, values, and norms (Gregory et al., 2015; Svahn et al., 2017; Wareham et al., 2014). These empirical studies have identified a range of competing demands at different levels of analysis. At the ecosystem level, competing demands may include standard vs. variety, control vs. autonomy, and collective vs. individual (Wareham et al., 2014). At the organizational level, Svahn et al. (2017) identified competing demands such as existing vs. requisite, product vs. process, internal vs. external, and control vs. flexibility. At the IT program level, competing demands may take the form of IT efficiency vs. IT innovation, IT standardization vs. IT differentiation, IT integration vs. IT replacement, IT program agility vs.

IT project stability, IT program control vs. IT project autonomy, and IT program coordination vs. IT project isolation (Gregory et al., 2015).

A few more recent studies have observed how an organization’s responses to paradoxical tensions influence the DT process (Soh et al., 2019; Wessel et al., 2021; Wimelius et al., 2021). Wessel et al. (2021) showed that DT often imposes significant changes to work practices that result in tensions. They found that while ongoing “reconciliation” actions by organizational members and management enabled DT to progress, the actions also altered the trajectory of the planned DT activities. Soh et al. (2019) found that the type of organizational responses to paradoxical tensions and the sequence of these responses could either enable the DT process to progress or stall. In particular, they noted that defensive measures led to the stalling of DT. Furthermore, Fuchs and Hess (2018) classified the actions taken to address tensions as “between-phase” (i.e., actions that disrupt deep structures and shift the organization into the next phase) and “within-phase” (i.e., actions that are more incremental in nature).

As such, these studies suggest that paradox theory would be helpful in explicating DT journeys, supplementing the teleological perspective of change in the literature (Besson & Rowe, 2012; vom Brocke et al., 2021) with a more interactive model that reflects the very real tensions that arise from and the responses that shape an organization’s DT (Chanias et al., 2019; Fortmann et al., 2019; Soh et al., 2019; Wessel et al., 2021; Wimelius et al., 2021; Yeow et al., 2018).

## 2.2 Paradox Theory

While prior DT studies often refer to the concept of tensions, the tensions they identify are grounded in the specific contexts of their respective studies, resulting in a range of labels as noted above (Gregory et al., 2015; Svahn et al., 2017; Wareham et al., 2014). Hence, it is difficult to compare across studies to understand the main types of tensions that are likely to arise in DT. Paradox theory provides a way to identify the main types of competing demands or tensions in a consistent manner (Soh et al., 2019). The varying DT journeys described also suggest a need to theorize the DT process dynamics that lead to variations in organizational experience and outcomes. Here, paradox theory also provides conceptualization of different organizational responses to paradoxical tensions, which contribute to virtuous or vicious cycles. These provide conceptual anchors for understanding why some organizational actions that address competing demands enable DT to thrive while others lead DT to stall. Below, we present the key concepts of paradoxical tensions, responses, and virtuous and vicious cycles.

Paradoxical tension is defined as elements that seem logical individually but inconsistent and even absurd when juxtaposed (Smith & Lewis, 2011). A seminal review of 360 studies of organizational paradoxes (Smith & Lewis, 2011) identified four categories of paradoxical tensions: performing, belonging, organizing, and learning. Performing tensions stem from the plurality of stakeholders that result in competing strategies and goals. Belonging tensions refer to contradictions in relation to identity and interpersonal relationships. Belonging tensions, summarized as “who we are” and “what we do,” focus on the clash between different values and the sense of belonging across individuals and groups, as well as the desire to promote shared identities and homogeneous uniformity (Luscher & Lewis, 2008). Organizing tensions surface when complex systems create competing designs and processes to achieve a desired outcome. Learning tensions emerge when dynamic systems change, renew themselves, and innovate. These efforts involve building upon and destroying the past to create the future, and they require the organization and its employees to acquire different competencies (Smith & Lewis, 2011).

Paradoxes are inherent to organizations and they remain latent until environmental conditions draw attention to the contradictory nature of the dualities (Smith & Lewis, 2011). Specifically, plurality, change, and scarcity are seen as enabling conditions for salience (Hargrave & Van de Ven, 2017; Jarzabkowski et al., 2013; Smith, 2014; Smith & Lewis, 2011). Plurality denotes a multiplicity of views in contexts of diffuse power (Denis et al., 2007); it expands uncertainty and surfaces competing goals (Cohen & March, 1974). Change spurs new opportunities for sensemaking as actors grapple with conflicting shortand long-term needs (Luscher & Lewis, 2008) and competing yet coexisting roles and emotions (Huy, 2002). Scarcity involves resource limitations— temporal, financial, or human (Smith & Lewis, 2011). Plurality, change, and scarcity are all likely to be present and may trigger paradoxical tensions in organizations embarking on DT.

Dynamics is an important but relatively understudied part of paradox theory. In a more recent authoritative review of 133 studies on paradox, Schad et al. (2016) found that only 18% dealt with dynamics. They noted that “neglecting the dynamics of paradox could overlook the persistence of tensions and associated virtuous and vicious cycles” (Schad et al., 2016, p. 35). Virtuous and vicious cycles are “iterative spirals or self-reinforcing sequences of events that grow out of the ways that actors process contradictions” (Putnam et al., 2016, p. 81).

Responses to tensions are key to whether these spirals or sequences are positive (virtuous) or negative (vicious) cycles (Lewis, 2018; Putnam et al., 2016).

The theory has identified two broad types of responses—“both-and” responses and “either-or” responses. Both-and responses require the acceptance of paradoxical tensions. In other words, the organization recognizes that it cannot simply choose one side as both poles of the paradox are important (Smith & Lewis, 2011). Management contributes to such acceptance by signaling the need to value both poles. Both-and responses to tensions involve attending to competing demands simultaneously and adopting regular intervals of stability and change (Putnam et al., 2016). They include actions that differentiate and integrate the poles over time (Andriopoulos & Lewis, 2009; Smith, 2014). Either-or responses emphasize and value one pole over the other. They include actions such as suppressing or isolating one pole or attempting to synthesize one pole with the other (Gilbert et al., 2018; Sundaramurthy & Lewis, 2003). Either-or responses tend to arise when there are strong organizational forces for inertia (Smith & Lewis, 2011), actors’ preferences for consistency (Smith & Lewis, 2011), feelings of entrapment, and perceived inability to solve tension-related problems, which result in the blocking of creative generation of acceptable alternatives (Putnam et al., 2016).

Both-and responses contribute to virtuous cycles, which are self-reinforcing spirals that result in learning and creativity, and stem from a growing awareness of tensions and the development of paradoxical thinking (Putnam et al., 2016). In a virtuous cycle, actors actively confront the discomforts of the paradox by seeking ways to address both poles. These both-and responses become embedded in the firm’s processes and increase actors confidence in dealing with the discomfort that the paradox creates (Jarzabkowski et al., 2013; Lewis, 2018). For example, in Jarzabkowski et al.’s (2013) study of Telco, managers responded to paradoxical tensions with the both-and response of adjusting. Organizing processes that embedded the adjusting responses were developed, allowing managers to interact with both poles of the paradox and move the project forward. Studies have found that virtuous cycles have a positive impact on organizational sustainability and long-term performance (Chung & Beamish, 2010; Schmitt & Raisch, 2013; Smith et al., 2011). They have also been known to foster creativity (Miron-Spektor et al., 2011), increase effectiveness in teams (Drach ‐ Zahavy & Freund, 2007), and enable innovation (Gebert et al., 2010).

Either-or responses contribute to vicious cycles, which are spirals of behavioral and structural contradictions that escalate out of control (Putnam et al., 2016). In a vicious cycle, actors seek to alleviate anxieties over paradoxes by reinforcing existing mindsets, and embedded structures and processes (Smith & Lewis, 2011). These either-or responses, while offering shortterm relief, often have unintended consequences such as losing sight of alternative perspectives (Smith & Lewis, 2011). For instance, Sundaramurthy and Lewis (2003) discovered that an either-or response that sought to reinforce high consistency with existing organizational culture led to groupthink and organizational decline. Studies have found that vicious cycles have a negative impact on organizational sustainability (Schad et al., 2016; Tsoukas & Cunha, 2017). They have also been known to deplete trust within an organization (Sundaramurthy & Lewis, 2003) and simplify and narrow organizational competencies (Lewis, 2018).

Responses often have long-lasting effects, as bothand/either-or actions are embedded into organizational procedures, thus constructing a system that is predisposed to virtuous or vicious cycles, (Jarzabkowski et al., 2013). In addition, it is important to be aware of the cumulative nature of responses to paradox over time; responses that are built into the outcomes of a response cycle likely shape the responses that occur in the next cycle (Jarzabkowski et al., 2013). However, it is possible to shift from a vicious to a virtuous cycle, as exemplified by Lewis’s (2018) study of Lego. Such shifts toward virtuous cycles require both-and responses that promote the equality of both poles, strengthen the weaker pole, and look beyond the paradox by focusing on desired outcomes (Huq et al., 2017).

## 3 Developing a Theory of DT Pathways

While paradox theory’s concepts of tensions, responses, and virtuous/vicious cycles provide a basis for theorizing DT pathways, process theory scholars have discussed the need for empirical data, given the inherent challenges of pure conceptual process theorizing without data (Langley, 1999; Van de Ven, 1992). Because of the fluid nature of pathways and the different contextual reasons that influence how they are formed and shaped, scholars argue that empirical process data is important for process theorizing (Cloutier & Langley, 2020; Langley, 1999; Van de Ven, 1992). In line with recent IS studies, we selected preexisting case research data as part of our process theorizing (Gallivan, 2001; Hanson, 2010; Wiener et al., 2019).

The process of selecting these preexisting cases is as follows: First, we established the criteria for selecting articles to be included in our theorizing. The article needs to (1) be a peer-reviewed qualitative case study that describes the DT process, (2) contain original data and analysis, and (3) have at least one author observing or studying the DT process (Gallivan, 2001; Hanson, 2010). These criteria can facilitate high-quality studies containing longitudinal data with detailed description of how the DT process unfolds in the cases.

Next, we conducted an extensive search on various academic databases (e.g., AIS library, Scopus) and Google Scholar using keywords such as digital transformation, IT transformation, IT-enabled change, IT-enabled transformation, case stud\*. While most DT research occurred in the 2010s (Reis et al., 2018; Vial, 2019), we used a wider range of years for the search (from 2000 to 2020) to retrieve as many potential articles as possible. The first round of search yielded 109 articles. After reading through the abstracts, we eliminated studies that were focused on the industry rather than the organization, as well as studies that did not describe the DT process or were snapshots of multiple DT cases.

Third, we read in detail the remaining 69 articles. From this second round of close reading, we further eliminated cases that did not contain rich, detailed data on the complete DT process, cases that contained DT actions but did not have clear indications of timeline and process, and cases that were narrowly focused on the digital technology implementation. Initially, we selected six different cases but after further analysis and discussion of each case, we decided to drop three case studies because their case study data did not cover a significant portion of the transformation process or they were unclear regarding whether the organizations made a strategic shift or were more operationally focused. We finally arrived at six articles covering three cases—the Volvo case study (Svahn et al., 2015; Svahn et al., 2017), the Hummel case study (Hansen & Sia, 2015; Yeow et al., 2018), and the DB Vetreib case study (Fortmann et al., 2018; Fortmann et al., 2019). These three cases were appropriate for our theorizing because they contained rich case data and analysis of the DT process for each of the organizations. Further, at least one of the authors had observed or studied the process and published supplementary materials and case studies that helped strengthen our understanding of the cases’ DT processes. These supplementary materials and cases provided different perspectives or missing details from the primary cases.

We adopted process theorizing techniques (Langley, 1999, Kouame & Langley 2018) and qualitative data analysis methods (Miles et al., 2018) (See the Appendix for details of our iterative analysis and theorizing). Using our analysis, we developed the overarching concept of the “DT pathway.” A DT pathway is an organization’s ongoing DT journey. Given the combined effects and dynamics of DT and paradox processes, there are multiple possible DT pathways (Cloutier & Langley, 2020). We summarize the three organizations’ DT pathways below. In the following section, we develop specific propositions regarding how the pathways unfold, offering illustrative examples from each of the three cases.

## 3.1 Volvo

Volvo Cars—a multinational automobile company— previously experimented with connecting a car to external infrastructure in the early 2000s as part of their then focus on safety and after-sales maintenance. However, the costs were a significant barrier to launching specific services at the time. In 2010, Volvo initiated their “Connected Cars” initiative, this time with the view to offering their customers a different experience interacting with Volvo’s cars and thus potentially increasing their revenue streams. Volvo’s executive team implemented various DT actions (e.g., creating the Digital Hub) to execute this new initiative. These actions triggered performing and belonging tensions among the executives and Volvo’s management as the “Connected Cars” initiative was radically different from their then focus as an automaker. Volvo’s executive team responded to these paradoxical tensions by refining their strategy for “Connected Cars” and changing their BM and processes. While these responses did not completely remove the tensions, they were effective in mitigating their effects and enabling Volvo to achieve an evolved strategy that focused on external partners and changes such as a new development group in the R&D department. Building on these achievements, Volvo was able to engage in several external collaborations to develop and launch software apps and services for the “Connected Cars” initiative. However, these new DT actions triggered organizing tensions because they clashed with how Volvo had traditionally built and developed products and worked with partners. Management responded by significantly changing their resource configuration (i.e., forming a new dedicated aftermarket service app unit) to better scale and support the new initiative and refining their strategy to complement their physical car products. These actions enabled Volvo to successfully launch their “Connected Cars” apps and solutions in the 2014 consumer electronics show.

## 3.2 DB Vertrieb (DBV)

DBV is a subsidiary of a large passenger transportation group in Germany that provides mobility services. In 1999, it launched an online sales initiative and created a small online IT unit to support its nascent online sales unit. In 2016, due to increased competition from companies offering a single platform for customers’ different transportation needs, DBV shifted to an integrated omnichannel strategy to provide their customers with a more integrated travel experience to stay competitive. This required integrating the by-then sizable online IT unit with the traditional IT unit. There were, however, performing and belonging tensions between these units, as each had its own targets and culture. DBV management’s response was to reaffirm the omnichannel strategy as being intertwined with the company’s digitalization strategy. They also introduced structural (e.g., creating new Digital Division and SCRUM teams) and process changes. Through the new division and processes, DBV achieved its DT goals of aligning the IT units with its omnichannel strategy and having the different units operate together. However, the belonging tension continued as former members of the online and traditional IT units still did not identify with the new Digital Division. DBV’s attempt to organize and standardize the processes of the Digital Division to support the entire enterprise also triggered organizing tension. The adoption of Agile and SCRUM processes meant that many staff from the former traditional IT unit struggled to learn how to work in the new division, thus leading to learning tension. Besides causing a high turnover in the new division, this also led to much debate on the design and adoption of new processes. DBV responded by appointing a coordinator for the transformation and forming a transformation governance board to further align the strategy with their processes. They also implemented an intensive, systematic training program to enable their staff to learn, understand, and adopt the standardized processes (through the Scaled Agile Framework [SAFe] approach). As such, by 2019, DBV was able to sustain its focus on the omnichannel strategy, albeit with persistent belonging and organizing tensions among staff who struggle with their new roles and the new processes. DBV management’s assessment of their DT was that it was an ongoing endeavor as some of the expected outcomes were not yet visible.

## 3.3 Hummel

Hummel is an established B2B sportswear company based in Denmark. In 2010, its management hired new staff to head the exploration of Hummel’s B2C online presence. The new head began to work with existing functional groups to understand what capabilities would be required for Hummel’s B2C online presence. This B2C initiative quickly triggered performing and belonging tensions in Hummel. A big issue was that existing staff identified Hummel solely as a B2B company. Hummel management’s response was to refine their strategy so that its B2C strategy would not cannibalize existing B2B business and place the nascent digital unit under the Marketing Department. They considered the B2C online presence as a tool for consistent branding rather than for sales, and they created structures and processes to reinforce this. At the same time, they supported the new digital unit’s work to develop new processes and systems for the new B2C channel. These actions mitigated the tensions and enabled Hummel to achieve significant B2C capabilities (e.g., pilot e-commerce sites) and consistent online branding. However, the need for existing B2B employees to support the growing B2C work triggered organizing tensions. Further, in 2013, with the development of B2C capabilities, the growing digital unit pushed to grow B2C e-commerce as they demonstrated that there was potential for more B2C online sales. This caused performing and belonging tensions to resurface. Hummel’s management responded by reinforcing the noncannibalizing strategy for its B2C online presence and putting in place restrictions to limit the B2C e-commerce activities. As a result, the B2C online capabilities were not fully leveraged for B2C sales. Instead, the new additions to the B2C online platform were designed to support B2B partner sales.

## 4 DT Pathways

We propose the concept of DT pathways—the varied journeys experienced by organizations as they make strategic shifts, leveraging digital technology and evolving the organization’s BM. Below, we theorize the concept of “DT pathway” by drawing on prior ITOT, DT research, and paradox theory, and iterating them with the empirical data from the excellent longitudinal studies of three DT journeys.

## 4.1 DT Pathways Initiation—Strategic Shift, Actions, and Resultant Tensions

We considered first how DT begins across the three case studies. We noticed certain similarities but also some differences. The initiation phase can be characterized as uprooting (Besson & Rowe, 2012), as each of the three organizations noted the digital-related disruptions in the market and initiated strategic shifts.

For Volvo, it was noted that by 2008, mobile phones using Apple’s iOS or Google’s Android were a growing part of people’s lives. They therefore wanted to leverage digital technology to enhance customer experience with their cars and, in so doing, enhance their competitiveness in the increasingly digital environment. In 2010, Volvo made “Connected Cars” a strategic focus area. For DBV, it was noted that new competitors had arisen in their passenger transportation industry, providing competing services as well as interfacing with customers through digital mobility platforms. Digital technology was becoming heavily embedded in customers’ daily lives, and DBV wanted to stay competitive by offering a single platform for customers’ different transportation needs. Therefore, they decided to shift from a multichannel strategy to an integrated omnichannel strategy. For Hummel, a B2B sports fashion supplier that had been selling to large sports retail specialists for over 80 years, the growing presence of online retailers led Hummel to extend its B2B to online retailers while its traditional retailers were scrambling to create web storefronts. Hummel’s CEO noted the rise of B2C in their industry and in 2010 decided to begin building capabilities in B2C so that they could pursue an omnichannel strategy.

In all three cases, the rise of digital technology in customer lives and the emergence of offerings in the digital space by competitors led to the recognition that each company would need to shift its strategy and oftentimes change its value proposition in order to stay relevant to its customers.

Proposition 1: DT is initiated when the organization recognizes the digital disruption in their market and adopts a strategic shift through leveraging digital technology.

All three organizations recognized the disruptions in their competitive marketspace and signaled significant shifts in strategy. These shifts led to major changes in their organizational structures and processes, but there were differences with regard to scope and scale among the three organizations. For example, the creation of a DT unit (DTU) to drive the transformation is a common action (Baiyere et al., 2020; Dremel et al., 2017; Fortmann et al., 2019; Khanagha et al., 2014; Svahn et al., 2017; Yeow et al., 2018). However, we observe that the scope and scale of the DTU varied across the three cases. At Volvo, in order to kick start their “Connected Cars” vision, a DTU that was highly cross-functional, comprising representatives from many units across the existing business, was created. The DTU planned sessions that exposed its members to industry trends and scenarios and encouraged brainstorming. At DBV, the decision to pursue an omnichannel strategy resulted in the merging of their traditional and online IT unit, which was then embedded within a new Digital Division. The online IT department had previously been part of a mobile sales unit and, at this point, comprised over a hundred people. At Hummel, the strategic decision to pursue B2C began with the hire of an experienced individual as the head of Digital. This new head of Digital explored existing business functions to uncover whether and how existing capabilities might be leveraged for B2C. Subsequently, another digitally savvy staff member was hired and the small, nascent DTU reported to the head of Marketing.

Compared to Hummel, Volvo’s and DBV’s actions in this initiation phase were broader in scope and scale. Looking further back in the timeline, we note that for both Volvo and DBV, the initiation of DT—signaled by the strategic shifts—had evolutionary underpinnings. In the previous decade, Volvo had developed capabilities in telematics that were sufficient to demonstrate the technological and functional feasibility of connecting a car with external infrastructure. Cost factors prevented this from becoming an economically viable business proposition at that time. Similarly, DBV had created a small online IT unit over a decade ago and embedded it within the nascent online sales division. The online IT unit grew in size over the course of a decade in tandem with the growth in online sales. In contrast, Hummel had little prior capability development in digital technology as they had been focused on their B2B business, which had a traditional ERP as their main backbone for operations and sales. They had almost no experience and capabilities with regard to digital technology for consumers.

Proposition 2: Prior evolutionary development of digital capabilities enables the DT initiation phase to be broader in scope and scale.

As organizations embark on the significant changes associated with DT, they run into challenges. The nature of the challenges and the organizational responses to them shape how DT pathways unfold. Here, we found it constructive to draw on paradox theory. Paradox theory identifies change, plurality, and/or scarcity as triggers of paradoxical tensions. We observe that the initial DT actions associated with the strategic shift introduced change to the organizations and, together with the diverse views and agendas that are usually present in organizations, triggered paradoxical tensions, specifically performing and belonging tensions.

At Volvo, within the first few months, discussions among the cross-functional representatives triggered performing tensions between meeting current commitments and facing long-term uncertainties about the new direction. Belonging tensions between being an established automaker and potentially being in the software business were also triggered. At DBV, the shift to an omnichannel strategy and the associated merger of online and traditional IT units also triggered performing and belonging tensions. Performing tensions arose between those from the traditional IT unit (whose KPIs had been meeting service level agreements related to reliability, scalability, security, and costs for the extensive backend systems) and those from the online IT unit (whose focus was on agility and innovation for its customer-facing systems). Belonging tensions arose between the different values and norms: The traditional IT unit had a culture of cost-efficiency and associated norms of having only two releases a year and prioritizing requests. In contrast, the online IT unit had the practice of rapid responsiveness to requests, with norms of six releases a year. At Hummel, the new head of Digital’s exploratory discussions with the existing functional departments quickly triggered performing tensions, specifically regarding the potential cannibalization of their B2B business. There were also belonging tensions: since Hummel had been a B2B company for over a century, employees did not view themselves as part of a B2C company.

It is interesting that the DT initiation actions triggered performing and belonging tensions in all three cases, despite the variations in DT scope and scale and prior evolutionary capability building. This is likely because the change signaled by the DT strategic shift and actions surfaced tensions between the need to meet existing goals and the desire to fulfill new, emerging goals. With the plurality that exists in most organizations, the change also surfaced tensions between the organizations’ existing identity, values, and norms and the emergent identity, values, and norms associated with DT. ## 4.2 DT Pathways Development: Responses to Tensions

After DT is initiated, the focus moves to actions that increase the capability and capacity for DT (Pelletier & Raymond, 2020). This is also consistent with the construction phase identified in the ITOT literature (Besson & Rowe, 2012). Among the few recent studies recognizing the challenge of competing demands (Gregory et al., 2015; Svahn et al., 2017; Wimelius et al., 2021), a handful have examined the organization’s responses to such competing demands (or paradoxical tensions) and noted that these responses shape DT over time (Fuchs & Hess, 2018; Soh et al., 2019; Wessel et al., 2021; Wimelius et al., 2021). We further develop this idea of responses to tensions as being critical to shaping the way that DT pathways unfold.

We sought to characterize the responses theoretically from paradox theory while grounding them in the DT context. At Volvo, responses to performing and belonging tensions took the form of evolving the “Connected Cars” vision to strategically incorporate a focus on leveraging external partners and ecosystems. A series of workshops were set up in 2011 for Digital Hub members to discuss the application of open innovation, technological platforms, and two-sided markets to Volvo. These sessions helped members shift their attention from internal collaboration to possible opportunities for engaging with external stakeholders. They also reconfigured their BM in terms of structures and processes that were aligned to the growing external partnership orientation. For example, in 2012, they created the App Development Group to connect internal engineers with external partners for the design of platform applications.

At DBV, responses to tensions took the form of evolving their omnichannel strategy by intertwining it with their corporate digitalization strategy, thus moving away from the previous divide between IT strategy and online business strategy. To enable this integrative business-digital strategy, changes were made to structures and processes to align the BM with the new strategy. A new Digital Division was created and a merged traditional and online IT unit was placed within it. A chief digital officer was appointed who would be part of the company’s executive management team. Innovation labs were created to work with the business and the newly merged IT unit. Process changes in support of agility were made, such as the adoption of Agile and SCRUM for the entire new Digital Division, to support both physical and online channel processes.

At Hummel, strong performing and belonging tensions prompted the company to articulate the DT strategy as noncannibalizing to the B2B business. Changes to the BM were made in line with this noncannibalization strategy. For example, the decision to develop an inhouse B2C system rather than leverage an external platform was to increase the B2B business’s control over potential cannibalization from B2C sales. The functional processes for supporting B2C were also designed by adapting and leveraging B2B processes, where possible, through eight cross-functional project teams comprising members from the existing business.

It is noteworthy that in their attempts to mitigate performing and belonging tensions, all three organizations evolved their strategies and made changes to structures and processes to align the BM to the new strategy. This complements the findings from prior research (Agarwal et al., 2022; Al-Ali & Phaal, 2019; Chaniot, 2019; Pelletier & Raymond, 2020) suggesting that following initiation, firms often focus on capability-building actions such as building crossfunctional teams, co-creation, and prototyping. In addition, we maintain that beyond the execution of these capability-building actions, there is a need to iterate with and evolve strategy. Further, capabilitybuilding actions can also be enacted in a thoughtful manner in response to the tensions faced.

Proposition 4: Responses to the initial performing and belonging tensions involve the evolution of strategy and changes to the BM.

Theorizing further, we ask how and why these responses help to mitigate tensions and influence the unfolding of DT pathways. Here, we turn again to paradox theory, which explains that organizations will attempt to find a way to meet competing demands, either by accommodating both poles (bothand) or by choosing between poles (either-or) (Smith & Lewis, 2011). This leads us to characterize strategy and BM responses to tensions as both-and or eitheror. Paradox theory further argues that both-and responses foster virtuous cycles while either-or responses lead to vicious cycles. This brings us to the following proposition:

Proposition 5: The strategy and BM responses to initial tensions can be characterized as both-and or either-or and these responses will influence the direction and pace at which DT pathways unfold.

Paradox theory explains that both-and responses lead to virtuous cycles by encouraging the ability to dynamically shift the emphasis back and forth between the poles (Putnam et al., 2016), and enabling coordination and integration between the two poles (Andriopoulos & Lewis, 2009). Creativity, learning, and coordination are developed, which contribute to the virtuous cycle.

Volvo’s responses for both strategy and BM (evolving strategy, structure, and processes to incorporate external partnering) can be characterized as both-and. They enabled the existing business to continue to meet its performance requirements while Volvo pursued DT by leveraging external resources. Interestingly, the initial DTU (the Digital Hub) was dissolved and replaced by another DTU structure— the App Development Group—to better align with the evolved strategy’s external orientation. The App Development Group was placed within the existing R&D unit, which also increased integration with Volvo’s existing business. The shift toward the leveraging of external ecosystems and the creation of a focused, external-facing unit enabled Volvo’s DT to move more quickly. Volvo’s App Development Group eventually included 30 people and had its own budget and relative autonomy. The group enabled internal engineers to co-design new applications, such as Volvo’s pilot Cars’ Roam Delivery Service, with external partners.

Proposition 5a: When strategy and BM responses are both-and, initial performing and belonging tensions are mitigated and digital capabilities targeted at new businesses are developed.

In contrast, either-or responses seek to avoid or combat tensions (Lewis, 2018), often by turning inward (Sundaramurthy & Lewis, 2003) and clinging to one agenda or past behaviors in order to enable consistency between two poles (Smith, 2014; Smith & Lewis, 2011). This may offer temporary relief and even progress (Lewis, 2018) but nevertheless reinforces existing mindsets and embedded structures and processes (Smith & Lewis, 2011).

At Hummel, the decision to make DT noncannibalizing was an either-or response for both strategy and BM. At the strategic level, it subordinated the DT initiative to the needs of the existing business. For the BM, resources were reconfigured and developed and the digital capabilities were subordinated to the existing B2B business. For example, Hummel made the decision to develop an inhouse B2C system rather than leverage an external platform to increase the B2B business’s control over potential cannibalization from B2C sales. The functional processes for supporting B2C were also designed by adapting and leveraging B2B processes, where possible, through eight cross-functional project teams comprising members from the existing business. While this move was potentially integrative, it also kept the B2C initiative within the firm grip of the existing B2B business. The result was continued development of digital capabilities (e.g., in-house e-commerce system, new content management system, and global branding web- and social media sites). However, these capabilities were used primarily for brand building in support of the B2B business (e.g., global branding websites). B2C e-commerce was only rolled out as a pilot. These either-or responses favoring the existing business enabled the development of digital capabilities. However, since they were subordinated to the needs of the existing B2B business, the company did not address the issues of evolving its identity and the need to weave B2C into its identity. The teams involved in developing B2C online capabilities also struggled with performance for B2C, since they had to continually ensure that they were not perceived as undermining B2B performance.

Proposition 5b: When strategy and BM responses are either-or, favoring the existing business, performing and belonging tensions remain salient, and developed digital capabilities are subordinated to the existing business.

Interestingly, paradox theory does not theorize the resultant dynamics and outcomes in situations where there is a mix of both-and and either-or responses. Further, the implicit assumption is that the either-or responses tend to favor the established business. DBV provides an interesting case where the responses were a mix of both-and and either-or; significantly, the either-or responses were in favor of the new digital directions. DBV’s omnichannel strategy is both-and because it promoted the integration of the physical and online channels. However, the BM responses were dominantly either-or, favoring the digital directions—the merged IT unit was embedded within a new Digital Division, headed by a CDO who drove digital innovation throughout the business. The traditional IT unit was transformed by adopting Agile and SCRUM processes. Agility was emphasized, with the CIO noting that they were expected to be five times faster to market. This either-or approach in favor of digital business directions left performing and belonging tensions salient. The new methodologies contributed to insecurities and sensitivities among the IT employees in the newly merged unit. The internal turnover rate was initially high. ## 4.3 DT Pathways Divergence—DT Growth, Resultant Tensions, and Responses

The DT actions in response to tensions, with resultant changes in the structures, processes, and development of DT capabilities, contributed to the growth of DT in the organizations. In all three cases, we note that the growth of DT began to significantly impact the dayto-day operations of the business. Paradox theory leads us to expect that this will trigger new tensions. At Volvo, the growth in software apps and services led to organizing tensions between the digital business’s rapid innovation cycles, its functional integration across workgroups, and its collaborative approach with partners versus the existing physical car business’s long planning cycles, high workforce specialization, and efficiency-focused contracts with suppliers. In DBV, the combination of adopting Agile in the form of the SAFe approach, formalized SCRUM teams throughout the new Digital Division, and continual provision of reliability and scalability to the business led to organizing tensions among former online IT staff members who struggled with reduced autonomy in the new division. Learning tensions were also triggered for former traditional IT staff members who struggled with the new process methods. In Hummel, the volume of B2C-related work grew. As logistics, sales, finance, and other functional support were provided by existing B2B units, organizing tensions surfaced because the B2B staff struggled to support both B2B and B2C operations.

Proposition 6: The growth of DT will increasingly impact operations and these changes, together with scarcity, will trigger organizing tensions and, in some cases, learning tensions.

Again, paradox theory suggests that an organization’s response (both-and or either-or) to this second wave of tensions will contribute to virtuous or vicious cycles (Putnam et al., 2016; Schad et al., 2016; Smith & Lewis, 2011). Given that the new tension arising at this point in the DT pathways is the organizing tension, we propose that:

Proposition 7: In addressing the subsequent wave(s) of tensions, DT pathway divergence occurs as the DT direction and pace differ, depending on whether the strategy and BM responses are bothand or either-or.

At Volvo, the response to the organizing tension for both strategy and BM was again both-and. The “Connected Cars” strategy continued to evolve with the articulation of the growing volume of apps and services arising from external collaborations as providing an aftermarket service. This is a both-and response that positions the growing business from DT as a complement to the physical car sales. Both-and changes to the BM mitigated the tensions between the different cycle lengths of the digital and existing businesses through the adoption of cloud technology to manage their operations. Further, tensions between existing and newer collaborative relationships were mitigated by hiring a new lawyer who provided a new template contract for the collaborative partnerships. The DTU shifted again as a new unit was created within the IT unit to ensure the scalability of aftermarket services. With these responses, DT at Volvo followed a growth trajectory with ongoing collaboration, a new aftermarket service application unit, as well as the launch of several “Connected Cars” apps and solutions within a short period of time.

Proposition 7a: When strategy and BM responses are both-and, the organizing tensions are mitigated. Digital capabilities continue to evolve rapidly and DT growth continues with significant momentum.

At Hummel, in addition to the organizing tension that arose due to the expanding scope of DT, performing and belonging tensions remained salient as they were not mitigated by the earlier either-or responses for strategy and BM. Further, encouraged by the growing set of digital capabilities developed, the DTU sought to leverage these capabilities by pushing for the growth of B2C e-commerce. This proposed change made the performing tensions even stronger as those in B2B sales predicted dire consequences if they angered their B2B partners with the B2C rollout. The responses to these performing, belonging, and organizing tensions were again either-or: the noncannibalization strategy was reaffirmed. The BM was also more strongly aligned with the noncannibalization strategy through restrictions on the customer base for B2C e-commerce as well as the products/services offered. New system add-ons to the B2C in-house platform were also positioned to support B2B partner sales. DT at Hummel stalled and the DTU head resigned.

Proposition 7b: When strategy and BM responses continue to be either-or, then performing, belonging, and organizing tensions remain salient; digital capabilities developed are not fully leveraged, and digital transformation stalls.

As noted in the theorizing leading to Proposition 5c, paradox theory is less clear about the influence of mixed responses of both-and and either-or. At DBV, belonging tensions were still salient due to the earlier mix of both-and strategy responses and either-or BM responses, even though the latter favored the new digital directions. As the pathway continued with the growing footprint of DT and the associated rise of organizing tensions, DBV’s strategy continued to be both-and (i.e., the omnichannel strategy was maintained without significant evolution), while actions to evolve the BM were a mix of both-and and either-or. Both-and structural changes were made to address the organizing tension, with better prioritization and coordination through the creation of transformation governance board and a transformation coordinator. The process changes were either-or, favoring DT. These included formalizing the SCRUM process as well as mandatory, systematic training to ensure that their staff understood how to better use the SAFe approach. The transformation governance board and coordinator helped mitigate some of the process issues by refining the way processes could be applied to specific conditions. Training on the new methodologies helped reduce the gaps in understanding, skills, and mindsets among staff members, which reduced some of the learning tensions. While DBV found that the mandatory training helped, discussions about how to adopt the SAFe approach were still being conducted. They also reported that it required significantly more time and investment to break down old methods of working and for the staff to fully internalize and adopt the SCRUM processes and SAFe approach. Specifically, SCRUM processes led to lengthy decision cycles and ongoing difficulties for the combined units. As a result, some staff members had problems delivering solutions based on the new requirements. The result was that DT moved at a slower pace and management was still waiting for the results of their DT at the end of the period covered by the study.

Proposition 7c: When strategy and BM responses are a mix of both-and and either-or—with either-or favoring the new business—organizing, learning, belonging, and/or performing tensions remain salient and digital capabilities continue to evolve and be leveraged at a slower pace.

## 5 Implications for Research and Practice

How and why DT moves from phase to phase is less understood because the construction and optimization/routinization phases of DT (and ITOT) tend to be understudied (Besson & Rowe, 2012). Further, each organization’s DT journey is likely to be different, resulting in varying DT outcomes (Davenport and Westerman, 2018; Westerman and Bonnet, 2021). We therefore introduce the concept of “DT pathways” to provide a way to systematically recognize the different challenges each DT journey faces and identify how and why DT pathways unfold in various directions and pacing over time.

## 5.1 A Conceptualization of DT Pathways

Figure 4 presents the overall conceptual DT pathways model developed in the preceding sections. With regard to how and why DT pathways begin, we note that the DT journey is initiated when the organization recognizes the digital disruptions in its markets. This prompts the organization to propose strategic shifts, often with the intent of offering customers a different value proposition. This strategic shift signals the intent to uproot and transform the organization (Besson & Rowe, 2012). Our model proposes an interesting interplay with evolutionary development, even in this uprooting phase. The cases we analyzed suggest that the presence of digital capabilities, previously developed gradually over time, enables broader scope and scale during the DT initiation and uprooting phase. Further, in this uprooting phase, belonging and performing tensions are likely to become salient as the strategic shift and accompanying DT actions raise issues of different identities and goals.

As the organization moves into the DT construction phase, there are significant organizational changes to structures and processes to enable the growth in DT (Besson and Rowe, 2012, Vial, 2019; Warner & Wäger, 2018). The changes in structures and processes and the accompanying growth often trigger organizing tensions, which arise from the competing demands of structures and processes that are aligned with the existing business versus those aligned with the new digitally enabled business. A second major contribution of the model is that it suggests when certain tensions are more likely to surface (i.e., belonging and performing during the uprooting phase and organizing in the construction phase).

Ideally, beyond the construction phase, DT would move into the routinization and optimization phase (Besson and Rowe, 2012). However, our theorizing of the DT pathways model suggests that the nature of the organizational responses to the tensions will influence the direction and pace of DT in terms of optimization and routinization. Paradox theory primarily suggests two types of responses—both-and and either-or. We further theorized that in the context of DT, both-and and either-or responses are enacted through changes to strategy and changes to the BM. Table 1 summarizes the propositions that explain how and why the DT pathways unfold. Propositions 5a-c, and 7a-c in particular provide propositions regarding the variations arising from different types of responses to the tensions. In the next section, we discuss the variations in pathways arising from different responses to tensions.

## 5.2 Multiple DT Pathways

When we examined the three cases, we found that two of them—Volvo and Hummel—had consistent bothand or either-or responses for strategy and BM changes, respectively. However, we also surfaced a third type of response when we examined the DBV case—that of a mixed response of both-and strategy and either-or BM change. Further, the either-or response favored the new DT directions, whereas paradox theory implicitly assumes that either-or responses reinforce the existing arrangements.

Below, we use the three case examples to illustrate the DT pathways that might unfold (see Figures 5a-c). Volvo’s DT pathway is consistent with what paradox theory terms a virtuous cycle. The DT strategic shift is associated with DT actions that are broad in scope and scale and enabled by the prior evolutionary development of relevant digital capabilities. Performing, belonging, and organizing tensions inevitably arise and, in each phase, the organization enacts both-and responses in strategy and BM. These multiple rounds of responses lead to the rapid development of new capabilities aligned with DT and the growth of new business opportunities. In this virtuous cycle pathway, if bothand responses manage to address tensions, the organization can move into the routinization and optimization phase (Besson and Rowe 2012). For example, new processes and legal templates for the DTrelated collaborative partnerships became routinized while the adoption of cloud technologies to deal with the growing volume became part of the optimization around the new and growing digitally enabled business.

Paradox theory also sensitizes us to the fact that even when the organization has moved to the routinization and optimization phase, tensions can recur (Tsoukas & Cunha, 2017). Environmental conditions may shift, thereby creating new tensions and destabilizing the current equilibrium. For example, in a recent study on the DT process of a telehealth system comprising an acute hospital and multiple nursing homes, Agarwal et al. (2022) noted that as the system was becoming routinized, the nursing homes suddenly faced new mandatory regulations from the country’s health regulator. This shift in the external environment introduced new performance goals that stretched the nursing homes, causing performing and organizing tensions to resurface. In this case, the telehealth system shifted quickly into another period of reconstruction, with both-and responses that helped nursing homes meet the new requirements by leveraging the telehealth system’s capabilities. Hence, given the increasingly dynamic nature of the environment, organizations will need to be ever-vigilant to the resurfacing of tensions and take appropriate steps to address them even in virtuous DT pathways.

Hummel’s DT pathway displays some of the dynamics of paradox theory’s vicious cycle. As established, the initial strategic shift and associated DT actions trigger performing and belonging tensions. However, the organization’s response is primarily either-or, favoring the existing business. As such, the digital capabilities developed are subordinated to the existing business and the tensions continue. As parts of the organization seek to leverage newly developed digital capabilities, tensions continue to escalate and responses continue to be either-or in favor of the existing business. Some DTrelated processes may survive and be routinized (e.g., Hummel’s new in-house e-commerce and content management system; also, web and social media sites continued operating specifically to support brandbuilding). However, because Hummel’s DT stalled, the DT pathway did not move significantly beyond the construction phase to optimization and integration.

DBV’s DT pathway is perhaps the most interesting case, as it is neither fully virtuous nor vicious. While it began similarly to the other two cases—a strategic shift, initial DT actions, and resultant performing and belonging tensions—its responses were a mix of bothand strategic and either-or BM changes. Furthermore, contrary to the implicit tenets of paradox theory, the either-or responses favored new directions rather than the existing business. We note that the tensions were somewhat mitigated but still salient. The organization continued to grapple with the routinization and optimization of new structures and processes. While there was some routinization of certain DT-related structures and processes (e.g., the formalization of SCRUM and SAFe and the governance board, as well as the merging of traditional and new digital business IT units), the organization was still working on its optimization efforts. Notably, DBV’s staff had not fully internalized the new approaches, and in some instances, the combined units took a longer time to make final decisions. The DT pathway continued to evolve, albeit at a slower pace, as the organization sought to further routinize and optimize the changes. In the following section, we further discuss the two pathways that go beyond the arguments of paradox theory—one arising from mixed responses and the other arising from either-or responses in favor of DT.

## 5.3 DT’s Third Paradoxical Dynamic: The Moderated Virtuous Cycle

Beyond contributing to our understanding of DT through the concept of pathways, the theorizing also contributes to paradox theory. Paradox theorists have noted that the understanding of the dynamics of paradox—how they play out as organizations respond to them—needs to be deepened (Cuganesan, 2017; Gilbert et al., 2018; Jarzabkowski et al., 2013; Smith & Lewis, 2011). Smith and Lewis’s (2011) articulation of virtuous and vicious cycles represents an important step forward in this. However, we found the core arguments, of bothand responses leading to virtuous cycles and either-or responses leading to vicious cycles, inadequate for explaining DBV’s case, which had both-and (strategy) as well as either-or (BM) responses. DBV’s pathway was neither fully virtuous nor fully vicious; it was one where DT moved forward but at a more gradual pace, with tensions continuing to be salient. We call this the “moderated virtuous” cycle, a third dynamic to paradox theory’s virtuous and vicious cycles.

We also note that DBV’s either-or responses favored new directions, whereas paradox theory tends to assume that either-or responses favor the existing business due to inertia. DBV chose to emphasize new digital directions by reconfiguring internal resources, subsuming the existing IT unit within a new Digital Division, and mandating the adoption of Agile and other processes to align with the digital pole, thus continuing tensions from the existing business pole.

The above observation of the mix of both-and and either-or responses, with either-or responses favoring DT, raises the question of how DT pathways may unfold when the approach is not mixed but rather is dominated by either-or responses in favor of DT for both strategy and BM. Paradox theory would argue that dominantly either-or responses would exacerbate tensions and derail changes. However, for DT, there may be certain conditions under which the organization may have no choice but to enact largely either-or responses. For example, if the organization’s existing business is becoming obsolete very quickly and the existing organizational capabilities are of relatively little value to the new digitally enabled business, the organization may find it best to “bite the bullet” and adopt a dominantly either-or approach, emphasizing the new DT-enabled business in both strategy and BM. The emphasis on one pole to the exclusion of the other will likely exacerbate tensions, and the organization will thus likely experience high turnover and the associated loss of some existing capabilities. The organization will need to weather the storm and go through a period of heightened risk as it experiences increased tensions and turmoil. However, if the organization is in a situation where it has little choice, given the rapidity with which its existing business is declining, then this tough approach may be required. Further research on cases that are dominantly either-or in favor of DT is needed in order to better understand the dynamics of tensions and responses in influencing the unfolding of this DT pathway under such conditions.

## 5.4 Implications for Practice

Organizations that proactively and routinely scan their environments are more likely to pick up on early signals of trends in digital technology. Such organizations have likely evolved their capabilities, through, for example, bottom-up initiatives that place the organization in a better position to experience the virtuous cycle of making the strategic shift to digitally enabled business through thoughtful both-and responses that can address the inevitable tensions that arise in the course of DT. Organizations that have not done this may have to make the shift to digital with little prior preparation; they may thus experience a vicious cycle through reactive either-or responses that favor the existing business, which results in a stalled DT. Alternatively, out of necessity, organizations may also take an either-or approach in favor of DT; this can lead to a successful shift to digital business but is also likely to result in a significant period of heightened tensions and tumult for the organization.

We suspect that the moderated virtuous DT pathway is a more typical pathway for most organizations than virtuous or vicious DT pathways. Even in organizations where the management team is able to articulate a bothand strategy, the shift usually requires major changes to the organization’s resource configuration, which may result in significant challenges for the organization’s BM. The actions taken to change the BM may emphasize new digital directions.

Assuming that organizations are able to pursue virtuous or moderated virtuous DT pathways, we highlight some of the ways in which organizational leaders can promote both-and responses, as the both-and mindset is associated with greater creativity, the avoidance of groupthink, and the narrowing of competencies in the long run. First, organizational leaders can seek to develop an overarching objective/strong vision that forms “a higher plane of understanding” (Bednarek et al., 2017, p.79). We see this in Volvo’s “Connected Cars” and DBV’s omnichannel strategic intent, which helped to keep the DT on course while a myriad of BM changes were being made. Leaders could also intentionally bring different groups of people together to consider the tensions from their various perspectives (Huq et al., 2017). Coupled with a higher-level integrative vision, this tends to generate responses that address the demands of both poles (e.g., DT and the existing business).

Second, leaders need to help individuals within the organization accept the both-and mindset and approach. Paradox theory notes that organizational employees are more likely to accept both-and approaches when they see evidence of others successfully embodying conflicting poles and/or managers putting self-interest aside for the overarching organizational objective (Bednarek et al., 2017). For example, a CEO who is succeeding in the established business but is nonetheless supportive of and keen to learn and adapt to DT gives employees confidence about the change. Leaders can also signal the value of both poles as the organization moves forward. Hargrave and Van de Ven (2017, (p. 330) note that “proponents of both elements accept the persistent coexistence of opposite elements because they have determined that conflict will be fruitless,” and thus are more open to both-and responses. Together these actions undertaken by leaders can help persuade managers whose interests are strongly aligned with the existing business to change their perceptions of DT as a threat and embrace the both-and mindset.

Third, we highlight an interesting practical both-and response implemented at Volvo. Our analysis suggests that Volvo’s decision to draw significantly on external partnerships eased the need to make substantial changes to their internal resource configurations. This approach can also be seen in their decision to use cloud technology to buffer their new digital needs, with the systems supporting the existing business. While we believe this is a useful addition to the repertoire of both-and responses, the extent to which organizations should rely on external resources as they reconfigure their BM is not a straightforward decision. This would depend on many factors, including the availability of appropriate partners and organizations’ views on the extent of internal digital capabilities needed.

## 6 Limitations and Further Research

This paper aims to theorize and account for why some organizations experience relatively smooth DT processes while others flounder. This paper introduces the concept of DT pathways, which presents an emergent view of organizations’ DT journeys. Specifically, we explain how emergent tensions and organizational responses to tensions are critical in shaping the way DT pathways unfold, especially in terms of how and why the direction and pace of DT vary from phase to phase. Drawing on secondary empirical data, we identify three plausible DT pathways that reflect the case data. However, we believe that there are likely other combinations of responses that may lead to different pathways beyond the three identified here. A limitation of this study is that we were only able to identify and analyze three rich DT journeys.

We believe the DT pathways model provides a new conceptual lens for describing, visualizing, and analyzing other organizations’ DT journeys—in particular, their initiation, the major tensions, and the different responses that shape their DT processes. One area for further research would be the question of how variations in the initiation of DT in terms of the extent of top-down vs. bottom-up initiatives influence the DT pathway. For example, in their study of two companies, Fuch and Hess (2018) found that bottomup initiatives failed to scale while Chanias and Hess (2016) found that bottom-up initiatives shaped DT strategy. Chanias et al. (2018) observed one company using both top-down and bottom-up processes in parallel with initial DT strategizing. Longitudinal empirical research using the DT pathways model could build on these different findings to further enrich our understanding of how DT pathways begin and unfold. As more DT pathways under different conditions are surfaced and studied, more recommendations grounded and tailored to different contexts can be developed, thus enriching our collective knowledge for managing the DT process.

## Appendix: Developing the DT Pathways Model

After we had selected the three rich case studies, we conducted our analysis using established process techniques (Langley, 1999, Kouame & Langley 2018) and qualitative data analysis methods (Miles et al., 2018). The analytical process was recursive and iterative between theorizing, analysis, and reflection. However, in order to provide clarity to the process, we present our analysis in the following sequential steps.

Step 1: Collection and development of chronological cases. We used the main case articles as well as supplemental materials to develop a chronological case for each company (Volvo, Hummel, and DBV). This provided a chronological narrative of the events in each company’s DT process. We were careful to include key changes and challenges experienced during the process as it unfolded. For example, in Volvo’s case, we used supplemental materials (master’s theses of collaboration between Volvo and universities) to trace the “Connected Cars” initiative in 2010 back to its early experimental efforts in connecting cars to external infrastructure in the early 2000s. In DBV’s case, we used different case studies to trace their 2016 omnichannel strategy to its online sales initiative in 1999 while in Hummel’s case, we drew on published cases and case data to trace its B2C online exploration in 2010 to its final e commerce activity in 2013.

Step 2: Analysis of each company’s digital transformation actions by phases. We iterated between each company’s chronological cases and the existing literature on ITOT and DT. We began our analysis of case data by applying temporal bracketing to identify the three different DT phases for each company, using ITOT and DT literature’s insight on the three phases of transformation—initiation/uprooting, construction, and routinization/optimization (Besson & Rowe, 2012)

Next, we analyzed the specific DT-related actions each company undertook within each phase and how these actions relate to the conditions in the organizational and industry contexts. This involved several iterations between existing theory and case data as we developed a deeper understanding of DT actions in the different phases. For example, in the initiation phase, we noted how each company reacted to the digital disruptions occurring in their industry by changing their strategy, followed by adopting specific DT actions. The observed shift in strategy was also consistent with prior research in DT. As we further reflected on the set of initial DT actions across the three cases, we noted that the three companies’ DT actions differed in scope and scale. As we pondered why, we traced further back to each company’s prior digital capabilities, and this led us to formulate Proposition 2, regarding the prior evolution of digital capabilities and the scale and scope of DT actions in the initiation and uprooting phase.

Step 3: Analysis of each company’s paradoxical tensions and responses. We then moved on to consider the challenges that companies face once they have initiated DT. Here, we found paradox theory helpful, as it provided a way to identify fundamental tensions and perspective on the types of responses, as well as their likely influence on the trajectory of each company’s DT. We examined each company’s challenges, identifying those that arose from competing demands of inherent opposing poles (i.e., paradoxical tensions). For example, we identified that Hummel experienced significant tensions between their sales team and the new digital unit; we realized that this was a form of belonging tension between the opposing existing identity of Hummel as a well-established B2B company and the new emerging B2C identity. This close analysis was similarly applied to the tensions in DBV and Volvo’s DT process. We continued to identify the paradoxical tensions for each company in the subsequent phases.

For each company, we then identified the triggers for the tensions as well as the actions taken to address the tensions and move DT forward. We developed a visual mapping strategy to lay out the dynamics. Using each paradoxical tension (e.g., belonging) as the visual anchor, we first connected the tensions backward to the triggers offered by paradox theory (e.g., change and plurality) and the DT actions that created these triggers. We then connected the tensions to the different types of responses and outcomes. We characterized the responses in two different ways by using paradox theory’s conceptualization of both-and / either-or and DT concepts of strategy or BM change.

Step 4: Iterative analysis of visual maps with theory and development of DT pathway model. Once we had the phases and visual maps that captured both the DT actions and paradoxical dynamics (triggers, tensions, responses, and outcomes) for each company, we began to compare within-case (i.e., across the phases) and across-case (i.e., across the three companies’ overall DT processes). This set of analyses revealed that all three companies experienced belonging and performing tensions in the initiation phase and organizing and learning tensions in the constructing phase. They faced similar tensions given the same triggers encountered during the DT process (i.e., change and plurality created by their initial DT actions and change and scarcity as they attempted to grow their DT within their organization). These insights gave rise to our Propositions 3 and 6.

This set of analyses also revealed differences across the three companies with regard to the types of responses to the paradoxical tensions and how those responses shaped the tensions—i.e., the tensions were mitigated or remain salient. This became the crucial set of insights that helped us develop the overarching concept of “DT pathways” as a way to theorize different organizational DT journeys. In our three cases, we found that both-and responses—which are DT actions—helped mitigate the tensions while those that were either-or in favor of the existing status quo did not. Whether the tensions were mitigated or not directly shaped the “pathway” of the company’s DT experience. For example, Volvo’s pathway proved to be consistent with paradox theory as the company was able to mitigate the various paradoxical tensions it encountered by consistently adopting both-and responses to achieve strong DT growth (Propositions 5a and 7a) while Hummel’s pathway revealed a stalled DT caused by its adoption of either-or responses in support of the existing business (Propositions 5b and 7b).

Our detailed DT analyses also identified a mix of both-and strategy and either-or BM changes that did not fit cleanly into paradox theory. In DBV’s case, we found DT progress continued at a slower pace due to its mixed both-and and either-or approach (Propositions 5c and 7c). Thus, we noted that the three different DT experiences emerged due to the combined effects and dynamics of DT and paradox processes (Cloutier & Langley, 2020) and we believe that one could chart other DT pathways and experiences through the combination of responses for different aspects of DT at different phases.
