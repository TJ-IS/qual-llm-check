# Advice Utilization in Combined Human-Algorithm Decision-Making: An Analysis of Preferences and Behaviors

Panda Kumar Sachin,<sup>1</sup> Aaron Schecter<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Universität Bern, Switzerland, panda.sachin@unibe.ch <sup>2</sup>University of Georgia, USA, aschecter@uga.edu

## Abstract

As artificial intelligence (AI) becomes more pervasive, humans will interact with autonomous agents more frequently and in deeper ways. While there is a significant body of work addressing the interface between a single human and a single AI agent, less is known about how individuals react to AI when they are part of human-agent hybrids, namely multiple humans and potentially multiple AI. These hybrid forms are unique in that advice is often given simultaneously, i.e., a human decision maker evaluates advice from other humans and algorithms at the same time. This scenario presents a boundary condition on the extant literature, as it is unclear how a human decision maker will differentially appraise a human advisor compared to an algorithmic advisor when their advice is simultaneous. This study presents the results of three experiments asking individuals to estimate property rental prices with the support of both human and algorithmic advice. We tested whether explicitly labeling an advisor as an algorithm rather than a human impacts how individuals perceive both the algorithm and another human advisor. We also examined the role of conflicting advice during simultaneous evaluation. Based on the results of 904 participants, we found that labeling an advisor as an algorithm resulted in a significantly significant algorithmic appreciation bias, even when an equivalent human was present. Further, we found that uncertainty induced by conflicting information weakened the appreciation effect, while agreement among advisors resulted in the strongest behavioral responses.

Keywords: Decision Making, Algorithmic Advice, Experiments, Human-Computer Interaction

## 1 Introduction

Algorithms are rapidly encroaching into the space of human decision-making across a number of industries (Brynjolfsson & Mitchell, 2017; Brynjolfsson & McAfee, 2017). Organizations are expending significant capital in building artificial intelligence (AI) capabilities, and, as a result, decision makers today are frequently faced with scenarios that involve the use of algorithmic decision aids. The investment in algorithmic capabilities carries both potential risks and rewards. On the one hand, algorithmic decisions are based on large training datasets, use objective criteria, and provide reproducible results (Brynjolfsson & Mitchell, 2017). On the other hand, there are risks that humans will become overly reliant on algorithmic predictions, leading to complacency and biased decisions (Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010; Wickens et al., 2015) or the erosion of unique and tacit human knowledge (Fügener et al., 2021). Consequently, as algorithmic decision aids become more embedded in work processes and routines, there is a growing need for information systems researchers to examine the dynamics of this hybrid decision-making system (Berente et al., 2021; Lyytinen et al., 2021; Baird & Maruping, 2021).

A limitation of research in this space is that extant studies tend to adopt the same decision-making paradigm: a human decision maker supported by a single algorithmic advisor or a human decision maker opting to receive advice from a single source (c.f. Dietvorst et al., 2015; Logg et al., 2019; You et al., 2022). Much of the empirical work on this topic treats algorithmic and human advice as strict alternatives (i.e., the advisor is an algorithm or human) or the decision to use AI advice as a binary choice (Logg et al., 2019). However, in practice, many organizations are deploying human-AI hybrids to accomplish complex tasks (Lyytinen et al., 2021; Rai et al., 2019; Shrestha et al., 2019; Tarafdar et al., 2022), and these teams potentially comprise multiple human and algorithmic actors. Such hybrids introduce an added layer of complexity, and it is not clear if the findings of extant research extend to such scenarios. Hence, this boundary condition imposed by emerging organizational realities necessitates a shift in the paradigm of human-AI collaboration studies, moving from a single decision maker and a single advisor to scenarios involving multiple decision makers and advisors. Further, because such situations inherently involve more uncertainty and are cognitively challenging (You et al, 2022), humans may exhibit different behaviors and preferences than those noted by prior research (Dietvorst & Bharti, 2020).

In this paper, we focus on one key differentiating element of human-AI hybrids, specifically the simultaneity of advice from human and AI sources. The simultaneous provisioning of advice introduces new considerations for the focal decision maker, including the management of information overload and the reduction of uncertainty regarding decisions (Lipshitz & Strauss, 1997). One particular challenge for decision makers is resolving conflicting advice, i.e., when the sources of advice disagree (Larrick & Soll, 2006; Yaniv & Milyavsky, 2007). In these cases, we posit that algorithmic appreciation or aversion alone is not enough to explain human decisions. Rather, these behavioral tendencies are fluid and can be amplified or attenuated by an individual’s attempts to reduce uncertainty. We further posit that the focal decision maker’s perceptions of their advisors will be impacted both by the type of advisor (human or algorithm) as well as the nature of the information provided and whether the advice is convergent or divergent. To summarize, we propose the following two research questions:

RQ1: Do decision makers exhibit greater or weaker algorithmic appreciation when algorithmic advice is presented simultaneously with another human’s advice?

RQ2: What role does agreement or disagreement among advisors play in the relationship between the simultaneity of advice and algorithmic appreciation?

We conducted a series of three experiments to operationalize our theoretical motivation of advicesimultaneity in human-algorithmic hybrids. In the first study, we manipulated the simultaneity construct only to compare the effects of a single advisor to the effects of two advisors simultaneously offering advice. In the second and third experiments, we manipulated both the simultaneity of advice and the degree of agreement or disagreement between the human and AI advisors. By changing the configuration of who agrees with whom, we tested how the uncertainty of the scenario affects algorithmic aversion/appreciation.

While our findings suggest a general tendency towards algorithmic appreciation, i.e., that decision makers will react more strongly to advice from algorithms and will report greater preference for an algorithmic advisor, this effect is significantly weaker when a human is simultaneously providing advice. We also found that the configuration of advice—i.e., whether advisors are in agreement or conflict—plays a meaningful role in how decision makers balance external advice. Overall, our findings support the central thesis that the simultaneous evaluation of human and algorithmic advice adds a new layer of complexity to decisionmaking and changes the extent to which humans utilize inputs from nonhuman actors in their judgments. This contributes to the prior literature by highlighting the fluidity involved in decision-making by humanalgorithmic hybrids.

## 2 Literature Review

## 2.1 Augmented Decision-Making and the Role of Algorithms

Making good decisions is a fundamentally difficult task. Decision makers often have to sift through multiple alternative solutions and evaluate their relative advantages. In other cases, alternative solutions may not even be present a priori, and the decision maker has to explore the space of viable options during the process of problem solving (Fleming, 2001; Shore et al., 2015). As such, individuals often take advice when making decisions. An important lens for viewing advice giving/taking is the judge-advisor system (Sniezek & Buckley, 1995), where judges are decision makers who seek external input and advisors provide recommendations to the judges. Advice can take multiple forms, such as a potential solution to a problem or information that helps in problem solving. Advice taking is a vital component of good decision-making (Bonaccio & Dalal, 2006) and generally makes decisions more accurate.

We are rapidly entering a world where nonhuman sources (algorithms) act as advisors for human decision makers. Using algorithms to augment human decisions is not new; statistical models have been used as early as the 17th century in actuarial businesses. What is new, however, is the scale at which algorithms have become ingrained in the decision-making landscape. Perhaps the most familiar example is the use of recommendation agents (RAs), which many individuals rely on to help them make purchase decisions (Komiak & Benbasat, 2008; Benbasat & Wang, 2005; Xiao & Benbasat, 2007). These agents provide personalized advice to help consumers manage information overload and reduce uncertainty. However, while these agents do provide decision support, they are only one type of advice source. For instance, other agents provide advice that is not tailored to the focal individual, such as estimates of home prices or stock prices (Ӧnkal et al., 2009).

With recent advancements in computational techniques and a preponderance of digital trace data, algorithms can now also mimic several aspects of human decision-making and are therefore being rapidly deployed by organizations for various tasks. For example, chatbots act as a first layer of customer assistance for many online retailers (Luo et al., 2019). Algorithms can also provide feedback and coaching to sales agents (Luo et al., 2021), deliver medical diagnoses (Buchlak et al., 2019), and determine the veracity and appropriateness of content on social media platforms (Roberts, 2019). During the COVID-19 pandemic, algorithmic reconstructions of the world were even widely used to guide policymakers’ decisions on mask wearing and social distancing (Leonardi et al., 2021). In sum, algorithmic advice taking is now a ubiquitous phenomenon with a wide range of applications, though how individuals perceive the recommending agent (e.g., Xiao & Benbasat, 2007) and how they make use of the advice is an ongoing question.

## 2.2 Human Utilization of Algorithmic Advice: Aversion and Appreciation

The integration of algorithmic sources across all levels of organizational decision-making has spurred lively discussions on how humans incorporate algorithmic advice into overall judgments, with two perspectives emerging. One set of scholars maintains that, despite statistical models’ long-standing superiority over humans in analytical tasks such as forecasting or prediction (Dawes, 1979; Meehl, 1954), humans exhibit aversion to AI advice (Dietvorst et al., 2015, 2018; Dietvorst & Bharti, 2020). Indeed, as a recent review of algorithmic utilization research shows, humans frequently discount evidence-based algorithmic judgments in favor of human intuitions (Burton et al., 2020). Essentially, there are some tasks and circumstances where humans are uncomfortable delegating decisions to an algorithm. A famous case of such aversion is the public criticism leveled against the now-defunct Bowl Championship Series (BCS) system <sup>1</sup> which utilized algorithms to determine the finalists of American college football. Several experimental results and case studies also indicate that individuals tend to have a lack of trust or aversion towards algorithmic predictions in certain circumstances (see Burton et al., 2020 for a review).

Several reasons have been proposed as to why this might be. Einhorn (1986) suggested that human judges have an inherently flawed understanding of the nature of error. In predictive exercises they strive for perfection, impractical as it may be to achieve. Dietvorst et al. (2015) used this line of reasoning to argue that humans will penalize algorithms when they see them make mistakes. Algorithms, like other advisors, will inevitably err in their predictive accuracy, and, when humans see them err, they lose confidence in their ability. The authors found consistent support for this argument. Other explanations have also been proposed for algorithmic aversion, such as task type (Castelo et al., 2019), the algorithm’s opacity (Dietvorst et al., 2015), the lack of control over final decisions (Dietvorst et al., 2018), and the context of the decision task (Castelo et al., 2021; Dietvorst & Bharti, 2020). For a deeper discussion on this topic, please refer to Burton et al.’s (2020) systematic review of the aversion literature.

Contrarily, other research suggests that human decision makers may appreciate algorithmic advice due to their increased faith in the predictive capabilities of cutting-edge models, particularly in the realm of quantitative tasks (You et al., 2022). In the context of expert systems, Dijkstra (1999) showed that humans tend to overrely on algorithmic advice, to the extent of utilizing the advice without any consideration of its accuracy. Recent studies have also challenged the notion that humans are averse to advice from algorithms. Logg et al. (2019) noted in their experiments that, across a variety of task contexts, individuals display robust algorithmic appreciation. They argued that individuals’ dismissal of algorithmic advice in favor of their own could simply be an indicator of their general egocentricity and not of algorithmic aversion (Krueger, 2003; Soll & Mannes, 2011; Yaniv & Kleinberger, 2000). When comparing advice from other humans and algorithms, human judges have been shown to exhibit a robust preference for the latter (Logg et al., 2019). Across a variety of prediction tasks, spanning from purely objective to purely subjective (Bogert et al., 2021, 2022; Logg et al., 2019), studies have shown that human decision makers tend to rely more heavily on algorithmic predictions than on human predictions.

While this nascent research stream has enriched our understanding of how humans utilize algorithmic predictions, the conflicting findings above hint at the fact that the real motor underlying people’s advice utilization behavior is more complex and nuanced. Unlike prior IT artifacts such as rule-based decision support systems or forecasting models, AI algorithms are characterized by a low degree of transparency (Rai, 2020; You et al., 2022), are often ascribed a degree of agency that others are not (Baird & Maruping, 2021), and are perceived as difficult to override (Dietvorst et al., 2018; Tarafdar et al., 2022). There may also be reasons to doubt the applicability of either algorithmic aversion or appreciation as a general rule in the current day and age. Today’s algorithms, trained on enormous datasets using sophisticated learning techniques, are significantly more advanced in their advice accuracy. Furthermore, literacy on algorithms may also be higher in today’s population due to frequent social encounters with AI agents (Olhede & Wolfe, 2018; Willson, 2017). The normalization of human-AI interaction could manifest in greater reliance on algorithmic advice and even lead to more favorable perceptions of artificial agents. This expectation of algorithmic appreciation is likely to be even stronger in the context of analytical tasks, where due to greater computational prowess, algorithms have a definitive advantage. Bogert et al. (2021), for instance, found that individuals relied on algorithmic advice more than the consensus of a human crowd during a counting task, especially as the problem became more difficult.

However, the greater complexity of algorithms and their “black box” nature can lead to skepticism or mistrust of algorithms (Rai, 2020), particularly in subjective or ethical matters (Castelo et al., 2019). As a result, humans interact with algorithms in ways that, while similar to, are unique from other technologies. As such, the question of how human actors respond to advice from their algorithmic counterparts is still an open one, and several factors may moderate whether, and to what extent, humans rely on algorithmic predictions. Indeed, recent research has highlighted that aversion or appreciation are contingent upon factors such as task type (Castello et al., 2019), uncertainty (Dietvorst & Bharti, 2020), and cognitive load (You et al., 2022).

## 2.3 Boundaries of Current Literature: Decision-Making with Human-AI Hybrids

Regardless of their orientation towards aversion or appreciation, both groups of scholars are aligned in their empirical outlook on advice-taking. Specifically, their paradigm views the advice offered by humans and algorithms as a dichotomy, operationalized in experiments by presenting focal decision makers with advice from either an algorithmic or a human advisor. However, contemporary literature has recognized the growing sophistication and complexity of human-AI hybrid systems, which can encompass various forms, from mere augmentation to complex assemblages (Rai et al., 2019; Shrestha et al., 2019). The latter, in particular, involves the dynamic coalescing of multiple human and AI competencies to make complex decisions and potentially generate new organizational knowledge (Fügener et al., 2021; Lyytinen et al., 2021). For instance, on microwork platforms, content moderators may need to screen content by simultaneously evaluating recommendations from human crowds and machine learning-based classification algorithms (Roberts, 2019). Similarly, elected officials responsible for public policy decisions must deliberate upon advice from multiple human advisors and simulation models prior to making determinations that can significantly impact a vast populace.

Hybrids involving multiple human and AI actors differ significantly from single human-single AI decisionmaking clusters explored in previous studies. Such structures introduce novel theoretical considerations regarding how humans incorporate AI advice. Particularly, since the simultaneous evaluation of multiple advice sources can heighten cognitive demands on a focal actor, this raises questions about whether focal actors’ advice utilization behavior changes. Do decision makers, facing an array of alternatives, increase their utilization and appreciation of algorithmic sources? Or, contrarily, does the heightened demand for information processing strain actors’ cognitive resources such that they place greater trust in their own intuitions?

A further boundary condition of the current literature is that the role of uncertainty in algorithmic advice utilization has not been clearly specified (Lipshitz & Strauss, 1997). Uncertainty is a crucial feature of decision-making and can affect the use of human versus algorithmic advice. Indeed, one prior study demonstrated that individuals may be more prone to using human advice when the outcome of a decision is less certain (Dietvorst & Bharti, 2020). The presence of uncertainty may be particularly relevant in situations involving simultaneous advice evaluation since advice sources can often diverge in their judgments. When multiple advisors give conflicting recommendations, the decision maker might have a more difficult time discerning the “correct” solution, even for an objective task. Consider again, for example, the case of content moderators on microwork platforms. How would they behave when receiving conflicting advice from crowds and AI? If a machine learning (ML) classifier flags a piece of content as inappropriate but a crowd of human users does not, how would decision makers behave? Similarly, how would elected officials make policy decisions when receiving conflicting advice from human and algorithmic models? While such questions raise the possibility that humans participating in hybrid decision-making scenarios will show altered appreciation/ aversion, the direction and magnitude of this change is not something the current literature can provide clear answers on.

The present study builds on the aforementioned literature in the following ways. First, we move away from the notion of algorithmic aversion or appreciation as conflicting forces. Instead, we argue that these constructs provide explanations for why individuals behave in certain ways in specific circumstances. Second, we assume that individuals are presented with human and algorithmic advice simultaneously, a key difference between this and previous studies. This change allows us to observe both differences in behavior and relative subjective comparisons. Third, we incorporate uncertainty, operationalized as agreement or disagreement among advisors, into our analysis of human-AI decision-making to extend previously studied scenarios. We argue that the information environment interacts with the latent forces of appreciation and aversion to create unique outcomes.

## 3 Experimental Design

To test the effects of simultaneous advice on individual decision-making, we conducted three total experiments in which participants estimated real outcomes using real data. We asked individuals to estimate the rental price of a property in a US city based on five pieces of data. This task was chosen in part because it is a reasonable task for many adults to complete, and it is also a salient use of algorithms in everyday life (e.g., looking at a Zestimate on Zillow.com). We followed prior research on algorithmic appreciation and aversion and used the judge advisor system format (see for example Bogert et al., 2021, 2022; Dietvorst et al., 2015, 2018; Logg et al., 2019; You et al., 2022) where participants make an estimate, receive advice, then have an opportunity to revise their estimate. In the following sections, we describe the shared features of the experiments and then present the results of each study.

## 3.1 Participant Recruitment

All participants in the study were recruited from Amazon Mechanical Turk and directed to an online survey. In total, there were six unique conditions (three in Study 1, one in Study 2, and two in Study 3). To achieve sufficient statistical power, we needed to recruit enough participants so that (1) results from one condition could be compared to any other and (2) individual differences within a condition could be compared. Using the software G\*Power 3.1, we calculated the minimum sample size to achieve 0.80 power at a significance level of 0.05 for an effect size of 0.33.<sup>2</sup> The required sample size is 143 per condition. This number also satisfies the same power conditions for a matched-pairs comparison (e.g., comparing responses within participant). Thus, for each study condition, we aimed to collect 150 samples. To account for potentially bad data, we recruited 180 MTurk participants for each study.

We followed best practice recommendations for collecting a valid Mechanical Turk sample and eliminating potential bots (Lowry et al., 2016). Specifically, users had to have a 98% human intelligence task (HIT) acceptance rate and also had to have completed over 100 HITs. We also restricted access to the task such that the same MTurk ID could not be used for more than one of the three studies. Participants were required to be from the United States, be over the age of 18, and not currently be members of the military. To further corroborate that participants were from the United States, we asked them the question: When do you celebrate Independence Day? Those who responded incorrectly were removed from the study. Overall, the task took participants approximately 6 minutes to complete and they were compensated US\$1 for their participation, equivalent to an average hourly rate of US\$10. In total, 904 individuals completed the study and were used in the analyses (445 in Study 1, 150 in Study 2, and 309 in Study 3).

## 3.2 Procedure

To create a decision-making scenario that would be both familiar to the average participant and plausible for an algorithm to be competent at, we asked participants to estimate the per-month rental price of a property in the United States based on a set of characteristics. We selected five properties for rent at the time of the experiment from the real estate website Zillow that varied in terms of price, type, location, and size. We chose the major metropolitan areas of Atlanta, Chicago, Dallas, New York, and San Francisco to cover different regions in the US and selected properties that ranged in size from 925 to 1,900 square feet. Participants were further informed that the purpose of this task was to gauge the ability of the average American citizen to correctly make estimations about the real estate market in different metropolitan cities. Specifically, we asked participants whether they currently owned/rented an apartment/ house/condo. Participants in the experiment were randomly shown the information for one of the five properties and then asked to provide an estimate of the per-month rental cost. Then, they were shown price estimates from their advisor(s) and given the opportunity to revise their estimate. The human advisor was given a neutral-sounding North American name, John or Lisa,<sup>4</sup> which was chosen randomly. The algorithm was given the name RentEstimatorBot and was described as “an AI algorithm trained to predict rental prices in American cities.” Figure 1 shows how the manipulation prompt appeared to experiment takers.

After recording their second estimate, participants were asked a series of questions regarding the task as well as demographic information. To ensure the validity of the experimental manipulations we implemented both manipulation and attention checks (Jia et al., 2017; Lowry et al., 2016). Immediately after completing the task, and before filling out the post-task questionnaire, participants were asked in which city the property was located (attention check). Further, just before the final debriefing, participants were asked about the source of their advice (a human, an AI algorithm, both a human and an AI algorithm, neither a human nor an AI algorithm). Participants who failed either of these checks were removed from the study.

Dependent variables: In all three studies we captured the same outcome variables: weight on advice (WOA) with respect to each advisor, preference for the advisor, and confidence in the advisors’ recommendations. This measure of WOA is, however, only effective when there is one source of advice; when two conflicting recommendations are given, this formula cannot distinguish between different directions of movement. In addition to the behavioral measure of WOA, we captured two cognitive measures related to participants’ perceptions of their advisors. Participants were asked about how useful they found the advisor to be, how satisfied they were interacting with the advisor, and how much they trusted the advice provided by that advisor (Xiao & Benbasat, 2007).<sup>5</sup> All responses were given on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1—not at all to 5—very much so. We aggregated these three measures into a single score, which we refer to as preference for advisor. The three questions we posed demonstrated strong agreement (Cronbach alpha = 0.82 for the human advisor; alpha = 0.84 for the AI advisor). In addition to this preference measure, we also captured confidence in each advisor. Specifically, we asked individuals to rate each advisor on how confident they were in the information provided, captured on a Likert scale from 1—not at all to 5— very much so. The complete list of items is provided in the Appendix.

Independent variables: The main independent variables for this study were binary measures capturing elements of the experimental condition that were determined with respect to each advisor present. The independent variables included (1) Was the advisor human or AI? (2) Were there one or two advisors? (3) Did the advisors agree with each other? (d) Did the advisor agree with the participant? We additionally captured several control variables.

First, we assessed the decision-making style using four questions from the general decision-making style inventory (Scott & Bruce, 1995). Scott and Bruce (1995) define decision-making style as “the learned, habitual response pattern exhibited by an individual when confronted with a decision situation” (p. 820). We specifically used the subscale for rational decisionmaking, defined as a “thorough search for and logical evaluation of alternatives” (p. 820). This scale was chosen because it captures the extent to which a participant will carefully consider alternative sources of information and come to a logical conclusion. Participants answered using a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1—strongly agree to 7—strongly disagree. This measure had a Cronbach alpha of 0.71.

Second, participants completed six items from the negative attitudes towards robots scale (Nomura et al., 2008), which we modified slightly to specify a more general “bot” rather than robots. This scale serves as a litmus test for an individual’s general predisposition regarding advanced technology like bots or AI. Again, we used the 7-point Likert scale. This measure had a Cronbach alpha of 0.80, indicating strong agreement among items. Finally, we captured basic demographic information on age, gender identification, education level, and reported ethnicity. These variables have all been shown to influence attitudes towards AI in various contexts, albeit not uniformly (Aggarwal et al., 2021; Jiang & Lee, 2017; Kaya et al., 2024; Zhang & Dafoe, 2020). In the Appendix, we list all items for the variables included in our study.

## 3.4 Overview of Studies

We addressed our two research questions using three total studies. To address RQ1, we conducted Study 1 where we manipulated whether there was a single advisor (human or AI) or two advisors (human and AI). To address RQ2, we conducted two further studies where the advisors were in agreement or in conflict. Studies 2 and 3 both involved two advisors (human and AI) but presented different patterns of agreement between the participant and the two advisors. Study 2 presented participants with two advisors in agreement. By contrast, Study 3 presented participants with one advisor (human or AI) who was in agreement with their initial guess and one advisor (human or AI)

who was in conflict with their initial guess (presenting unique advice). All three versions of the study used the same decision-making task and collected the same information, and all three versions were applied between subjects. Thus, the results in terms of both behaviors and perceptions can be directly compared.

## 4 Study 1

## 4.1 Methods

Manipulation: In Study 1, we manipulated the configuration of advisors with three different conditions. The first condition involved a single human advisor, i.e., after making an initial guess, participants were shown the estimate only from a purported “other person.” To ensure that the quality of advice was consistent across trials and comparable across properties, the advisor always gave an estimate that was, on average, 15% higher <sup>6</sup> than the participants’ original guesses. The second condition involved a single AI advisor, such that participants were only shown the estimate of “an algorithm.” As with the first condition, the AI advisor always gave an estimate that was, on average, 15% lower than the participants’ original guesses. Finally, in the third condition, participants were exposed to advice from both a human and an AI simultaneously, with the AI advice 15% lower and the human advice 15% higher. After their initial guess, the participant was then shown advice from the human and advice from the AI; both numbers were shown together in random order on the screen. Thus, both advisors were equally close to the participants’ estimates (and as close as they were alone), but in opposite directions. In addition to the three core manipulation conditions, we also implemented a control condition to establish a baseline level of behavior. For participants in the control condition, individuals were presented with advice with no source attribution (i.e., “A suggested price is…”); the advice was either 15% higher or lower than the participant’s initial guess in order to be consistent with the other conditions.

Participants: We aimed to recruit approximately 150 individuals for each of the three conditions. To account for potentially poor data quality, we recruited a total of 180 individuals from MTurk for each case. After removing individuals who failed the criteria outlined before, failed the screening question regarding real estate, or did not complete the task, we were left with 84 individuals in the control condition, 152 individuals in Condition 1, 147 individuals in Condition 2, and 146 individuals in Condition 3 (N = 445 total). The sample was 44.5% female, 55% male, and 0.5% nonbinary; 64.3% had a bachelor’s degree or higher and 86.3% were between the ages of 25 and 54.

## 4.2 Results

Before discussing the main results for Study 1, we compare the control condition (no source attribution) to Conditions 1 (human advice only) and 2 (AI advice only). We report the behavioral measures for each condition (WOA and proportion of trials with answer change) to determine if attributing advice to a source led to more or less advice taking. In Figure 2 we summarize the findings of the control condition and single advisor conditions. We found that for individuals in the control condition, the average WOA was 0.06, compared to 0.38 for a human advisor and 0.49 for an AI advisor; both of these values are significantly greater than those for the control condition $( { \mathrm { t } _ { 2 2 5 } } = 9 . 1 5 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 ~ { \mathrm { a n d ~ t } _ { 2 2 9 } } = 1 1 . 6 9 , p <$ 0.001, respectively). Similarly, participants changed their answers 72% of the time in the human condition, 76% of the time in the AI condition, and 13% of the time in the control condition. Again, these results are statistically significant $( \chi _ { 1 } ^ { 2 } = 7 1 . 4 7 , p < 0 . 0 0 1$ and $\chi _ { 1 } ^ { 2 }$ $= ~ 8 1 . 0 7 , ~ p ~ < ~ 0 . 0 0 1$ , respectively). These results collectively indicate that labeling advice as coming from a specific advisor (human or AI) does indeed lead to greater behavioral change. We next discuss our main analysis, which compares single advisors (human or AI) to Condition 3, involving two advisors simultaneously.

The main results for Study 1 are summarized in Figure 3, which we now describe. We first compare the decision-making behavior of individuals in Conditions 1 (human advice only) and 2 (AI advice only). We found that the average WOA with respect to the human was 0.38, while the average WOA with respect to the AI was 0.49; this difference is statistically significant $( \mathrm { t } _ { 2 8 9 } = - 2 . 7 4 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ ). Further, the rate at which individuals took advice was not significantly different (0.72 versus 0.76), suggesting that people in the AI advice condition reacted more strongly to comparable advice. Next, we consider the decision-making behavior of individuals in Condition 3, i.e., those who received simultaneous advice from a human and an AI. Note that the advice from both sources was equally far from the decision maker’s original estimate. We found that the WOA with respect to the human was 0.09, while the WOA with respect to the AI was 0.21; the difference is statistically significant $( \mathrm { t } _ { 2 7 9 } = - 3 . 4 6 , p <$ 0.001).

We also found that this difference in WOA was largely driven by different rates of advice utilization; 16.45% of subjects moved towards the human, 33.55% moved towards the AI, and 50% did not change their estimate. The difference in movement between human advisor and AI advisor is statistically significant $( \chi _ { 1 } ^ { 2 } = 1 0 . 9 7$ $p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , indicating that given equal advice in either direction, an individual is more likely to revise their estimate such that it becomes closer to the AI. Figures 3a and 3b illustrate these findings.

We next examine how participants perceived their advisors across conditions. When individuals received advice from only a human or AI, they demonstrated a marginal preference for the AI, rating the human at 3.76 and the AI at $3 . 9 2 \ ( \mathrm { t } _ { 2 9 1 } = - 1 . 8 3 , p < 0 . 1 0 )$ . In other words, all else being equal people attributed slightly more satisfaction, trust, and usefulness to an AI advisor. Similarly, individuals reported higher confidence in the solutions provided by an AI (3.61) compared to those provided by a human (3.30); this difference is significant $( \mathrm { t } _ { 2 9 1 } = - 2 . 7 5 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . In Condition $^ { 3 , }$ where participants received advice from a human and an AI simultaneously, there was no significant difference in participants’ satisfaction, trust, and usefulness ratings for the advisors. It is possible that this result indicates that when presented with two options, both of which are equally different, the decision maker does not find either to be more informative or useful. However, participants did report higher confidence in the AI (3.27) compared to the human (2.91); the difference is statistically significant $( \mathrm { t } _ { 3 0 1 } = - 2 . 9 1 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ ). These findings are presented in Figures 3c and 3d.

## 7 Overall Findings and Summary

Finally, we consider our results in their entirety and compare the relative effects of the different studies. We begin by examining the effects of moving from a single advisor to two advisors and evaluate how this changes the overall levels of the four dependent variables in this study and how these effects vary by advisor type. In

Figure 6 we plot the average values across all three experiments of the dependent variables for the single advisor case (solid circle) and the joint advisor case (empty circle). In general, we found that simultaneous advice reduced the weight on advice, the proportion of cases where a change was made, the preference for an advisor, and the confidence in an advisor. Essentially, adding uncertainty associated with a second advisor made participants less likely to change their responses and less comfortable overall with their choices. This pattern is true for both human and AI advisors. For WOA and advisor preference, both dependent variables were reduced by roughly the same amount for human and AI advisors. However, for the proportion of trials moved and confidence in the advisor, the reduction for AI advisors was less than that for human advisors. These results suggest that there is heterogeneity in how simultaneity affects the relationship between the decision maker and the advisor.

Building on this analysis, we wanted to determine how going from one advisor—AI or human—to two advisors can change behaviors and beliefs contingent upon the agreement among the advisors and the decision maker. For our analysis, we specifically considered the differences in evaluation and behavior with respect to a human advisor and an AI advisor. We examined differences because they capture the relative preference for a human versus an AI advisor, rather than the absolute magnitude of the preference. This approach is consistent with the perspective that algorithmic appreciation or aversion is a tendency to react differently to an algorithm than a human. In Study 1.3 (divergent advisors), both the human and AI advisors are presented simultaneously but provide the same advice as in Studies 1.1 and 1.2. We found that the difference in WOA was effectively the same $( d = - 0 . 1 1 4 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ on average, but individuals moved closer to the AI’s suggestion significantly more frequently $( d = - 0 . 1 7 1 , p$ < 0.001) and were more confident in the AI relative to the human advisor $( d = - 0 . 3 6 2 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . In other words, given two competing suggestions from an AI advisor and a human advisor, both of which were equidistant from the original guess, participants moved towards the AI advisor 17% more frequently, compared to the human advisor, and were relatively more confident in the AI advisor. This finding suggests that when presented with simultaneous advice, rather than one or the other, decision makers will tend to favor the AI advisor.

In Studies 2 and 3 we varied whether advice was consistent or in conflict among the advisors and decision makers. For Study 2 (convergent advisors), the AI and human advisor both provided nearly identical advice, with both sources of advice differing from the decision maker’s original guess. In this case, decision makers demonstrated a significantly greater preference for $( d = - 0 . 2 7 8 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and confidence in the AI advisor $( d = - 0 . 3 9 3 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . In other words, despite both advisors giving the same advice, participants in our study favored the AI advisor to a much greater extent. One possible explanation is that when the AI advisor’s advice is corroborated by a third party, i.e., a human advisor, the focal decision maker is validated in their preference for the AI advisor and thus expresses greater satisfaction and confidence.

Finally, when one advisor agreed with the decision maker and the other disagreed, we found the greatest deviations from the single advisor case. When the human advisor gave advice that was consistent with the participant’s initial guess (human agrees), we observed a significant tendency for the decision maker to adjust their guess (WOA) in the direction of the AI advisor (d $= - 0 . 1 7 3 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 \rangle$ ). This result makes sense given the tendency to move towards the average recommendation of the advisors. We also found that participants tended to exhibit a preference for the human advisor in this case $( d = 0 . 3 8 0 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ but were not significantly more confident in the human advisor compared to the AI advisor. In contrast, when the AI advisor agreed with their initial guess (AI agrees), participants did not exhibit greater WOA towards the human advisor. In other words, decision makers did not move significantly closer, on average, to the human advisor, even though that would represent a move towards the average. Further, participants significantly favored the AI advisor in this case $( d = - 0 . 6 9 5 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and were more confident in the AI advisor (d = -0.913, p < 0.001). Indeed, in Study 3.2 (AI agrees), we observed the greatest discrepancies in reported preferences and reported confidence, suggesting that an AI advisor agreeing with a decision maker is significantly more impactful than a human advisor agreeing.

To summarize, our findings suggest that high levels of disagreement (three unique answers) cause the greatest discomfort for decision makers, making them unlikely to act on advice. Conversely, two external advisors converging on a common solution appears to prompt strong action and positive beliefs. When one advisor diverges, they tend to be viewed more negatively, regardless of whether they are AI or human. However, this penalty is far greater for human advisors than for AI advisors. Overall, the results presented in the previous sections and summarized in Figures 6 and 7 demonstrate that decision makers will evaluate an advisor differently if (1) there are multiple advisors, (2) the advisors are AI rather than human, and (3) there is agreement or conflict in the advice provided. Indeed, our experiments reveal that the extent to which people accept advice and how they perceive their advisors cannot be understood using only one of the given dimensions. ## 8 Discussion & Conclusions

Over the past decade, the expanding frontiers of statistical learning have transformed AI from a mere buzzword to a valuable resource that can provide strategic advantages to businesses. Today, corporations, in industries ranging from financial investing to creative writing, are investing heavily in developing AI tools to augment the decision-making ability of their workforce. However, the real value of any IT investment is derived from how people actually use the technology (Devraj & Kohli, 2003); as such, both scholars and practitioners are interested in definitive and concise accounts of how humans utilize AI inputs in their decision-making. While recent studies have added valuable insights on this subject, we argue in this paper that the theoretical paradigm and accompanying empirical designs employed in extant studies do not fully capture the emerging realities of human-AI decision-making. Specifically, while several studies have relied on a decision maker receiving advice from a single human or AI source, human-AI hybrid systems are becoming more complex and may comprise multiple heterogeneous actors (Rai et al., 2019; Shreshta et al., 2019), making it necessary for decision makers to simultaneously provision advice from multiple sources. This is an emergent reality of practice, which is an important boundary condition for theory and something that experimental designs should mirror. With this as the primary motivation, we conducted a series of three experiments that required human subjects to make judgments when simultaneously receiving advice from both human and AI sources.

Our study yields three noteworthy findings. Firstly, it reveals that human decision makers in hybrid configurations exhibit significant behavioral and cognitive deference towards AI sources in comparison to human sources, assuming all other factors remain equal (Study 1). The results from Study 2 further highlight this tendency, showing that participants expressed a stronger inclination towards AI advice than human advice even when provided with almost identical recommendations from both sources. Furthermore, findings from Study 3 indicate that even when human advisors endorsed their decision-making judgments, participants were more likely to adjust their decisions based on conflicting AI advice. In contrast, when AI advisors validated participants’ decisions and human advisors disagreed with them, participants were less inclined to revise their initial decisions, thus indicating that AI endorsement boosts decision makers’ confidence. Overall, these three outcomes provide compelling empirical evidence supporting the hypothesis that humans tend to prefer AI advisors over human advisors in hybrid configurations, i.e., a tendency towards algorithmic appreciation even with multiple advisors (Logg et al., 2019).

Our second major finding is that while decision makers tend to favor algorithmic advice, the likelihood of them utilizing that advice and the magnitude of utilization is significantly reduced when a second advisor is present (Study 1.3, Study 3) unless both advisors are in total agreement (Study 2). Further, our study participants tended to have less positive views of the advisors and feel less confident in these cases. The average values for all four dependent measures in Figure 6 illustrate this point. Essentially, our findings suggest that receiving conflicting advice from a human and AI advisor leads individuals to become more resistant to advice and fosters a more negative outlook.

Third, and building on our second finding, our results show that the level of uncertainty in a decision-making scenario is a determinant of AI advice utilization. When there is a single advisor, there is no conflict in the information provided. Further, in scenarios with low levels of uncertainty—such as in Study 2, where both human and AI advisors demonstrated convergence—individuals will tend to exhibit a significant increase in their usage of AI advice. However, as the level of uncertainty increases—as in Studies 1.3, 3.1, and 3.2—individuals will tend to exhibit a marked decrease in both the likelihood and extent of AI advice utilization. It is worth noting, however, that when an advisor confirms the beliefs of the decision maker (Study 3), the individual will tend to have a more positive perception of and greater confidence in that advisor, suggesting a form of confirmation bias. This expressed preference is even stronger when the beliefs of the decision maker are confirmed by an AI advisor (versus a human advisor).

## 8.1 Theoretical Implications

Several theoretical and practical implications follow from these findings. First, our findings provide valuable insights into the ongoing discussion about whether humans are appreciative or averse toward algorithmic predictions. When solving problems involving logic, one study showed that participants in an experiment trusted the same solution more when it came from an expert system (algorithm) than when it came from a human (Dijkstra et al., 1998). When making quantitative judgments under uncertainty, individuals have been shown to weigh algorithmic advice higher than human advice (Logg et al., 2019). Of course, individuals do not strictly prefer the advice of algorithms over other humans, even in relatively objective settings (Dietvorst et al., 2015; Dietvorst & Bharti, 2020). However, prior studies have also demonstrated that algorithmic aversion can be mitigated by augmenting AI advice with another human, producing a hybrid system (Longoni & Cian, 2020; Luo et al., 2021; Shrestha et al., 2019). Overall, our study lends support to the notion of algorithmic appreciation by showing that, even in hybrid configurations, individuals display a comparative preference for AI advice.

Further, we demonstrate that the preference for algorithmic advisors is not purely behavioral. Research has shown that individuals evaluating both an AI advisor and another human advisor consistently show a preference for the AI agent, all else being equal (Xiao & Benbasat, 2007). A potential consequence of this finding is the possibility of overconfidence after receiving advice (Soll et al., 2021) and how this can be exacerbated by an algorithmic advisor. In our study, when an AI advisor agreed with the participant or a human advisor, participants were significantly more confident in their decisions. This finding could indicate that humans rely upon algorithmic advisors by default in certain scenarios, potentially leading to incorrect conclusions or complacency (Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010). Introducing an algorithmic advisor might also exacerbate the tendency for decision makers to reject outside information when making decisions collaboratively (Minson & Mueller, 2012).

Secondly, and more importantly, our study argues that there are unexplored boundary conditions in the present literature and leverages a novel experimental design to probe these conditions. Specifically, it sheds light on the pivotal role of simultaneity and the resultant uncertainty in shaping decision makers’ utilization advice in human-AI advising hybrids. We show that the extent to which humans utilize AI advice is contingent on the hybrid configuration, i.e., a sole advisor or simultaneous advice. In hybrids where humans receive advice from an AI advisor only, they tend to revise their existing outlook 75% of the time. However, adding a human advisor to the hybrid drastically reduces the probability that decision makers will incorporate AI advice into their judgments and negatively impacts their views of the advisors. One possible explanation for this could be that having multiple advisors increases the cognitive load on decision makers (Lipshitz & Strauss, 1997), thus reducing the likelihood that they will utilize the AI advice. Indeed, as recent studies on this topic have shown, cognitive load and related constructs are potential mediators that can explain how humans utilize AI (You et al., 2022). This increased cognitive burden could be due to the inscrutable nature of algorithms (Tarafdar et al., 2022), making it unclear why the algorithm disagrees. We thus posit that creating assemblages of multiple humans and potentially multiple AIs could make decision-making extremely difficult for individuals as they try to reconcile competing information and unique sources.

By foregrounding the role of simultaneity, we were also able to explicate another relevant construct, the degree of uncertainty in decision-making. Indeed, uncertainty is a ubiquitous feature when evaluating the advice of multiple advisors and can be exacerbated when the advice is not consistent. In joint-evaluation situations, it is not uncommon for different advisors to provide contradictory advice to decision makers (Larrick & Soll, 2006). In fact, having an array of unique alternatives is likely to be a precondition for making effective decisions (Mannes, 2009; Yaniv & Milyavsky, 2007). However, multiple conflicting inputs can also increase the degree of uncertainty in a decision-making situation. Prior work suggests that individuals tend to discount advice from sources that diverge from the group (Harries et al., 2004), as well as advice that significantly differs from an initial estimate (Schultze et a., 2015). This poses a challenge for studies of AI-augmented decision-making, for it is unclear how individuals will reconcile agreement or disagreement between themselves, human advisors, and AI advisors. Would humans, confronting a highly uncertain scenario, rely more on AI objectivity and appreciate its inputs, or would the uncertainty overwhelm decision makers such that they rely more on their original intuitions?

Our findings tend to suggest that decision makers do indeed discount divergent advice, as expected. However, this effect is contingent upon which advisor is in conflict. An AI providing dissenting advice is not discounted in the same way as a human advisor, suggesting that people may be more open to receiving unique information from an algorithm than from a generic human alternative. In fact, in our study, when the algorithm provided divergent information, the decision maker was much more likely to incorporate that information and break away from their anchoring bias, despite being less confident in the algorithmic advisor. By contrast, a dissenting human advisor did not have the same effect. This finding suggests that AI advisors may be more effective for debiasing individuals than more static decision support systems (cf., George et al., 2000), in part because the algorithmic advisor is presented as an autonomous participant in the decision-making scenario rather than a reminder or nudge.

## 8.2 Practical Implications

Our findings also have implications for managerial practice. Even as AI becomes ubiquitous in the workplace, there remain unresolved questions and challenges confronting managers. For instance, from a wide array of possible hybrid structures (Shrestha et al., 2019), what configuration should managers choose to deploy? Our results suggest that the response to this question may hinge on, among other considerations, the particular goals that managers wish to accomplish. Deploying an AI model and maintaining the data infrastructure necessary for its continuous improvement requires significant capital investment. As such, top management teams who sanction such investments may wish to ensure that their workforce will exhibit increased utilization of and reliance on AI in decision-making practices. This scenario could take place in settings where the organization would benefit from greater standardization or quality control. Our study suggests that managers seeking to maximize the utilization of AI by workers can achieve this objective by pairing single human and AI actors into dyadic configurations, i.e., avoiding larger groupings where advice comes from multiple sources. Algorithms could also be designed to help individuals avoid different forms of bias; for example, decision support systems have been shown to be successful in minimizing the negative effects of anchoring biases (George et al., 2000).

Alternatively, managers may also have objectives related to preserving the unique knowledge and skills of human actors, even when AI is involved (Fügener et al., 2021). Despite the hype surrounding AI, its overall proficiency in performing certain tasks is still not absolute. Moreover, AI models lack transparency and have been known to perpetuate existing human biases in decision-making. The errors made by AI can have consequences ranging from mild to potentially catastrophic. For instance, on March 9, 2022, an AIpowered Indian air defense system miscalculated the threat perception and erroneously fired a missile into neighboring Pakistan’s airspace, escalating tensions between the two nuclear-powered adversaries. Other recent events, such as the botched launch of Google's chatbot Bard, have heightened concerns among leaders about the overreliance on AI and the potential deskilling of the workforce (Fügener et al., 2021). As a result, managers may also be motivated to preserve human skills and knowledge. Pairing multiple human and AI advisors into a hybrid configuration can enhance the diversity of available options and reduce the potential for uncritical acceptance of AI suggestions.

Such hybrid configurations could also mitigate other potential unintended outcomes due to human partiality to algorithms. For instance, the relative sidelining of human advisors could mean a loss of their motivation to participate, which might in turn result in employee turnover. Regarding performance outcomes, preferential treatment of algorithmic advisors could also lead to suboptimal outcomes—when algorithms falter, so will the group due to overreliance on the technology. Furthermore, future collaboration between workers could also be impacted by favoritism bestowed upon AI advisors at the expense of human advisors. If humans continuously show distrust towards each other’s solutions, they will be less willing to work together during later collaborations.

Finally, our finding that rising uncertainty— manifested by conflicting advice—diminishes the utilization of AI advice among humans also has some implications for practitioners. Companies may invest in AI particularly to leverage its objectivity during uncertain situations. Studies have indicated that even basic statistical approaches, such as weighted means, demonstrate superior performance in generating objective predictions (Larrick & Soll, 2006), and this gulf is only expected to widen as tasks become more complex and decisions more uncertain. Consequently, in certain task domains, the advice of a third-party human and an algorithm are more likely to diverge, creating uncertainty for the decision maker. In such situations, our research reveals that human reliance on any advice diminishes significantly but potentially less so for algorithmic advice. Thus, managers must create strategies to cultivate trust in AI aids among human decision makers in uncertain environments, such as developing specific training material and embedding cues in the technology artifact such as displayed level of confidence or explanation of solutions.

## 8.3 Future Research Directions

Beyond the present study, we believe there are multiple avenues for future research at the boundaries of this emerging research topic. One fertile ground for future research is humans and AI in more complex decision-making structures, such as workgroups and teams (Baird & Maruping, 2021). The current focus of the advice utilization literature is at the level of individual decision makers; however, with its rapid adoption, it is likely that AI is also influencing collective decision-making or collective intelligence (Woolley et al., 2015) more broadly. In this study, we found evidence that adding additional actors into hybrid systems can significantly change how individuals behave and assess their peers, particularly when there is significant conflict in the information (e.g., Study 1). Moving the scope of research from individuals to groups and teams is certain to reveal new tensions and generative mechanisms in AI-augmented collaborative decision-making.

A second idea that could help move the frontiers of this literature is the role of different hybrid configurations in influencing human-AI interaction. Hybrid decisionmaking structures can follow several ideal-typical forms (Shrestha et al., 2019), with humans playing different roles in each. While some hybrids may employ sequential imbrication of human and AI decisions, others may employ an aggregation model. Do humans behave differently in different configurations? What patterns of behavior emerge based on specific configurations? For instance, human members of a group might feel a lack of autonomy when they do not have control over the decisions made by algorithms (Peters et al., 2020). Alternatively, groups with multiple humans and multiple algorithms working together might create unique dynamics among the human subgroup or between humans and algorithms. Methods such as fuzzy set QCA could be used to build meaningful comparisons across unique configurations by identifying consistent patterns in each human-AI hybrid.

Third, in our discussion of managerial implications, we treated the twin goals of greater AI utilization and preserving unique human knowledge as mutually conflicting. It is highly likely that instead of treating these goals as orthogonal, managers might strive to achieve a harmonious and mutually reinforcing balance between them, leading to potential metalearning (Lyytinen et al., 2021). Exploring which hybrid specifications can lead to such an ambidextrous pursuit of these two seemingly contradictory goals is another idea that future research could pursue. Finally, it is possible that our findings may not generalize to other kinds of decision-making tasks. On the one hand, the task presented in this paper reflects the typical use of algorithms: e.g., restaurant recommendations, driving directions, and stock forecasts. Thus, while the task is analytical, it applies to a wide array of common interactions with AI. On the other hand, with the growing prevalence of more advanced models such as generative AI, people will likely begin to make moral judgments in concert with AI recommendations or might produce creative works in tandem with an algorithm. In these cases, future work should consider how individuals can balance human advice with potentially divergent AI advice.
