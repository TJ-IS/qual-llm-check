# Seeing the Trees or the Forest? The Effect of IT Project Managers Mental Construal on IT Project Risk Management Activities

Abstract. Prior research has suggested that information technology (IT) project managers (PMs) often fail to take adequate steps to manage project risks and that this may contribute to the high failure rate associated with IT projects. Knowing when to pay attention to the trees versus the forest may play an important role in IT project risk management. We draw on construal level theory (CLT) to offer a fresh theoretical perspective on IT project risk management as it is uniquely suited to understanding why IT PMs may focus on the forest (abstract, high-level construal) or the trees (concrete, low-level construal). Specifically, we integrate CLT with IT project risk management and theorize how IT PMs’ mental construal affects four key IT project risk management activities: (1) risk identification, (2) risk impact assessment, (3) risk response planning, and (4) risk response enactment. We recruited a total of 805 IT PMs for this research and conducted a series of four laboratory experiments. In our experiments, we found that seeing the trees is beneficial in risk identification, risk impact assessment, and risk response planning. Specifically, we found that a concrete (relative to abstract) mental construal of a project leads IT PMs to identify a greater number of risks associated with the project (Experiment 1), and a concrete (relative to abstract) mental construal of a project risk leads IT PMs to perceive a greater potential impact of the risk (Experiment 2) and to perceive that more effort and resources are required for responding to the risk (Experiment 3). In contrast, we found that seeing the forest is beneficial in risk response enactment in that an abstract (relative to concrete) mental construal of a risk response leads IT PMs to be more willing to enact the risk response (Experiment 4).

## Introduction

The Mars Climate Orbiter was a robotic space probe developed and launched by NASA to study the Martian climate, atmosphere, and surface changes (Oberg 1999). Nine and a half months after launch, the space probe approached Mars at a lower altitude than planned and disintegrated due to atmospheric stresses, causing a \$125 million loss for NASA (Hotz 1999, Lloyd 1999). Investigations later revealed that this debacle was caused by a software error that resulted from engineers failing to pay attention to details in the math used for the trajectory calculation (Hotz 1999, Oberg 1999).

Attention to detail is important in identifying project risks because project managers (PMs) must understand the project in sufficient detail to recognize potential problems. Indeed, attention to detail during the planning phase of a project can help PMs to cope with the risks that can plague projects (Matta and Ashkenas 2003). Further, prior work has suggested that lack of attention to detail during the planning stage of a project can contribute to a high level of uncertainty and risk (Ward and Chapman 2003).

Prior research has also suggested that during the early stages of a project IT PMs can get so focused on the desirability of incorporating certain features that they may not pay sufficient attention to project risks (Boehm 1991). This behavioral tendency can be understood as focusing more on the forest (the desirability aspect of the project) than the trees (the feasibility aspect of the project) and is captured by the phrase “missing the trees for the forest” (Alter et al. 2010, p. 436). This suggests that focusing on the trees (versus the forest) may be beneficial during the early stages of a project. In risk management terms this would mean paying attention to detail in identifying risks, assessing their potential impact, and planning appropriate responses. However, research suggests that even when PMs conduct risk impact evaluations and develop risk response plans, they often do not actually enact such risk response plans (Mignerat and Rivard 2012). Thus, when it comes to risk response enactment, it actually may be better to shift one’s focus from feasibility (i.e., trees) to desirability (i.e., forest).

In this research, we draw on construal level theory (CLT) which has the potential to offer a fresh theoretical perspective on IT project risk management as it is uniquely suited to understanding why IT PMs may focus on the forest or the trees in a given situation. Furthermore, we aim to theorize and empirically investigate during which risk management activities focusing on the trees is beneficial.

CLT proposes that people understand events, objects, or actions through mental construals (abstract, high-level or concrete, low-level construals) (Trope and Liberman 2010). Construal level is also understood as seeing the forest (abstract thinking) or seeing the trees (concrete thinking) (Dhar and Kim 2007). Further, people perceive a bidirectional relationship between these construals and the psychological distances. As psychological distance increases, construals “become more abstract, and as level of abstraction increases, so too would the psychological distances people envisage” (Trope and Liberman 2010, p. 440). Moreover, prior CLT research has found several aspects (such as desirability/feasibility, hypotheticality, category/exemplar, and pros/cons) that are related to construal level. In our research context, construal level refers to the extent to which IT PMs think about a project, a risk, or a risk response in an abstract or concrete manner (i.e., IT PMs’ mental construals). In this research, we integrate CLT with IT project risk management, and theorize how IT PMs’ mental construal affects four key IT project risk management activities: (1) risk identification, (2) risk impact assessment, (3) risk response planning, and (4) risk response enactment.

For theory-driven research that extends an existing theory, researchers must incorporate the theory within a specific context and explain how the theory would operate within that context (Eisenhardt and Graebner 2007). Further, Colquitt and Zapata-Phelan (2007) suggest that the two highest levels of theory building research involve identifying new constructs and examining previously unexplored relationships. Our research extends an existing theory (i.e., CLT) by identifying constructs associated with four key IT project risk management activities and examining the unexplored relationships between IT PMs’ mental construal and these context-specific constructs. In the remainder of this paper, we provide an overview of relevant literature on both IT project risk management and CLT, followed by the development of our hypotheses. Next, we describe the experiments conducted to test these hypotheses and the results that were obtained. Finally, we discuss the implications of our findings for both research and practice.

## IT Project Risk Management

IT projects are notoriously difficult to manage because they involve coordinating a complex set of activities often spanning both intra- and interorganizational boundaries and must often be undertaken against a backdrop of both market and technological uncertainty. Schedule and budget overruns are common and such projects are often unsuccessful, in part, because PMs frequently fail to adequately identify and manage the risks that can and often do materialize (Flyvbjerg and Budzier 2011). A risk is an event that can cause a threat to the successful completion of an IT project (Schmidt et al. 2001), and examples of some common IT project risks include changing scope/objectives and lack of adequate user involvement (Keil et al. 1998).

IT project risk management has become an important topic of interest to practitioners and has thus received attention from IS scholars over the years (see, e.g., Lyytinen et al. 1998). Project risk management is understood as a process that involves a series of activities (Taylor et al. 2012, PMI 2017). First, an IT PM identifies risks associated with a project and assesses the potential impact of each identified risk.<sup>1</sup> Then the IT PM must estimate the effort and resources required to respond to the identified risks and decide whether to enact risk responses.

Prior research has found that project risks can have significant direct effects on project outcomes (Barki et al. 2001, Wallace and Keil 2004, Wallace et al. 2004). Further, it has been found that project risks can reduce the positive effects of project controls on project performance (Keil et al. 2013, Liu 2015). To date, IT project management researchers have focused considerable attention on advancing normative knowledge concerning project risk management (Moeini and Rivard 2019b), including developing tools or frameworks for identifying and assessing project risks (e.g., Barki et al. 1993, Keil et al. 1998, Schmidt et al. 2001).

Several risk management researchers adopted contingency approaches and suggested that an organization’s project risk management strategies should b dependent on particular risks to which the project is vulnerable (see, e.g., Barki et al. 2001). Ropponen and Lyytinen (2000) found that environmental contingencies (e.g., the organizational environment) influence several project risk areas (e.g., scheduling, functionality, requirements management, and personnel management). Based on this, they suggested that having a detailed understanding of the environmental context, along with sound risk management practices, is central to successful risk management. More recently, Windeler et al. (2017) suggested that empowering leadership in project teams can be an effective strategy to cope with the technical risk factors associated with information systems development.

Further, a number of scholars have attempted to theoretically explain what shapes people’s risk perception and risk-taking behavior. Early research in this domain includes developing a taxonomy of factors that shape individuals’ risk perception (Slovic 1987) and identifying heuristics and biases that can influence decision-making under uncertainty (Tversky and Kahneman 1974). Risk management scholars have suggested that in organizational settings managers process risks in a way that is distinctively different from the rational decision-making model (March and Shapira 1987). For example, March and Shapira (1987) suggested that managers are “quite insensitive to estimates of the probabilities of possible outcomes; their decisions are particularly affected by the way their attention is focused on critical performance targets; and they make a sharp distinction between taking risks and gambling” (p. 1404). Further, Lampel et al. (2009) suggested that a risk can be a rare event and may be processed through a form of sensemaking that focuses on the salient features of the event.

In the IT project management literature, Lyytinen et al. (1998) challenged the notion that risk management decisions are always based on rational choices and objective calculations of alternatives, suggesting that “managers follow a less precise calculus” (p. 235) when dealing with risks. Further, Keil et al. (2000) showed that decision makers’ risk perception about a project can positively influence their willingness to continue a troubled IT project, a phenomenon known as escalation of commitment. Most recently, Moeini and Rivard (2019a) suggested that IT PMs’ risk response decisions are driven by perceived risk exposure and risk response attitude.

Our research contributes to the body of knowledge on IT project risk management by demonstrating the impact of seeing the trees versus the forest in specific risk management activities. Further, CLT suggests that mental construal is fundamental to understanding individuals’ judgment and choice (Trope and Liberman 2010) and by integrating CTL with IT project risk management we are able to shed light on IT PM’s judgment and choice when engaged in specific risk management activities.

## Construal Level Theory

Abstract mental construals can be understood as bigpicture thinking in which people focus on the overall idea or gist of the construal target. In contrast, con crete mental construals are more detail-oriented and people focus more on specific characteristics of the construal target. A number of different aspects are related to construal level, and these aspects influence and are influenced by construal level (Trope and Liberman 2010).

Psychological distance is one important aspect related to construal level. People use a higher construal level to understand a target that is more psychologically distant (Trope and Liberman 2010). Prior CLT research has shown that there are different types of psychological distance (temporal distance, spatial distance, social distance, and hypotheticality). For example, people construe an event that is unlikely to occur (high psychological distance) in a more abstract manner than an event that is likely to occur (low psychological distance) (Wakslak et al. 2006). CLT suggests that all forms of psychological distance are interrelated. In this research, we focus on the hypotheticality aspect of construal level as it is highly relevant in the context of risk management, which involves dealing with uncertain future events that may or may not occur.

In addition to psychological distance, prior CLT research has shown that there are several other aspects related to construal level. These aspects include desirability/feasibility, category/exemplar, and pros/ cons. The connections between these aspects and construal level have been well established in the literature (see, e.g., a review of CLT by Trope and Liberman 2010). Specifically, abstract mental construal leads people to focus on desirability (versus feasibility), category (versus exemplar), and pros (versus cons). Descriptions of each aspect and its relationship with construal level are shown in Table 1.

We focus on these four aspects related to construal level as they are highly relevant in the context of IT project risk management and represent essential features of CLT. Theorizing the effects of construal level requires one to consider the target of construal. For example, in the context of project risk management IT PMs may think about a project, a risk, or a risk response, and these construal targets may differ depending upon which risk management activity an IT PM is engaged in. Specifically, IT PMs must think about the project in order to identity risks associated with it, the risk when assessing the potential impact of a particular risk and when estimating the effort and resources required to respond to a particular risk, and the risk response when deciding whether to enact a particular risk response (see Table 2). ## Development of Hypotheses

In this section, we theorize the effect of IT PMs’ mental construal in relation to each of the four IT project risk management activities: risk identification (H1), risk impact assessment (H2), risk response planning (H3), and risk response enactment (H4). In theorizing these relationships, we draw on different aspects related to construal level and specific risk management activity/ construal target combinations. Specifically, we identify the aspects that are applicable to each risk management activity and theorize the connections between these aspects and the risk management activities.

## IT PMs’ Mental Construal of a Project and Risk Identi<sup>fi</sup>cation (H1)

Risk identification is typically performed in the planning phase of project management which occurs after the initiation phase (PMI 2017). In other words, once a project is approved PMs start to get involved in project risk management activities, beginning with risk identification. This activity involves identifying potential future events that can occur and have an adverse impact on successful completion of the project.

Risk identification is a mental exercise in which PMs think about what might go wrong during the project and how this might affect the feasibility of the project. Both desirability and feasibility are important in making business decisions (Fitzsimmons and Douglas 2011) and IT PMs must consider both desirability and feasibility concerns when managing projects. Desirability involves the value associated with reaching a goal, whereas feasibility involves the means used to reach a goal (Trope and Liberman 2010). CLT suggests that when an action is construed abstractly “desirability concerns should receive greater weight over feasibility concerns” (Trope et al. 2007, p. 89). CLT further suggests that people assign different weights to desirability and feasibility concerns depending on their mental construal (abstract or concrete). More specifically, people place relatively more weight on the desirability of an action when the action is construed abstractly, whereas people place relatively more weight on the feasibility of an action when the action is construed concretely (Liberman and Trope 1998). Thus, CLT implies that when IT PMs think in abstract terms about an IT project, they may focus more attention on the desirability aspect of the IT project, and comparatively less attention on the feasibility aspect of the project. In managing IT projects, the feasibility aspect involves all the details, including specific risks, that must be dealt with in order to successfully complete the project, whereas the desirability aspect involves the high-level goals and the value to be derived from the project. This suggests that a concrete mental construal can be more beneficial when it comes to risk identification, as it may help IT PMs to focus on the feasibility aspect of the project and thus identify risks more easily.

In addition, during project planning IT PMs commonly decompose the project into phases, deliverables, and work packages. This practice typically involves the development of a work breakdown structure (WBS) in which the work required to carry out a project is broken down and organized in a hierarchical structure leading to the identification of discreet workpackages (PMI 2017). The WBS is a critical input for risk identification as it facilitates identifying potential risks at the micro level (PMI 2017). In other words, using the WBS allows IT PMs to understand specific risks in relation to each work-package. This indicates that thinking about specific examples of work involved in a project may be more beneficial in risk identification than viewing the project as a broader category of work. CLT suggests that segmentation of an action is driven by de-abstraction and aided by concrete mental construal (Trope and Liberman 2010). For example, prior CLT research found that people partition a future activity into many different subactivities when primed to construe the activity in a concrete manner (Fujita et al. 2006). This suggests that a concrete mental construal can be more beneficial when it comes to risk identification, as it may help IT PMs to focus on the specific examples of work-packages underlying the project and thus identify risks more easily.

## IT PMs’ Mental Construal of a Project Risk and Risk Impact Assessment (H2)

Once the risks associated with a project are identified, the next step is for IT PMs to assess each identified risk. The key benefit of this activity is that it enables IT PMs to identify and focus on high-priority risks (PMI 2017). Risk impact assessment involves determining what the potential impact on the project would be if the risk materialized.<sup>2</sup>

CLT explains that psychologically distant (near) events are construed abstractly (concretely) and abstract (concrete) events are perceived as psychologically distant (near) (Trope and Liberman 2010). Further, there are several different manifestations of psychological distance that are known to impact construal level, and one of them is hypothetical distance (hypotheticality). A hypothetically near event is one that is highly probable, whereas a hypothetically distant event is one that is highly improbable (Wakslak et al. 2006). High probability events are processed more concretely and felt to be more “real” compared with low probability events. In assessing the potential impact of a project risk, a concrete mental construal (e.g., a risk is probable) may lead IT PMs to perceive the risk to be more real and threatening whereas an abstract mental construal (e.g., a risk is improbable) may lead IT PMs to perceive the risk to be less real and not as threatening. This suggests that a concrete mental construal con cerning a project risk will affect IT PMs’ risk impact assessment, causing them to perceive a greater potential impact of the risk compared with an abstract mental construal.

In addition, in assessing risk people may decompose the structure of the risk so that each component can be evaluated in detail (Moeini and Rivard 2019b). Project risk also can be categorized by specific sources of risk, and this can help PMs understand specific sources from which a project risk may arise (PMI 2017). This exercise may aid IT PMs in determining the potential impact that each risk source can have on project objectives. This suggests that thinking about specific sources of risk may influence risk impact assessment such that IT PMs perceive a greater impact of a risk when they think about specific sources of the risk. From CLT, we know that thinking of specific examples of an event is associated with concrete mental construal. Therefore, a concrete mental construal concerning a project risk may cause IT PMs to perceive a greater potential impact of the risk compared with an abstract mental construal.

Based on the above arguments, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2. IT PMs with a concrete mental construal of an IT project risk will perceive a greater potential impact of the risk than IT PMs with an abstract mental construal of an IT project risk.

## IT PMs’ Mental Construal of a Project Risk and Risk Response Planning (H3)

After risk assessment, the next activity in IT project risk management is risk response planning (Moeini and Rivard 2019b), which involves estimating the amount of effort and resources required to respond to the risks that have been identified and judged to have high impact.

Prior research on judgment and decision-making suggests that people tend to underestimate the time that will be required to plan for a future event and later realize (as the event gets closer) that they cannot complete all of tasks associated with the event (Kahneman and Tversky 1982). According to CLT, people pay greater attention to details when a future event is felt to be real and psychologically close (e.g., when an event is highly probable). This indicates when a future event is psychologically distant (e.g., when hypotheticality is high), people may ignore specific details associated with the event. In contrast, when a future event is psychologically close (e.g., when hypotheticality is low), people are more apt to consider specific details associated with the event. This suggests that a concrete mental construal concerning a project risk will affect IT PMs’ risk response planning, causing them to estimate that more effort and resources will be required to respond to the project risk compared with an abstract mental construal.

In addition, prior research on judgment and decisionmaking suggests that predictions of future experiences are subject to a variety of biases that stem from underweighting contextual and incidental features (Kahneman et al. 2006, Gilbert and Wilson 2007). According to CLT, these biases are accentuated when people pay more attention to abstract attributes of an event rather than specific details pertaining to the event (Trope and Liberman 2010). In practice, decomposing risk into specific categories is known to help develop effective risk responses as it allows PMs to focus attention and effort on specific areas of risk (PMI 2017). Further, risk can be represented in a hierarchical structure with many subrisk events or categories; this practice is referred to as developing a risk breakdown structure (RBS) (PMI 2017). This suggests that in risk response planning a concrete mental construal (relative to an abstract mental construal) concerning a project risk may help IT PMs to focus more on specific examples or concrete instantiations of the risk. Further, thinking about specific examples of the risk may help IT PMs to understand what may be required to respond to the risk in a concrete manner. This suggests that a concrete mental construal concerning an IT project risk may cause IT PMs to be more conservative in risk planning (as compared with an abstract mental construal). More specifically, IT PMs with a concrete mental construal may generate more conservative estimates of how much effort and resources will be required to respond to a project risk compared with IT PMs with an abstract mental construal.

Based on the above arguments, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 3. IT PMs with a concrete mental construal of an IT project risk will perceive that more effort and resources are required to respond to the risk than IT PMs with an abstract mental construal of an IT project risk.

## IT PMs’ Mental Construal of a Risk Response and Risk Response Enactment (H4)

After risk response planning, the next activity in IT project risk management involves determining whether to enact a risk response to deal with a particular risk that might undermine the project (Moein and Rivard 2019b). Prior research suggests that enacting appropriate risk responses is important for project success (Barki et al. 2001).

When deciding whether to enact a risk response, research suggests that PMs identify the benefits and costs of a potential risk response (Boehm 1991). Further, Moeini and Rivard (2019a) suggest that IT PMs attitude toward a risk response consists of two dimensions: expected desired effects and potential side effects of enacting a risk response. In an empirical study, Moeini and Rivard (2019a) found that the expected desired effects are more important than the potential side effects in shaping IT PMs’ attitude toward a risk response, which, in turn, positively influences IT PMs’ intention to enact the risk response. Researchers also suggest that risk responses are often not enacted because PMs do not perceive them to be worth pursuing due to time and cost constraints (Kutsch and Hall 2009). From CLT, we know that people pay more attention to desirability than feasibility of an action when the action is construed in an abstract manner. This indicates that an abstract mental construal of a risk response may lead IT PMs to focus more on desirability (versus feasibility) of enacting the risk response, thus influencing their risk response enactment decisions.

In addition, decisions about future actions (e.g., responding to project risks) are evaluated based on one’s considerations in favor of (pro) and against (con) the actions. In deciding whether to enact a risk response, IT PMs must evaluate the pros and cons associated with it. Prior CLT research suggests that pros are more salient in making decisions for actions that are construed abstractly whereas cons are more salient in making decisions for actions that are construed concretely (Eyal et al. 2004). This is because considerations against an action tend to be subordinate to considerations in favor of the action (Eyal et al. 2004). In other words, cons tend to be only relevant if there are sufficient pros supporting the action. Therefore, in considering whether to enact a risk response, an abstract mental construal of the risk response may lead IT PMs to focus primarily on the pros associated with the risk responses and fail to consider arguments against taking the risk response. In contrast, a concrete mental construal of the risk response may lead IT PMs to be more likely to consider arguments against taking the risk response.

Based on the above arguments, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 4. IT PMs with an abstract mental construal of a risk response will be more willing to enact the risk response than IT PMs with a concrete mental construal of the risk response.

## Overview of Experiments

In this research, we conducted a series of four experiments to investigate the role of IT PMs’ mental construal across the four project risk management activities (risk identification, risk impact assessment, risk response planning, and risk response enactment). We chose the experimental method because the major goal of this research was to determine if there was a causal relationship between IT PMs’ mental construal and how they engage with various project risk management activities. Internal validity is of paramount concern in testing causal relationships between variables and the experimental method is universally acknowledged as providing high internal validity, making experiments the most appropriate choice for establishing causal relationships. In each experiment, we adapted an existing construal level manipulation approach and manipulated IT PM’s mental construal through a specific aspect related to construal level: desirability/feasibility (Experiments 1 and 4), hypotheticality (Experiment 2), and category/ exemplar (Experiment 3).<sup>3</sup> Further, each manipulation was designed to have a specific construal target, such as a project (Experiment 1), a risk (Experiments 2 and 3), or a risk response (Experiment 4). In what follows, we discuss the design and procedure of each experiment as well as the results obtained (please see Online Appendix A for an overview of the experiments and Online Appendix B for the constructs and measures used in each experiment).

## Experiment 1: Risk Identi<sup>fi</sup>cation Experimental Design and Participants

Experiment 1 was designed to test H1 (risk identification) and involved a basic randomized design comparing two treatments (Shadish et al. 2002, p. 258) in which we manipulated IT PMs’ mental construal of an IT project to be either concrete or abstract. We recruited a total of 68 IT PMs (29 males and 39 females) in North America through a survey panel services provider and randomly assigned them to one of two experimental groups: the concrete mental construal group and the abstract mental construal group.<sup>4</sup> IT PMs who participated in Experiment 1 were, on average, 39 years old and had, on average, 9 years of experience in IT and 6 years as an IT PM.

## Decision Task, Procedures, and Measures

IT PMs were asked to read a vignette about an IT project that involved building an analytics-based advising system at a large university (the actual vignette used is shown in Online Appendix C) and identify the risks that they perceived with the IT project. A vignette is “a short, carefully constructed description of a person, object, or situation, representing a systematic combination of characteristics” (Atzmüller and Steiner 2010, p. 128). Vignette-based studies have been used in a wide variety of scientific fields, including social, behavioral, and health sciences (Alexander and Becker 1978, Bachmann et al. 2008, Wallander 2009) as well as in IS research (Keil et al. 2000, Lee et al. 2012). A major benefit of using vignettes in scientific studies is that researchers are able to manipulate specific variables of interest through a written stimulus while controlling other factors, offering “a glimpse into how individuals thoughts, feelings, behaviors, and decisions are affected by factors that may not be easily accessible in real-life situations because of confounding sources of variability that cannot be controlled” (Evans et al. 2015, p. 161). In our research context, the use of a vignette-based approach was deemed valuable because it was not feasible to have PMs make decisions based on their own individual projects. Such an approach would have made it very difficult to control for the wide variety of differences across projects (e.g., size, application type), thus limiting our ability to probe the causal relationship between IT PMs mental construal and risk management activities.

The experiment was conducted in an online setting in which IT PMs visited our web-based experiment via a link provided in the invitation email. Upon visiting the web-based experiment, IT PMs were asked to read the vignette of an IT project, introduced to a construal level manipulation, and asked to identify the risks that they perceived with the IT project and write them down using a numbered list. IT PMs were also asked to answer two questions pertaining to risk propensity. For risk propensity, we adopted the measurement item used by Keil et al. (2000) and created a second (reverse scaled) item. Risk propensity is a dispositional factor that can be defined as the tendency of a decision maker to take risky actions (Sitkin and Pablo 1992) and was included as a control variable. Next, IT PMs were asked to answer a series of questions regarding the perceived level of effort required to complete the manipulation task (Muraven et al. 1998, Fujita et al. 2006), the perceived level of difficulty associated with the manipulation task (Muraven et al. 1998, Fujita et al. 2006), selfcontrol (Muraven et al. 1998, Fujita et al. 2006), willpower (Muraven et al. 1998, Fujita et al. 2006),<sup>5</sup> and positive and negative affect (using the positive and negative affect schedule (PANAS; Watson et al. 1988). These variables were included to rule out the possibility that any differences observed between concrete and abstract mental construal groups might be due to other confounding factors that may have been inadvertently introduced by our construal level manipulation. Further, IT PMs were asked whether they were aware of the purpose of this research and whether they thought that any task influenced their performance on any subsequent tasks or the way they answered the subsequent questions. These questions were included to examine whether the IT PMs were able to guess the purpose of the experiment (Gino et al. 2011). Lastly, IT PMs were asked to provide demographic and work experience-related information and thanked for their participation.

## Construal Level Manipulation

Our construal level manipulation was modeled after the why/how approach that is widely used and accepted in the CLT literature. “High- and low-level construals differ . . . in whether end states of actions (the “why” of activities) or the means by which actions are accomplished (the “how” of activities) are emphasized” (Wakslak and Trope 2009, p. 54). Thus, the why/how approach is commonly used to manipulate the desirability/feasibility aspect of construal level; the purpose of an action (i.e., why an action is undertaken) speaks to the issue of desirability, whereas the means of accomplishing an action (i.e., how to perform an action) speaks to the issue of feasibility (Trope and Liberman 2010). Prior research manipu lated construal level by instructing subjects to complete a task that involves describing why or how an action is performed (Freitas et al. 2004, Stephan et al. 2011). For example, Freitas et al. (2004) instructed subjects to consider either why they would engage in an activity to improve and maintain health (abstract mental construal), or how they would engage in the same activity (concrete mental construal). In our experiment, we asked IT PMs to write down either why (abstract mental construal) or how (concrete menta construal) the project team is implementing the system.

## Results

Manipulation Check. We conducted an independent manipulation check. To do this, we recruited an independent group of IT PMs (different from those who took part in the actual experiment), presented them with the same construal level manipulation used in the actual experiment, and conducted a manipulation check using an established construal level manipulation check task. An independent manipulation check avoids potential problems that might result from placing a manipulation check before or after the decision task. Specifically, inserting a manipulation check task prior to the decision task might have created possible confounds. Further, because the effect of a construal level manipulation can be fleeting, it would have been more difficult to detect if the manipulation check were done after subjects completed the decision task. Our manipulation check confirmed that IT PMs in the abstract mental construal group showed a higher level of abstract thinking than IT PMs in the concrete mental construal group. The detailed procedure and results of the manipulation check are reported in Online Appendix D.

Assessment of Measurement Reliability and Validity, and Correlations Among Constructs. First, we assessed the reliability and validity of our measurement items for risk propensity. Specifically, we found that the risk propensity measures had adequate loadings and acceptable Cronbach’s alpha. Following the guidelines suggested by MacKenzie et al. (2011), we confirmed that the average variance extracted (AVE) exceeded the recommended threshold (see Online Appendix E for details). Second, we examined the correlations among constructs to assess if the focal construct (IT PMs’ mental construal) was significantly related to other constructs hypothesized to be in its nomological network. It was found that IT

PMs’ mental construal had a significant negative correlation with the number of project risks identified, indicating good nomological validity (see Online Appendix E).

Descriptive Statistics and Hypothesis Testing. In our experiment, IT PMs were asked to identify project risks and write them down using a “numbered” list. We evaluated the list of project risks identified by each IT PM and confirmed that each risk was separate and distinct (i.e., no redundancies and no instances of multiple similar risks that could be counted as a single risk). We then counted the number of risks identified by each IT PM. We conducted an ANCOVA to compare the average number of project risks identified by IT PMs in the concrete versus abstract mental construal groups. We included risk propensity, IT PM experience, and gender as covariates in the ANCOVA, as these variables might affect IT PMs’ risk identification. The results of the ANCOVA indicated that IT PMs in the concrete mental construal group $( n = 3 2 , \mathrm { M } = 2 . 5 0 , \mathrm { S D } = 0 . 6 2 )$ identified a greater number of risks associated with the project than did IT PMs in the abstract mental construal group (n = 36, M = 1.72, SD = 1.19), and this difference was statistically significant (F(1,63) = 11.96, $p < 0 . 0 1 , \eta _ { \mathrm { p } } ^ { 2 } = 0 . 1 7 )$ , thus offering support for H1.

Ruling out Rival Explanations. We examined whether the abstract mental construal manipulation task differed from the concrete mental construal manipulation task in terms of both the perceived level of effort required to complete the task and the perceived level of difficulty associated with the task. Further, we examined whether the construal level manipulation had unintended effects on self-control, willpower, positive affect, and negative affect. If such unintended effects occurred, they could represent confounds in that we could not be certain that it was the difference in IT PMs’ mental construal per se that caused the results that we observed. To rule out this possibility, we conducted a MANOVA, and the results indicated that the construal level manipulation did not have a significant effect on any of these variables at the p = 0.05 level. Thus, we ruled out a number of potential rival explanations, giving us increased confidence that the difference in project risk identification we observed was due to IT PMs’ mental construal. We also examined the responses by IT PMs about the purpose of the research and found no evidence indicating that they were able to guess the purpose of the experiment.

## Experiment 2: Risk Impact Assessment Experimental Design and Participants

Experiment 2 was designed to test H2 (risk impact assessment) and involved a basic randomized design comparing two treatments in which we manipulated

IT PMs’ mental construal of a project risk to be either concrete or abstract. We recruited a total of 70 IT PMs (34 males and 36 females) in North America through the same survey panel services provider used in Experiment 1.<sup>6</sup> IT PMs who participated in Experiment 2 were, on average, 41 years old and had, on average, 9 years of experience in IT and 6 years as an IT PM.

## Decision Task, Procedures, and Measures

Experiment 2 involved the same vignette used in Experiment 1 and was also web-based. After reading the IT project vignette, IT PMs were presented with five risks associated with the project (they were told that the project team had identified these five project risks), introduced to a construal level manipulation, and asked to estimate the impact that each risk would have on the project should it materialize (0: lowest impact and 100: highest impact). The five risks that IT PMs were asked to evaluate were: (1) data quality issues during data migration, (2) failing to determine requirements due to a lack of participation from students, (3) privacy issues that may arise dealing with student records, (4) communication problems with the software testing vendor, and (5) failing to launch the system as scheduled. IT PMs were also asked to answer two questions pertaining to risk propensity (the same measures used in Experiment 1 were used in Experiment 2). Next, IT PMs were asked to answer questions pertaining to positive and negative affect (using PANAS) (Watson et al. 1988). We included positive and negative affect to rule out the possibility that any differences observed were due to changes in affect caused by our construal level manipulation.<sup>7</sup> Further, IT PMs were asked whether they were aware of the purpose of this research and whether they thought that any task influenced their performance on any subsequent tasks or the way they answered the subsequent questions. These questions were included to examine whether the IT PMs were able to guess the purpose of the experiment. Lastly, IT PMs were asked to provide demographic and work experience-related information and thanked for their participation.

## Construal Level Manipulation

The construal level manipulation used in Experiment 2 was based on hypotheticality. Hypotheticality refers to how probable a future event is (Armor and Sackett 2006). Several CLT studies have manipulated construal level through hypotheticality. For example, in an experiment that tested the causal effect of construal level on how people categorize objects (Wakslak et al. 2006), participants were asked to imagine that they were planning on engaging in a series of four activities that were described as either “almost certain to occur” (low hypotheticality) or “almost certain not to occur” (high hypotheticality). In our experiment, we told participants that each of the five risks associated with the IT project was either “almost certain to occur” (low hypotheticality) or “almost certain not to occur” (high hypotheticality).

## Experiment 3: Risk Response Planning Experimental Design and Participants

Experiment 3 was designed to test H3 (risk response planning) and involved a basic randomized design comparing two treatments in which we manipulated IT PMs’ mental construal of a project risk to be either concrete or abstract. We recruited 70 IT PMs (34 males and 36 females) in North America to take part in Experiment 3, and randomly assigned them to either the concrete or abstract mental construal group. IT PMs who participated in Experiment 3 were, on average, 36 years old and had, on average, 12 years of experience in IT and 5 years as an IT PM.

## Decision Task, Manipulation, Procedures, and Measures

As in the previous experiments, this experiment was conducted in an online setting. The experiment involved identifying specific risks (concrete mental construal) or general categories (abstract mental construal) for 16 risk areas associated with developing a sales management system for external sale (see Online Appendix C). Our construal level manipulation was modeled after the category/exemplar approach that has been widely used in the CLT literature. Specifically, prior CLT studies have manipulated construal level by asking participants to come up with either superordinate categories or subordinate exemplars of objects. For example, Fujita et al. (2006) provided participants with 40 words (e.g., computer, movie, lunch), and asked them to write down either a superordinate category (abstract mental construal) or a subordinate exemplar (concrete mental construal) of each word. For example, if the word “dog” was given participants in the abstract mental construal group might write down “animal” while participants in the concrete mental construal group might write down “poodle.”

In our experiment, IT PMs were told that they have been assigned as a PM to lead a project that is expected to deliver a sales management system for external sales. Further, IT PMs were informed that the PM and the project team have identified a list of 16 IT project risk areas.<sup>8</sup> We then introduced the manipulation of construal level. Similar to the approach used by Fujita et al. (2006), in the abstract mental construal group we asked IT PMs to write down a general category that each risk area belongs under (e.g., if the risk area is “market demand,” a more general category might be “external risk” as market demand is an example of external risk). In the concrete mental construal group, we asked IT PMs to write down a specific risk that falls within each area. In our experiment, IT PMs in both concrete and abstract mental construal groups were presented with the exact same list of risk areas, but they were asked to come up with either a subordinate exemplar or a superordinate category for each risk area.

Following the construal level manipulation, IT PMs were asked to indicate how much effort and resources they thought would be required to respond to each of the 16 risk areas on a 7-point scale. IT PMs were then asked to answer questions pertaining to risk propensity using the same scales used in the previous experiments. Next, IT PMs were asked to answer a series of questions that pertain to the perceived level of effort required to complete the manipulation task (Muraven et al. 1998, Fujita et al. 2006), the perceived level of difficulty associated with the manipulation task (Muraven et al. 1998, Fujita et al. 2006), selfcontrol (Muraven et al. 1998, Fujita et al. 2006), willpower (Muraven et al. 1998, Fujita et al. 2006), and positive and negative affect (using PANAS) (Watson et al. 1988). These variables were included to rule out rival explanations. In addition, IT PMs were asked whether they were aware of the purpose of this research and whether they thought that any task influenced their performance on any subsequent tasks or the way they answered the subsequent questions. Lastly, IT PMs were asked to provide demographic and work experience-related information and thanked for their participation.

Robustness Check. In Experiment 3, we used an integral manipulation in which we manipulated IT PMs’ mental construal within the context of a decision task. To add robustness to the findings, we conducted an additional experiment employing an incidental manipulation (i.e., one that occurs outside the context of the decision task). Both integral and incidental manipulations of mental construal have been used before in CLT research (see, e.g., Wakslak and Trope 2009) and using both types of manipulations adds robustness to the findings regarding the effect of mental construal. ## Experiment 4: Risk Response Enactment Experimental Design and Participants

Experiment 4 was designed to test H4 (risk response enactment) and involved a basic randomized design comparing two treatments in which we manipulated IT PMs’ mental construal of a risk response to be either concrete or abstract. A total of 70 IT PMs took part in Experiment 4 (43 males and 27 females), and they were randomly assigned to either the concrete mental construal group or the abstract mental construal group. IT PMs who participated in Experiment 4 were, on average, 36 years old and had, on average, 9 years of experience in IT and 6 years as an IT PM.

## Decision Task, Procedures, Manipulation, and Measures

As in the previous experiments, Experiment 4 was conducted in an online setting. IT PMs were asked to read a vignette of an IT project that involved developing an application to read and translate the barcodes that are used to track packages (see Online Appendix C). In the vignette, “conflict among team members over the best development methodology to employ” was introduced as a project risk that had been identified. After reading the vignette, IT PMs were asked to complete a task that was designed to induce either a concrete mental construal or an abstract mental construal of a risk response. Specifically, the construal level manipulation was modeled after the how/why approach widely used and accepted in the CLT literature (Freitas et al. 2004, Stephan et al.

2011). Using this approach, the construal level is manipulated by instructing subjects to write either how or why they would do an activity. In our experiment, we instructed IT PMs to write either how or why they would enact a series of four risk responses: (1) mitigate the risk, (2) reserve resources to manage the risk, (3) prevent the risk, and (4) monitor the risk.<sup>9</sup> Next, IT PMs were asked to indicate whether they would: (1) mitigate the risk, (2) reserve resources to manage the risk, (3) prevent the risk, and (4) monitor the risk. IT PMs were also asked to answer a series of questions regarding risk propensity, the perceived level of effort required to complete the manipulation task, the perceived level of difficulty associated with the manipulation task, self-control, willpower, and positive and negative affect (using the same scales as in the other experiments), and to provide demographic information. Lastly, IT PMs were asked whether they were aware of the purpose of this research and whether they thought that any task influenced their performance on any subsequent tasks or the way they answered the subsequent questions and thanked for their participation.

## Results

Manipulation Check. We recruited an independent group of IT PMs (different from those who took part in the actual experiment) and conducted an independent manipulation check. Our manipulation check confirmed that IT PMs in the abstract mental construal group showed a higher level of abstract thinking than IT PMs in the concrete mental construal group. The detailed procedure and results of the manipulation check are reported in Online Appendix D.

Assessment of Measurement Reliability and Validity, and Correlations Among Constructs. First, we assessed the reliability and validity of our measurement items for risk propensity by examining factor loadings, Cronbach’s alpha, and AVE. The results of our analyses indicated good reliability and validity (see Online Appendix E). Next, we examined the correlations among constructs to assess if IT PMs’ mental construal was significantly related to other constructs hypothesized to be in its nomological network. Our analysis showed that IT PMs’ mental construal had a significant positive correlation with risk response enactment decision (see Online Appendix E).

Descriptive Statistics and Hypothesis Testing. First, we examined the means and standard deviations of risk response enactment decision across the concrete versus abstract mental construal groups. We found that for each of the four risk responses IT PMs in the abstract mental construal group were more willing to enact a risk response than those in the concrete mental construal group (see Table 5). Next, we created a composite measure for risk response enactment decision by computing the mean across the four risk responses (i.e., risk mitigation, risk reserve, risk prevention, and risk monitoring). We then conducted an ANCOVA, to compare the concrete versus abstract mental construal groups. We included IT PM experience, gender, and risk propensity as covariates. Ruling out Rival Explanations. We conducted a M-ANOVA to examine whether the construal level manipulation had significant effects on the perceived level of effort required to complete the task, the perceived level of difficulty associated with the task, self-control, willpower, positive affect, and negative affect. Results indicated that our construal level manipulation did not have a significant effect on any of these variables at the p = 0.05 level. We also examined the responses by IT PMs about the purpose of the research and found no evidence that they were able to guess the purpose of the experiment.

Robustness Check. To add robustness to the findings obtained in Experiment 4, we conducted an additional experiment employing an incidental manipulation of mental construal. The results of this additional experiment were consistent with the results of Experiment 4, thus adding robustness to our findings (see Online Appendix G for the details of this additional experiment and its results).

## General Discussion

Reports continue to suggest that inadequate risk management is a leading cause of IT project failure (Flyvbjerg and Budzier 2011, Bloch et al. 2012) and that lack of attention to details during the planning phase of a project may be a contributing factor. In this research, we integrate CLT with the literature on IT project risk management to investigate previously unexplored relationships concerning how IT PMs mental construal affects a series of four risk man agement activities: (1) risk identification, (2) risk impact assessment, (3) risk response planning, and (4) risk response enactment. We recruited a total of 805 IT PMs for this research and conducted a series of four laboratory experiments. In our experiments, we found that a concrete (relative to abstract) mental construal of a project leads IT PMs to identify a greater number of risks associated with the project (Experi ment 1), and a concrete (relative to abstract) mental construal of a project risk leads IT PMs to perceive a greater potential impact of the risk (Experiment 2) and to perceive that more effort and resources are required for responding to the risk (Experiment 3). Further, we found that an abstract (relative to concrete) construal level of a risk response leads IT PMs to be more willing to enact the risk response (Experiment 4). In the remainder of this paper, we discuss the implications for both research and practice, followed by the limitations of our work and a brief conclusion.

## Implications for Research

This research has important implications for IT project risk management, for CLT, and for IT project management.

## Extending our Understanding of IT Project Risk Management

Much of the prior research on IT project risk management has focused on the development of checklists (e.g., Schmidt et al. 2001), frameworks (e.g., Lyytinen et al. 1996), questionnaires (e.g., Wallace et al. 2004), risk management models (e.g., Lyytinen et al. 1998), and risk response strategies (e.g., Ropponen and Lyytinen 1997). Furthermore, risk scholars have suggested that individuals and managers do not always conform to the normative decision-making model (March and Shapira 1987, Lyytinen et al. 1998). In a recent review article, Moeini and Rivard (2019b) suggested that there is a relative scarcity of studies aiming to understand why IT PMs manage risks differently from normative prescriptions. Although several studies have aimed to offer theoretical explanations (from a nonrational decision-making perspective) of risk perception in general (Slovic 1987), risk-taking behavior in organizations (March and Shapira 1987), and IT project risk management (Lyytinen et al. 1998), we are not aware of an attempt to provide a theoretically based understanding of why IT PMs often fail to pay attention to details during the planning phase of a project when it comes to risk management. By drawing on CLT, we offer fresh insights into how IT PMs’ mental construals can lead them to focus on the trees or the forest and how this can influence critical risk management activities. In our experiments, we found that seeing the trees is beneficial in risk identification, risk impact assessment, and risk response planning. In contrast, we found that seeing the forest is beneficial in risk response enactment.

Given the obvious and long-standing question of why IT project risk management theory is not having a greater impact on IT project risk management practices, our research suggests that along with other possible explanations (e.g., a lack of time to engage in risk management activities, a lack of adequate culture to support risk management practices, or perfunctory approaches to risk management), one simple explanation is that IT PMs’ mental construals impact their judgment and choice when dealing with risks and impede them from getting the maximal benefit from the risk management activities that they do perform. 10

In addition, in theorizing the relationships between IT PMs’ mental construals and risk management activities, we focused on specific aspects related to construal level and their applicability to specific construal target/risk management activity combinations. Specifically, our theorizing suggests that (a) desirability/feasibility and category/exemplar are relevant for project construal in risk identification, (b) hypotheticality and category/exemplar are relevant for risk construal in risk impact assessment and risk response planning, and (c) desirability/feasibility and pros/cons are relevant for risk response construal in risk response enactment.

## Extending our Understanding of CLT

Our research extends CLT by theorizing and then showing empirically how mental construal affects context-specific activities that are important in IT project risk management. By incorporating CLT within the context of IT project risk management, we explain how the theory operates within that context, which represents a distinct contribution (Eisenhardt and Graebner 2007). In terms of CLT, IT project risk management represents a novel context, and by exploiting this novel context we were able to consider novel dependent variables that have not previously been investigated by CLT scholars. This novelty extends to the relationships that we examined and lends new insight into what is influenced by construal level. If we consider that theory consists of constructs, assumptions, relationships, and boundary conditions, our contribution to CLT lies in bringing new constructs into focus in the form of context-specific DVs and new relationships between construal level and these DVs.

## Expanding Our Understanding of IT Project Management

One of the key problems in IT project management relates to optimism (Brooks 1995). Our research also offers a new theoretical perspective in understanding this IT project management problem. In the IT project management literature, optimistic estimation has been the most commonly cited form of optimism, that is, underestimating the time required to complete a task or a project (Abdel-Hamid and Madnick 1989, Brooks 1995, Shmueli et al. 2016). However, research suggests that optimism can manifest itself in different ways, such as overlooking or underestimating risk (Lovallo and Kahneman 2003). Thus, the dependent variables investigated in our research can be understood as different manifestations of optimism: overlooking project risks, underestimating risk impact, underestimating the effort and resources required for risk response, and not enacting a risk response.

To date, researchers have offered limited explanations as to why optimism might be so prevalent in software development. In his seminal book on software engineering The Mythical Man-Month, Brooks (1995, p. 14) suggested that optimism is a key problem in software development and that “all programmers are optimists.” Similarly, Jones and McLean (1970, p. 5) suggested that “as a class, programmers tend to be eternally optimistic. . . they fail to be realistic about the problems of system design and the time necessary to complete a complex system.” More recently, researchers suggested that managers are more prone to optimistic estimation than programmers (Jørgensen and Molokken-Ostvold 2004). Much of the prior research seems to suggest that optimism may be a personality issue. Another explanation for optimism in software development is concerned with the nature of software development work and technologies. Abdel-Hamid (1988) suggested that “poor visibility” in software development is a factor contributing to the underestimation problem. Against this backdrop, our research offers a new perspective that advances the scholarly understanding of optimism in IT project management by focusing on IT PMs’ mental construal. Specifically, our research shows that an abstract mental construal can cause optimism in risk identification, risk impact assessment, and risk response planning, but can reduce optimism in risk response enactment.

Thus far, only a few methods have been proposed and tested for combating optimism in project planning. We believe that a CLT lens can help to explain why these methods may be effective. Reference class forecasting is one method for addressing the problem of optimistic estimation and it involves predicting the resources required for a project by identifying a class of similar projects that have been completed in the past (Kahneman and Tversky 1982, Flyvbjerg 2006). Prior IT project management research found that providing reference information about actual completion times of similar tasks reduces time underestimation, scope overload, and overrequirement problems (Shmueli et al. 2016). CLT has the potential to offer insights into why reference class forecasting is useful in reducing optimism. Plans tend to be abstract and feel psychologically distant, but looking at the actual outcomes of past projects (which are concrete and psychologically near) may reduce optimism.

Task segmentation also has been shown to be effective in reducing optimism in project planning. In a series of experiments, Forsyth and Burt (2008) found that the time allocated for a single task was smaller than the sum of times allocated for individual subtasks (of the single task). In software development, decomposing large projects into smaller components is a recommended practice for time estimation (Bajaj et al. 2006, Halkjelsvik and Jørgensen 2012), and empirical evidence suggests that this technique can improve time estimation (Connolly and Dean 1997, Hill et al. 2000, Jørgensen 2004). We believe CLT offers insights concerning why task segmentation can be effective. CLT explains that when we think in abstract terms, we tend to categorize things into broader categories (e.g., animal). In contrast, when we think in concrete terms, we tend to categorize things into more specific subcategories (e.g., dogs) (Liberman et al. 2002). We suggest that dividing a task into subtasks involves deabstraction and leads people to think more concretely about the task, hence reducing optimism.

## Implications for Practice

Our research has important implications for practice because it offers a path toward the development of interventions (based on manipulation of construal level) that can improve the practice of IT project risk management. One possible intervention is to have IT PMs incorporate how exercises into their risk identification practice. Once a project is selected and approved, IT PMs should focus on how specific elements of the project will be implemented as this can help them think through implementation plans in greater detail, helping them to identify holes or cracks in the plans.

Another possible intervention is to have IT PMs separately evaluate the probability and impact of risks as they work through the activity of risk assessment. Because our research suggests that high probability risks are perceived to have higher impact (because of reduced psychological distance) and low probability risks are perceived to have lower impact (because of increased psychological distance), IT PMs may underestimate the potential impact of risks that are less likely to occur if the assessment of probability and impact are done together. Prior research has suggested that many IT projects fail because of improbable events that have a significant impact (Flyvbjerg and Budzier 2011). Thus, we suggest that IT PMs adopt the practice of evaluating probability and impact independently (i.e., when a PM evaluates the potential impact of a risk, the information about the estimated likelihood of the risk is not presented), as this can help reduce the potential mistake of underestimating the risk impact.

A third possible intervention relates to risk response planning, where we suggest that decomposing risk into specific risks can help to combat optimism in risk response planning. This approach is consistent with the concept of developing a risk breakdown structure (RBS), which is a hierarchical organization of project risks arranged by risk categories (PMI 2017). An RBS organizes various risk areas and identifies the specific risks associated with each risk area. The idea is that developing an RBS can help to identify risks that one might not otherwise think of. As demonstrated in our research, the risk decomposition exercise that is inherent to the development of an RBS can reduce potential optimism associated with risk response planning. As part of the RBS process, we further suggest that organizations adopt the practice of identifying specific examples of each risk area (i.e., concrete mental construal) prior to estimating the effort and resources required for risk response, as this should help to generate a more conservative estimate and reduce optimism.

Finally, it is important for IT PMs to enact appropriate risk responses. We recommend that organizations advise IT PMs to focus on the pros (abstract mental construal), rather than the cons (concrete mental construal), associated with risk responses. An abstract mental construal will help IT PMs focus on arguments in favor of enacting risk responses, rather than arguments against doing so.

## Limitations and Directions for Future Research

As with any research, our work is not without limitations, and it is appropriate to discuss these along with some suggestions for future research. To investigate the impact of IT PMs’ mental construal on IT project risk management activities, we chose the experimental method, because it allowed us to create the type of highly controlled setting conducive to investigating causal relationships. Experiments are sometimes criticized for not being sufficiently realistic, for not capturing the complexities of actual organizational settings, or for being carried out with student subjects who may lack the domain specific knowledge required to engage with the stimulus material in the same way as a practicing manager. Nonetheless, the experimental method, including the use of vignettes, has been widely employed in research on judgment and decision-making, including the Nobel prize-winning work of Kahneman and Tversky (1979, 1982) and there is a long tradition of using this approach within the field of information systems (Benbasat 1990).

While experiments are sometimes criticized due to perceived weakness in terms of external validity, they offer a level of internal validity that is unparalleled relative to other research methods. This trade-off of higher internal validity and lower external validity is commonplace in experiments and should not necessarily be construed as a weakness. As noted by Cook and Campbell (1979, p. 84), “jeopardizing internal validity for the sake of increasing external validity usually entails a minimal gain for a considerable loss.” Experimental designs should therefore be evaluated on whether they are likely to increase our understanding of human behavior, not on whether they mimic organizational settings (Dobbins et al. 1988).

We recognize that experiments of the type we conducted tend to sacrifice some external validity in exchange for their high internal validity. Even though we did our best to create realistic vignettes and decision choices, we acknowledge that there is a certain artificiality associated with creating a controlled experiment that is isolated from an actual work setting. Thus, further research is warranted to confirm that the effect of IT PMs’ mental construal would translate to actual project management settings and in situations that involve large and complex IT projects. Along these lines, an obvious direction for future research is to develop risk management interventions based on CLT, test their efficacy in field settings, and refine such interventions as needed to improve risk management practices and outcomes.

While more research is needed to confirm and apply our findings in field settings, we believe our work offers strong empirical findings and a new path forward for understanding and improving IT project risk management. The fact that we conducted multiple experiments, examined multiple manipulations of construal level, used both integral and incidental manipulations of construal level, conducted rigorous manipulation checks, and took steps to rule out rival explanations, adds robustness to our findings. We also note that our research involved the participation of 805 practicing IT PMs who were in a good position to possess the domain specific knowledge and experience needed to understand the vignettes that were presented and to make decisions regarding the IT project risk management activities.

In addition, in each experiment we utilized one specific manipulation approach that is focused on one aspect related to construal level. While CLT suggests that all these aspects are interrelated, one may ask whether the same results can be obtained if a different manipulation approach that is focused on a different aspect is utilized. Therefore, further empirical investigation employing different manipulations may be useful to extend the findings of our research Further, in each experiment we focused on a specific construal target (project, risk, or risk response) and one may ask whether the same results can be obtained when the construal target changes. For example, in Experiment 4, we manipulated IT PMs’ mental construals of a risk response and investigated its impact on risk response enactment, but whether one’s construal of a risk itself can also influence his or her risk response enactment was not investigated in this research. We think that in deciding whether to enact a risk response, it is more natural for PMs to construe the risk response than the risk itself.<sup>11</sup> If IT PMs’ construals are focused on a risk, an abstract mental construal might lead IT PMs to not enact a response (as the risk would feel psychologically more distant and less real). Therefore, we suggest that further research is warranted to investigate the effect of IT PMs mental construals of a project risk on risk response enactment.

Our work also opens new avenues of research for IT project management researchers who may find it fruitful to explore the effect of mental construal in other problem areas that extend beyond risk management, such as the reluctance to report bad news on troubled IT projects (Smith and Keil 2003), the escalation of commitment to troubled IT projects (Keil et al. 2000, Lee et al. 2012), and the tendency to underestimate the required development effort on IT projects (Jørgensen and Grimstad 2011). Finally, in our research we conducted additional experiments for robustness checks in only 2 of the 4 experiments, and we acknowledge this as a limitation.

## Conclusion

Risk management is widely believed to be critical for successful IT project management. Still, the situation in most organizations is that risk management activities are performed in a perfunctory manner. Even in organizations that make a serious attempt to put risk management tools into practice, it is not clear that IT PMs are able to reap the maximal benefits that could be derived from engaging in risk management. Among the things that must change if we are to advance both IT project risk management theory and practice is a solid theory to inform our understanding of how IT PMs can engage in a more productive way with risk management activities. In this research, we introduce a recognized theory from psychology—construal level theory—to examine previously unexplored relationships involving the impact of IT PMs’ mental construal on various risk management activities. Our research shows that IT PMs’ mental construal can influence a broad range of project risk management activities. IT PMs may not be able to see the “trees” in risk identification, risk impact assessment, and risk response planning. Further, they may miss the “forest” when deciding whether to enact a risk response. We believe that a CLT lens not only improves our theoretical understanding of IT project risk management but also offers a promising avenue for improving IT project risk management practices by pointing the way toward interventions that can be used in actual project settings.
