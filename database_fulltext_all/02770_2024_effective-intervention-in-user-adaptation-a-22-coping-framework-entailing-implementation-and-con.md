## Abstract

Despite the proliferation of information technology applications worldwide, successful technology implementation in organizations remains a formidable challenge. Whether organizations can actualize the benefits of new technology depends critically on how end users evaluate and cope with it. Various intervention practices have been proven to be effective in facilitating user adaptation in the existing literature. However, research that systematically examines the impacts of intervention practices across implementation stages and usage contexts is still rare. Leveraging coping theory, we propose a 2×2 framework under the conditions of pre-/post-implementation stages and mandatory/voluntary usage contexts to investigate how various intervention practices adjust user appraisals of new technology via different coping mechanisms. We then extend the investigation into the downstream job outcomes and make comparisons of relevant relationships across usage contexts. Our empirical findings from two unique organizational settings, featured opposite degrees of usage voluntariness, support consistently significant effects of intervention practices, beliefs updating, and effects of usage behavior on job outcomes in both contexts while suggesting nuanced differences between the two contexts. Our research sheds light on how to manage technology implementations and help users cope with the change effectively in different contexts via various intervention practices in the technology implementation process.

Keywords: Intervention Practices, Coping, Technology Implementation, Mandatory Usage, Voluntary Usage

## 1 Introduction

Since the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, organizations in many industries and sectors have encountered an unprecedented demand to revamp their operations with new information technology (IT; Herath & Herath, 2020; Kodama, 2020; Richter, 2020). The effective implementation of new IT systems in organizations remains a challenge, with failure rates of IT projects exceeding 75% in some cases. <sup>1</sup> One core challenge is that the introduction of technology often disrupts established business practices (Jasperson et al., 2005). As a type of IT-enabled organizational transformation, a technology implementation leverages digital technology to support value propositions and enhance organizational identity (Wessel et al., 2021). While it is often intended to align with organizational goals (Berente et al., 2016), employees may not always share the same vision as upper management when it comes to their daily work routines. Like any other change initiated by management, employees, as end users, are likely to respond with reservations and skepticism to the disruptions that technology implementations may introduce (Joshi, 1991). This often results in maladaptation, leading to resistance to usage and poor performance (Kim & Kankanhalli, 2009; Tarafdar et al., 2015).

To address the problem of maladaptation, Beaudry and Pinsonneault (2005) proposed the coping model of user adaptation (CMUA), where users themselves make cognitive and behavioral efforts to adapt to salient IT changes in their work environment, such as a technology implementation. Later iterations of the model incorporated the affective aspects of the coping process (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2010; Stein et al., 2015). However, a limitation of prior research on coping with new IT implementation concerns its static nature. These studies have primarily focused on user coping and adaptation strategies in the post-implementation stage (Bala & Venkatesh, 2016) and have not clearly indicated when users make cognitive appraisals or whether these appraisals evolve during the course of an IT implementation process (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005; Lazarus & Folkman, 1984). In fact, technology implementations involve multiple stages, from initiation and adoption to adaptation, acceptance, routinization, and infusion (Cooper & Zmud, 1990). Longitudinal research on technology implementation often dichotomizes the process into the pre-implementation stage, where users’ initial expectations for the new IT emerge, and the postimplementation stage, where users evaluate the new IT after using it (e.g., Sykes, 2020). Users may employ different coping mechanisms across these implementation stages. For instance, during the preimplementation stage, users may adopt proactive coping and attempt to learn the new IT or modify its form before the change occurs (Aspinwall & Taylor, 1997). In contrast, their response to the new IT after the implementation can be seen as reactive coping (Pirkkalainen et al., 2019). While Lazarus (2000) has called for more longitudinal research on coping in psychology, this call has not been adequately addressed in understanding user adaptation to technology implementations over time. In this paper, we heed this call by elaborating on different coping mechanisms that come into play during user adaptation to new IT from the preto post-implementation stages.

Another limitation of previous research is that most studies solely focus on each user’s individual reactions in coping processes while overlooking the impact of contextual factors (Skinner & Zimmer-Gembeck, 2007).

For example, users who have little control over technology adoption in a mandatory usage context may share common concerns about the new IT change and collectively address the challenge, known as communal coping (Lyons et al., 1998). In contrast, users with more volitional control over technology adoption in a voluntary usage context can choose to opt out even if they encounter common problems, resulting in more individual-oriented coping. In our research, we draw on coping theory in psychology to examine how users adapt to a new technology implementation across different IT usage contexts. We focus on the contrast between mandatory and voluntary usage contexts, given that contextual voluntariness pertains to users’ fundamental coping resources, such as psychological control (Taylor & Stanton, 2007).

Built upon this 2×2 (Pre-/post-implementation × Mandatory/voluntary usage contexts) framework, our research aims to explicate how management can intervene via different practices to facilitate users’ coping with new technology implementation, and moreover, affect usage behavior and job outcomes. Both psychology and management research have emphasized the role of intervention in promoting adaptive coping and meaningful organizational change (Skinner & Zimmer-Gembeck, 2007; Stouten et al., 2018). Existing research has defined management intervention in a technology implementation as various practices that facilitate user acceptance of new IT and examined its influence (Jasperson et al., 2005; Sabherwal et al., 2006). However, how different intervention practices assist users in adapting to new IT while considering implementation stages and usage contexts has not been explicitly explored in existing technology acceptance models (TAM; Davis et al., 1989; Venkatesh & Davis, 2000; Venkatesh & Bala, 2008). In this research, we first theorize how different intervention practices adjust user appraisals of new technology based on different coping mechanisms across implementation stages and usage contexts. We then examine how these appraisals evolve over a technology implementation and eventually affect usage behavior and job outcomes. Furthermore, we compare the relationships in our research model between the mandatory and voluntary usage contexts. In particular, we aim to address the following research questions:

RQ1: How do management intervention practices shape user appraisals by helping users cope with new technology from the pre- to post-implementation stages?

RQ2: How do management intervention practices shape user appraisals by helping users cope with new technology across mandatory and voluntary usage contexts?

RQ3: How do user appraisals translate into job outcomes, and are there any other contextual differences in the user adaptation process?

We empirically tested our research model using two distinct datasets collected from organizational settings where end users had opposite degrees of volitional control over their usage of new IT. The first context was completely mandatory—police officers in an Asian country were obliged to use a new mobile device with a communications and control application. The second context was voluntary—university students were invited to use a new set of collaborative applications built on the Microsoft cloud platform. Our research findings reveal significant but nuanced differences in the effects of intervention practices, beliefs updates, and the impact of usage behavior on job outcomes between the two contexts.

Our research contributes to the literature on technology implementation and user coping in several ways. First, we expand on the CMUA assumption, which regards coping as a static and individual effort, by factoring in IT implementation stages and usage contexts. We propose a 2×2 framework considering pre-/postimplementation stages and mandatory/voluntary usage contexts to enhance our understanding of how intervention practices facilitate user adaptation to new IT via different coping mechanisms. Our framework highlights the dynamic nature of user coping mechanisms during the technology implementation process, where users proactively cope in the preimplementation stage to prepare for the new IT change and adapt reactively in the post-implementation stage to address challenges and understand the situation. Additionally, users’ coping strategies vary across IT usage contexts, with communal coping being more prevalent in mandatory usage contexts and individual reactions dominating in voluntary usage contexts. To effectively facilitate user coping under different conditions, management should focus on tailored intervention practices.

Second, building on the existing technology acceptance literature, we classify and illuminate the critical role of different intervention practices, such as training and user participation in facilitating user proactive coping in the pre-implementation stage and feedback handling and perceived change fairness in facilitating user adaptation reactively in the postimplementation stage. We examine how these different intervention practices help users cope with new IT changes by shaping their appraisals of usefulness and ease of use of the new technology. Additionally, we build a longitudinal research model to account for the process of user appraisals, usage behavior, and job outcomes, investigating how the upstream intervention practices influence desired usage behavior and utilitarian and affective job outcomes subsequently. A deeper understanding of these mechanisms can assist organizations in mitigating the performance dip during a technology implementation.

Third, we contribute to the existing understanding of how contextual voluntariness shapes technology acceptance (Kroenung & Eckhardt, 2015; Wu & Lederer, 2009) by comparing the effectiveness of intervention practices and other relationships across the user adaptation process between mandatory and voluntary usage contexts. While contextual differences in technology acceptance and its impact on individuals have been studied previously (Jeyaraj, 2020; King & He, 2006), little research has been dedicated to understanding how to help users cope with maladaptation in technology implementations across different usage contexts. Our empirical analyses from two unique technology implementation settings, i.e., one mandatory and one voluntary, reveal differences in the magnitude of impacts between the contexts. This comparison between mandatory and voluntary usage contexts is timely and relevant, given the increasing importance of emerging technology in organizational life, particularly in the post-COVID-19 era. Our study sheds light on how to manage technology implementations and assist users in coping with change effectively in different contexts via using various intervention practices.

## 2 Theoretical Background

## 2.1 Technology Implementation Process and Different Usage Contexts

IT implementation enables organizations to transform how they operate and create value (Riera & Iijima, 2019; Wessel et al., 2021). Existing research on IT implementation recognizes it as a multistage process consisting primarily of the pre-implementation stage and the post-implementation stage. In the pre-implementation stage, organizations provide training sessions and opportunities for users to participate in modifying and enhancing the application of new technology, anticipating that users will have positive expectations of new IT (Markus & Tanis, 2000; Saeed & Abdinnour, 2013), which would subsequently boost technology acceptance (Marler et al., 2009; Saeed et al., 2010). Then, when the new technology is rolled out, users are either encouraged or obliged to adopt it to support their daily operations. In the post-implementation stage, the rollout phase is critical (Bala & Venkatesh, 2013), as user performance generally deteriorates before this effect is ameliorated in this period (Mcafee, 2002; Ross, 1999). To minimize the disruption caused by users’ incumbent habits (Polites & Karahanna, 2012) and reduce performance dips, organizations can support users in a variety of ways, such as handling users’ feedback and providing them with a sense of procedural justice while managing the change (Caldwell et al., 2004; Hsieh et al., 2011). Notably, the existing literature on technology acceptance and success focuses primarily on one stage or the other—either pre-implementation or post-implementation. More longitudinal studies are needed to comprehensively understand how to manage technology change effectively via intervention practices over the implementation process (e.g., Abdinnour & Saeed, 2015; Bala & Venkatesh, 2016; Venkatesh & Davis, 2000).

Apart from the differences between the stages of implementation, contextual voluntariness is another important contingency that critically affects the effectiveness of a technology implementation (Venkatesh et al., 2003; Venkatesh & Bala, 2008). The findings of previous research indicate that contextual voluntariness moderates the impacts of users’ beliefs or attitudes toward the new technology (Kroenung & Eckhardt, 2015; Wu & Lederer, 2009). However, based on the meta-analysis by Sabherwal et al. (2006), a large portion of technology acceptance studies are unclear about whether usage was voluntary or mandatory (74.2%). Among the rest, most studies belong to the voluntary usage category (19.5%). A very small percentage of studies focus on the mandatory usage context (4.9%), which deserves more attention, as organizations worldwide have made telework and digitalization an essential part of their business models. Only 1.4% of reviewed studies in Sabherwal et al. (2006) used a combination of voluntary and mandatory usage contexts.<sup>2</sup> Moreover, the findings of previous technology acceptance literature cannot address the potential problem of how to help users cope with maladaptation across different usage contexts through intervention practices during a technology implementation. Given these drawbacks, we found it imperative to compare the mandatory and voluntary contexts to fill in the research gap on how intervention practices have different effects on a technology implementation based on contextual voluntariness.

## 2.2 Intervention for User Adaptation

Over the past two decades, intervention has received significant attention in terms of its impact on facilitating employees’ technology adoption and usage (Venkatesh & Bala, 2008; Jasperson et al., 2005). Following the line of thought above, we reviewed the technology implementation literature with a particular focus on intervention practices. As shown in Table 1, we classified relevant studies depending on which technology implementation stage and usage context the focal intervention practice was involved in.

Overall, the intervention practices from existing research can be summarized into three major categories: training (e.g., Amoako-Gyampah & Salam, 2004; Lee & Xia, 2011), user participation (e.g., Bala & Venkatesh, 2016; Hartwick & Barki, 1994), and management support (e.g., Kim & Kankanhalli, 2009; Lewis et al., 2003). Bala and Venkatesh (2016) made similar categorizations in terms of implementation characteristics. Jasperson et al. (2005) further divided intervention sources into users, peers, technology experts, and managers. For the sake of parsimony, we simplify intervention, describing it as a changemanagement initiative that occurs primarily in the dyad between management and users (Umble & Umble, 2002). Thus, management support here accounts for different types of support provided for a technology implementation, other than training and user participation.

Management interventions and corresponding user reactions vary across implementation stages (Venkatesh & Bala, 2008). In the pre-implementation stage, intervention practices generally focus on enhancing users’ knowledge and understanding of the rationale behind implementation of the new technology. In contrast, in the post-implementation stage, the focus of interventions generally shifts to support for change management, such as problem solving, to help users cope with any maladaptation. As summarized in Table 1, prior research on technology acceptance has investigated the effects of training and user participation in both the preand post-implementation stages. The studies investigating training and user participation only after the technology had already been rolled out have mostly focused on a single stage (see Sabherwal et al., 2006 and Sharma & Yetton, 2007 for meta-analyses). While these studies only measured post-implementation training and user participation for practical reasons,<sup>3</sup> conceptually, these intervention practices may have already appealed to users at the initiation of the new technology. Regarding management support, most previous studies, either generic or specific, have concentrated on its impact in the post-implementation stage. Venkatesh and Bala (2008) positioned incentives for usage as crucial preimplementation interventions and treated organizational as well as peer support as critical post-implementation interventions. Given that we use management support as an umbrella term that encompasses different types of support, we categorize continuous management support as a crucial post-implementation intervention.

Based on Table 1, we also assessed the three major types of intervention in terms of their representation in the voluntary versus mandatory usage contexts. Although the differences were subtle, we did observe more interest in training in the mandatory usage context than in the voluntary usage context. However, we found no difference in user participation interest across the two contexts. In terms of management support, while it generated more interest in the voluntary usage context in generic terms, specific types of support, like feedback handling, incentives, or punishment, generated greater interest in the mandatory usage context. Nevertheless, the amount of research interest, as represented by the number or percentage of studies in the literature, did not directly indicate which intervention matters more under which circumstances. In the following section, we draw on coping theory and propose a 2×2 framework across implementation stages and usage contexts in which intervention practices execute different effects by facilitating user coping in the context of a technology implementation.

## 2.3 Coping with Technology Implementation

Coping theory has been applied to explain how users adapt to a technology implementation (Bala & Venkatesh, 2016; Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005). Beaudry and Pinsonneault’s CMUA (2005) regards new technology as a singular event and analyzes how users cope with it via cognitive appraisals and adaptation strategies. Beaudry and Pinsonneault (2010) as well as Stein et al. (2015) further extended CMUA to illustrate how users’ affective responses affect technology use. Built on CMUA, Bala and Venkatesh (2016) investigated the impacts of implementation characteristics, such as user participation and management support, on not only users’ cognitive appraisals, affective responses, and adaptation strategies but also on job outcomes such as performance and satisfaction. However, their studies only examined the implementation characteristics within one stage and evaluated the research model solely in the voluntary usage context.

To be more specific, these studies primarily focused on user coping and adaptation strategies in the postimplementation stage. For example, after users judge the change either as an opportunity or a threat and evaluate their control over the change, they generally resort to problem-focused or emotion-focused coping after system use (Bala & Venkatesh, 2016; Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005). However, we note that coping is not always about responding to the present opportunity and threat (Carver & Connor-Smith, 2010). Instead, it can also concern an expected future stimulus and thus constitutes proactive coping, referring to “efforts undertaken in advance of a potentially stressful event to prevent it or modify its form before it occurs” (Aspinwall & Taylor, 1997). Both proactive coping and its counterpart reactive coping have been recently found to counteract the negative impact of technostress on IT-enabled productivity (Pirkkalainen et al, 2019). Therefore, it is necessary to differentiate user coping before and after the occurrence of a technology implementation. Thus, we propose that there are intervention practices that can facilitate user adaptation via proactive coping before an implementation and those which can facilitate user adaptation via reactive coping after an implementation. As discussed in Section 2.2, training and user participation can already be appealing for users at the initiation of new technology as a means of preparing them for the change and thus facilitating their proactive coping. On the other hand, continuous management support such as feedback handling and practice of ensuring change fairness is more critical after the implementation to help users solve problems and make sense of the situation, thus driving reactive coping.

Moreover, the effectiveness of intervention practices and their underlying coping mechanisms may also vary across different usage contexts. In the mandatory usage context, users have little or no control over technology adoption and face shared concerns about the new IT change. Under these circumstances, users are likely to make joint efforts to confront the challenge together. Management can enact intervention practices to facilitate such communal coping of users as a collective (Lyons et al., 1998). In the voluntary usage context, users have more volitional control over technology adoption. Under these circumstances, even if they face some common problems, they can opt out freely. Thus, their response and coping mechanisms regarding the new technology will be more individual oriented. As such, users’ coping mechanisms may also vary in terms of whether the problem has a communal or individual orientation across different usage contexts. Furthermore, comparing the two types of interventions in the pre-implementation stage, training encompasses various ways for users to learn about the new technology and is usually conducted collectively. Meanwhile, training often requires inputs and resources from both management and users. Therefore, it is more likely that communal coping will be needed in the mandatory context. Conversely, since user participation usually requires more inputs and conscious efforts from individual users, it is more likely that individual-oriented coping will be needed in the voluntary context. Comparing the two types of intervention practices after the implementation, feedback handling is more problem focused and deals with the reality of the situation by pooling resources together (Moos & Billings, 1982), which is more likely to be imperative in the mandatory context. As for change fairness, since it involves personal attempts to define the meaning of the situation and requires more individual efforts, it is more likely to be needed in the voluntary context.

We illustrate the 2×2 framework in Figure 1 to indicate the coping mechanisms that different intervention practices can facilitate in accordance with the two implementation stages (pre vs. post) and the two usage contexts (mandatory vs. voluntary). In addition, we aim to differentiate the effectiveness of relevant intervention practices in each implementation stage between mandatory and voluntary usage contexts. We propose the research model and hypothesize the relationships in the next section.

## 3 Research Model and Hypotheses Development

We build the research model to examine the impact of intervention practices from pre- to post-implementation stages. Previous literature has shown that effective intervention practices facilitate technology use by shaping user beliefs regarding the usefulness and ease of use of the new technology (Agarwal & Prasad, 1999; Amoako-Gyampah & Salam, 2004; Brandon-Jones & Kauppi, 2018; Lee & Xia, 2011; Marler et al., 2006; Venkatesh, 1999). Some studies have further investigated other implementation success indicators, such as user attitude, satisfaction, and performance (Aiman-Smith & Green, 2002; Bala & Venkatesh, 2016; Hunton & Beeler, 1997; Klein et al., 2001). Here, we take a synoptic approach, theorizing first how different intervention practices help users adapt to the new technology by adjusting their appraisals of its usefulness and ease of use and then theorizing how the appraisal further translates into attitude, usage behavior, and job-related outcomes such as satisfaction and performance. Figure 2 illustrates the research model, which depicts a two-stage model of technology implementation. We focus on hypothesizing (1) the impacts of the four intervention practices as identified in the 2×2 coping framework on user beliefs, (2) belief updating from the pre-implementation stage to the rollout phase, and (3) user attitudes resulting from belief updates and further leading to deep usage of the new IT, which in turn affects job outcomes. As cumulative research on technology acceptance has already thoroughly investigated the relationships among beliefs, attitudes, and usage behaviors (Davis et al., 1989; Venkatesh & Davis, 2000; Venkatesh & Bala, 2008), we take these relationships as given in the current model. Consistent with Bala and Venkatesh (2016), we consider job performance and job satisfaction as the two indicators of job outcomes following deep usage of the new technology.

## 3.1 Intervention Practices and Appraisals

## 3.1.1 Pre-Implementation Stage

Training, as defined here, offers a range of methods for users to acquire knowledge about the new IT within the organization. Common training approaches in technology implementation include but are not limited to prior education, vendor training, in-house training, and selfstudy (Igbaria et al., 1995). Given the potential lack of technology comprehension in the pre-implementation stage, users often experience uncertainty about the impending changes, which can lead to negative assessments of the upcoming implementation. Training plays a pivotal role in enabling users to proactively adapt to the new IT by equipping them with knowledge of the new technology’s features (Ajami & Mohammadi-Bertiani, 2012) and fostering a sense of preparedness within the local community (Zain et al., 2005). Specifically, before a new technology is deployed, organizations need to manage user expectations (Somers & Nelson, 2004) and enhance their capacity to effectively utilize the new technology (Bradley & Lee, 2007). Through training, users can engage in in-depth learning and develop a precise understanding of various features of the new technology (Agarwal & Prasad, 1999; Bala & Venkatesh, 2016). Consistent with prior research on technology acceptance (e.g., Agarwal & Prasad, 1999; Amoako-Gyampah & Salam, 2004; Marler et al., 2006; Venkatesh, 1999), we consider users’ perceived usefulness and ease of use as the key outcomes of their appraisal of the new technology. Consequently, we propose that training can enhance these two aspects of user beliefs during the pre-implementation stage.

Furthermore, training can enhance the organization’s absorptive capacity as a collective entity (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990), enabling users to collectively face and manage the changes associated with the new IT. Therefore, training alleviates users’ feelings of uncertainty and threat by promoting communal coping as opposed to individual coping. In comparison to users’ voluntary self-education, training necessitates a more significant investment of management effort to provide users with the necessary resources to be well-prepared for the new IT. Consequently, training is particularly appealing in the mandatory context and facilitates a more collective approach to coping in the pre-implementation stage. We argue that the impacts of training on user beliefs are stronger in mandatory contexts compared to voluntary ones. Thus, we posit:

H1: Training has positive effects on a user’s perceived (a) usefulness and (b) ease of use of the new technology in the pre-implementation stage, and (c) these effects are more pronounced in the mandatory usage context than in the voluntary usage context.

User participation encompasses the behaviors, tasks, and activities that users or their representatives engage in while the new technology is still under development (Hartwick & Barki, 1994; He & King, 2008). Previous research has sometimes treated user participation as a distinct concept or used it interchangeably with user involvement, where users perceive the new technology as important and personally relevant (Hartwick & Barki, 1994; Sabherwal et al., 2006; Venkatesh & Bala, 2008). When users actively participate, they have the opportunity to provide input in the process of customizing the new technology (Hunton & Beeler, 1997). This proactive involvement encourages users to constructively engage with the new IT. Actively participating in the developmental phase enables users to gain a deeper understanding of the reasons behind the implementation and internalize the necessity of the new technology, perceiving it as useful (Sabherwal et al., 2006). Further, user participation empowers users to contribute to the specification of technological features and interfaces and to enrich their application in work routines. This involvement allows users to take ownership of the new technology (Lin & Shao, 2000), resulting in a higher perception of ease of use. Therefore, we suggest that user participation positively influences user beliefs regarding both the usefulness and ease of use in the preimplementation stage.

Furthermore, in contrast to the communal coping needed in mandatory usage contexts, user participation emphasizes the volitional and conscious efforts of individual users themselves. Hence, it is more likely to be effective in facilitating individual-oriented proactive coping. Consequently, we are more likely to observe stronger impacts of user participation on user beliefs in the voluntary usage context compared to the mandatory usage context. We posit:

H2: User participation has positive effects on a user’s perceived (a) usefulness and (b) ease of use of the new technology in the pre-implementation stage, and (c) these effects are more pronounced in the voluntary usage context than in the mandatory usage context.

## 3.1.2 Rollout Phase Following Implementation

Management support has been disproportionately examined as an intervention practice in the postimplementation stage. Among the various forms of support observed in technology implementation, feedback handling pertains to management’s ability to effectively manage users’ feedback on the new technology (Hill et al., 2012). Once the new technology is rolled out, users often encounter real challenges adapting to it in their daily operations. Feedback handling is especially conducive to reactive coping, enabling users to solve problems and improve user appraisals in the postimplementation stage.

According to social exchange theory, the quality of the relationship between employees and employers relies significantly on each party’s contributions to the other’s well-being (Cropanzano & Mitchell, 2005). Users highly value management’s responsiveness, particularly when they encounter various situations immediately after the rollout of the new technology. Many instances of user feedback may arise from mismatches between the new technology’s features and established routines during the rollout phase. Effective feedback handling enables management to adjust the new technology based on users’ firsthand experience, thus alleviating users’ concerns about its usage. Under these circumstances, users can adapt more effectively to the new technology while addressing compatibility issues, leading them to perceive the new technology as useful. Brandon-Jones and Kauppi (2018) found that maintaining professionalism in providing ongoing support to users facing problems contributes to their belief in the ease of using the new technology. Therefore, we propose that feedback handling can enhance users’ perception of usefulness and ease of use during the rollout phase following implementation.

Furthermore, feedback support is more crucial when users engage in behavior that is not entirely voluntary (Cheng et al., 2002). In a mandatory usage context, users have limited control and autonomy in dealing with the situation. It is essential for them to solve problems and overcome challenges jointly with management. Thus, we contend that feedback handling by management is more essential in the mandatory context than in the voluntary usage context, as it facilitates users’ reactive and communal coping, focusing on problem-solving. Accordingly, we posit:

H3: Feedback handling has positive effects on a user’s perceived (a) usefulness and (b) ease of use of the new technology in the rollout phase following implementation, and (c) these effects are more pronounced in the mandatory usage context than in the voluntary usage context.

In addition to feedback handling, another critical aspect of management support following implementation pertains to management’s attention to procedural consistency, the provision of accurate information, and involving employees in the change process (Leventhal et al., 1980). While studying person-environment fit in response to organizational change, Caldwell et al. (2004) identified change fairness as a pivotal element in change management. They argued that individuals undergoing change are more likely to react favorably to the transition when they are fully informed about it.

Amoako-Gyampah and Salam (2004) showed that transparent communication about enterprise resource planning (ERP) projects enables users to cultivate shared belief in the usefulness and ease of use of the ERP system. Sonenshein and Dholakia (2012) noted that individuals are more likely to adapt positively to change when the reasons for the change are adequately explained. Caldwell et al. (2004) illustrated that in the presence of change fairness, employees’ beliefs tend to align more closely with organizational values. In applying the concept of change management to technology implementation, users are more inclined to embrace the value of new technology when change fairness is present, ultimately leading to a higher perception of the new technology’s usefulness. Additionally, when users receive a great amount of information about the new technology in a fair and equitable manner, they develop a stronger sense of control over the new technology and believe that less effort would be required for its utilization, resulting in a higher perception of ease of use.

Furthermore, like procedural justice, change fairness is particularly valued in individualistic cultures (Leung & Lind, 1986). In comparison to the mandatory usage context, users in the voluntary usage context have more control over technology adoption and take on greater personal responsibility for adapting to the new technology. In this case, change fairness can be an effective intervention practice since it can help users define the meaning of the situation in an individualistic context, facilitating individual-oriented coping. Therefore, it is more essential in the voluntary context than in the mandatory context. Accordingly, we posit:

H4: Change fairness has positive effects on a user’s perceived (a) usefulness and (b) ease of use of the new technology in the rollout phase following implementation, and (c) these effects are more pronounced in the voluntary usage context than in the mandatory usage context.

## 3.2 Belief Updates from Pre-Implementation to Rollout Phase

Research on the impact of intervention practices in technology implementation has traditionally focused on either the pre-implementation or post-implementation stages. Nonetheless, it’s worth noting that the initial stage of IT implementation can significantly affect the later stages (Duarte & Costa, 2012). Consequently, it is crucial to recognize that users continually update their beliefs formed in the early stage as they become more accustomed to the new technology during the implementation process (Kim & Malhotra, 2005). The adaptation level theory (Helson, 1964) posits that existing cognitions evolve as individuals encounter new stimuli in novel experiences. In this process, incumbent beliefs and attitudes persist over time (Petty et al., 2006). Previous studies have empirically supported the direct impact of pre-usage beliefs in usefulness and ease of use on the corresponding postusage beliefs in the new technology (Bhattacherjee & Premkumar, 2004; Kim, 2009; Maier et al., 2012; Sun, 2013). Building on this cumulative evidence, we also hypothesize that user beliefs in usefulness and ease of use in the pre-implementation stage affect their corresponding beliefs in the new technology in the rollout phase following implementation.

Furthermore, in a mandatory usage context where the new technology fully replaces the incumbent IT, users face a greater challenge in adapting to the new technology. Conversely, for users in a voluntary usage context, adapting to the new technology is more of a choice. Therefore, in the context of mandatory usage with an elevated challenge, it is imperative that users have faith in the performance expectancy of the new technology (Califf et al., 2020). Without such a belief, the new technology is unlikely to meet the user’s needs. Consequently, perceived usefulness carries more significance in the mandatory usage context compared to the voluntary usage context, resulting in a stronger carryover effect of user beliefs in usefulness from the preimplementation to the post-implementation stage.

Conversely, in the voluntary usage context, users tend to prioritize the convenience of the new technology. If the new technology is expected to be easy to use, they are more inclined to give it a try to see if it lives up to its promise. If not, they can readily explore alternative options. Therefore, we contend that in the voluntary usage context, perceived ease of use before the implementation is more likely to be positively confirmed by perceived ease of use after the implementation. Considering this, we propose that:

H5: (a) A user’s pre-implementation belief in the usefulness of the new technology has a positive effect on the same belief in the rollout phase following implementation, and (b) this effect is more pronounced in the mandatory usage context than in the voluntary usage context.

H6: (a) A user’s pre-implementation belief in the ease of use of the new technology has a positive effect on the same belief in the rollout phase following implementation, and (b) this effect is more pronounced in the voluntary usage context than in the mandatory usage context.

## 3.3 Job Outcomes in the Rollout Phase

Previous research has amassed substantial evidence of a performance dip during the rollout phase, which is recognized as the most disruptive period in the entire technology implementation process (Hakkinen & Himola, 2008; Mcafee, 2002; Ross, 1999). Given the significance of the performance dip in the rollout phase, along with our focus on elucidating the effects of intervention practices on user beliefs and belief updates across the pre- and post-implementation stages, we extend our investigation to encompass user adaptation’s influence on users’ job outcomes. Drawing on the work of Bala and Venkatesh (2016), we consider both utilitarian and affective job outcomes—namely, job performance, which pertains to users’ contribution to achieving organizational goals (Rich et al., 2010), and job satisfaction, reflecting the extent to which users find their job fulfilling and congruent with their values (Janssen, 2001), through their utilization of the new technology.

Consistent with the literature on technology acceptance (Davis et al., 1989; Venkatesh & Davis, 2000; Venkatesh & Bala, 2008), we assume a similar linkage from user beliefs to attitude and, subsequently, to usage behavior in our current research. Furthermore, to enable contextual comparisons, we focus on deep usage behavior, which involves using the advanced features of the new technology to address core aspects of their tasks (Burton-Jones & Straub, 2006). Previous research has established that technology acceptance is contingent on contextual voluntariness (Kroenung & Eckhardt, 2015; Wu & Lederer, 2009). Going beyond this, we explore how deep usage behavior affects user job outcomes and the contextual differences in this process. A better understanding of the process can provide insights into overcoming potential performance dips.

Leveraging new technology is expected to enhance organizational productivity and improve users’ job performance (Hsieh et al., 2011). In situations where users cannot fully utilize the new technology’s features, a performance dip is commonly observed in the rollout phase, hindering the organization’s ability to realize the benefits of technology implementation (Li et al., 2013). In contrast, in cases where users can deeply adopt the new technology, they effectively adapt to it by employing its advanced features to complete their tasks, which positively influences their job performance. Past research, such as Burton-Jones and Straub (2006), has identified a positive correlation between deep structure usage and task performance especially for tasks requiring cognitive engagement. Bala and Venkatesh (2016) demonstrated that exploiting technological features fosters improvements in job performance. Furthermore, we contend that the relationship between deep usage behavior and job performance is more pronounced in the mandatory usage context, where the new technology is a vital tool for job execution. The extent to which users can harness the new technology’s feature in their work directly affects their job performance. In contrast, in the voluntary usage context, the new technology is optional to perform their jobs, making the influence of deep usage behavior on job performance relatively less critical. Accordingly, we propose:

H7: (a) A user’s deep usage behavior of the new technology in the rollout phase has a positive effect on their job performance, and (b) this effect is more pronounced in the mandatory usage context than in the voluntary usage context.

We also investigate the relationship between deep usage behavior and job satisfaction. Technostress and the resulting job dissatisfaction often arise when users have not yet fully mastered a new technology. To regain their confidence and a sense of control, users should quickly establish a routine for using the technology. Once they can effectively harness its advanced features, they are more likely to master the new technology necessary to meet their job requirement and, in turn, find satisfaction in their work again. Building on the findings of Bala and Venkatesh (2016), which indicated a positive correlation between technology exploitation and change in job satisfaction, we contend that the linkage between deep usage behavior and job satisfaction is more pronounced in the voluntary usage context compared to the mandatory usage context. Beyond task completion, users in the voluntary context are also concerned about how the new technology aligns with their work values. The more users leverage the advanced features of the new technology at work, the greater the likelihood that the technology will assist them in fulfilling their tasks effectively, leading to a stronger sense of accomplishment and job satisfaction. Consequently, we propose:

H8: (a) A user’s deep usage behavior of the new technology in the rollout phase has a positive effect on their job satisfaction, and (b) this effect is more pronounced in the voluntary usage context than in the mandatory usage context.

## 4 Empirical Analyses

We evaluate the research model in two contexts where users have contrasting degrees of volitional control over their usage of a new IT.

## 4.1 Study 1: Mandatory Usage Context

We first examine the research model in a mandatory context where police officers in an Asian society were obliged to use a communications and control (CC) system to support their daily operations. As a typical paramilitary organization, the police department (PD) has highly centralized authority vested in a formalized hierarchy that values efficiency and discipline (Van Maanen, 1975). As a critical part of police work, “information work” (Ioimo & Aronson, 2003; Bouwman & Wijngaert, 2009) relies heavily on the CC system to enhance work efficiency in the police department. To use the CC system, patrol police officers are equipped with a mobile device to communicate with the control center in their day-today work. Two generations of the CC system have been deployed over the years. The current change involves the transformation from the second generation (CCII) to the third generation (CCIII). Compared to CCII, CCIII was designed with more advanced digital features to identify scenes and related persons accurately, provide sophisticated information support, and make optimized suggestions. As a result, patrol police officers can minimize their response time to calls for police services and improve decisionmaking by better controlling and using the system.

While patrol police officers were required to use CCIII, management intervened to promote user acceptance of the new system. The training program was launched about two months before CCIII rollout. Eighty percent of the police officers whose work was directly affected by the system change joined the formal training program. Through training, participants learned how to apply new features of CCIII in their daily work to ensure that they could use the new system competently. Trained police officers were asked to share what they had learned in the course with other colleagues. In addition, training materials and introductory notes were uploaded onto the intranet for self-learning purposes. Meanwhile, a project team comprised of developers, senior management, and serving officers was established to monitor the development and deployment of CCIII. They were assigned to collect detailed information on user requirements by organizing focus groups and evaluating staff suggestions. Police officers who were not invited to the focus groups could express their views through email or on a website set up by the project team. After the rollout of CCIII, the project team continued to oversee the implementation process by providing resources and notifications, as well as soliciting feedback from police officers. A help desk was set up to promptly answer users’ operational queries. Focus groups were held to gather user input for system enhancement. Based on user suggestions, CCIII had some minor upgrades. Implementation progress was communicated to police officers via the intranet.

## 4.1.1 Data Collection

We conducted two waves of data collection before and after CCIII implementation. In T1 (one month before the implementation), we measured training, user participation, and users’ pre-beliefs concerning the use of CCIII. We collected data again one month after CCIII rollout (T2), allowing for a period wherein users either adapted to and exploited new features of the system or suffered a performance dip. Management support, like feedback handling and change fairness, post-beliefs, attitude, deep usage, and two job outcomes were all measured at this stage.

With the help of PD, we recruited a random sample of patrol police officers who differed in their work experience, gender, age, educational background, etc., to take part in this study. Participants were assured that the data collection was for academic use only. In T1, 542 valid responses were received. The second wave of surveys was distributed only to those who had returned surveys in the first wave. We tested the model using pooled samples from T1 to T2. After removing responses with missing values, our final sample consisted of valid responses from 420 patrol police officers. To assess the potential issue of response biases, we conducted pairwise comparisons of participants’ demographics between those in T1 and T2 and those who dropped out in T2 (see Table 2). Most of the comparisons are not significant, with pvalues greater than 0.05, suggesting no systematic response bias in our sample. The only exception was that some police officers with less work experience did not fill out the survey in T2 (p-value of the working years between 2 and 3 is 0.05). We took this variable as a control in our analysis, and the results are robust.

## 4.1.2 Measures

We measured PU (perceived usefulness), PEOU (perceived ease of use), and attitude using items from Davis et al. The scale of training was adapted from items in Amoako-Gyampah and Salam (2004). ## 4.2 Study 2: Voluntary Usage Context

We evaluated the same research model in another context, one in which students from a major university in Asia were invited to migrate to cloud-based services provided by Microsoft 365. Microsoft 365 enables students to access the full-featured experience of Microsoft tools like Word, Exchange, Excel, SharePoint, Teams, etc. from anywhere on any device with cloud features. Another benefit was the offer of a lifetime email with greater email storage as an alternative to the incumbent webmail system embedded in the intranet of the university. If students chose not to migrate, they could continue using the webmail system and office tools with similar functionality. As such, adopting the new application was completely voluntary.

The university management started promoting Microsoft 365 about 45 days before the rollout by hanging banners and posters around the campus, offering briefing sessions, and recruiting early adopters, who used the trial system and shared opinions about their usage experience. Training sessions were held where interested students could learn about new features of Microsoft 365 to improve their usage efficiency. A website with updates on implementation was developed for students to search for relevant information. Before the Microsoft 365 rollout, a project team composed of officers from Microsoft, senior management, and the university’s technology staff was formed to promote Microsoft 365, collect information on user requirements, and answer queries. Students were also invited to attend roadshows, meet with representatives of the project team, try out new functions, and ask questions. After the Microsoft 365 rollout, an invitation email was sent to every student at the university, informing them of the event and inviting them to migrate. The project team continued monitoring the implementation and soliciting students’ feedback. The new application had minor upgrades following the rollout that occurred during our data collection period. All the upgrades were communicated to students via email and posted on the dedicated website.

## 4.2.1 Data Collection

As in Study 1, we conducted two waves of data collection before and after the implementation. With the support of the IT service center, we sent survey invitations to all undergraduate students at the university. In T1, 1,097 students returned the questionnaires. The second wave of data collection was conducted one month after rollout (T2), and we received responses from 503 students. Among them, 282 had migrated to Microsoft 365. Since we were interested in understanding user adaptation to the new technology, we targeted these 282 adopters and assessed the model using pooled samples from T1 and T2. To assess potential attrition effects and response bias, as in Study 1, we applied pairwise comparisons of students’ demographics between those who answered in T1, those who answered in T2, and those who dropped out in T2. The evidence suggests that common method bias does not present a threat in this study.

## 4.3 Results

## 4.3.1 Results in Two Usage Contexts

We evaluated the structural models in both studies using PLS. As shown in Figure 3, the path coefficients of the model in both studies are all significant.

Intervention practices significantly affected user beliefs at each stage of implementation. In the preimplementation stage, training had strong effects on PU and PEOU, although the effect of training on PU in Study 2 was marginal (p-value = 0.07). This supports H1a-b. Users welcomed the opportunity to give input and participate in the pre-implementation stage. This contributed significantly to improving their beliefs in the new technologies at the same stage as well, supporting H2a-b. In the rollout phase, both feedback handling and change fairness were found to be significantly related to user beliefs in the new technologies at the same time, thus supporting H3a-b and H4a-b. In addition to intervention effects, PU and PEOU in the pre-implementation stages were significantly related to the corresponding beliefs in the rollout phase. This suggests that users adjusted their post-belief anchoring to their pre-beliefs. Therefore, H5a and H6a are supported.

## 4.3.2 Nuances between Mandatory and Voluntary Usage Contexts

To test the set of hypotheses on contextual differences in user adaptation to new technologies, we conducted several analyses to compare the standardized coefficients of the structural models in these two studies. First, we assessed the measurement invariance for the common measures according to Henseler et al.

(2016). <sup>11</sup> The results show that the common measurements are compositionally invariant across the two studies. Given that measures of deep usage and user performance had to be variant based on different technologies in two contexts, we considered it infeasible to conduct multigroup analysis (Henseler et al. 2016). Thus, we made comparisons between the two usage contexts via a statistical t-test based on the 1,000 path coefficients generated by PLS bootstrapping in each context. Table 6 summarizes the results of the analyses and shows that all coefficient comparisons between the two contexts are significant at the 0.05 level.<sup>12</sup>

As shown in Table 6, in the pre-implementation stage, the effects of training on PU and PEOU were both significantly stronger in the mandatory context than in the voluntary context. Thus, as hypothesized in H1c, training appears to be more appealing for the communal coping needed in the mandatory context before the implementation. In comparison, user participation had significantly stronger impacts on shaping user beliefs in the voluntary context than in the mandatory context, supporting H2c.

## 5 Discussion

User participation enhanced proactive coping and involved more conscious efforts from individuals, which is more important in the voluntary context before the implementation. In the rollout phase, feedback handling had a greater impact on user beliefs in the mandatory context than in the voluntary context. As hypothesized in H3c, feedback handling focused on problem-solving and was more important in the mandatory context to address reactive coping in a collective way shortly after the implementation. In contrast, the effects of change fairness on PU and PEOU were stronger in the voluntary context than in the mandatory context shortly after the implementation. This is consistent with the assumption that users in the voluntary usage context right after the implementation took a more individualistic approach in coping with the change and thus cared more about change fairness. This supports H4c.

Regarding contextual differences in belief updating, PU (PEOU) is more likely to be carried over from the pre-implementation stage to the rollout stage in the mandatory (voluntary) context. These findings are consistent with the argument that users are more likely to be concerned about the performance expectancy of the new technology in the mandatory context but will likely pay more attention to the convenience of an optional new technology in the voluntary context. This supports H5b and H6b. However, regarding contextual differences in job outcomes, we found support for a stronger impact of deep usage on job performance in the mandatory context (H7b) but not on job satisfaction in the voluntary context (H8b). The results suggest that the necessity of using the new technology to perform one’s jobs in the mandatory context is not only more likely to affect the utilitarian outcome in terms of job performance, but overcoming challenges faced in performing mandatory tasks and effectively adapting to a new IT can also result in better affective outcomes, such as job satisfaction.

This paper examines user adaptation in IT implementation with a focus on examining the role of intervention practices across implementation stages and usage contexts based on a 2×2 coping framework. By building the research model from the pre- to postimplementation stages, we account for the process of user appraisal, usage behavior, and job outcomes. The research findings support the significant impacts of four intervention practices that can shape user appraisals via different coping mechanisms in the pre- and postimplementation stages, with user appraisals further transmitting the effects into usage behavior and job outcomes afterward. Moreover, we draw on two contexts that present a salient difference in users’ volitional control over usage and unveil how the effects of intervention practices and the further effects in the downstream process vary with usage contexts. Our results have important theoretical and practical implications.

## 5.1 Theoretical and Practical Implications

Our research contributes to the intersection of information systems (IS), psychology, and organizational management. First, we developed a 2×2 framework to represent what intervention practices facilitate user adaptation to new IT via different coping mechanisms under conditions in pre-/postimplementation stages and mandatory/voluntary usage contexts. One crucial psychological insight manifested in our work is that coping should be a developmental process. While prior IS research on CMUA demonstrated the cognitive, affective, and behavioral components of coping in user adaptation (Bala & Venkatesh, 2016; Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005, Stein et al., 2015), relevant cognitive appraisals, affective states, and adaptation strategies cannot be attained all at once and may vary over the course of the technology implementation. Our study indicates that the two pillars of technology acceptance—user beliefs in usefulness and ease of use—can be carried over from the pre-implementation stage to the rollout phase following implementation. More importantly, the construal of these beliefs is more likely to be shaped by different intervention practices, which facilitate proactive coping before the implementation and reactive coping after the implementation.

Second, instead of viewing coping as an individual affair, we contribute to the ongoing discussion in psychology on coping as an interactive and communal process (Lyons et al., 1998; Skinner & Zimmer-Gembeck, 2007). Previously, IS research on CMUA has mostly concentrated on how users adapt to the implementation themselves (Bala & Venkatesh, 2016; Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005) and thus neglected the important role that contexts could play in affecting user adaptation. Our endeavor in this study is to bring forward what is typically considered a backdrop, showcasing nuances of user adaptation across different usage contexts. That is, users who face a shared IT change in the mandatory usage context are likely to cope collectively with a communal orientation, while users in the voluntary usage context can opt out freely, so their coping is more individual-oriented. By combining users’ coping focus with management’s intervention practices in each condition, our 2×2 framework can further illustrate which intervention best facilitates what type of coping under what circumstances.

Third, like prior literature on coping in relevant domains, we acknowledge the pivotal role that intervention plays in facilitating individual adaptation (Skinner & Zimmer-Gembeck, 2007; Stouten et al., 2018; Venkatesh & Bala, 2008). We simplify coping as an issue to be addressed in the user-management dyad. Building on the 2×2 framework, we identify the critical role that two sets of intervention practices play before and after technology implementation, respectively. Moreover, we compare the effectiveness of these intervention practices between the mandatory and voluntary usage contexts to further illuminate their different impacts. By building the research model over the IT implementation process capturing the process from user appraisal to usage behavior and job outcomes, we provide an integrated view of how management produces interventions during IT implementation, thereby mitigating potential negative impacts on users’ utilitarian and affective job outcomes.

In the new normal following the COVID-19 pandemic, organizations have become more technology driven, making it more critical than ever for practitioners to understand how to effectively implement new technology in organizations across different contexts. Our integrative research model with cross-context comparisons over time has such practical implications. Given the indispensable and mandatory nature of technology usage, management could consider integrating a training program that involves as many parties as possible locally to enhance the communal coping of end users before the rollout of a new technology. After the rollout, management could then redirect their attention to feedback from end users to develop feasible solutions and foster user coping strategies with a problem-solving focus. Although the mandatory usage context is likely to be more challenging for end users, effective management intervention could lead to better job outcomes in terms of both user satisfaction and performance.

In cases where technology usage is voluntary, management could intervene effectively through other means. Before the rollout of a technology, users should be encouraged to help plan for the implementation in order to foster proactive individual coping strategies. After rollout, management should pay attention to issues concerning procedural justice in order to appeal to users’ individualistic preferences and promote reactive coping. Essentially, implementing a new technology in a voluntary context is about offering a convenient alternative to the incumbent technology. Therefore, in such cases, management should develop interventions that focus on the individual to facilitate user adaptation and effective technology usage.

## 5.2 Limitations and Future Research

Our findings should be viewed in light of their limitations. First, our findings involved a comparison between two IT implementation contexts that represent opposite ends of the spectrum regarding users’ volitional control over system usage. While we acknowledge that factors like system complexity, management efforts, and measurement variances between these contexts could offer alternative explanations for the observed differences, the voluntariness of system usage is a significant distinction that we specifically investigated. A more robust approach would be to evaluate user adaptation to the same technology with equivalent levels of management involvement, with some users exposed to mandated technology use and others to voluntary use. Further, we did not account for contexts where the voluntariness of system usage falls between the two extremes. Future research could directly measure contextual voluntariness in the model to further unfold its impact on user adaptation to a new technology on a continuum.

Second, our current research focused on evaluating four management interventions within the 2×2 coping framework. It is crucial to note that these interventions are not the only interventions that could influence outcomes in these conditions. Future research could examine other intervention practices to further enrich the model. For instance, alternative management support, such as incentive/penalty schemes adopted by organizations during IT implementation, could be explored. Moreover, our studies relied on self-reported user data to measure intervention practices. Future research could employ objective data or engage in field experiments to establish causal relationships. Additionally, future examination of intervention practices should consider specific technical details tailored to each context, such as the design, process, and content of training for various technology usage tasks.

Third, our study focused on examining how different intervention practices help users adapt to new technology during the pre-implementation stage and the rollout phase. However, our results did not capture user adaptation at other time points—for example, during long-term routinization. To address this limitation, future work could collect longitudinal data at multiple time points to capture the trajectory of changes in user appraisals, usage patterns, and job outcomes over time. This could further enhance the understanding of the intricate process of user adaptation to IT implementation.

Finally, the investigation of intervention practices could be extended to their effects on regulating technostress to assess their effectiveness in helping users adapt to new technology in the face of challenge and hindrance stressors (c.f. Califf et al., 2020; Maier et al., 2021). This research avenue holds great potential and would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the personal and organizational impacts of intervention practices in the context of technology implementation.

## About the Authors

Yue (Katherine) Feng is an assistant professor in the Department of Management and Marketing, Faculty of Business, Hong Kong Polytechnic University. She received her PhD in information systems from the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology. Her research aims to understand individual behaviors on social media, digital marketing, and IT innovation and implementation in different contexts, using a variety of research methods such as econometrics, field experiments, surveys, and applied machine learning models. Her work has been published in leading journals such as MIS Quarterly and Information Systems Research.

Nadia Yin Yu, PhD, earned her doctoral degree in management from the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology in China in 2017. She currently serves as an assistant professor in the People and Organizations Department at NEOMA Business School in France. With an academic background that includes visiting positions at the University of Maryland in College Park, MD, USA in 2014, and the University of Texas at Dallas in Richardson, TX, USA in 2018, Nadia’s research has been published and featured in journals and magazines such as the Journal of Vocational Behavior, IEEE Transactions on Engineering Management, Equality, Diversity and Inclusion: An International Journal, and Forbes. Her research pursuits are centered on exploring the impact of interpersonal contexts, including team dynamics like shared perceptions as climate, on employee performance at both individual and collective levels. Additionally, she investigates the evolution of individual beliefs, orientations, and initiatives over time in the workplace. Her research aims to comprehend and effectively manage these changes, factoring in both psychologica and organizational perspectives.

Kar Yan Tam is dean of the School of Business and Management and Chair Professor of Information Systems, Business Statistics and Operations Management at the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology. His current research interests include fintech, business analytics, and digital transformation. Kar Yan’s publications have appeared in MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Management Science, and Journal of Management Information Systems. He is currently on the editorial board of a number of IS journals. Kar Yan has extensive consulting experience with major corporations and government agencies. He is currently on the board of AACSB and EFMD and served as president of the Asia Pacific Association of Business Schools.

Michael C. Lai completed his PhD in information systems from the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology in 2009. Following a distinguished career with the Hong Kong Police Force, where he served as a chief superintendent of police, Dr. Lai retired from law enforcement. He has been recognized for his extensive managerial experience in public service, demonstrating significant achievements as both an operational police commander and a skilled crime investigator. As an expert in Information Security, Dr. Lai has earned respected credentials, including Certified Information Systems Auditor (CISA) from ISACA and Information Security Management Systems Lead Auditor, certified by the International Organization for Standardization (ISO). In his current academic role, Dr. Lai contributes his wealth of knowledge and expertise to the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology as an adjunct associate professor. He is also honored with the position of Honorary Dean at the Hunan Guoke Computility Digital Science and Technology Research Institute, furthering his commitment to the advancement of digital science and technology.
