# Impact of Online Word of Mouth on Channel Disintermediation for Information Goods

HO CHEUNG BRIAN LEE AND XINXIN LI

HO CHEUNG BRIAN LEE (brian\_lee@uml.edu; corresponding author) is an Assistant Professor of Operations and Information Systems, Manning School of Business, University of Massachusetts Lowell. He obtained his Ph.D. from the School of Business, University of Connecticut. Dr. Lee’s primary research interests include the economics of information systems, crowdsourcing, and platform strategies. His work has appeared in Information Systems Research and Decision Sciences.

XINXIN LI (xinxin.li@uconn.edu) is an Associate Professor of Operations and Information Management at the School of Business, University of Connecticut. She received her Ph.D. from The Wharton School at University of Pennsylvania. Dr. Li’s research interests lie at the intersection of information systems and marketing, with an emphasis on the implications of new technologies to consumer welfare, firm pricing and competitive strategies. Her work has been published in Management Science, MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, and Marketing Science, among other journals. She is an Associate Editor for MIS Quarterly.

ABSTRACT: With the recent advances in digital technology, creators of information goods such as books, songs, or videos can now self-publish and sell their work directly to consumers, without the help of traditional publishers. In this study, we construct an analytical model to examine the role of online word of mouth in this trend of disintermediation. Online word of mouth can reveal product quality for experience goods and raise awareness to accelerate product diffusion. Intuitively, one would expect that with online word of mouth, creators would be more likely to skip publishers and sell directly to consumers. However, our results suggest that online word of mouth may, in fact, encourage more creators to use publishers for high quality work. Our model also makes predictions on the conditions under which online word of mouth benefits publishers and for what types of products and creators it has the most impact.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: Information goods, content goods, self-publishing, channel disintermediation, online word of mouth, eWoM.

## Introduction

Digitization has significantly reduced the cost of producing, manufacturing, and distributing information goods, such as books, songs, and videos [7]. Many platforms that distribute information goods allow users to both publish and consume information goods without the help of commercial middlemen in promoting or launching the products (e.g., Amazon Kindle, YouTube, and SoundCloud, etc.). Given the prevalence of these platforms, the creators of information goods are now able to skip the publishers (the distribution intermediaries in the information goods supply chain) and directly reach consumers through online self-publishing platforms or their own website [26, p.187–198]. This action of creators bypassing the publishers and selling directly to consumers is called self-publishing, and this phenomenon of eliminating the traditional intermediaries from the distribution channel can be called disintermediation.

Among all the categories of information goods, disintermediation is currently most prevalent in the e-book supply chain. Since Amazon established its online selfpublishing platform in 2007, the size of the self-publishing community has been increasing exponentially. In 2013, R.R. Bowker, an ISBN issuing agent, announced that at least 400,000 ISBNs were issued for self-published books,<sup>1</sup> some of which even became New York Times bestsellers without any help from publishers.<sup>2</sup> Some commentators argue that because it is now much easier for authors to self-publish their books on the internet and earn a higher royalty, publishers will gradually lose their position in the market [18]. Others counter-argue that publishers remain an attractive option for authors given the publishers’ significantly higher advertising budget compared to individual authors [1].

In this paper, we add to this discussion by exploring the types of creators who are likely to move from traditional publishers toward self-publishing. More importantly, we also study the role of online word of mouth (WOM) in influencing the creators channel choice between selling through publishers and self-publishing.

We construct an analytical model to study the channel choice of a creator who chooses between self-publishing and selling through the traditional intermediary (i.e., a publisher) to maximize profit. The creator creates an information good whose quality is unknown to consumers prior to purchase. Consumers make their purchase decisions based on their expectations of the product’s quality. Not all consumers in the market are aware of the product. Product awareness (i.e., the proportion of consumers who are aware of the product) is affected by advertising spending, the reputation of the creator, and the rate of product diffusion (from early adopters to consumers arriving in later periods). Consumer demand is determined by both product awareness and the purchase decisions of the consumers who are aware of the product. The creator’s profit is a proportion of the sales revenue, which is called the royalty rate. To choose between the two channels, the creator trades off between a higher royalty rate (i.e., a higher percent of the sales revenue) but potentially lower total sales in the self-publishing channel [1, 18] versus the lower royalty rate but potentially higher total sales driven by the larger advertising budget of the publisher [1, 25].

We find that creators with high reputation are more likely to choose self-publishing than creators with low reputation because a high reputation can compensate for a lower advertising budget in raising product awareness. Furthermore, the channel choice of the creators can be affected by the presence of online WOM, including product reviews, forum discussions, and social network messages, and so forth.

Online WOM has two prominent effects here: first, it affects consumers’ expected product quality by revealing other consumers’ experience of product quality [74], and second, it enables consumers to quickly spread product information to more potential buyers. That is, more consumers will know about the existence of the product due to the enhanced rate of product diffusion [20]. Intuitively, one would expect that these two effects are able to help creators promote their products, especially if product quality is high, and therefore should be able to compensate the individual creators’ disadvantage in advertising budget and favor the choice of self-publishing over selling through a publisher, which leads to disintermediation. Nevertheless, we find that online WOM can encourage some creators with high quality products, who would otherwise choose to self-publish if there were no online WOM, to sell through a publisher. This retains the publisher’s intermediary role between the consumers and the creators, which we call a reintermediation effect. Therefore, online WOM can be a doubled-edged sword for the traditional intermediary.

Such mixed impact of online WOM can be explained as follows. When there is no online WOM, consumers have no means to learn the quality of the product before purchase, so the creator, as well as the publisher (if the creator chooses to sell through the publisher), makes decisions based on consumers’ expected product quality which is the same regardless of the actual product quality (due to the inability for a separating equilibrium to sustain in this case). Because online WOM informs consumers about the quality of the products, the creator, as well as the publisher, is now able to make decisions based on the actual product quality. This incentivizes the publisher to increase its advertising effort for high quality products but at the same time lower its advertising effort for low quality products. As a result, the publisher channel becomes more appealing to some creators with high quality work under the influence of online WOM (a reintermediation effect), and yet at the same time loses to the self-publishing channel on some creators with low quality work due to online WOM (a disintermediation effect). Surprisingly, the reintermediation effect of online WOM on high quality products is generally stronger than its disintermediation effect on low quality products. This is because the increased advertising effort for high quality work due to online WOM is further amplified by the enhanced product diffusion, the other effect of online WOM.

Our results also suggest the type of creators who are likely to be affected by online WOM, that is, who are likely to change their channel choice once online WOM becomes available. When there is no online WOM, regardless of actual product quality, creators of low reputation will choose to use the publisher channel and creators of high reputation will choose to self-publish. The presence of online WOM affects the channel choice of creators with different reputations differently. Specifically, creators of very high reputation are not affected by the presence of online WOM and they still choose to self-publish. Creators of low reputation still use the publisher channel for high quality products, but will disintermediate the publisher under the influence of online WOM if the quality of their product is low. Other creators whose reputation is in the middle range will self-publish their low quality product, but will reintermediate the publisher for high quality products once online WOM becomes available. Apart from the main findings, we also explore how online WOM changes the optimal pricing and advertising strategy, as well as its impact on consumer welfare.

This paper contributes to the debate over the creators’ choice between selling through publishers and self-publishing, and it points to the important role of online WOM in influencing this channel decision. Online WOM does not necessarily favor self-publishing and may not always benefit the consumers. Our findings thus provide important managerial implications for creators, publishers and the websites that facilitate consumer communications in terms of how to utilize online WOM to improve profit or consumer welfare.

## Literature Review

This work relates to studies on digital disintermediation. Because the advent of internet technology has significantly reduced the cost of search and distribution, especially for information goods, traditional intermediaries face the risk of being eliminated from the electronic market [57]. Bailey and Bakos [6] argue that intermediaries may continue to play important roles in electronic markets because of their capability of providing market information and of aggregating and matching supply and demand. Bhargava and Choudhary [10] further suggest network externalities as an additional aggregation benefit of information intermediaries. Due to the online social interactions [50], online platforms have become increasingly popular and provide an alternative channel for consumers to acquire information and thus potentially disintermediate the traditional middlemen. Chiang [16] further suggests that manufacturers may bypass distribution intermediaries if the manufacturers’ competence to engage in direct sales through product diffusion is high. Chiang and Jiang-Li [15] show that a large market share of an internet service provider encourages content providers to disintermediate the third party content delivery networks to obtain a higher profit. Our work contributes to this stream of work by analyzing how online WOM, including product reviews, forum discussions, social network messages, and so forth, impact this trend of digital disintermediation. We differ from the existing research in disintermediation by first analytically examining how a feature of online platforms (online WOM) interacts with the disintermediating process. Our results suggest that although online WOM is able to facilitate dissemination of product information and assists matching between supply and demand to improve product diffusion, potentially supplanting some functions of intermediaries, it can in fact reintermediate the intermediaries for high quality products.

This work is also related to studies on channel coordination [3, 16, 17, 33, 49, 69]. This stream of work mainly focuses on how manufacturers coordinate multiple channels in which they both sell through intermediary channels (retailers) and sell directly to consumers. In contrast, our paper examines the mutually exclusive channel choice scenario of either self-publishing or selling through publishers (i.e., intermediaries) and focuses on the impact of information technology (e.g., online WOM) on the content creators’ channel choice.

Lastly, this work relates to the rapidly growing literature of online WOM. Earlier work in this literature has primarily been empirical and focused on the impact of online WOM on product consumption [14, 19, 20, 22, 24, 28, 31, 44, 48, 53, 75, 76]. Relatively fewer analytical studies have examined sellers’ incentive to manipulate reviews [21] or adopt different review disclosure policies [45], sellers’ decision to supply consumer-generated product information [12], the impact of price on product reviews [47], the impact of online reviews on firms’ pricing strategy for repeat purchase products [46], and the joint impact of online reviews and past sales volume information on firms’ pricing strategy [52]. None of these studies, however, have discussed the role of online WOM in the distribution channel. A recent paper by Kwark et al. [43] examines a channel coordination setting and shows how online reviews affect upstream competition and consequently the profits of both upstream and downstream players in a supply chain. Sun [66] analyzes the incentives of the manufacturers and retailers to disclose product information under different channel structures for distribution, which have implications for how online WOM that discloses product information by consumers affects the profits of manufacturers and retailers. Unlike these studies, this paper studies an entirely different question by endogenizing the choice of channel structure and exploring how online WOM affects this channel choice of content creators.

## Main Model

Consider a creator who produces an information good and has a choice to sell the product either through a publisher or by self-publishing. We first describe our model setup and discuss the role of online WOM by modeling two separate scenarios: the scenario without online WOM (scenario <sup>o</sup>) and the scenario with online WOM (scenario <sup>w</sup>). In each scenario, we analyze the creator’s choice between the two channels, the publisher channel (channel <sup>Pb</sup>) and the self-publishing channel (channel <sup>Sp</sup>). We then compare the creator’s optimal choice in the two scenarios to examine the role of online WOM in channel disintermediation. The product is sold in two periods. Throughout the paper, we use superscript <sup>c</sup> to indicate <sup>c</sup>hannel $( c \in \{ P b , S p \} )$ , use the first subscript <sup>s</sup> to indicate <sup>s</sup>cenario $( s \in \{ o , w \} )$ , and use the second subscript <sup>T</sup> to indicate <sup>t</sup>ime period $( T \in \{ 1 , 2 \} )$ . The main variables used in our model are listed in Table 1.

## Periodic Demand

Because information good is a type of experience good [70], we model the value of the product as two components: unobservable quality and observable features. The unobservable quality, $q \ ( 0 { < } q { < } 1 )$ , is a draw from a distribution <sup>F</sup> with mean $\mu .$ Consumers know about the distribution <sup>F</sup>, but cannot observe the realization of <sup>q</sup> before consumption. For example, the quality of a book or a movie is hard to evaluate before it is read or watched. Consumers form an expectation of the quality of the product before purchase, based on the distribution of $q ,$ or if online WOM is available, the shared experiences from other buyers of that product. We denote this expected quality as $q e _ { s , T } \ ( 0 { < } q e _ { s , T } { < } 1 )$ , and allow it to vary across scenario s and time T. Consumers are able to inspect the observable features of the product before purchase but may have different preferences. For example, some consumers may prefer violent movies whereas others prefer romantic ones. To capture this heterogeneity, we introduce a misfit cost $t x _ { i } ,$ , where $x _ { i }$ measures the distance between the product and consumer $i \ ' \mathbf { s }$ ideal product in the preference space (related to the features of the product that can be observed before purchase), which is uniformly distributed between 0 and $1 , ^ { 3 }$ and <sup>t</sup> is the unit distance cost. We assume that <sup>t</sup> is sufficiently large (<sup>t</sup>>1 so that the market is never fully covered.<sup>4</sup> Let $p _ { s , T } ^ { c } \left( p _ { s , T } ^ { c } \geq 0 \right)$ represent the price of the product. The product’s utility for a consumer of type <sup>i</sup> is thus $q - t x _ { i } - p _ { s , T } ^ { c }$ . The consumer’s purchase decision is based on the expected utility $q e _ { s , T } - t x _ { i } - p _ { s , T } ^ { c } .$ The consumer will purchase the product if the expected utility is greater than the utility of the outside option, which is normalized to zero.

The product is sold in two periods with an equal-sized population of consumers arriving at each period and staying in the market for one period.<sup>5</sup> We thus normalize the market size in each period to 1. Among the consumers who arrive in period <sup>T</sup> (in channel c under scenario s), only a fraction $( M _ { s , T } ^ { c } )$ are aware of the product and each of them has a unit demand. $M _ { s , T } ^ { c }$ is between 0 and 1. If $M _ { s , T } ^ { c } = 1$ , all the consumers who arrive in period T are aware of the product. If $M _ { s , T } ^ { c } = 0$ , none of these consumers are aware of the product.

The demand in each period is determined by the fraction of the consumers who are aware of the product $( M _ { s , T } ^ { c } )$ in this period multiplied by the fraction of those aware consumers who decide to buy the product $( d _ { s , T } ^ { c } ) . \ : d _ { s , T } ^ { c }$ can be derived by finding the marginal consumer whose expected utility is equal to zero. From $q e _ { s , T } - t x _ { i } - p _ { s , T } ^ { c } = 0$ , we have $\begin{array} { r } { x _ { i } ^ { * } = \frac { q e _ { s , T } - p _ { s , T } ^ { c } } { t } } \end{array}$ , and therefore $d _ { s , T } ^ { c } = \frac { q e _ { s , T } - p _ { s , T } ^ { c } } { t }$ . The demand for the product in period <sup>T</sup> (in channel c under scenario s), denoted as $D _ { s , T } ^ { c }$ , can thus be derived as:

$$
D _ {s, T} ^ {c} = M _ {s, T} ^ {c} \frac {\left(q e _ {s , T} - p _ {s , T} ^ {c}\right)}{t}\tag{1}
$$

## Product Awareness

Product awareness is influenced by three factors: advertising effort [75], the reputation of the creator [41, 74], and the rate of product diffusion [8]. In the following section, we discuss the rationale for including these three factors in our model.

The diffusion models in Marketing (such as the Bass Model) characterize product adoption as a process of products “diffusing” from current adopters to potential adopters, through means such as word of mouth that raise product awareness and encourage potential adopters to imitate the early adopters (e.g., [8, 61, 63, 67]). The argument is that the more consumers who have purchased a product, the more likely that other potential consumers will become aware of the product (e.g., by hearing about it during conversations) and consider buying the product. Correspondingly, we identify natural product diffusion as the first factor that influences product awareness and follow the literature to model it as the product of two factors: the number of consumers who have purchased a product $( D _ { s , T - 1 } ^ { c } )$ multiplied by the diffusion coefficient $( f _ { s } )$ <sup></sup>. Because online WOM significantly increases the speed and the scale of information diffusion from current adopters to potential adopters, we assume that the diffusion coefficient $( f _ { s } )$ is larger when there is online WOM than when there is no online WOM. $f _ { s }$ is similar to the imitation coefficient in the Bass Model, which is typically lower than $\frac { 1 } { 2 }$ [49, 50, 55]. Therefore, we assume $\begin{array} { r } { 0 < f _ { s } < \frac { 1 } { 2 } } \end{array}$

In addition to natural product diffusion, studies in the diffusion literature also point out external influences on product awareness (e.g., [8, 39, 55]). The external influences typically include stimulus from sellers, such as corporate advertising and brand name, which is independent of the number of people who have adopted the product previously. This motivates the other two factors that we identify in our setting: advertising and creator reputation.

It has been well acknowledged in the advertising literature that advertising effort affects the awareness of a new product due to the exposure created by the advertisements (e.g., [36, 37, 64]). Advertising typically serves to raise awareness and invite consumers to search for and recognize the promoted products [58]. Therefore, advertising can affect product awareness, resulting in a higher adoption rate [39, 73], but its content is not necessarily informative of quality (e.g., [59, 73]). Given the limited bandwidth of the communication, advertising usually serves as an invitation for search [58] but does not have much an impact on the perceived quality [23]. Because the advertising effort is usually concentrated at the beginning after product release [27, 37, 39], we assume that advertising is spent only in the first period, and so has a direct effect on product awareness only in the first period. We thus denote the advertising effect in period 1 (in channel $c$ under scenario s) simply as $A _ { s } ^ { c }$ without the subscript $T . A _ { s } ^ { c }$ captures the fraction of the consumers who are aware of the product due to advertising, and is between 0 and 1. Compared to a publisher’s advertising budget, an individual creator’s self-publishing advertising budget is often negligible.<sup>6</sup> Therefore, we normalize the advertising effect in self-publishing channel to zero, that is, $A _ { s } ^ { S p } = 0$

Brand has long been recognized for its role in affecting the consideration set of the consumers in the market (e.g., [38, 40, 54]). The reputation of a creator delimits the fraction of the consumers who are aware of the creator. The more popular (with high reputation) a creator is, the more followers the creator will have (e.g., the subscribers to their social network fan pages or blogs) and the more people will be aware of the product that the creator creates. From this aspect, the reputation of a creator is like the brand of an information good that can increase the awareness of any product that falls under the brand. It is similar to the “star power” of a movie in its role of affecting awareness to the movie [20]. The higher the reputation of the creator is in publishing, the stronger the creator’s brand becomes [40, 72] and the greater the fraction of customers who are aware of that creator [40]. In our model, we define the reputation of a creator as the fraction of the consumers who are aware of the products because of the reputation of the creator and denote it as R. In comparison to advertising, which is a transient effort [37, 39], reputation is intrinsic to the creator and thus can have a lasting effect over the product’s lifecycle. Therefore, we assume that the effect of reputation on product awareness (R) is the same across channel (c), scenario (s) and time (T). M _ {s, 1} ^ {c} = A _ {s} ^ {c} + R\tag{2}

## Timeline and Channel Profits

The timeline in the model can be described as follows. Prior to period 1, the creator makes a choice between selling through a publisher and self-publishing. If the creator chooses to self-publish, the creator will be the content owner. Otherwise, the publisher will be the content owner. The content owner chooses the first-period advertising level. Then, in each period, the content owner sets the price, and the consumers who arrive in that period make purchase decisions based on their expected utility.

To examine the creator’s optimal choice between the two channels, publisher and self-publishing, we first derive the creator’s profit in each channel. The two channels differ in their profit sharing arrangements. In practice, a creator usually gets a significantly smaller portion of revenue if the creator sells through the publisher than under self-publishing.<sup>8</sup> Therefore, we denote the royalty rate in channel <sup>c</sup> as $r ^ { c }$ and assume that $r ^ { S p } > r ^ { P b } ~ ( 0 ~ \leq ~ r ^ { c } \leq 1 )$ . We derive the total revenue, $\Pi _ { s } ^ { c }$ , which is the sum of the revenue in period 1 and period 2 in channel c under scenario s:

$$
\Pi_ {s} ^ {c} = p _ {s, 1} ^ {c} D _ {s, 1} ^ {c} + p _ {s, 2} ^ {c} D _ {s, 2} ^ {c}\tag{4}
$$

If the creator self-publishes, the advertising effect is normalized to zero. From Equations (1) to (4) we can derive the total revenue $( \Pi _ { s } ^ { S p } )$ , the creator’s profit $( \pi _ { s } ^ { S p } )$ and the online self-publishing platform’s profit $( \xi _ { s } ^ { S p } )$ in the case of self-publishing under scenario s as:

$$
\Pi_ {s} ^ {S p} = p _ {s, 1} ^ {S p} R \frac {\left(q e _ {s , 1} - p _ {s , 1} ^ {S p}\right)}{t} + p _ {s, 2} ^ {S p} \left(R + f _ {s} R \frac {\left(q e _ {s , 1} - p _ {s , 1} ^ {S p}\right)}{t}\right) \frac {\left(q e _ {s , 2} - p _ {s , 2} ^ {S p}\right)}{t}\tag{5}
$$

$$
\pi_ {s} ^ {S p} = r ^ {S p} \Pi_ {s} ^ {S p}\tag{6}
$$

$$
\xi_ {s} ^ {S p} = \big (1 - r ^ {S p} \big) \Pi_ {s} ^ {S p}\tag{7}
$$

If the creator sells through the publisher, the publisher sets the advertising spending. We assume that to achieve the targeted advertising effect of $A _ { s } ^ { P b }$ , the publisher incurs a cost of $k A _ { s } ^ { P b ^ { 2 } }$ . This cost function captures the notion that the marginal cost of getting additional consumers through advertising increases as more consumers are already informed. We assume advertising is sufficiently costly $( k { > } \bar { K } )$ such that the publisher never finds it optimal to make everyone aware of the product through advertising.<sup>9</sup> This assumption also allows us to focus on the more interesting results and avoids the corner solution in which the advertising benefit of the publisher always dominates such that the creator always chooses the publisher, regardless of the creator’s reputation and the quality of the product. In the case of selling through the publisher, the total revenue $( \Pi _ { s } ^ { P b } )$ , the creator’s profit $( \pi _ { s } ^ { P b } )$ , and the publisher’s profit $( \xi _ { s } ^ { P b } )$ under scenario s are:

## Role of Online WOM

The presence of online WOM has two effects: a quality-revealing effect and a diffusion-enhancing effect. First, many studies suggest that online WOM enables consumers to assess the quality of the product before purchase (e.g., [12, 13, 26]). Chen and Xie [12] show that consumer reviews are more credible than information provided by third parties or sellers. Gao et al. [30] show that online ratings are strongly correlated with the quality of physicians in the context of medical services. Zhao et al. [74] show that consumers are able to infer product quality through referencing experiences of peer consumers. Wu et al. [71] also suggest that consumers can learn about their preferences and update their expectations of a product’s value with the help of online reviews. Therefore, following the literature, we assume in our main model that if online WOM is available, in either channel, consumers are able to estimate the true quality before purchase, that is, $q e _ { w , 1 } = q e _ { w , 2 } = q . \sp { 1 0 }$ If there is no online WOM, in either channel, consumers have no information other than that $q$ is a draw from the distribution <sup>F</sup> with mean $\mu ;$ therefore, they form their expectation of the quality of the product in both periods based on the mean of the distribution, that is, $q e _ { o , 1 } = q e _ { o , 2 } = \mu . ^ { 1 1 }$ 1

Second, online WOM also facilitates information dissemination regarding consumer demand and product popularity and thus has the potential to affect product diffusion. As discussed earlier, product diffusion is the product of two factors: the number of consumers who have purchased a product $( D _ { s , T - 1 } ^ { c } )$ multiplied by the diffusion coefficient (<sup>f</sup>s). Online WOM significantly increases the speed and the scale of information diffusion from current adopters to potential adopters, and thus has the potential to enhance the diffusion coefficient $f _ { s }$ (e.g., [20, 31]). For example, with the emergence of online social networks, product information can be disseminated much faster and more widely [5] than through traditional word of mouth, which is mostly limited to family and friends [11]. Prior studies on online reviews suggest that online WOM has a tangible impact on sales, which they attribute to the buzz or the awareness raised by online WOM [24, 51]. Researchers have also shown that consumers can be incentivized to effectively recommend products via electronic communications to their peers under careful designs [42, 67]. The interactive functions of current internet technologies such as Mashup API [61] also enable the shared contents to be more vivid and in many different forms such as videos, blogs, and product reviews. These options give consumers more incentives to share content [34] in an online setting. Therefore, given the potential of online WOM in raising product awareness to enhance diffusion [9], we assume in our main model that the diffusion coefficient is higher under scenario <sup>w</sup> than under scenario $^ { o . }$ Specifically, we assume $f _ { w } = a + \Delta a .$ , and $f _ { o } = a ,$ , where $a$ is the diffusion effect due to traditional product diffusion and Δ<sup>a</sup> (Δ<sup>a</sup>>0) captures the enhanced diffusion effect due to online WOM.

## Analysis and Results

Optimal Picing and Advertising and the Influence of Online WOM

In this section, we derive the optimal prices and the publisher’s optimal advertising effort for each of the two scenarios, with online WOM (scenario w) and without online WOM (scenario o). We then examine how the presence of online WOM affects the prices and advertising effort.

For each of the two scenarios, we replace $q e _ { s , T }$ and $f _ { s }$ in Equations (5) through (10) with the corresponding values in the Role of Online WOM section and then derive the optimal prices and the publisher’s optimal advertising effort by finding the first order conditions to maximize the content owner’s profit in each channel $( \pi _ { s } ^ { S p }$ under self-publishing and $\xi _ { s } ^ { P b }$ in the publisher channel, respectively). The result is given in Lemma 1. The proofs of all the lemmas and propositions are in the online Appendix.

Lemma 1: The optimal first period price $( p _ { s , 1 } ^ { c } { } ^ { * } )$ , the optimal second period price $( p _ { s , 2 } ^ { c } { ^ * } )$ , and the publisher’s optimal advertising effort $( A _ { s } ^ { P b ^ { * } } )$ under the two scenarios (with presence and absence of online WOM) are:

$$
\begin{array}{l} A _ {o} ^ {P b ^ {*}} = \frac {\mu^ {2} (1 - r ^ {P b}) (a \mu + 4 t) ^ {2}}{1 2 8 k t ^ {3}}, A _ {w} ^ {P b ^ {*}} = \frac {q ^ {2} (1 - r ^ {P b}) ((a + \Delta a) q + 4 t) ^ {2}}{1 2 8 k t ^ {3}} \\ p _ {o, 1} ^ {c *} = \frac {\mu (4 t - a \mu)}{8 t}, p _ {w, 1} ^ {c *} = \frac {q (4 t - (a + \Delta a) q)}{8 t} \\ p _ {o, 2} ^ {c *} = \frac {\mu}{2}, p _ {w, 2} ^ {c *} = \frac {q}{2} \end{array}
$$

As shown in Equations (5) and (8), the second period price only affects the profit from the second period, whereas the first period price not only has a direct effect on the profit from the first period but also has an indirect effect on the profit from the second period through diffusion effect. Therefore, while the optimal price in the second period is chosen simply to maximize the profit from the consumers who are aware of the product in the second period, when choosing the optimal price in the first period, the content owner has to also consider its influence on the expected profit from the second period. Therefore, the optimal first period price is in fact a function of the optimal second period price. Formally, we can rewrite $\begin{array} { r } { { p _ { o , 1 } ^ { c } } ^ { * } = \frac { a { p _ { o , 2 } ^ { t } } ^ { * } \left( { p _ { o , 2 } ^ { c } } ^ { * } - \mu \right) + 4 \mu t } { 8 t } } \end{array}$ and $\begin{array} { r } { p _ { w , 1 } ^ { c } { ^ * } = \frac { ( a + \Delta a ) { p _ { w , 2 } ^ { c } } ^ { * } \left( p _ { w , 2 } ^ { c } { ^ * } - q \right) + 4 q t } { 8 t } } \end{array}$

It is also interesting to note that the optimal prices are the same in the two channels (publisher and self-publishing). This is because the demand in each period is determined by two terms that are multiplicative: the fraction of the consumers who are aware of the product $( M _ { s , T } ^ { c } )$ multiplied by the fraction of those aware consumers who decide to buy the product $( d _ { s , T } ^ { c } )$ . As a result, the factors that only affect market awareness but not purchase decisions of those aware consumers, such as advertising, will not affect the optimal prices. Instead, the optimal prices are determined by the factors that do have an impact on purchase decisions of those aware consumers, such as consumers’ expected quality of the product. Since consumers’ expected quality only varies across scenarios (with or without online WOM), but does not vary across channels, naturally the optimal price also does not vary across channels.

A direct comparison of the optimal prices under the two scenarios suggests the following proposition.

Proposition 1a: The optimal second period price is higher (lower) under the impact of online WOM if the actual product quality is higher (lower) than the mean of its distribution, i.e., if <sup>q</sup>>μ(<sup>q<</sup>μ).

Proposition 1b: The optimal first period price is lower under the impact of online WOM except if the actual product quality is higher than or equal to the mean of its distribution $( q \geq \mu )$ and the additional product diffusion due to online WOM is relatively small $\begin{array} { r } { ( \Delta a \le \frac { ( q - \mu ) ( 4 t - a ( q + \mu ) ) } { q ^ { 2 } } ) } \end{array}$

Propositions 1a and 1b suggest that the impact of online WOM on the optimal prices differs in different periods. Specifically, the second period price is solely determined by consumers’ expected quality. By revealing the actual quality, online WOM changes consumers’ expected quality from the mean of the distribution $( \mu )$ to the actual quality of the product $( q )$ . Therefore, the second period price is higher under online WOM as long as $q { > } \mu$ (Proposition 1a). In contrast, when setting the first period price, the content owner needs to consider not only the quality-revealing effect but also the diffusion effect from the first period demand to the second period, which gives the content owner an incentive to lower the initial price to increase demand in period 1. Because online WOM enhances the diffusion effect, it further increases the content owner’s incentive to lower the first period price. Therefore, compared to the second period price, the first period price is more likely to be lower under the influence of online WOM than without online WOM. The first period price can, however, be higher under the influence of online WOM if the incentive to increase price due to its positive quality-revealing effect $( q { > } \mu )$ dominates the additional incentive to lower price due to its diffusion-enhancing effect $\begin{array} { r } { ( \Delta a < \frac { ( q - \mu ) ( 4 t - a ( q + \mu ) ) } { q ^ { 2 } } ) } \end{array}$ (Proposition 1b).

Direct comparison of the publisher’s optimal advertising effort under the two scenarios in Lemma 1 suggests the following proposition.

Proposition 2: The publisher’s advertising effort is higher under online WOM except if the actual product quality is lower than or equal to the mean of its distribution $( q \leq \mu )$ and the additional product diffusion as a result of online WOM is relatively small $\begin{array} { r } { ( \Delta a \le \frac { ( \mu - q ) ( a ( q + \mu ) + 4 t ) } { q ^ { 2 } } ) } \end{array}$

Similar to the first period price, the publisher’s advertising effort is also affected by both the consumers’ expected quality at period 1 $( q e _ { s , 1 } )$ , and the product diffusion effect $( f _ { s } )$ on period $2 \mathrm { { : } s }$ demand, but here the direction of the two effects is the same. The higher the consumers’ expected quality, the higher their willingness to pay, which gives the publisher a higher incentive to advertise because of the higher expectation of return. A stronger diffusion effect also incentivizes the publisher to increase advertising to increase demand in period 1 because the first period demand has a stronger influence on second period product awareness. Because online WOM reveals the actual quality of the product and enhances the product diffusion effect, as long as the actual quality $( q )$ or the additional diffusion due to online WOM (Δ<sup>a</sup>) is sufficiently high, the advertising effort is higher under online WOM. The only exception is if both the actual quality and the additional diffusion are low, then the advertising effort can be lower under online WOM.

Prior research suggests that online WOM can be a complement [35] or a substitute [27] to advertising. In our model, both online WOM and advertising increase product awareness and therefore naturally become a substitute for each other. However, our results suggest that the relationship between online WOM and advertising depends on the quality of the product $( q )$ and the diffusion-enhancing effect as a result of online WOM (Δ<sup>a</sup>). Online WOM can be a substitute for advertising if the quality of the product is low, or if the diffusion-enhancing effect of online WOM is weak, or if the product has a niche market that limits the effectiveness of product diffusion (because a larger t makes the condition $\Delta a \leq \frac { ( \mu - q ) ( a ( q + \mu ) + 4 t ) } { q ^ { 2 } }$ more likely to be satisfied). In other cases, online WOM and advertising can be complementary in improving content owner’s profit.

Proposition 3 suggests that a highly reputable creator is more likely to choose selfpublishing than a less reputable creator. This applies to both scenarios, with or without online WOM. From this aspect, online WOM can be beneficial to publishers. This is further confirmed in the next section in which we explicitly examine the impact of online WOM on the publisher’s profit.

## Consumer Surplus and the Role of Self-Publishing and Online WOM

In this section, we first explore how the emergence of a self-publishing channel affects consumer surplus. We then explore the impact of online WOM on the profitability of the creator and the publisher as well as consumer surplus, assuming that the content owner follows the optimal prices and advertising levels in Lemma 1 and the creator follows the optimal channel selection in Proposition 3. Given its complexity, the exploration is done numerically.

Although the opportunity of self-publishing benefits creators, its impact on consumer surplus is mostly negative, compared to the situation when self-publishing is impossible. The only exception is if actual product quality is low and online WOM is unavailable. Figure 5 presents the numerical results regarding the impact of a selfpublishing channel. The intuition can be explained as follows. With or without online WOM, in the self-publishing channel, fewer consumers are aware of the product due to the lack of publishers’ advertising. Therefore, the introduction of a self-publishing channel generally decreases consumer surplus because of lower product consumption as a result of lower product awareness. However, if online WOM is not available, the low product awareness can be good for consumers if product quality is low because fewer people will buy the product that they would not buy if they knew the product quality before purchase. This explains why selfpublishing benefits consumers if product quality is low. This benefit, however, does not apply if online WOM is available because everyone is informed of the quality before purchase.

In markets with self-publishing, how does online WOM affect the market outcomes? We answer this question in Figure 6. We find that except in the region where the creators always select self-publishing regardless of online WOM (e.g., if <sup>R</sup> is greater than approximately 0.13), the publisher benefits from online WOM if the product quality is relatively high (the light grey area in Figure 6b). Similarly, the creator also benefits from online WOM if the product quality is relatively high (the light grey area in Figure 6a). This is because, again, two effects of online WOM are at work: a quality-revealing effect and a diffusion-enhancing effect. The diffusionenhancing effect expands market size and therefore always benefits the publisher and the creator. The quality-revealing effect, however, is not always beneficial to the publisher or the creator; it is beneficial only if the actual quality of the product is relatively high. Thus, taking both effects together, online WOM benefits the publisher and the creator only if the actual product quality is sufficiently high. Also, in Figure 6b, there is a region (the dark grey area) where the publisher’s profit is not affected by online WOM. This is where the creators always choose to self-publish regardless of the presence of online WOM. In this case, the publisher’s profit is always zero.

We also explore how consumer surplus is affected by the presence of online WOM. Unlike creator profit, consumers are generally better off with online WOM if product quality is low or if creator reputation is low (the light grey region in Figure 6c). Here, the diffusion-enhancing effect of online WOM always benefits consumers because it enables more consumers to become aware of and purchase the product and also provides incentive for the content owner to lower price as well as increase product awareness that enables more consumers to purchase. However, the quality-revealing effect of online WOM may affect consumer surplus negatively. For example, revealing a high quality encourages more consumers to buy, but they have to pay a higher price. As a result, consumers benefit from the presence of online WOM mostly for low quality products, for which the revealed low quality induces a lower price as well as discourages consumers from purchasing a disappointing product. Consumers also benefit from online WOM if product quality is high but the creator’s reputation is low. For those products, the benefit mainly comes from the enhanced product awareness and the resulting higher sales of satisfying products.

It is also worth noting that if product quality is high and the creator’s reputation is low, online WOM, in fact, benefits all of the three parties: creators, publishers, and consumers. In this region, online WOM generally favors the publisher channel. This again suggests that online WOM may not necessarily promote self-publishing, and as a result of reintermediation, consumers and creators can be better off as well.

## Model Extensions

We examine four model extensions to check the robustness of our main results. In the first extension, we allow the publisher to take into consideration channel competition when selecting its advertising effort. Specifically, the publisher may deviate from its optimal advertising effort in order to win over the creator from selfpublishing, for which the publisher gets zero profit. Under this competitive advertising strategy, the publisher will maximize its profit under two additional constraints: first, the advertising level is set such that the creator’s profit from the publisher channel is at least as high as the profit from self-publishing such that the creator will choose the publisher channel, and second, the publisher’s profit is nonnegative.

In the second extension, we alternatively assume that under scenario <sup>w</sup>, online WOM will not be able to reveal product quality completely until the second period. Specifically, we examine the situation in which online WOM reveals product quality only in the second period, and in the first period, consumers’ expected quality is equal to the mean of the distribution for product quality (μ) unless other signals that reveal product quality exist, such as price signals or advertising spending. We solve the separating equilibrium numerically and then derive the creator’s optimal channel choice by selecting the channel that generates the higher profit for the creator.

In the third extension, we examine a duopoly setting with two competing creators, and numerically derive the equilibrium outcome. Additionally, in the fourth extension, we allow the mean of the distribution for product quality to be correlated with creator’s reputation.

Our main results regarding the creator’s channel choice and the influence of online WOM on this choice (Propositions 3–5) continue to hold in these model extensions. The details of these model extensions are in the online Appendix.

## Conclusion and Discussion

This study examines how the availability of the self-publishing channel affects the channel choices of the creators and consumer welfare for information goods and, more importantly, the role of online WOM in this channel competition. This is one of the first few studies that examine the impact of online WOM on the supply side of the market as opposed to the demand side.

We find that creators with high reputation are likely to be swayed toward the new self-publishing option, but higher product quality tends to favor the option of selling through publishers. Compared to the case in which self-publishing is not available, the existence of this new publishing option may hurt consumers except if there is no online WOM and the product quality is low.

The presence of online WOM can affect the channel choices of the creators and consumer welfare. Online WOM reveals product quality to consumers before purchase and enhances product diffusion to raise consumer awareness. These two effects of online WOM directly affect the content owners’ pricing and advertising spending choices. Specifically, the content owner tends to lower the introductory price unless the product quality is high and the additional product diffusion as a result of online WOM is relatively small. The content owner, if it is the publisher, is inclined to increase advertising spending unless the product quality is low and diffusion-enhancing effect of online WOM is relatively small.

By affecting the content owner’s pricing and advertising, ultimately online WOM affects the creator’s optimal channel decision. Specifically, online WOM reintermediates publishers by urging some creators of medium reputation to sell their high quality work through publishers instead of self-publishing, but at the same time, online WOM also disintermediates publishers by permitting some creators of low reputation to self-publish their low quality work rather than going through publishers. In addition, the reintermediation effect of online WOM on high quality products is particularly strong compared to its disintermediation effect on low quality products. The presence of online WOM benefits the creators and the publishers mainly if product quality is high, and benefits the consumers mainly if product quality or creator reputation is low. There are, however, situations in which online WOM benefits all parties (the creator, the publisher and the consumers), such as if product quality is high and the creator’s reputation is low.

Our key model parameters can be estimated by doing market research. For example, one may estimate the reputation of a creator (R) by doing surveys or using metrics such as the subscribers of a creator’s social network fan page or blogs. Quality of a product (q) and heterogeneity in consumers’ preferences for the observable features of the product (t) can be estimated using questionnaires, surveys and focus groups. Other parameters can be estimated by drawing references from similar products in the market. For example, the mean of the distribution for product quality (μ) can be estimated using the average quality of similar products produced by the creator in the past. Diffusion coefficients can also be estimated by utilizing the sales history of similar products [55, 56]. This type of data can be purchased from market research firms like Nielsen.

Our results have several important managerial implications. First, although the emergence of the new online platforms offers an opportunity for creators of information goods to disintermediate the middlemen and reach consumers directly, traditional middlemen also benefit from online WOM to help sustain their competiveness. Surprisingly, although online WOM potentially supplants some functions of intermediaries, it does not always provoke disintermediation (i.e., self-publishing) especially if product quality is high. A higher diffusion-enhancing effect by online WOM also more likely favors the publisher over self-publishing. Publishers should embrace online WOM as a new tool in their marketing mix to stimulate product awareness and encourage online consumer conversations regarding high quality products.

Second, online WOM can be a substitute or complement to advertising, and the publisher should carefully balance between the two. Our results suggest that because online WOM reveals the quality of the product to consumers before purchase and enhances product diffusion, it is optimal for a publisher to increase its advertising effort for high quality products to leverage the two effects of online WOM to amplify the advertising effect, suggesting a complementary effect between advertising and online WOM for high quality products. At the same time, the publisher should cut down its advertising budget for low quality work because online WOM will weaken the advertising effect of such work by revealing the lower-thanexpected quality and suppressing sales, suggesting a substitution effect between advertising and online WOM for low quality products. It is therefore even more critical for the publisher to distinguish between high and low quality products if online WOM is available.

Third, from the perspective of self-publishing platform providers such as Amazon, our results provide suggestions on the types of creators and products that are likely to be attracted by self-publishing. Self-publishing platform providers might want to focus their marketing efforts on attracting high reputation creators or helping creators whose product has a relatively low quality. Alternatively, self-publishing platform providers can take on the challenge to design better incentive schemes and enhance their competitiveness to win over those creators who are more likely to be attracted to publishers, for example, the creators with low reputation but high quality work.

Finally, online WOM is beneficial to consumers mainly if product quality is low. Therefore, the websites that aim to facilitate online WOM communications to improve consumer welfare should focus mainly on screening out low quality work such that consumers will not regret their purchases.

Our analysis is intended to be largely prescriptive rather than descriptive, making it hard to find anecdotal evidence to directly support our results. However, there is evidence that some key insights behind the results in our model are relevant in practice. For example, interviews of self-publishing authors suggest that these authors recognize the importance of their reputation or “brand” when making decisions between going with publishers and self-publishing, and some high reputation authors turned down offers from publishers after self-publishing became available.<sup>18</sup> This is consistent with our result that high reputation authors are more likely to choose self-publishing over the publishers. The observation that some authors with decent reputation insisted on looking for a publisher for their high quality work before going to self-publish because of the additional market awareness publishers can bring<sup>19</sup> is also consistent with our result regarding the channel choice of the authors with intermediate reputation. The role of online WOM is also recognized in the trend of self-publishing. A TBRC business research report suggests that self-publishing authors in the book industry can leverage social media and other technological platforms to market their products instead of relying on the promotion of publishers.<sup>20</sup> Some self-publishing authors have also cited online WOM as a main factor influencing their decision to go with self-publishing due to the benefit of online WOM in helping authors promote their work.<sup>21</sup> While online WOM is predominately considered as a contributor to the competitiveness of self-publishing by authors and the press, our model further considers the role of online WOM in revealing quality and the resulting strategic response from publishers by increasing advertisement investment on high quality work. From this aspect, our prescriptive analysis suggests the possible reintermediation effect of online WOM as a result of the publishers’ strategic response in advertising, which has been largely overlooked by the press.

Our model is limited in several aspects that call for future research. First, in line with practices in the e-book industry, we model revenue sharing between the publisher and the creators using a fixed royalty rate that is lower for creators using a publisher and higher for creators choosing self-publishing. It would be interesting for future studies to examine other contractual models for sharing fixed costs, variable costs, and revenues (such as wholesale contracts and performance based royalty). Second, to focus on the key tradeoffs between the publisher channel and the self-publishing channel, we stylize our model of product awareness and quality expectation. In particular, we assume quality expectation is independent of creator reputation, because the actual product quality is independent of creator reputation. While this is generally a reasonable assumption because creators of low reputation may create a product with either high or low quality, our model can be expanded in future research to accommodate correlations between creator reputation and product quality. Finally, in this study, our focus is on how the two major effects of online WOM suggested by the previous literature (i.e., quality revealing and diffusion enhancing) affect creators’ channel choice. We leave the characteristics of online WOM itself to be studied in future research.
