# Online support for business processes by electronic intermediaries

Steve Muylle <sup>a,</sup>⁎, Amit Basu <sup>b,1</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Vlerick Leuven Gent Management School, Ghent University, Belgium

<sup>b</sup> Cox School of Business, SMU, Dallas, TX 75275, USA

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 7 February 2006 Received in revised form 6 February 2008 Accepted 14 February 2008 Available online 21 February 2008

Keywords: Electronic intermediaries Business process Typology

## a b s t r a c t

Electronic intermediaries (EIMs) are Internet-based organizations that enable other organizations to interact with each other in business processes. While EIMs have become a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant sector of the Internet economy, theory and guidelines for planning the set of business processes an EIM can support online are sparse. This paper presents an approach to plan the speci<sup>fi</sup>c online processes supported by a particular EIM. The approach draws on conceptual research on business process engineering, EIMs, and e-business architectures. Three categories of processes, together with their respective sub-processes, are identi<sup>fi</sup>ed and described. The approach then provides guidance in the choice of processes, based upon two factors — <sup>fi</sup>rst the resource base of the EIM, and second, its evolutionary context. The approach is demonstrated with respect to a set of existing EIMs. The insight gained from this analysis is that the business performance of an EIM can be enhanced by considering its resource base and evolutionary context when planning its business processes.

## 1. Introduction

Although much of the commercial use of the World Wide Web in its early years focused on online selling (e-commerce), a wider variety of e-business initiatives (collectively described as e-business) have emerged in recent years. Such initiatives have led to changes in the structure of business processes in many industries. While some e-business initiatives have helped <sup>fi</sup>rms disintermediate key business processes by interacting directly with their suppliers and customers [19,39], other e-business initiatives have motivated the evolution of new types of electronic intermediaries (EIMs) in Internet-based channels [5].

As a result, EIMs have become a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant sector of the Internet economy. Companies like Yahoo, Google, eBay and Amazon have become household names and major players in the investments markets. In addition to these poster children, numerous EIMs in the business-to-business (B2B) environment have persisted — after a consolidation and closure phase at the start of the decade. eMarket Services, for instance, a not-for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t project funded by the trade promotion organizations of various countries, provides a non-exhaustive directory of over 630 active electronic marketplaces around the world [www.emarketservices. com February 2008]. The emergence of such EIMs affects the way business is done across industries by increasing <sup>fi</sup>rms' access to new customers, expanding supply choices available to <sup>fi</sup>rms, and lowering transaction costs for all participants [31]. As a result, the importance of EIMs is increasing considerably, either as Internet-based platforms for trade support or as gateways to connect and integrate through the Internet [36,41].

However, EIMs are still a relatively new and poorly understood type of business. There is little theory and few guidelines to help entrepreneurs, managers and investors understand, plan and evaluate the possible functional scope of an EIM where the functional scope is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the set of business processes the organization supports online, although there is a growing literature on EIMs that is <sup>fi</sup>lling the gap. In the absence of a conceptually based classi<sup>fi</sup>cation, EIM scholars and practitioners have no solid foundation for investigating and planning online support for business processes by an EIM. Therefore, the goal of this paper is to develop a comprehensive typology of business processes an EIM can support online. By means of such a typology both scholars and practitioners can improve their understanding of the functional scope of an EIM. In addition, the typology can serve as a basis to decide on the choice of processes an EIM can support online. We identify two key factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence the choice of processes that an EIM can support. These are the resource base of the EIM, and its evolutionary context. For a set of active EIMs, we show how the typology allows the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of online process support, and how EIM resource base and evolutionary context in<sup>fl</sup>uence the selection of appropriate business processes to support.

The paper is structured in six sections. We review the literature on business process engineering, the functional scope of EIMs, and e-business architectures in Section 2. In Section 3, based on the literature review, we present a typology of online processes an EIM can support. We then describe how the resource base and evolutionary context of an EIM in<sup>fl</sup>uence the appropriate functional scope of an EIM, in Section 4. To illustrate the use of this approach, we provide the key insights from the results of a qualitative study of active EIMs in Section 5. Finally, in Section 6, we conclude with avenues for further research.

## 2. Literature review

In this section, we review three streams of research in the existing literature that in<sup>fl</sup>uence our approach and its contribution. We start by examining the literature on business process engineering, and then consider the literature on EIMs and e-business architectures.

## 2.1. Business process engineering

The business process engineering literature advocates that adopting a process view of the business is a particularly useful way for studying the relationship between information technology and the way business is carried out [17,25]. Such a view acknowledges the natural partnership of information technology and business process design [18] and calls for moving from functional perspectives to looking at the fundamental process of the business from a customer driven, cross-functional perspective [24]. While much of business process engineering has taken place within organizations improving internal ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, the advent of the commercial Internet has allowed <sup>fi</sup>rms to create and adopt a new generation of processes based on Internet technology that allows them to alter, and improve the performance of their crossorganizational processes with customers, suppliers, partners, and even competitors [11].

In de<sup>fi</sup>nitional terms, business processes can be seen as “a set of logically related tasks performed to achieve a de<sup>fi</sup>ned business outcome” [[18], p.12] where a process is “any activity or group of activities that take an input, add value to it, and provide an output to an internal or external customer” [[26], p.9]. In this paper, we keep with this de<sup>fi</sup>nition of business process and focus on an EIM planning and supporting crossorganizational business processes between organizations interacting in its network. For example, [12] discussed the use of e-business (and Web services) to reinforce business processes in a <sup>fi</sup>rm.

## 2.2. Electronic intermediaries

Much of the existing research in the area of electronic intermediation has focused on electronic marketplaces [35]. [13], for instance, identi<sup>fi</sup>es three market-making functions that can be supported by electronic markets: identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of potential trading partners, selection of a speci<sup>fi</sup>c partner, and execution of the transaction. In an Internet-based setting, [5] describes two functions that are provided by Internet-based electronic marketplaces: (1) matching buyers and sellers; and (2) facilitation of transactions. The process of matching buyers' demand with sellers' product offerings involves buyers' determination of product features offered by sellers as well as the aggregation of different products by the marketplace, search of buyers for sellers and of sellers for buyers, and price discovery. The facilitation of transactions comprises logistics, payment settlement, and the establishment of trust. In a similar vein, [4] proposes four electronic market intermediary roles suggested by the literature: (1) aggregation of buyer demand or seller products; (2) protection of buyers and sellers from opportunistic behavior by becoming an agent of trust; (3) facilitation of the market; and (4) matching of buyers and sellers.

Given that the approaches to classifying electronic market functions may vary signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in their detail yet have many mutual elements, [28] states that a common language and categorization of key processes is needed and provides a generalizable model featuring <sup>fi</sup>ve key trade processes (search, valuation, logistics, payments and settlements, and authentication), which they consider important to managers who must select and design new trading models.

In addition to market-making in electronic markets, EIMs can offer a variety of other functions. It can be argued that EIMs stimulate new roles in which participants transact, collaborate, and integrate, coordinated by an “electronic hand” (as opposed to Adam Smith's [42] notion of an “invisible hand” guiding the process of buyer–seller matching though price discovery). However, academic research in this area has been very sparse to date, and has often taken a narrow perspective on such mechanisms. In particular, the notion of an EIM assuming roles other than that of marketmaker has not received much attention in the literature.

In a setting of Internet-based electronic markets for procurement of non-production related items, [40] distinguishes between traditional support of buying and selling processes, and additional services for buying companies such as back-end system integration and reporting, decision or negotiation support. Also, [30] introduces the notion of all-inone markets which combine multiple trading mechanisms on a common platform and allow participants to bene<sup>fi</sup>t from integration functionality for leveraging long term supplier partnerships, [14] focuses on collaboration electronic marketplaces highlighting interaction in the areas of planning, supply chain management, new product development, and product lifecycle management, and [2] discusses support for negotiation processes.

Based on a synthesis of prior research, [16] shows that EIMs not only offer basic market functions (aggregation, matching and facilitation) but also support management needs and technology adaptation. Management needs include capturing and analysis of online transactions data, provisioning of product information and purchasing expertise for better sourcing decisions, and streamlining of work<sup>fl</sup>ow and the promotion of collaboration. Technology adaptation includes extension of the connectivity of trading networks through systems integration, implementation of technical standards, and information technology outsourcing. In a similar vein, a Delphi study on the future of business-to-business electronic marketplaces in Germany found that support for interaction between participating <sup>fi</sup>rms will become as important as transactions with a strong emphasis on streamlining processes like supply chain management [27].

In conclusion, as can be noted from the literature review on EIMs, several frameworks have been proposed to assist EIMs in con<sup>fi</sup>guring and planning their online operations. In many of these studies, the functions and processes supported are presented as representative rather than exhaustive, though some authors (e.g., [28,29]) claim to identify comprehensive sets, at least for speci<sup>fi</sup>c categories such as online trade.

## 2.3. e-Business architectures

Another stream of literature that de<sup>fi</sup>nes EIMs in terms of functional scope in online environments is work on ebusiness architectures, where the term ‘architecture’ refers to a multi-layered speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of services and/or processes. [43] proposed the eCo architecture, specifying a collection of services, ranging from low-level network services to industryspeci<sup>fi</sup>c market services. The primary focus of eCo was to promote an object-oriented approach to e-business based interoperable services and applications and was geared towards the development of a reliable market structure, and the evolution of standards that would be sustained by technology and software vendors. From a managerial perspective, [6,8,37] propose a multi-layered Electronic Commerce Architecture (ECA) that enables businesses, technology providers, as well as intermediaries to examine their roles in the online environment. The ECA consists of three levels of services: network services, commerce services, and content services. Network services are services that support the network (e.g., the Internet) through which electronic commerce is implemented. Commerce services relate to the generalizable model of key exchange processes developed by [28]. Content services are services that are speci<sup>fi</sup>c to a particular industry or domain. The ECA serves as a technically and managerially sound foundation for studying electronic commerce initiatives and their adoption and diffusion.

In summary, while the three streams of literature discussed in this section augment scholars' and practitioners understanding of the functional scope of an EIM, a comprehensive framework that consolidates the insights drawn from these streams of literature and classi<sup>fi</sup>es EIM online process support is still needed. The remainder of this paper describes such a framework.

## 3. Processes supported by electronic intermediaries

In order to address the limitations discussed in the previous section, we present a typology of EIMs in terms of their functional scope. A typology is a conceptually based classi<sup>fi</sup>cation (in contrast to an empirically derived taxonomy)

that provides a theoretical foundation for investigation [23]. The typology is based on the literature review and most closely builds upon the electronic market analytic framework of [16] and the Electronic Commerce Architecture (ECA) framework of [6]. In addition, it adopts a process view of the business to identify how EIMs can enable other organizations to interact with each other in business processes.

Below, we classify the processes relevant to EIMs, and discuss the associated sub-processes, practices and technologies. We then identify a set of roles based on the process categories that EIMs can use to describe their functional scope.

## 3.1. Online processes for EIMs

The following types of processes are proposed in our typology:

1. Trade processes: These support buying and selling online. The buying process starts with a realization that a purchase has to be made. Sometimes, the buying process starts with the buyer having a well-de<sup>fi</sup>ned need for a speci<sup>fi</sup>c product. However, buying is not always purposeful, and buyers sometimes have to be engaged even before they identify the product they want, or even before they are conscious of the desire for the product [33]. The process is completed when the product and/or service has been delivered to the buyer. In keeping with [28] and [6,8,37], <sup>fi</sup>ve key trade processes are identi<sup>fi</sup>ed:

• Search: This is the process of <sup>fi</sup>nding relevant entities and objects for any business trade. As part of this, search mechanisms enable sellers and buyers to locate each other to exchange goods and services. Possible mechanisms include online catalogs, search engines, directories and portals.

• Authentication: This process helps ensure the authenticity of the parties involved, as well as the quality of the products and services being traded. Buyer/seller authentication involves registration and quali<sup>fi</sup>cation, which can be achieved through mechanisms such as user ID and password, digital certi<sup>fi</sup>cates, valid <sup>fi</sup>nancial information, bonding and escrow services, and feedback systems. Product authentication can include product speci<sup>fi</sup>cations and quality information, input from third-party authorities and experts, quality assurance and inspection processes, and bonding and escrow services [7].

• Valuation: This is the process of price discovery, which can be of two types: (1) <sup>fi</sup>xed pricing; and (2) dynamic pricing. Fixed pricing is usually achieved through catalogs for standardized products (including integrated catalogs that offer products from multiple sellers). Dynamic pricing can be supported through templates for online negotiations, auctions, and exchanges.

• Payment: This supports payments for online purchases. Online support for payment requires provision of electronic payment instruments at the transaction level. It also includes mechanisms such as shopping carts and electronic wallets, account-based payments, electronic billing and payment, and <sup>fi</sup>nancing. Implementation of a payment process often involves integration with credit card companies, banks, and transaction service companies.

• Logistics: This process enables the movement of products and resources within and between trading entities. At a basic level, it includes shipment and delivery of purchased products from the seller to the buyer. However, it may also include monitoring and tracking of all material <sup>fl</sup>ows, production <sup>fl</sup>ows and work-in-process inventory. flows, production flows and work-in-process inventory.

These <sup>fi</sup>ve trade processes not only correspond to the generalizable trade processes of [28] and the ECA commerce processes of [6,8,37], they also subsume the basic market functions of aggregation, matching, and facilitation as proposed in [16]. Furthermore, aggregation, matching, trust, and facilitation, as suggested in [4], and the typical services described in [40] are consistent with the trade processes category.

2. Decision support processes: These processes enable individuals and <sup>fi</sup>rms to obtain information and use analytical models that enhance their ability to make effective business decisions. They can be supported at two levels. The <sup>fi</sup>rst level is where the <sup>fi</sup>rm can better monitor and plan its own operations, independent of other <sup>fi</sup>rms. The second level is where the <sup>fi</sup>rm enables multiple participant <sup>fi</sup>rms to interact with each other in ways that helps each of them make better decisions. Key decision support processes are:

• Configuration: This process helps buyers de<sup>fi</sup>ne needs, and helps buyers and sellers to interact to develop a product that can meet those needs. Support for con<sup>fi</sup>guration may involve a con<sup>fi</sup>gurator tool in the case where buyers can assemble products from standardized components. For more complex requirements, buyers may need electronic requirement determination tools such as requests for information, proposals and quotes (RFI, RFP, RFQ), or collaboration platforms for manual interaction. With respect to technical product design, con<sup>fi</sup>guration also includes sharable Computer Aided Design (CAD) tools and communication facilities such as shared workspaces and application sharing.

• Collaboration: This process facilitates interactions between participant <sup>fi</sup>rms that support joint or collaborative work between multiple people in one or more organizations, using Web-based computer and communication technologies. Collaboration can be used within any relevant business domain, including product development and after-sales support, and requires tools for interactive communication such as conferencing, whiteboarding, electronic brainstorming and voting, bulletin boards and shared data repositories.

• Business intelligence: This includes the provision of information about the business, market conditions and trends. The scope of the market information can include any and all industries, and within each industry, all or most of the relevant <sup>fi</sup>rms. An electronic intermediary can serve a useful role for this purpose by aggregating data from multiple <sup>fi</sup>rms. More sophisticated business intelligence includes analytical tools, so participants can model and analyze their activities as part of their planning processes. While the decision support processes category is grounded in the management needs category of [16], it also captures the interaction dimension of [27] and [14], as well as some of the additional services of [40].

3. Integration processes: Integration processes help <sup>fi</sup>rms to integrate their information, computing and communication systems, on either an intra-<sup>fi</sup>rm or an inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm basis. These processes enable the automation of business tasks across the boundaries of different component information systems. Although intra-<sup>fi</sup>rm integration can sometimes be facilitated by an EIM, the most likely utilization of the integration processes of an EIM is across participant <sup>fi</sup>rms. This can occur both in vertical (or value-chain) integration, where a <sup>fi</sup>rm integrates its systems with those of a supplier or customer/distributor, as well as in horizontal integration, where a <sup>fi</sup>rm integrates its systems with similar systems in a horizontal partner (e.g., by creating integrated catalogs of products or consolidated procurement systems). Speci<sup>fi</sup>c integration processes include:

• Data integration: This is the lowest level of integration. Data integration allows a <sup>fi</sup>rm's software applications to access its partners' databases. A <sup>fi</sup>rm that supports data integration processes enables data to be shared between multiple entities regardless of the speci<sup>fi</sup>c database structures, software and systems that each entity employs.

• Application integration: This involves integration of both data and applications (e.g., order, payment, accounting, inventory and work<sup>fl</sup>ow systems). A promising approach to application integration today is through the use of object-oriented systems and mechanisms such as eXtensible Markup Language (XML) (such mechanisms are often described as “Web services”). Integration through an EIM is particularly useful when a <sup>fi</sup>rm transacts or interacts with multiple parties (e.g., a supply chain with multiple suppliers), since it avoids numerous bilateral integration efforts.

The integration category is consistent with the technology adaptation element of [16], and the integration dimension of [30] and [40].

## 3.2. EIM roles

The typology of processes that an EIM can support online serves as a useful basis for de<sup>fi</sup>ning different possible roles for the EIM in the network of organizations that it interacts with, as illustrated in Table 1. Each of the roles is characterized by the speci<sup>fi</sup>c process category or combination of categories offered by the EIM. While an EIM adopting an exchange role only supports trade processes, an EIM adopting a value-added exchange role also supports decision support processes. Likewise, an EIM taking on a business process integrator role supports both decision support and integration processes. The most elaborate role is the full service provider, which supports trade, decision support, and integration processes.

## 4. Selecting processes for an electronic intermediary

In this section, we draw upon the literature to identify two key factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence the choice of role and processes that an EIM can support online. These are the resource base of the EIM and the EIM's evolutionary context respectively. We describe each of these factors in turn, along with prior work on their signi<sup>fi</sup>cance, and then propose a conceptual model relating these factors and functional scope (and role) in planning and coordinating an EIM for better business performance.

## 4.1. EIM resource base

The functional scope of an EIM can be considered to depend upon the set of resources available to it, i.e. its resource base. This dependence of the set of processes an EIM can support on its resource base is consistent with the consolidated resource-based and activity-system views of strategy, according to which the resource base a <sup>fi</sup>rm deploys in<sup>fl</sup>uences the activities in which it engages. These activities then have product-market implications which supply a basis for evaluating <sup>fi</sup>rm performance [20].

Resources are generally de<sup>fi</sup>ned broadly and refer to the resource portfolio a <sup>fi</sup>rm holds, whether by history, accident, or design. A variety of resource typologies exist in the literature providing classi<sup>fi</sup>cations of resources in tangible, intangible, and personnel-based resources [21,22], and human, business, and technology resources [32,38]. In an IT setting, [9] draws on [21] and distinguishes between physical IT infrastructure, human IT resources, and intangible IT-enabled resources. In line with [9], we consider the EIM resource base to comprise computer resources, human resources, and partnerships. While this categorization is not exhaustive, it keeps with the literature and utilizes a limited number of key resources rather than enumerating all probable resources potentially relevant to EIMs [9]. Also, computer resources, human resources and partnerships have to be considered collectively because of the wide range of possibilities in the IT and e-business resource base. While we do not consider <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources determined by the EIM's ownership and revenue model, we do recognize that <sup>fi</sup>nancial limitations do constrain the quality and sophistication of its business processes. In other words, we assume that the amount of money available to the EIM impacts the implementation rather than the choice of its business processes.

Computer resources include hardware and software relevant to EIMs, which may seek to deploy search engines, catalog organizers and integrators, auction software (both single item and combinatorial), digital certi<sup>fi</sup>cate management systems (including certi<sup>fi</sup>cate authority servers), database management systems, CAD-type tools, Web-conferencing and collaboration tools, groupware tools, business analytics tools, tools for designing Application Programming Interfaces (APIs) and middleware for Electronic Document Interchange (EDI) and XML-based application integration, and Web service con<sup>fi</sup>guration and support tools. Clearly, the deployment of such resources in<sup>fl</sup>uences the extent to which an EIM can support trade, decision support, and integration processes online. It is of interest to note that, as information technology for Internetbased systems evolves, software packages that combine many of the above resources are becoming available, and thus the individual resources do not always have to be acquired piecemeal.

In keeping with [9], the critical dimensions of human resources for EIMs include the technical and managerial skills of the EIM's management and employees. As such, these skills are considered a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant factor in the choice of the activities and processes that can be supported by the EIM.

With respect to partnerships, [1] refers to partnering as an alternative to ownership or control of resources (either through building or acquiring). In a virtual market, a <sup>fi</sup>rm can access resources through partnering yet rivals may have easy access to substitute resources as well. In an EIM setting, typical partnerships include payment processors and <sup>fi</sup>nancial institutions, third-party authentication providers and trustees, shipping and delivery partners, market and industry-speci<sup>fi</sup>c information providers, software support providers, and system integrators.

While the ability of an EIM to support the online business processes of its participant <sup>fi</sup>rms is limited by its existing resource base, this base can be modi<sup>fi</sup>ed to suit changing priorities. However, it is easy to underestimate the complexity of such changes, since the processes that are affected are all “front-of<sup>fi</sup>ce” customer-facing processes. Poor implementation of a new process by an EIM can not only lead to losses with respect to that process, but can also turn off or discourage customers from dealing with that EIM at all.

In summary, the functional scope of an EIM is considered to depend on the resource base that the EIM can deploy, in terms of computer, human and partnership resources. It is important to note, however, that the functional scope of an EIM does not depend just on its resource base. As discussed in the next section, even if all the necessary resources are available, the EIM may choose not to support certain processes, due to its evolutionary context.

## 4.2. EIM evolutionary context

Given the short history of EIMs, very few studies have investigated the impact of evolutionary factors such as the origin, ownership and reach (in terms of <sup>fi</sup>rms that can participate in the EIM), on the functionality and processes that the EIM offers. Indeed, while EIMs have been classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as neutral versus biased (towards either buyers or suppliers), vertical (industry focused) versus horizontal (germane across industries), and open versus closed (with participants vetted for legal and <sup>fi</sup>nancial probity), there is a paucity of studies linking such evolutionary factors to EIM functional scope [31,36].

One such study is [30], where it is argued that the emergence of different types of online market structures (as opposed to hierarchical coordination mechanisms) is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by structural characteristics of the industry and the needs of buyers and sellers. Another approach is presented in [29], where the EIM's ownership structure is used to categorize the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of different types of processes. Other studies do not explicitly factor in EIM role or its evolutionary context when de<sup>fi</sup>ning its functional scope.

Many EIMs are pure-play intermediaries, namely <sup>fi</sup>rms that are independent entities with only online operations. However, it is also possible for a set of <sup>fi</sup>rms that have of<sup>fl</sup>ine business relationships to set up an online intermediary for speci<sup>fi</sup>c business processes spanning one or more of the major process categories discussed above. Our categorization of EIM evolutionary contexts is as follows:

1. Pure-play: This is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as an EIM created by an independent <sup>fi</sup>rm that is a pure intermediary, with no ownership interests in any <sup>fi</sup>rm participating in the EIM. A good example of this is eBay, the online auction house. While eBay has recently acquired some specialized retailers such as half.com (which runs both auctions and dealerships for used books), for the most part, eBay does not participate in any auctions on its Web site.

2. Closed online extension: This is an EIM owned by one or more principals (i.e., <sup>fi</sup>rms that are participants in the EIM). The owners may have pre-existing relationships with each other through traditional of<sup>fl</sup>ine interactions. The primary motivation for such an EIM is to exploit online process ef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies to improve the existing interactions and inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm processes within the group. An example of this is the Covisint EIM (a subsidiary of Compuware Corporation) established by automakers and their suppliers. The goal of the EIM is not to acquire new participants (in the form of new suppliers), but to streamline and improve the supply chain for the major automakers. Although new entrants are allowed into the EIM their selection and authentication is conducted of<sup>fl</sup>ine prior to their joining the EIM.

3. Open online extension: This is a hybrid of the two earlier types. It is set up by an initial consortium of <sup>fi</sup>rms, but the set of participant <sup>fi</sup>rms changes quite frequently. It is easy to see that such an EIM can result from a strategic shift of an initially closed online extension EIM. An example of such an EIM is FreeMarkets (acquired by Ariba in 2004), which supports online registration and authentication processes for new suppliers.

The priorities of an “online extension” intermediary may be different from that of a pure-play intermediary. For instance, a pure-play intermediary that adopts the exchange role has to provide extensive authentication capabilities for both products and parties (traders). On the other hand, a “closed online extension” exchange may have to provide only product identity authentication, and may not have to provide any participant authentication, and little or no product quality authentication. This classi<sup>fi</sup>cation is related to that of [29], where three categories of online exchange are considered, namely private exchanges, consortia exchanges and independent exchanges. In that approach, the primary discriminator between the alternatives in the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation was the ownership of the exchange. Our classi<sup>fi</sup>cation extends the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation to two dimensions, ownership and openness (where openness refers to support for online admittance and authentication of new participants). One category from [29] that we do not distinguish is the private exchange, since it is essentially a closed extension with a single owner.

## 4.3. EIM conceptual model

An EIM that clearly identi<sup>fi</sup>es a role and chooses processes that are consistent with that role can be expected to enhance business performance. Resource base and evolutionary context, however, are expected to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the EIM's functional scope, as shown in Fig. 1. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, an EIM that is pursuing a speci<sup>fi</sup>c role needs to consider its resource base and evolutionary context in planning its functional scope for enhanced business performance. As such, the two factors are orthogonal to the three categories of processes. Hence, resource base and evolutionary context have to be considered in addition to the relevant business process(es) when planning an EIM.

Business performance is typically de<sup>fi</sup>ned in <sup>fi</sup>nancial terms (i.e., EIM pro<sup>fi</sup>ts, revenues, transaction volume), and/or organizational growth (expansion of the EIM in terms of of<sup>fi</sup>ces, customers, employees, partners, geography, and industry). Yet, these perspectives are not always appropriate. Indeed, an EIM can also be evaluated in terms of value added for the participant <sup>fi</sup>rms that invest in the EIM. This value-add may entail member revenue increases, cost savings, and better decision making. The latter is in line with the coordination science literature (e.g., [34]) in which decision rights and their distribution are linked to business performance.

## 5. Illustration

In this section, the use of the conceptual model is illustrated through a study of several active EIMs. A brief description of the study method is followed by a discussion of key insights.

## 5.1. Method

A qualitative study was conducted based upon a key informant approach [10]. In 2003, multiple key informants in a set of active B2B EIMs were asked to apply the approach presented in this paper to their respective EIMs. Their input was obtained through semi-structured in-depth telephone conferences and face-to-face meetings, based on questionnaires and forms provided in advance. Given the cross-functional nature of the study, knowledgeable informants (having various responsibilities such as Market Manager, Marketing Manager, Director International Operations, Director of Business Development, Network Solutions Director, CIO, Global Account Director, VP Negotiation Solutions, VP of Marketing and Strategy, Head of European Marketing, Managing Director) participated from each EIM. Key informant bias was minimized through the use of multiple, knowledgeable informants for each EIM [3].

Ten EIMs participated in the study. A heterogeneous set was used in order to obtain extensive insights into EIM functional scope [15], and included EIMs of various ages operating in various industries and geographical regions (see Table 2).

Data was collected in three, iterative stages. First, key informants were sent an interview guide by e-mail in 2003, which explained the framework and included questions (in an online activity scope form) about the EIM and its support of the processes identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the approach. About one to two weeks later, participants provided their responses via face-toface or telephone interviews lasting about 90 min each. These responses were augmented in some cases with company documentation and online demonstrations of EIM process support.

Second, the online activity scope forms were sent to the key informants for validation purposes. The key informants were asked to review and possibly augment the entries in the form and clarify any modi<sup>fi</sup>cations, based on which the data in the forms was revised where necessary.

Third, in 2005, the key informants were sent an online activity scope follow-up form by e-mail seeking to assess how the EIM's role and functional scope had evolved. Also, the informants were asked to disclose information on key <sup>fi</sup>nancial, organizational, and participant value-add metrics for both 2002–2003 and 2004–2005. Data was collected through telephone or face-to-face interviews with the key informants, guided by the online activity scope follow-up form. In total, eight EIMs provided follow-up data. Two of the EIMs (Free-Markets and GoodEx) in our 2003 set were acquired by an EIM outside that set, Ariba, for which we included a key informant in our 2005 set. Also, one of the respondents in 2003, E2Open, a technology consulting services provider, declined to participate in the follow-up analysis.

## 5.2. Key insights

One key observation from the qualitative study is that, after reviewing the framework, all of the respondents were able to classify their EIM's role and functional scope without dif<sup>fi</sup>culty, and most felt that the framework not only made sense, but also was a valuable basis for planning their EIM's online business process support. All the respondents perceived the framework to be comprehensive, and no omissions or errors were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed (see Table 2 for an overview of EIM roles; see Appendix A for an overview of EIM functional scope).

As an example, the 2003 data for i-Faber indicates that this EIM positioned itself as an exchange. In its exchange role, i-Faber focused on providing online support for trade processes for 75 buyers and 1200 suppliers in Italy across various industries such as Maintenance Repair and Operations (MRO), oil and gas, petrochemicals, and building and construction. Online catalogs and online supplier and buyer search functionality were offered to support the search process. Supplier and buyer authentication were supported through online ratings, scores and a buyer community rating facility, while product authentication was ensured through attachments with product descriptions. Online support for valuation included online RFx and reverse auction functionality.

By contrast, GNX, an EIM in the retail industry founded by Sears, Carrefour and Oracle in February 2000, positioned itself in 2003 as a full service provider, providing extensive online support in the area of trade processes, decision support, and integration. It supported trade processes such as online buyer and supplier search in its perishables exchange, supplier and product authentication through online registration mechanisms, and online dynamic pricing through reverse auctions, online RFPs, and a perishables exchange. In the area of decision support, it offered con<sup>fi</sup>guration through online RFPs, and supported online collaboration geared towards supply chain management, vendor performance management, and product development. Furthermore, GNX assisted retailers in gathering company-level information by providing auction history online and allowing them to keep track of what they are buying across divisions. Finally, GNX supported data and application integration between retailers and suppliers through data sharing and synchronization of key performance indicators, inventory level data, and sales data, for Vendor Managed Inventory (VMI) and Collaborative Planning, Forecasting, and Replenishment (CPFR) purposes.

A second observation from the qualitative study is that the prioritization of process development in many of the EIMs that we studied is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by their resource base and evolutionary context, consistent with our model (see Fig. 1). For example, InterSources, a Western European EIM operating across multiple industries, has leveraged its technology resources such as an allocation optimization tool for buyers and a scenario tool to support trade and decision support processes. It has also leveraged a partnership with Open Ratings (www.openratings.com) for enhanced business intelligence support. These have helped InterSources evolve its role as a value-added exchange. At the same time, while it considers itself a pure-play EIM, InterSources has been increasingly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by a few large participant <sup>fi</sup>rms to develop integration services for their trading activities, thereby moving it towards an online extension context and full service provider role. In this case, both resource base and evolutionary context have been signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the EIM's functional scope, albeit in different ways. The impact of evolutionary context was also observed in the case of i-Faber. This pure-play exchange initially focused on online trade process support for any customer. However, it has started speci<sup>fi</sup>c support in categories outside the trade process category due to pressure from a few large customers.

A third observation from the qualitative study is that those EIMs that reconciled resources and evolutionary context with role and functional scope performed consistently well over the two-year study. To pursue our InterSources example, its major resource enhancements in the area of decision support, as well as its main focus on online trade and decision process support as a pure-play EIM went hand in hand with signi<sup>fi</sup>cant growth in volumes from €50 million to over €2 billion and from 2 to 15 participants, while it sustained operating pro<sup>fi</sup>tability since Q4 2003. Likewise, i-Faber, reinforced its position as a pureplay exchange in Italy, and Eastern Europe, through the adding of online scouting which was quickly adopted by all participants. i-Faber is pro<sup>fi</sup>table since the second semester of 2004 with €1.1 billion in transaction volume for the year. Also, the number of buyers and suppliers shot up from, respectively, 75 and 1200, to 200 and 5400.

Another case in point is TradCom, a closed extension integrated exchange in the MRO market in Belgium, the Netherlands, and Luxembourg, which has reinforced its position through signi<sup>fi</sup>cant enhancements in logistics and integration. It has stayed away from decision support, and has been very successful, focusing on the suppliers in its closed extension network. Yearly transaction volume has increased from €2 million to €8 million with 2100 customers, and suppliers have grown from 5 to 18 <sup>fi</sup>rms. In addition, suppliers enjoyed revenue increases as customers of other participant, complementary, suppliers entered the network.

An example of an EIM that shifted its role and functional scope is GNX, which viewed itself as a full service provider in 2003. By 2005 GNX had reinforced its trade process and decision support capabilities, both internally and through a merger with World Wide Retail Exchange (WWRE). In the area of trade processes and more speci<sup>fi</sup>cally authentication, for instance, GNX started to provide information on ISO compliance, compliance with labor laws and social laws in various countries, together with multi-language support, and aggregation of authentication data and processes. In decision support, and speci<sup>fi</sup>cally in business intelligence, GNX started to combine time-series based forecasting and coordination of retailer and supplier forecasts (GNX's strength) with itembased business intelligence (product features, packaging, etc.) from WWRE's data pooling. Thus, it was moving towards a value-added exchange model. It became pro<sup>fi</sup>table for the <sup>fi</sup>rst time in 2004 and increased its transaction volume between 2003 and 2005 from \$5 billion per year to \$1 billion a month. This shift to a value-added exchange was also consistent with the <sup>fi</sup>rm's key performance indicators such as transaction volume, number of participants and process bene<sup>fi</sup>ts for customers through VMI and CPFR programs. In effect, GNX realized that it operated in two contexts — for its founding participants/owners, it was effectively a closed extension, but for grocery suppliers in its reverse auction or perishables exchange, it operated as an open extension. Recognizing this enabled it to offer different levels of support to the two constituencies, which was more cost effective than trying to be “all things to all people”.

A contrasting example is ChemConnect, a global full service provider EIM established in 1995 in the chemical feedstocks, chemicals and plastics industry. This pure-play intermediary was spreading itself too thin, with uneven focus across categories. Trade process support in the form of a commodity exchange took up the lion share of the <sup>fi</sup>rm's resources and time. As a result, consortium members were down from 10 in 2003, to 3 in 2005, employees had reduced from 75 to 34, the number of customers had remained the same, the number of auction events had dropped from 450 to 270, and document transfer volume had shrunk (from 40,000/Quarter to 25,000/Quarter). This EIM could probably have done better by focusing on its trade processes as an exchange or a value-added exchange.

Another contrasting example is RubberNetwork, an EIM which is enhancing its decision support because of owner demands in view of its “community bene<sup>fi</sup>ts” charter. Yet, its owners are mutual competitors so information sharing has to be limited to non-strategic items. In effect it is functioning as a closed extension full service provider rather than as the open extension integrated exchange it set out to be. The number of customers has stayed at nine, and the number of employees is down from 60 to 54. Its most signi<sup>fi</sup>cant performance bene<sup>fi</sup>t has been in transaction volume and cost savings in purchasing as a value-add for members. It is questionable if this EIM would survive if it would be operating in a pure-play setting.

The insight gained from these observations is that EIM management can bene<sup>fi</sup>t from considering their resource base and evolutionary context in the process of planning the EIM's functional scope and the prioritization of its business processes.

## 6. Conclusion

This paper contributes to the literature on Internet-based electronic intermediaries by presenting an approach for scholars and practitioners to understand and plan the functional scope and role of EIMs. Drawing on conceptual literature on business process engineering, EIMs, and e-business architectures, a comprehensive typology of business processes for EIMs is derived, consisting of trade, decision support, and integration processes. Furthermore, the approach provides guidance in the choice of appropriate business process support, based upon two factors — <sup>fi</sup>rst the resource base of the EIM, and second, its evolutionary context. Finally, the approach is demonstrated with respect to a set of active EIMs.

A comprehensive typology of business processes offers a number of interesting directions for further research on EIMs. The framework can be used as a resource for outcome-based

empirical studies in academic research, as such data becomes available. Another direction involves an extension of the investigation of the drivers for supporting speci<sup>fi</sup>c processes and the level of support for its associated sub-processes. Given the variety of different roles that can be assumed, a relevant question is what drives the choice among these roles. The choice of role could be driven by a number of factors beyond resource base and evolutionary context, such as the <sup>fi</sup>rm's lifecycle stage (e.g., early stage start-up seeking market penetration), the number and involvement of other entities (e.g., competitors in a consortium-based setting), the stage of IT integration in the industry (-ies) in which the <sup>fi</sup>rm operates, the structure of the industry and the information intensity of the affected business processes. These types of factors have been considered in the literature on designing other types of IT-intensive initiatives, and would be a useful extension of the current work, adding to EIM design and management methodology.

Finally, it should be noted that our study does not include EIMs established by non-pro<sup>fi</sup>t standard setting consortia or government agencies. The factors relevant for such organizations when planning their functional scope is an interesting area for future research.
