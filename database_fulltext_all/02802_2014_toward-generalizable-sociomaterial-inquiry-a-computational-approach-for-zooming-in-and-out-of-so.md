# TOWARD GENERALIZABLE SOCIOMATERIAL INQUIRY: A COMPUTATIONAL APPROACH FOR ZOOMING IN AND OUT OF SOCIOMATERIAL ROUTINES<sup>1</sup>

James Gaskin Brigham Young University, Provo, UT 84602 U.S.A. {james.gaskin@byu.edu}

Nicholas Berente University of Georgia, Athens, GA 30602 U.S.A. {berente@uga.edu}

Kalle Lyytinen Case Western Reserve University, Cleveland, OH 44106 U.S.A. {kalle@case.edu}

Youngjin Yoo Temple University, Philadelphia, PA 19122 U.S.A. {youngjin.yoo@temple.edu}

In this paper, a computational, mixed methods approach that combines qualitative analysis with a novel approach to sequence analysis for studying the entanglement of human activities and digital capabilities in organizational routines is described. The approach is scalable across multiple contexts and complements the dominant idiographic modes of sociomaterial inquiry. The approach is rooted in the epistemology of a “rational reconstruction” consistent with the interpretive stance underlying the sociomaterial position. It arms researchers with the means to seek and uncover regularities in the ways human activities and digital capabilities become entangled across contexts by enabling the identification and articulation of generalizable patterns of sociomaterial activity. The computational approach is founded on sequence-analytic techniques that originated from the field of computational biology (genetics), but are now gaining popularity in the study of temporally ordered social phenomena such as organizational routines. These techniques are extended by drawing upon theoretical insights gained within sociomaterial scholarship on how the digital and the social become entangled. By detecting the variation in activities, actors, artifacts, and affordances that comprise what we denote a sociomaterial routine, the approach directly attends to ways in which human actors and the material features of technology become entangled in patterns of practice. Beyond motivating and describing the approach, the different insights that researchers can generate through its application in the study of the digitalization of organizational routines are illustrated. We conclude by suggesting several lines of inquiry that can enrich sociomaterial research.

Keywords: Sociomaterial, sociotechnical, rational reconstruction, computational social science, methodology, sequence analysis, routines, mixed methods, organizational routines, generative grammar, lexicon, lexical notation

## Introduction

In recent years, the notion of sociomateriality (Leonardi 2011; Leonardi and Barley 2008; Orlikowski and Scott 2008) has emerged as a powerful and prominent ontological lens to explore the mutually constitutive and emergent relationship between humans and nonhuman technologies. Drawing on earlier research on sociotechnical webs (Kling and Scacchi 1982), ensembles (Bijker 1995), actor networks, assemblages, and associations (Latour 2005), or sociomaterial entanglements (Pickering 1995), the sociomaterial turn draws attention to the way that digital technologies are intrinsically embedded into the fabric of local practices and conditions. Neither the material features within technology use nor their impacts are predetermined. Instead, they endogenously emerge through a performative and emergent process “as the functionalities of technologies are negotiated, shaped, bypassed or undermined in situ” (Kallinikos 2011, p. 12).

The dominant approach to the study of sociomateriality emphasizes idiosyncratic appropriations and the constant “remaking” of digital technologies within local contexts (Kallinikos 2011). Such studies have offered insights into how local practices and digital technologies become inseparable and mutually reshaping, especially given the malleable and symbolic nature of digital systems (Orlikowski 2000). Technologies and local practices, indeed, intertwine in unpredictable, emergent ways (Robey et al. 2003). The corpus of sociomateriality research to date has solidly demonstrated the importance of material artifacts in practice and describes how digital technologies are idiosyncratically appropriated across contexts (Leonardi 2011; Pentland and Feldman 2008).

The emphasis of inquiring only within idiosyncratic local negotiations, however, may overlook what might be common patterns of activity and sociomaterial associations across multiple contexts (Scott 1990; Weick and Browning 1986). Local practices, no matter how unique and creative, draw upon and enact inherited, structural properties of habitualized patterns of action (Bourdieu 1977; Giddens 1984). Technologies-in-practice are fundamentally an outcome of continuing sedimentation of disparate and parallel technology developments that continually clash with institutionalized rules and resources (Arthur 2009; Boland et al. 2007). As such, patterns of action—such as paying a bill, hiring an employee, or designing a car—involve combinations of human activities and orchestrations of diverse, but relatively stable, technological resources. Although these patterns of action will serve different purposes and are carried out over multiple time periods and contexts, they still share common mutually constitutive social and technological elements, forming regularities of their interactions across contexts and over time. Therefore, several scholars have indicated that the next frontier of sociomaterial scholarship is about unpacking latent regularities in observed patterns of association between the social and the digital elements across contexts (Latour 2010; Leonardi and Barley 2008; Pollock and Williams 2008; Sandberg and Tsoukas 2009). These scholars do not call for generalizability in the statistical sense (see Lee and Baskerville 2003), but rather seek an understanding of general patterns and consistencies of sociomaterial phenomena across contexts.<sup>2</sup>

If such generalized inquiry into sociomaterial patterns of association were to be attempted in organizational contexts, one avenue would involve studying organizational routines and their technological and social elements. We define an organizational routine as “a repetitive, recognizable pattern of interdependent actions, involving multiple actors” (Feldman and Pentland 2003, p. 96). Organizational routines are tangible empirical phenomena (Feldman and Pentland 2003) that can be viewed as sociomaterial assemblages (Pentland et al. 2011). The study of organizational routines demands intensive field work that involves collecting swaths of narrative data concerning how actors interacted with diverse artifacts, for which purposes, and under which conditions (Pentland et al. 2011). In order to pursue a generalizable sociomaterial inquiry using such a body of data, we need to go beyond the local narrative and use methods to identify commonalities and variations across contexts. These methods would need to capture general patterns in data representing mutually constitutive entanglements of their social and digital elements in the routines. In short, this would involve a variant of a mixed method approach (Creswell and Clark 2010; Mingers 2001; Tashakkori and Teddlie 2010) where rich data about routines is transformed into formats that support scalable and rigorous analysis across contexts, thus enabling data and theory triangulation, augmentation, and expansion across research contexts (Venkatesh et al. 2013).

Such an approach calls for an articulation of an analytic language, or lexicon, that is consistent with the sociomaterial ontology, but enables rich idiographic sociomaterial phenomena to be “rationally reconstructed” (Habermas 1979) according to a set of analytic categories. In this way, rich, detailed qualitative data can be readily used to inform the constant comparison across contexts, leading to theoretical generalizations (Ragin 1987). Within Habermas’s rational reconstruction, a lexicon is conceived as a mid-level language that pushes a scholarly community to interact, compare, and create generalizable claims. Such a lexicon can be thought of as a “generative grammar” (Chomsky 1966; Pentland and Reuter 1994) that enables extensive analysis and inferences to identify patterns in rich, localized sociomaterial data. A welldeveloped lexicon helps researchers navigate multiple levels of analysis across contexts and over time. It enables the “zooming in and out” from local practices within particular contexts to identify broad patterns across contexts (Latour 2010; Pollock and Williams 2008; Sandberg and Tsoukas 2009).

In this essay, we propose a lexicon for sociomaterial analysis of routines. In particular, we detail an approach where, once sociomaterial routine data are encoded (reconstructed) using the lexicon, they can be analyzed using sequence-analytic techniques originally developed in genetics and evolutionary biology to detect commonalities and variations (Yoo 2012). Recently, these techniques have also gained currency in the social sciences (e.g., Abbott 1995; Feldman and Pentland 2003; Pentland et al. 2011; Shoval and Isaacson 2007; Wilson 2001). Our approach goes beyond previous usage of the sequence-analytical techniques in social inquiry, however, by attending specifically to sociomaterial elements. Armed with such an approach we can detect common patterns and differences in sociomaterial routine variations across contexts (organizations, locales) (Gaskin et al. 2011b) and their change over time (evolution) (Gaskin et al. 2011a; Lyytinen and Newman 2008; Yoo 2012).

The main contributions of this paper are epistemological and methodological. The proposed approach is rooted in wellestablished theoretical positions that characterize patterns of human action as organizational routines (Abbott 1995; Nelson and Winter 1982; Pentland and Reuter 1994), while ontologically it is consistent with the sociomaterial position about routines (Pentland and Feldman 2008; Pentland et al. 2011). By drawing on event sequencing methods, we outline an innovative computational mixed methods approach to generalizable inquiry into sociomaterial routines together with the rationale and the logic underlying the approach, its theoretical foundations, and methodological implications. By doing so, we lay a foundation for empirical application and continued extension of generalized sociomaterial inquiry.

The organization of this paper is as follows. First we attend to research in sociomaterial practice, and then introduce our analytic lexicon to describe sociomaterial routines. We then describe the steps of a reconstructive, mixed methods (qualitative and computational) approach for analyzing sociomaterial routines, and discuss validity and reliability threats associated with the approach. We conclude by discussing the potential benefits and pitfalls of the approach, with a brief reflection on how to advance generalizable sociomaterial inquiry in the future.

## Sociomaterial Routines

Any careful reading of the past half-century of organizational theory suggests that dynamic and reciprocal assemblages of technologies<sup>3</sup> and systems constitute organizational practice. This perspective dates back originally to early Marx but was made popular by the Tavistock studies after the second world war. Trist (1981) argued that organizations are comprised of nested sociotechnical systems consisting of human and technological elements “intertwined in a complex web of mutual causality” (p. 13). Instead of treating technology as an exogenous force, the sociotechnical tradition emphasizes the dynamic and mutual interplay between people and technologies that jointly, and through recursive interactions, generates organizational outcomes (Bijker 1995; Latour 1987; Lyytinen and Newman 2008; Orlikowski 2000; Williams and Edge 1996). Building on this intellectual tradition, the recent sociomaterial turn has explicitly highlighted that the material and the social are inseparable as antecedents or outcomes of organizational action (Orlikowski 2007; Pickering 1995).<sup>4</sup> As a result, boundaries between humans and nonhumans blur; both act on each other and constitute each other in what Pickering (1995) referred to as the “mangle” of practice. Humans and nonhumans are ontologically inseparable in practice and emerge as relational categories that underlie any technology-in-practice (Orlikowski 2010). Consequently, while it is acceptable to analytically distinguish between the two, the sociomaterial position cautions us that neither of the two should be considered solely exogenous—that is, as the prime mover.

The current dominant empirical inquiry to sociomateriality involves mostly ethnography-based interpretive case studies. While such approaches have provided useful and rich insights in understanding the constitution of idiosyncratic sociomaterial practices across contexts (e.g., Leonardi and Barley 2010; Orlikowski 2007; Wagner et al. 2010), they do not lend themselves to the exploration of regularities into the shape of sociomaterial assemblages’ changes over time and across contexts. Furthermore, the current approaches are largely ahistorical (Kallinikos 2011), failing to detect long-term evolutions in sociomaterial practices often caused by seemingly innocent changes, such as employing a new digital tool in a small portion of the practice (Arthur 2009; Boland et al. 2007; Tyre and Orlikowski 1994). Yet, changes in sociomaterial practice do typically occur across longer periods of time.

In this essay, we suggest that sociomaterial practices can be analyzed as enacted rule-based routines or patterns as a way of complementing the current idiographic methods of sociomaterial inquiry. Routines represent regularities in patterns of organizational practice and can be viewed as sequences of activities (Pentland 2003; Salvato and Rerup 2011); they evolve through variation, selection, and stabilization and therefore involve a temporal dimension (Nelson and Winter 1982). An enacted routine thus forms a basic unit of analysis for the study of variation and patterns associated with sociomaterial practices (Nelson 2009). Within routines, multiple actors carry out activities where they appropriate multiple tools (digital and physical) to carry out affordances through which they produce some outputs (Becker and Lazaric 2009). For example, a routine for designing the chassis of a car includes many interrelated and iterative activities performed by multiple actors working together using a variety of tools. Although actors may be following a well-entrenched institutionalized script for these design activities, the enactment of the scripts can vary from context to context. Within a given organization, each time a chassis is designed there are similarities in the patterns of material practice (repetition sequences of activities and tool) and also variations. Similarly, the chassis design routine across organizations may have similarities and variations (in both activities and tools). The sociomaterial turn in organizational scholarship has highlighted the way material elements of practice such as digital technologies are entangled within the routine and its variation.

Thus, drawing on the sociomaterial view, we define a sociomaterial routine as a repetitive, recognizable pattern of enacted and interrelated activities, comprised of social and material elements in the pursuit of a specific organizational output. In this way, the study of sociomaterial practice— viewed as enacted routines—is particularly well-suited to the identification of regularities across sociomaterial practices in ways that account for multiple levels of analysis; variations in human actors; technologies; complex, historically rooted sequential practices; and their evolutionary change.

## A Generalizable Inquiry into Sociomaterial Routines

The aim of our generalizable sociomaterial approach is to enable the formulation and validation of generalizable theories associated with sociomaterial practices by following the composition and change of sociomaterial routines over time, across contexts, or across different types of routines. Below, we describe an approach to do this by first establishing the epistemological basis (rational reconstruction) and then the process of inquiry (data sampling, encoding, and analysis) that informs our approach.

## The Generative Structure of Sociomaterial Routines

A generative structure acts like grammar, providing information about components and their potential permissible arrangements. Table 1 shows our proposed generative structure of a sociomaterial routine and its underlying categories. By adopting a generative view, we conceptualize routines as hierarchically and recursively composed sets of interdependent activities (Nelson and Winter 1982). Moreover, activities within routines vary in their internal structures. While earlier studies on routines have primarily treated activities as an atomic, single-dimensional construct (see Abbott 1995; Feldman and Pentland 2003; Pentland et al. 2009), we develop a molar activity construct that has a multidimensional internal structure and can attend to the social and material elements of the routine. The structure consists of a stable set of sociomaterial elements and a generative set of their possible combinations. In other words, instead of merely capturing the type of an activity, and the sequence of activities in a routine, we capture information about the key dimensions of sociomaterial entanglement that constitute each activity: its actors, location, tools, and artifacts. This enables us to unearth the dynamic and evolving sociomaterial content of activities, while recognizing that the same sort of activity can be carried out by different actors, or at different locations, or using different tools, or even involving different affordances.

This model provides a simple way to represent the complex and nested nature of sociomaterial routines. According to the model, a sociomaterial routine is an ordered set of activities, which can be represented as a hierarchically organized ordered set of generative elements. Furthermore, a routine with multiple subroutines can be represented using a superset of several sets, each representing a sociomaterial routine. This nested and recursive structure of sociomaterial routines, consisting of activities with their “genetic” generative elements, enables us to study the varieties and regularities in sociomaterial routines.

Our model allows us to capture not only how a sociomaterial routine is constituted but also how it varies across contexts and evolves over time. The same types of activities within the same sociomaterial routine can involve different configurations of underlying generative elements. In addition, the internal structures of activities will evolve over time due to the introduction of new affordances permitted by new digital tools, new physical environments, or acquisition of new skills and learning by the actors. Overall, we can observe a wide variety of diverse material and social elements entering into a range of relationships for the same activity type at different times, generating variation in sociomaterial compositions that make up routines. Ostensibly, sociotechnical entanglements within any sociomaterial routine will vary widely, particularly over time, and depending on whether, and to what extent, human agency or material agency initiate change in the composition of the routine (Leonardi 2011).

Observed, interrelated, and repeatedly enacted sociomaterial routines constrain and enable social and material elements that comprise subsequent activities. Existing sociomaterial routines constrain the range of material and social elements that can be present at any time and can be orchestrated during the enactment of a routine. Therefore, a set of interrelated and repeatedly enacted sociomaterial routines provides for latent elements that underlie activities, which generally are described as rules, schemata, and resources (Giddens 1984). These provide the law-like regularity, stability, and continuity as observed when actors execute activities. At the same time, due to processes of structuration, enacted routines form the source for variation reflected in slower but constant change in the latent elements and their composition (Cyert and March 1963; Feldman and Pentland 2003; Nelson and Winter 1982; Pentland and Reuter 1994).

Our inquiry into the structuring of sociomaterial routines draws upon epistemological position that Habermas (1979) described as rational reconstruction. By adopting this position, we aim both to accommodate the demand for interpretation of local sociomaterial practice, while at the same time seeking to identify general regularities across practices. Habermas proposed the method of rational reconstruction for social sciences as an alternative to both the objectivist paradigm of scientific method and the subjectivist paradigm associated with the hermeneutic method (Pedersen 2008). The former emphasizes theoretical generality at the expense of understanding meanings in context, whereas the latter does the reverse. The epistemological position of rational reconstruction involves addressing the pre-theoretic elements of practice in order to generate a deep structure associated with that context, which enables local expressions to emerge. This deeper structure, in turn, can be made readily generalizable across other contexts. For example, while addressing the ways individuals communicate, a rational reconstruction of these communication patterns can involve applying a taxonomy of grammatical elements and comparing these grammatical elements across contexts (Pedersen 2008). Logic and Chomsky’s (1955, 1966) theory of generative grammar are some original examples of reconstructive frameworks.<sup>5</sup>

Habermas (1983) proposed rational reconstruction generally as an approach that allows communities of social scientists to make sense of the intersubjective understanding of people who share a lifeworld.<sup>6</sup> A lifeworld is a phenomenological notion indicating the domain of shared experience for a collection of local actors. In order to form some level of intersubjective interpretations of experience in a lifeworld, actors need some accepted and agreed-upon, rationally ordered scaffolding to capture local knowledge. Rational reconstruction is a method that seeks to understand a particular lifeworld by structuring an appropriate lexicon for analyzing practices within that lifeworld and enabling its scaffolding. This lexicon acts as the scaffolding structure for social scientists concerned with a particular domain, thus allowing them to form an intersubjective understanding of (and thus potentially assess, compare, critique, etc.) the weakly causal, determining relations associated with that lifeworld (essentially allowing social scientists to identify regularities). Thus, rational reconstruction is a process by which scholars make relevant structures of a lifeworld visible (objectified), which then enables them to rationally deal with it (Habermas 1983).

Rational reconstruction in our context refers to a process whereby investigators seek to reconstruct the informant’s skillfully mastered knowledge of rules that underlie sociomaterial practices in order to understand the uses, purposes, contexts, and regularities associated with digital technologies embedded in that practice. The investigator, together with the informant, attempts to reconstruct the know-how—the ability of a community of actors to act skillfully—in an explicit way (i.e., Ryle’s 1949 know-that; see also Pedersen 2008). Rational reconstruction always takes place with respect to a cultural system (Habermas 1983), which provides a theoretical framework that guides the interpretation of local acts in terms of a standard theoretical lexicon. A lexical framework is intended to accommodate “the totality of fundamental concepts and ways of understanding that articulates a preunderstanding of everything the members of the linguistic community may encounter in the world” (Habermas 2003, p. 53). This framework allows the inquirer to observe regularities in local sociomaterial practices across a variety of situations by creating a “sameness of reference” (Habermas 2003, p. 34) across contexts.

The challenge we face when examining varieties in observed sociomaterial routines involves discovering and developing the basic lexical framework that researchers can use to identify the elements that make up the compositions of social and technological elements observed in sociomaterial routines. For theoretical generalizability, the lexicon must be founded on theory-driven taxonomic principles (de Jong and Marsili 2006). Such taxonomies have been widely applied in many fields of inquiry, from botany to studies of organizational behaviors. In our case, such an empirically grounded and valid lexical framework offers a systematic foundation to organize, understand, and explain diversity in observed routine enactments, and allows for building generalizable theories. It is important to carefully attend to the selection and validation of the theory base of the lexicon as a foundation for developing generalizable models of sociomaterial routines. By its very nature, any such lexicon allows us to make theoretical statements about sociomaterial practices and reveals postulated relationships within that domain (Bacharach 1989; Doty and Glick 1994).

We next formulate a lexical framework of the elements that make up a sociomaterial routine. The lexicon is founded on a systematic review of the literature on routines and different types of organizational activities and our field work on design routines (Gaskin et al. 2010a; Gaskin et al. 2011a; Gaskin et al. 2010b; Gaskin et al. 2011b) and consists of seven elements: (1) the type of activity that is being carried out; (2) the configuration of actors; (3) the activity location; (4) the tool modality; (5) the tool affordance being enacted within the activity (constitutive sociomaterial relationship); (6) the artifact type used and/or produced by the activity, and (7) the dataflow being used or produced during the activity (constitutive relationship).<sup>7</sup> The basic elements of activity and actors are rooted in standard sequence-analytic techniques. Beyond this, we have added elements that are informed by both the sociomaterial position broadly, and by studies that have heeded attention to the nature of digital artifacts. Specifically, in line with the sociomaterial position, we explicitly attend to the nature of artifacts. In doing so, although they are mutually constitutive, one can analytically distinguish directionality (imbrications) of either human or material agency (Leonardi 2010). To unpack this mutual constitution, we also model the aspect of the artifact type, or tool, in practice— what we refer to as the tool modality—and emphasize the specific nature of appropriation of the artifact in that practice, which we capture in the term tool affordance. Finally, to address information-centric aspects of the use of digital artifacts, we also address the directionality of the dataflow and the virtual nature of organizing or its geographical distribution, or activity location.<sup>8</sup> Table 2 provides the definition for each of these elements (more fully detailed in Appendix A) and enlists the set of possible values that we have chosen to represent variations in each element.

To represent any activity as an element of a sociomaterial routine we adopt a shorthand representation of a concatenated string of values drawn from this lexicon for each activity. Each element in this seven-tuple contains either a reference to a social or a material element or their combinations. Overall, by using the lexicon, we “reduce the complexity of empirical phenomena to few and easy to remember categories” (de Jong and Marsili 2006, p. 214). We emphasize, however, that the suggested lexicon is not a substitute for idiographic local accounts of sociomaterial practice that provide the foundation for the derivation of the seven tuple. In contrast, it complements such inquiry by abstracting common elements—similar to a grounded theory process. The encoding of the structure of sociomaterial routines based on the lexicon, in fact, relies inevitably on local, interpretive accounts of activity. The use of the lexicon offers us a way to enter into a double hermeneutic by reading and analyzing the first level actor accounts (Giddens 1976). By moving to the second level theory-laden accounts, which can be shared with the study participants, we can then seek to generalize patterns of activities in valid ways. To summarize, this lexicon classifies and labels elements of sociomaterial routines and expresses their constitutive relationships by using its seven elements to characterize sociomaterial activity. This representation can then be used to synthesize and aggregate variations within those elements across multiple levels of analysis by using computational methods, which will be reviewed next.

## Sampling, Collecting, and Encoding Sociomaterial Routine Data

An approach rooted in the use of the lexicon and focused on generalizable results is organized into three steps. The first step involves the determination of the sample and conducting in-depth field studies to collect data. We emphasize that the data collection effort needs to be systematic, both vertically and horizontally. Vertically, one needs to collect information on all elements of activities and routines (along with their nested structures, if needed). Horizontally, one needs to cover the complete sequence of activities for enacted routines of interest, bounding the routine in a way that is sensible to the participating actors (Silverman 2009). Furthermore, the investigators must collect situational information about the organization to gain a rich, contextualized understanding of the strategic, institutional, technological, and environmental context that shapes the enacting of routines.

Second, once the researchers have determined the sampling strategy (along with the key informants), the data collection can involve combinations of qualitative data collection such as interviews and observations with the compilation of existing electronic trace data, archival data, or data logging. In-depth qualitative data collection in the sociomaterial position—combined with a focus on capturing sequences of activities in a systematic manner—is an appropriate strategy for characterizing sociomaterial routines in rich detail and in a manner that promotes generalizable inquiry. However, given the explosion of trace data (i.e., big data; see Davenport et al. 2012), opportunities will undoubtedly present themselves to complement these analyses. Either way, extensive data collection strategies are necessary to ensure a rich account of how the enactment of the routine takes place and the precise composition of sociotechnical elements that comprise sociomaterial routines. Consistent with the epistemology of rational reconstruction, researchers should involve their informants to the degree possible in reconstructing their lived routines. The data corpus is sufficiently complete when (1) researchers collect data on each activity in a sociomaterial routine with a beginning and end sensible to informants; (2) all sociomaterial elements of the taxonomy can be coded for each activity; and (3) the researchers have an understanding into the institutional, cultural, and organizational context of the routine. When these points have been met, a level of saturation point will have been reached in the data collection (Yin 2011), and the investigator is ready to encode and analyze the dataset for sociomaterial compositions and their variation.<sup>9</sup>

The third step is to encode the rich field data into the generic lexicon of sociomaterial elements. Consistent with this lexicon, researchers capture variations in activities and routines by changing a specific value for an element. This allows them to trace how the sociomaterial composition changes (in terms of variation in the configuration of the seven sociomaterial elements) as the enacted routine unfolds. By capturing such detailed information for each of the seven elements, the encoding provides a systematic, yet nuanced, representation of sociomaterial variation of activities. Researchers can use this encoding simultaneously across multiple sites to build a data corpus with a consistent set of analyzable elements.

To record, encode, and validate routines in the field, a graphical notation can be useful to illustrate the structure and composition of studied sociomaterial routines. Visual representations of complex routines are easier to understand and validate for both investigators and their informants. Furthermore, the visual support environment can be customized to expand the proposed lexicon or to adapt generic symbols to a local context. In order to build such a visual support environment, we developed a formal meta-model—a rule-based grammar—that specifies all plausible variations of element values for each activity, and their relationships. Such a metamodel specifies, on a high level, how to encode all allowable variations of sociomaterial activities. Using the meta-model, we can graphically present and enforce the order, the completeness, and a set of valid relationships among lexical elements that represent an enacted routine (Jarke et al. 2009). As in most exploratory, data-driven research approaches, the investigators cannot anticipate all salient theoretical constructs a priori. Therefore, the formal meta-model also permits customization of the lexicon and its representation, should such needs arise.<sup>10</sup> Due to its formal basis, the developed meta-model can also be rendered into a computerreadable form so that its use can be enforced by the computer and its support automated.

We chose MetaEdit+ (Jarke et al. 2009),<sup>11</sup> a graphical meta-CASE tool to represent the meta-model of the lexicon as a set of consistent rules and model relationships in a GOPRRbased<sup>12</sup> conceptual model (Tolvanen and Rossi 2003). MetaEdit+ affords theoretical openness with regard to inquiry categories and analysis methods, since it can generate multiple samples of data from the encoded routines using its query language. This allows us to flexibly look for different alternative sociomaterial regularities present in the data corpus. MetaEdit+ also allows us to run large queries to scrape its backend database in order to generate pertinent information (such as the seven elements previously listed) necessary for (re)constructing all lexical sequences of activities in string format. These analyses cannot be accommodated by human effort as the number of data points in our largest analyses have exceeded 3,000 activities.

The snippet of a graphical model of a sociomaterial routine represented in MetaEdit+ shown in Figure 1 is an example of a model of a routine that represents the very early activities of a building design and construction project at a large American construction company. In addition to the seven elements listed previously in Table 2, the model also captures information about the sequence in which activities were enacted, thus enabling longitudinal analyses of change over time—that is, evolution.

## Analyzing Lexical Sequence Data

The final stage in our approach is to analyze the lexical sequences generated from MetaEdit+ using sequence analysis techniques and other computational methods. The approach onward is insensitive to the way that the encoded lexical representation of the routine was developed. If it is encoded by other means than using MetaEdit+, it is immaterial with regard to the output. Table 3 summarizes the key steps, output, tools, and ways of using the outputs during the sequence analysis. For readers who want to know the details of the approach, we provide in Appendix B a step-by-step illustration of the analysis procedure using real data. We also offer interpretations of some empirical findings generated by this approach and related practical implications. Overall, the goal of this sequence analysis is to discover and analyze patterns in sequentially ordered lexical data by utilizing a computational method that compares these lexical patterns as sequences of activities or elements based on comparative metrics.

Most available sequence-analytic methods rely on an approach that evaluates multiple alignments between lexical strings through optimal matching (MacIndoe and Abbott 2004). We have adopted this technique as it offers an established and validated computational method to analyze large sequences of lexical representations (Wilson 2006). Optimal matching alignment compares the complete set of pairwise permutations of a set of lexical sequences. The technique takes the entire lexical sequence of activities into account for multiple comparisons in order to produce an identity matrix (the inverse of a distance matrix), which can then be used to produce clusters and measure the degree of variation exhibited within and among groups of lexical sequences. From these clusters, one can determine if reliable patterns exist among lexical sequences of activities (Salvato and Rerup 2011). The computational technique used offers several benefits over traditional clustering techniques. One benefit is that sequencing techniques permit one to take categorical (nonnumeric) data and find patterns in them, if the “cost” of aligning elements can be reasonably assumed or specified.<sup>13</sup> The approach also produces alignment scores that show the degree of similarity (or change) between sequences (i.e., between enacted sociomaterial routines in our case). For a more detailed account of optimal matching in social sciences, refer to MacIndoe and Abbott (2004).

To date, however, sequence-analytic techniques applied in the social domain have characterized each social event using a stable single value (see Abbott 1995; Pentland et al. 2009). In a sense, these representations have focused on single-level social events and the lexicon describes the range of social or spatial activities and their strict temporal or spatial order. Therefore, to characterize aspects of sociomaterial variation, we had to expand the encoding method to account for process data involving internal structures of activities and then revise related sequence-analytic techniques to analyze and detect sociomaterial variation within these activities nested across multiple levels. Thus, our approach offers the benefits of computational scalability demonstrated in sequence analysis studies (such as Pentland et al. 2011), combined with much of the contextual and idiosyncratic richness of traditional sociomaterial studies (Barley et al. 2011; Leonardi and Bailey 2008; Wagner et al. 2010). However, at the same time, the approach encourages investigators to be process-centric, analyzing entire routines, from start to finish (in contrast to more segmented approaches such as Barley et al. 2011; Leonardi and Bailey 2008). Further, it enforces the systematic treatment of all routine elements and activities within a given routine (in contrast to studies such as Wagner et al. 2010).

Finally, we can analyze routines at the element level in terms of their sequential order. To this end, we can generate firstorder Markov chain models, or transition matrices that estimate the likelihood of a particular element value (e.g., activity type) given the current element value (Pentland et al. 2010). For example, one can predict the likelihood of a collocated activity following a remote activity, or the likelihood of a validate activity following a generate activity. Using these types of transition matrices, one can also statistically compare sequences of sociomaterial activity to determine if their sequential order is roughly equivalent or statistically dissimilar. ## Validity and Reliability

Since we leverage rational reconstruction as our epistemological position (Habermas 1979), evaluations of truth-claims by necessity cannot appeal directly to objective reality because this reality does not make itself directly accessible. Instead, our understanding of reality is mediated by the cultural systems of meaning and values. Thus, any validity claims, and critiques of those claims, must be made in relation to—and with the appropriation of—the relevant lexical framework. Habermas (1979) identifies five modes by which discourse associated with truth-claims can occur (see also Lyytinen and Hirschheim 1988):

• Theoretical—based on evidence and logic

• Practical—based on social norms, appropriateness, and acceptability,

Aesthetic—based on good taste and standards of value

• Therapeutic—based on sincerity or honesty

• Explicative—based on clarity and intelligibility

Any claims made in an analysis should be built in anticipation of a critique from one of these modes of discourse. Of course, research in the social sciences is primarily concerned with theoretical discourse, and as such, like in any other research methodology that strives toward generalizability, demonstrating validity and reliability of the lexical coding and sequence analysis forms a key issue in justifying the methodology. While using the methodology, the investigator can employ several measures to ensure both reliability and validity during the data collection and analysis process. Table 4 outlines possible threats to reliability and validity for each step, and mitigation strategies for each threat. The steps included in this table relate to the entire cycle of data collection, analysis, and validation as outlined earlier. Investigators implementing the approach outlined in this essay should thus be aware of and address these threats in order to ensure usable and meaningful results for generalizable sociomaterial inquiry.

In addition to theoretical discourse, however, it is important to note that critiques from other modes of discourse (i.e., practical, aesthetic, therapeutic, explicative) may also come into play, particularly given the interpretive bent of scholars of a sociomaterial position. Accordingly, perhaps the most challenging critique may come in the form of a practical critique. By abstracting practice to limited lexical elements, researchers might be accused of making essentialist assumptions, abstracting too much, and thus missing the whole point of a detailed case-based contextual analysis. To this objection, the very artifact of the lexicon holds the answer. Any findings related to this approach can only be considered with respect to the associated lexicon and the double hermeneutic related to mapping the contextualized sociomaterial practice to this lexicon. Seeking generalizable findings in this context is related to the validity of the local interpretations, as only they can provide a framework for deriving valid generalizations.

The analysis artifact itself will not derive or identify patterns but will enable the researchers to meaningfully identify and construct patterns from the data. A reengagement with the qualitative data and with the actors and their contexts is certainly in order to elaborate upon and validate the observed generalities. Furthermore, many of the contemporary interpretive and critical approaches emphasize the role of stakeholders, political processes, and normative outcomes (see Guba and Lincoln 1989), and these all can be accommodated by this approach but are not necessarily inherent in our formulation.

## Discussion

How can we reconcile the assumption that there is a world existing independently of our descriptions of it and that is the same for all observers with the linguistic insight that we have no direct, linguistically unmediated access to “brute” reality? – Juergen Habermas (2003, p. 2)

In this essay, we describe a new approach rooted in the Habermasian position of rational reconstruction for studying generalizable patterns of association in sociomaterial practices. To this end, we develop a lexical framework that helps identify generic, generative structure in sociomaterial routines. Overall, the lexicon provides a common ground for comparing and analyzing sociomaterial routines. We also devise a computational mixed methods approach to conduct the comparison and develop generalizable inquiry. The approach involves deploying a set of sequence-analytic computational methods that can detect and analyze variations and patterns among elements of sociomaterial routines. Our approach complements existing idiographic approaches founded mainly on qualitative data analysis using ethnographies (e.g., Leonardi 2011; Orlikowski 2007).

Recently, Pollock and Williams (2008) distinguished between two “waves” of information systems research. The first wave emphasized the value and impact of technologies, whereas the second wave emphasized local action. The dominant strategy in sociomaterial research has largely been consistent with this second wave of information systems studies, which engages in a thorough case study of a single organization or process followed by the invention of an applicable localized sociomaterial vocabulary as one moves ahead (e.g., Jonsson et al. 2009; Carlo et al. 2012; Majchrzak et al. 2000; Orlikowski et al. 1995), often describing very similar phenomena with a plethora of different labels such as assemblages, ensembles, webs, apparatus, imbrications, entanglements, intertwining, entwining, embedding, mangle, associations, and so on. This search for a new vocabulary has generated rich insights and vivid narratives but, at the same time, growing bewilderment about how these findings and the labels attached to them relate to one another. Pollock and Williams accordingly point out weaknesses with second-wave studies:

this body of research tends to produce somewhat “unbalanced” and “reductive” accounts. In much of this writing, undue emphasis is given to local actors in technology implementation and use. Reading this work, we note how attention is focused almost always on immediate action, as if various actors were creating and recreating the world as if from scratch (p. 276).

Essentially, the more intimately researchers focus on particular, local case descriptions, the more difficult it is to understand connections between practices (Sandberg and Tsoukas 2009). To resolve this problem, the third wave of information systems studies should provide a means for both accommodating local contexts and comparing across contexts in time and space (Pollock and Williams 2008). In order to do this, Pollock and Williams suggest that researchers should leverage innovative methodologies that help researchers navigate multiple levels of analysis and pursue more generalizable insight. In the case of sociomaterial routines, however, the feasibility of thoroughly examining a greater sample—for example, making cross-organizational comparisons and detecting organizational and environmental factors that may affect such sociomaterial routines—has simply been beyond the reach of current approaches (Pentland et al. 2009).

The approach we detail here offers scholars some tools to study the way the social and the material become entwined in organizational practices over space and time. This is accomplished through a computational mixed-methods design that integrates, triangulates, and expands theory and data at every step of the process (Venkatesh et al. 2013). Theoretically, it covers the critical elements in published local accounts of sociomaterial practices. Let us consider Orlikowski’s (2007, p. 1440; bracketed words added) detailed explanation of Google Search as a prime example of sociomaterial entanglement:

The performance and results of a Google-based search are sociomaterial. The Google search engine is computer code [artifact (#6)] that was produced [activity (#1)] and is maintained [activity (#1)] by software engineers [actors (#2)], that executes [activity (#1)] on computers [tools (#4), location (#3)] (configured with particular hardware and software elements), and whose operation [affordance (#5)] depends on the millions of people [actors (#2), location (#3)] who create [activity (#1), dataflow (#7)] and update [activity (#1), dataflow (#7)] web pages [artifacts (#6)] everyday, and the millions of people [actors (#2), location (#3)] who enter [activity (#1), dataflow (#7)] particular search terms into it [affordance (#5)]. The result is a constitutive entanglement of the social and the material—“‘a mangling of human and material agencies” Pickering (1995) or what Suchman (2007) calls “a creative sociomaterial assemblage.”

By including a wide range of elements that constitute the sociomaterial entanglement, this approach ensures completeness during data collection, and thereby affords a consistent structure across multiple data collection efforts—thus allowing for meaningful comparisons. Utilizing the abstraction lexicon allows the scholar to zoom out and compare sociomaterial routines across space and time using proposed computational methods. For example, going back to the example of Google search, if one would code different search behaviors using our method, one can analyze how search behaviors evolve over time and across different contexts, thus allowing us to identify what becomes institutionalized and stable, while other behaviors remain in the periphery. To then investigate the observed variations in such sociomaterial routines, one could zoom back in to interpret the interviews, observations, and archival data that were collected to understand the meanings and motivations behind the variations in contexts. We offer some empirical demonstrations of how this zooming in and out happens in Appendix B.

The metaphor of a zooming has been proposed by a number of scholars to describe the evolution of methods for understanding and analyzing cross-context data (Pollock and Williams 2008; Sandberg and Tsoukas 2009). Latour (2010), in his recent commentary on the work of Tarde, points out how researchers can now, indeed, “zoom” within and across contexts with the help of contemporary computational methods:

All too often, fields have been divided between number crunching, devoid (its enemies claim) of any subtlety; and rich, thick, local descriptions, devoid (its enemies say) of any way to generalize from these observations. Many domains have abandoned the hope of proving any point by transforming quantities into qualities, and qualities into quantities....Many have despaired, as a consequence of ever being able to develop a scientific social science…the best that can be hoped for is to obtain some political or literary effects on readers....For the first time in the history of science, the same data may look just as familiar to those who come from the “natural” sciences as those who might come from the “interpretative” ones” (pp. 147, 161).

The trace data now widely available—triangulated with detailed local accounts—enables us to blur the line between the two approaches. It is less about qualitative versus quantitative than it is about toggling back and forth between parts and wholes, particulars and generalities (Latour 2010). The approach we have developed offers a mixed methods systematic, qualitative, and computational approach for analyzing rich process data. This type of approach enables more generalizable and pragmatic theory generation from rich process data, something that earlier seemed “a long way off” (Pentland et al. 2009, p. 65). This strategy is well aligned with the idea of “dynamic practices” that underlies the sociomaterial position (i.e., the preoccupation of Bijker (1995)). By combining inquiry into dynamic practices through both rich description and systematic cross-case comparison, the approach is consistent with the growing wave of mixed methods research (Creswell and Clark 2010; Tashakkori and Teddlie 2010). However, this approach is fundamentally different in that it is a flexible computational approach rather than a traditional quantitative method that involves relationships between abstractions or proxies (what Latour (2005) refers to as “imaginary” latent constructs) and instead offers a deeply empirical approach for identifying and comparing patterns. This computational approach is a third-wave methodology that draws on pragmatism and uses alternative modes of inquiry in complementary ways at different stages of research (Pollock and Williams 2008).

Consistent with our computational approach, we look to the field of genetics as a reference theory and inspiration for understanding such sociomaterial evolution (Arthur 2009). The field of genetics was founded on the need to observe and analyze variations in underlying inner cell structures (i.e., DNA), which governed their variation and growth. As is now well known, all genetic information can be described as concatenations of four nucleotides (represented by letters G, A, T, and C), which make up the DNA of all known species. Varying combinations of the four nucleotides allows for infinite varieties of genetic information to be stored and transferred. They also generate the amazing diversity of flora and fauna that we observe. The simple lexicon of the genetics field provides a notation for representing genetic building blocks, or primitives, which enables meaningful comparisons between individuals, organisms, and species, and also helps to explain their evolution. Similarly, the fixed list of values for each sociomaterial element identified in the lexicon can act as similar “genetic” elements, or primitives, that aid in capturing the regularities that are present in sociomaterial routines. By detecting changes in those regularities we can also elucidate evolutionary processes among routines (Nelson and Winter 1982). For example, referring back to a construction case we have studied, as a result of introducing new digital 3D capabilities, design routines for specific parts of building design underwent a significant, punctuated change wherein an average of 27 percent of the activities changed for each new round of design projects (see Appendix B). The systematic observation of such variation and the drivers of this variation without an event-sequencing approach would have been close to impossible. In this regard, the approach offers a more powerful way to detect and measure the evolution of embedded materiality over time when compared to existing sequence approaches (Pentland 2003; Pentland et al. 2009, 2010). This evolution can also be detected at multiple levels, including activity variation (or affordance variation and proportions, or proportions of activity types, and so on), activity sequencing (transition patterns, patterns of subsets of activities), iteration, or overall change in the structural elements of sociomaterial routines.

As a computational, data-driven research approach, this work also contributes to new methodological research, which has sought to capture, reveal, and analyze processes, routines, and social/behavioral patterns that express sequences of events (e.g., Abbott 1995; Pentland 2003; Salvato 2009). Recently, Lazer et al. (2009) proposed the use of new algorithms to analyze large-scale networks as a basis of computational social science. Our use of algorithms to analyze sequence data as proposed here offers another promising avenue of pursuing computational social science. Existing computational methodologies for analyzing routines tend to focus on simple sequences of activities (Abbott 1995; Pentland and Reuter 1994), while at the same time neglecting the human and material elements that comprise those activities. This is problematic because the interplay of human and material agencies, or capacities for action, are critical elements of enacted routines (Orlikowski 2007). Focusing on routines only as sequences of activities neglects the way these activities can qualitatively change based on their sociomaterial assemblages. It is not sufficient to focus solely on patterns of action, we must also augment this with an understanding of patterns of association between human and material elements of routines (Pentland and Feldman 2008). It is also important to avoid giving either the human or the nonhuman primacy in the analysis a priori. Thus, a sociomaterial perspective can seek to leverage more of the use of sequence analytic techniques.

Finally, from a practice-oriented perspective, the value of this approach comes in many forms. At the very least, the lexicon and process modeling grammar can act as a systematic documentation method of an organization’s current routines and could be used with or without the computational analysis as a means for comparing the anatomy of sociomaterial routines across contexts. Any industry practitioner is well aware of how the documentation of organizational routines (i.e., business processes) can provide value in itself and insight into ways to assess and improve the routine. The use of a standard schema enhances the ability to analyze and compare routines across a broader community over time. We have found in our own field studies that the simple act of recording and codifying the actual process of a complex design project and its review generates a large number of “aha” moments for the informants. The process analytics we discussed above provide another measurable benefit. Managers often cannot decisively point out why a project may have overrun or what happened when. Most design projects are simply too complex and involve too great a variety of sociomaterial moving parts to mentally make sense of. The analytics regarding the sequence and ratio of each taxonomic element (e.g., activity type, actor configuration) provide one means to systematically review how a project is progressing and how it is structured (or retrospectively, how it transpired), and then identify potential concerns. For example, we could often identify how frequently a certain type of activity or routine was being iterated or how long it took for a particular design object to be developed and through which steps. Another practical application of the approach is to show the extent of evolution from one project to another. Placing an actual “number” (an evolutionary rate) on the extent of lowlevel change in the activities that comprise a design project from one instantiation to the next is simply infeasible using existing analysis methodologies. Such an analysis informs managers about the rate and scope of change, which potentially reflects the cost of change (Hannan and Freeman 1984). Finally, in the long term, many practical benefits can be somewhat analogous to benefits of biological studies. What initially appeared in genetics as an interesting and isolated scientific exercise in describing species—using a set of four underlying elements—has been transformed into a scientific revolution in medicine, agriculture, and how we understand ourselves. In the long term, we might observe a similar transformation in our understanding of organizational practices as data about those practices becomes digitized and readily analyzable.

## Limitations, Opportunities, and Future Directions

We note several limitations, opportunities, and future directions in our current approach. First, the approach, like any sociomaterial inquiry, places a heavy burden on data collection, validation, and analysis, and demands extensive effort. The approach can be as effort-intensive as any thick qualitative field inquiry when using an interview- or observationbased approach. We have also observed that the lexicon helps center the data collection and guarantees its completeness. There are, however, new opportunities to use digital traces of routine data that can ease and augment traditional data collection. They will also scale up the data collection efforts (Pentland et al. 2010). This may require simplifications to the data encoding and analysis but may, at the same time, offer the possibility to carry out extensive super-large analyses involving thousands of routines and tens of thousands of activities. The current tools and computational machinery already allow for this.

Second, the suite of methodological tools is also quite different from the typical quantitative analysis tools acquired by information systems researchers and other social scientists in their formal training. This places another barrier. We believe, however, that over time some of these challenges can be overcome by better analysis and visualization tools, which we are actively pursuing. In our extant work (e.g., Gaskin et al. 2011a; Gaskin et al. 2011b), we have found that the rampup time and effort to establish a grammar and taxonomy are great, but then the data collection and analysis of additional data is nominal and incremental, because a framework already exists for systematic inquiry and analysis.

Third, in this essay we have applied this methodology only to the analysis of design-focused sociomaterial routines. We have already begun using these methods to study the evolution of digital products and dynamic communication patterns in other types of collective actions, including open source software development, where available digital traces are abundant (Zhang et al. 2012). We have no reason not to expect this methodology to be extendable to all types of processes involving multidimensional discrete structures and to all other contexts involving structural variation over time or across space. But this requires careful theoretical calibration of the lexicon to each specific study site and careful review of the desired extent of either accuracy or generalizability in the inquiry.

Fourth, as we continue to develop this method, we are increasingly aware of the complexity of sociomaterial routines and our inability to perfectly reconstruct them using abstract lexica and general grammars. Capturing the “felt” quality of a routine (or the “meaning”; see Pentland and Feldman 2007), which is part of its situatedness (Ciborra and Willcocks 2006), is not the intention of the lexicon. This quality (i.e., Why is it done the way it is done? What is the “tone” of the performance? What does it mean to the actors?) is difficult to capture even in idiographic studies but remains, nevertheless, an important part of routine performance—further emphasizing the need for third-wave studies that can leverage lexicons as well as idiographic data. In many ways, this lexical limitation parallels the limitation of human language. The thoughts, feelings, and intentions humans have are all intermingled and context specific; they evolve and are improvised as needed—just as sociomaterial practice has been described. Language and grammars were invented (or evolved) to rationally reconstruct these messy internal webs for conveyance to others. “Like everything metaphysical the harmony between thought and reality is to be found in the grammar of the language” (Wittgenstein 1953, p. 112). Nevertheless, we still find ourselves limited by the restrictions of a relatively abstract language; we simply cannot perfectly reconstruct how we feel or what we mean. In response to this limitation, we must simply encourage those wishing to adopt our approach to be sure to complement the efforts toward generality (zooming out) with enough level of attention to situated meanings in context (zooming in), particularly if situated meaning and the felt quality of routines is important to the researchers.

Fifth, organizational routines are not solely sequential. They are dynamic, iterative, conditional, and parallel. We are continuing to develop ways to deal with these features of routines. For example, one way to capture iteration, or looping sets of activities, in the visual lexical notation involves creating blocks of observed repeated activities. Such repeated activities generate painstaking repetition in drawing such iterated sequence. It also becomes very messy visually. To resolve this issue, we have created an abstraction for iteration objects; that is, parameterized sequences of blocks of activities that represent iterated processes. Each iteration object has its own process model associated with it, as well as a property defining how many times the process is being iterated in that part of the sequence. This iterated process acts as any other process governed by the modeling notation and can have an infinite number of iterations associated with it. Further, we are also currently limited in our ability to meaningfully analyze the presence of parallel processes and conditional branches (although we can draw them), because the established event sequencing methods assume a strict transitive order for all activities. Hence, we currently need to use certain conventions to handle how parallel activities and conditional branches become just strictly sequenced. For example, we can assign an artificial (but logical) order to the parallel activities, and we can assign a probability to conditional branches. We do not know, however, to what extent the analysis results are sensitive to these conventions. Future research should, therefore, develop new algorithmic techniques to analyze such situations.

Finally, one must keep in mind that the mutual constitution of human and material agency is a complex interplay. We approximate this interplay now with two relational concepts— tool affordance and tool modality—and these analytically separated concepts clearly abstract away from the continuity and inseparability of technologies-in-sociomaterial-practice. While this is most certainly the case, it is important to keep in mind that when seeking to make general statements about a tightly integrated reality, it is sometimes necessary to make simplifying approximations. Even Heidegger, who was perhaps the philosopher most concerned with the inseparability of human action from tools, made distinctions between different aspects of tools in involvement “wholes” (i.e., withwhich, in-which, toward-this, in-order-to, for-the-sake-ofwhich; see Rouse 2005).

Along these lines, it is important to note that the epistemological and methodological approach we describe is intended to be ontologically neutral. Whether we look at organizational routines as the interaction of discrete social systems and technological systems (Bostrom and Heinen 1977) or as ontologically inseparable sociomaterial practice (Orlikowski 2007), researchers from a variety of perspectives can use the approach. The only stipulation is that they accept the epistemological position that using a pre-theoretic lexicon can help identify robust patterns associated with phenomena of interest, and that these phenomena involve sequences of activities involving humans and nonhumans. If their approach meets these criteria, then it does not matter whether they take a systems view of organizations, an institutional view of routines, or a view of routines as situated practice. The reconstruction using the lexicon will allow comparison, but the interpretations will vary based on ontological assumptions. Use of this novel approach can open up fresh opportunities for theorizing. For example, the approach has been used to engage theorizing about the variation of digitally enabled design routines (Gaskin et al. 2011b). Future theorizing can build on this and extend it to the evolution of sociomaterial routines, the effects of enmeshing IT over time, and the impact of changing roles, changing collaboration methods, and affordances, among others. The approach we describe should act as an enabler for researchers wishing to make general sense of sociomaterial routines and especially for those seeking to discover patterns across contexts.

## Conclusion

Organizations are in a continuous state of evolution, or becoming (Benson 1977). They have unpredictable and emergent character and are, therefore, highly problematic to analyze (Bijker 1995; Tsoukas and Chia 2002). Arthur (2009) notes that technological evolution, with its combinatorial nature, shares similarities with biological evolution and also has an emergent character. Therefore, when new digital tools become embedded in organizational practices and transform them at an unprecedented rate, we must be attentive and explore the process of becoming by seeking to understand what remains stable and what changes. Our approach offers one way to detect variations, similarities, and differences as it helps detect variations in sociomaterial routines in a generalizable manner, rather than viewing the social or material separately (as is currently the typical generalist research practice; see Kallinikos 2011).

Clearly those scholars who want to emphasize situated meanings and highly faithful accounts of “lived” sociomaterial routines may not find much use for our approach. However, even those conducting second-wave studies may still benefit from the more systematic data collection principles highlighted in the proposed methodology as they keep an eye on longitudinal or cross-context comparability of data and findings (i.e., toward more generalizable sociomaterial inquiry). On the other hand, if scholars are looking to abstract away from local context, to a degree, and look to uncover general patterns in sociomaterial practice, this approach is an initial step in that direction.

One of the most daunting and deterring tasks of studying and interpreting sociomaterial practices is the job of making sense of the mounds of data, finding themes and patterns, and then relating/generalizing these findings in a way that contributes to theory and practice. Initially, it appears to a scholar that each case is unique, and thus the “entry fee” for such a study (and for entering the conversation) is intimidating. What has been learned in one study only partially informs what can be learned in another study. Thus, a good deal of work must be repeated for each case. The approach we have developed seeks to add a new level of common ground to analysis that builds on the rich, idiographic data collection, but then provides the option to reuse theoretically grounded themes, labels, patterns, relationships, etc., to make sense of the data rather than re-creating these from scratch for each case. This can be done once (although iteratively and hermeneutically), and then reused and adapted across multiple contexts. The approach is also conducive to multilevel analysis—the rich detailed descriptions of case studies complement the standardized notation of the sociomaterial routines compared across contexts.

To conclude, sociomaterial studies seem to draw on the assumption that each study context is unique. Accordingly, they seek to account for those unique features and how they explain the observed behaviors and outcomes (Weick 1989). In contrast, our approach assumes that contexts are similar on certain grounds and provides a way to inquire, observe, and detect those sociomaterial regularities and their patterns. Both approaches offer a distinct and valuable angle for how organizational practices evolve, jointly, and a means for data and theory triangulation. We believe that the inevitable tension that emerges from idiographic and generalist accounts of sociomaterial practices will offer one of the fruitful avenues for future inquiry in the design and the dynamic and expansive use of information technology in organizations.

## Appendix A

## Details of the Sociomaterial Lexical Elements for Design Routines

## Activity Type

Activity type refers to the general purpose of each basic element within a sociomaterial routine. Activity types form an essential element of any part of a sociomaterial routine, because each different type of activity will typically invoke different combinations of social and material elements based on regularities and constraints that the activity type dictates while composing the other elements of that activity. For example, a brainstorming session may involve direct synchronous group communication, whereas validating a design may require only a single individual with a laptop. In order to render routines and their parts comparable across different contexts, we adopted a task classification scheme by McGrath (1984) that suggests four generic activity types: (1) generate, (2) choose, (3) negotiate, and (4) execute. Based on our field studies to analyze design routines, we have added to this list (5) validate (Bucciarelli 1994; Henderson 1991), (6) transfer (Daft 2009), and (7) training (Clark and Voogel 1985). As noted, an activity may include from one to several tasks.

## Actor Configuration

Actor configuration refers to the number and grouping of the actors involved in the activity (Jin and Levitt 1996). The configuration of actors is not only a necessary social component of an activity, but also has important material regularities for how activities are composed. For example, collocated actors may use physical tools (e.g., whiteboards), whereas distributed users may use only digital tools (e.g., VOIP). Thus the social configuration of actors is inseparably connected with the material configuration of tools and related communication facilities. Actors can be exclusively configured as (1) an individual, (2) a group, (3) many individuals, (4) many groups, (5) or individuals and groups. Th distinction between many individuals and a group is that a group shares a single functional purpose, whereas many individuals each have their own separate functional purpose. For example, many individuals can work on different parts of a product design, whereas a team can work on the task of reconciling or reviewing those parts.

## Activity Location

Location refers to a locale where a sociomaterial activity takes place (Armstrong and Cole 2002). Like actor configuration, the location has both social and material implications and related regularities. Location often affects the tools used for manipulating work objects, and the number and types of actors involved. When actors are located in reference to each other, they it form an important factor for influencing how to coordinate the activity (Armstrong and Cole 2002; Eckert et al. 2005; Jonassen and Kwon 2001; Majchrzak et al. 2000; Perry and Sanderson 1998; Purvanova and Bono 2009; Robey and Jin 2004). Activities can be performed collocated (actors together in one place), locally (actors distributed at the host site), remotely (actors away from the host site), or in some combination of these—mixed.

## Tool Modality

Tool modality refers to the underlying materiality of the functional affordances that the tool offers while being used in a sociomaterial activity. Tools come with specific material features (Markus and Silver 2008) that are afforded through their physical or digital modalities (Leonardi 2010; Leonardi and Barley 2008, 2010). Thus, for each tool, we code whether the modality of the tool is digital (the functional affordance of the tool are enabled via digital means—basically software) or physical (the functional affordances of the tool are enabled through physical means—like analogous hardware). In any given work context, the physical and the digital modality of work are tied together, and give meaning to each other (Davenport and Pearlson 1998; Jin 2002; Rennecker 2002; Robey and Jin 2004). In this vein, Robey et al. (2003) note that work capabilities can be enhanced by increasingly intertwining or differently intertwining physical and digital modalities. We place emphasis here on the modality rather than tool composition, because, inevitably, what is digital cannot be completely independent from the physical. In the end all digital is ultimately founded on some physical components (Leonardi 2010; Leonardi and Barley 2008).

## Tool Affordance

Affordances refer to the opportunities for use offered, provided, or furnished by the technology for the user during a specific task. As such, we want to capture relational information between actors who participate in an activity, and what they do with the tools, by encoding tool affordances. Fundamentally, tools, whether digital or physical, can be used for multiple purposes by different actors within different tasks. Hence, tool affordance encodes a socially constructed (and interpreted through double-hermeneutic) relationship between the tool (technology) and a (set of) users in the studied activity (Gibson 1977; Markus and Silver 2008). Affordance is, therefore, not inherent to a tool, a task, or an actor, but to a context where the tool is being used by actors for a specific task (Carlo et al. 2011). At the same time, information concerning the modality of a tool is incomplete without information regarding the nature of its enactment. We integrated the list from Leonardi and Barley (2008) and Henderson and Cooprider (1994) while developing a list of tools affordances. We chose to base our list primarily on Henderson and Cooprider as they offer higher accuracy for describing alternative ways in which tools can be enacted by actors. Their list also overlaps considerably with Leonardi and Barley’s typology and covers all of their affordances except a “storage” affordance, which we have added. We removed their “support” and “infrastructure” affordances, as we observed in our coding that they were subsumed by the “cooperative” use affordance. Thus we currently assume six affordances for tools: (1) representation, (2) analysis, (3) transformation, (4) control, (5) cooperative, and (6) storage.

## Artifact Type

Artifact type refers to the modality of an artifact that is being manipulated as an object of work within a sociomaterial routine. During routine enactment, actors manipulate a variety of physical and digital artifacts, each intended to represent or support some aspect of their work. Again, artifact type is socially constructed and the artifacts can be primarily distinguished by their uses and the purposes they serve in different practices, such as communications (e-mail, memos, etc.), sketches, diagrams, requirements, and specifications (Rosenman and Gero 1996). In our taxonomy we employ five different artifact types: specification, draft/prototype, implementation, process planning, and knowledge. We capture information on artifact types because they are a central and often persistent component of sociomaterial routines. For example, documentation often carries over from one activity to another—being generated during one activity, updated during another, and used as input for yet another. These material artifacts can also have determining effects on social elements of activity. For example, process planning documents can dictate future communications and organizing. Thus the type of artifact being utilized or generated is intertwined with the social aspects of structuring an activity.

## Dataflow

Finally, the dataflow refers to how a tool affects an artifact or relates to it. Artifacts can be an input for a tool, or an output of a tool, or they can be updated by the tool as part of the activity. We capture information on the tool/artifact connection, in order to detect the patterns of dataflow in a routine. Are artifacts being constantly updated? Used only a couple times as inputs? Or are they constantly being produced? While the tool/artifact connection is clearly a material relationship within a routine, it also has social ramifications. For example, the desired connection may affect which actors need to participate in an activity. If an activity needs to output a report of design possibilities, then this artifact may need to be generated by multiple actors, some with specific roles (such as architect, engineer, or designer), and the location of those actors will probably need to be collocated. Conversely, if a bill of materials needs to be generated (output), then a single individual (or automated procedure) can do this remotely.

## Step 5. Sequence Analysis Using Transition Matrices

In addition to the sequence alignment and the descriptive statistics, we can also produce Markov chains, or transition matrices, to describe the state changes in activity types, actor configurations, or locations.<sup>5</sup> Transition matrices estimate the probability of one state following another, and are thus the truest sequence analysis. For example, transition matrices can answer the question

• For BuildMAX, what type of activity is most likely to follow a “validate” activity?

• How often do validate activities follow generate activities?

The transition matrix takes as an input the temporal sequence of all activity types (or whichever element you desire), and then estimates the probability that one state will follow another. Building a transition matrix by hand is not feasible for even moderately sized processes (anything greater than about 20 activities). Accordingly, we have written an automated procedure in Excel that generates the frequency transition matrix, expected transition matrix, and probability transition matrix. Tables B4-6 represent each of these three kinds of matrices, while Figure B5 shows one way to visually depict a probability transition matrix.

The frequency transition matrix is the exact count of state changes from one activity type to another. The probability transition matrix is the likelihood (percent) that one activity type will follow another based on the frequency transition matrix. The expected transition matrix is the expected transition frequency between each pair of activity types based solely on the count of each activity type in the routine. For example, if we know that a routine with 10 activities has only two types of activities—choose and negotiate—both occurring five times, then we can expect choose to follow negotiate 50 percent of the time. However, if choose occurs eight times and negotiate occurs two times, then we would only expect choose to follow negotiate 20 percent of the time. When there are more than two values, the calculations get more difficult; hence, we have written procedures to automate these calculations. Additionally, using the frequency transition matrix (what the state changes actually were) and the expected transition matrix (what we would expect based on the number of each value), we can then conduct a chi-square difference test to determine if there is interdependence and predictability among the activities or whether the order is essentially random.

We can also take observed transition matrices from multiple processes and compare them to determine if the sequence of activities is roughly equivalent or statistically different. Using this approach we can actually produce p-values to determine structural (and sequential) equivalence. This is done using software called UCINet<sup>7</sup> which allows quadratic correlation tests between matrices. For example, MEP 2009 and 2011 are not statistically similar in their sequential transitions (Pearson Correlation = 0.35, p = 0.25), whereas MEP 2007 and 2011 are significantly dissimilar (Pearson Correlation = -0.50, p = 0.04), as shown in Table B7. In an ongoing research effort, we are currently exploring the possibility and usefulness of using transition matrices to simulate sociomaterial routines (Liu and Pentland 2011). Such simulations would open up possibilities to answer new questions about the effects of inserting into or removing specific social or material aspects from a process.

## Step 6. Cluster Analysis in MEGA

Finally, Clustal produces a dendrogram file that can be used to draw hierarchical and phylogenetic trees in DNA mapping programs like MEGA (Kumar et al. 2004).<sup>8</sup> These trees offer insights into the clustering structure of sequences of activities in routines. In practical terms, this helps identify activities that utilize the same resources and have the same intent. Such information may be useful to project managers seeking to modularize to reduce design costs. Notice in Figure B6 that routine clusters are driven primarily by activity type. For example, the clusters in the six o’clock position are all transfer activities, but are from four different projects from four different years. These trees also show degrees of difference between sequences. Branch lengths are an estimate of the distance (or difference) between the sequence on that branch and the nearest parent branch. For example, the MEP 2009 and 2011 validate activities (eleven o’clock) are quite different from the others, as is th sequence labeled “s2 2007 Validate” (around the two o’clock position), which appears on its own

To test the reliability of these clusters, we may resample the sequences using a bootstrap feature available in Clustal. This feature collapses all bootstrapped trees to form a consensus tree—a tree that represents consistent “true” structure. The output of the procedure is a PHYLYP tree file (.phb), which can be viewed in MEGA. The consistency of cluster structures is represented by the number of times a particular cluster appeared divided by the total number of resamples. These percentages are nonparametric confidence values for the reliability of each cluster—much like Cronbach’s alpha, or composite reliability scores. Accordingly, percentages above 0.70 indicate reliable clusters.<sup>9</sup> Finally, trees should be visualized as “unrooted,” because this part of the approach is intended for detecting cluster structures, whereas rooted trees imply ancestral relations, or a common ancestor.
