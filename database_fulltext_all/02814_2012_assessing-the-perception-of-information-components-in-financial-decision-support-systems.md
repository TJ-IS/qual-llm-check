# Assessing the perception of information components in <sup>fi</sup>nancial decision support systems

Ofir Ben-Assuli ⁎

Faculty of Business Administration, Ono Academic College, Israel

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 22 February 2012 Received in revised form 25 July 2012 Accepted 17 September 2012 Available online 24 September 2012

Keywords: Decision making Investment planning Financial decision making Perceived value of information

## a b s t r a c t

Estimating the contribution of DSS to <sup>fi</sup>nancial consulting decision-making is attracting considerable interest in the fast-growing <sup>fi</sup>eld of banking DSS. This study evaluated the perceived role of banking DSS in the decision-making of investment counselors. A questionnaire was submitted to 40 investment counselors to determine the comparative importance of DSS information components. Data were analyzed using two complementary methods (analytical hierarchy processing and Neumann–Segev). The most important information components were customer's and investment risk classi<sup>fi</sup>cation, and customer goals and nature of investment. The results differed across administrative ranks and as a function of the user's experience level. © 2012 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

## 1. Introduction

In the past few years, information systems have entered the <sup>fi</sup>eld of investment counseling, where they are used to support the work of bank employees in charge of customer portfolios. These bankers assist customers in making investments in a way that suits their lifestyles and <sup>fi</sup>nancial goals. Traditionally, bankers simply remembered their customers and their customers' pro<sup>fi</sup>les and tried to design investment portfolios adapted to each. This approach may have been appropriate in the past, but is less so in an age in which there are so many <sup>fi</sup>nancial instruments that be<sup>fi</sup>t a far wider classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of customer types and investment types.

Vahidov and Kersten [37] described the major changes in the business environment in the past thirty years and in particular, the globalization of economy and the growing complexity of economic relationships. These in turn contribute to the complexity of the decision problems faced by decision makers [11]. Additionally, important <sup>fi</sup>nancial decisions require reasonably accurate forecasts based on various probabilistic information components such as forecasting future medical needs and costs [39], household <sup>fi</sup>nancial planning in years to come [35], etc. It seems virtually impossible for an investor to utilize all the available information in the short time needed to respond to market trends. Therefore, a control mechanism is needed to help bankers equilibrate deliberation and timely decision-making [36]. All the above clearly point to the increasing need for sophisticated decision support tools.

Although a DSS should improve decision quality, research has shown that at times the opposite is true or that implementation of a system has no effect at all [20]. This is not entirely unexpected since multiple variables, issues and contexts are involved. Nonetheless, DSSs have a vast potential for supplying complete, uniform, exact, up to date, accessible and reliable banking information, which can improve decision quality and reduce risks and uncertainties that stem from lack of information [5]. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, information about customers, such as their goals and the nature of their investments, the investment horizon, the customer's risk classi<sup>fi</sup>cation, investment history, etc. can enhance the decision making processes of bankers who deal with investment counseling.

Assessing the value of such information as part of the decision making processes is one of the most important topics in several research <sup>fi</sup>elds that deal with organizational information, and has been studied extensively over a wide range of disciplines that deal with information systems in organizations [2]. Components of the system must be examined using variables that relate to the characteristics of the information to best maximize utility. According to Ahituv [1] there are three main characteristics: time variables, for instance system response time, frequency of receipt of data, etc., content variables, for instance relevancy, precision, suitability of data to what the user wants, aggregation of data, etc., and format variables such as visual presentation, interactive visual analytic tools [31], interactive media, arranging data in tables versus graphs, graphic design, etc. Ideally, each variable in the utility function should be clearly de<sup>fi</sup>ned and possible to measure, and the relationship between the variables in the utility function and the cost variables should be known and mathematically de<sup>fi</sup>ned. In such a situation, optimization tools help choose the most appropriate system. In reality, however, the maximizing utility method has several shortcomings related to problems of measurement [27].

The main purpose of this study was to assess the perceived value of information obtained from a DSS designed to assist the decision-making processes of bankers who deal with customer investment counseling. As of<sup>fi</sup>cially stated by the bank, the DSS characterizes the customer in terms of purpose of investment and risk classi<sup>fi</sup>cation, takes into account current macroeconomic parameters, and then constructs and presents a documented recommendation for investment.

This paper is organized as follows. The next section reviews the literature regarding user expectations and evaluations of DSSs and a perceived value of information approach, and details the objectives of the present study. Section 3 presents the analysis tools used to assess the perceived value of information; namely, the AHP (analytical hierarchy processing) method [29] and the Neumann–Segev method of analyzing correlations [26]. The results are presented in Section 4, and discussed in Section 5. Section 6 covers limitations and future research directions, and Section 7 presents practical conclusions and recommendations for improvement in the speci<sup>fi</sup>c DSS surveyed here.

## 2. Background and research approach

## 2.1. Background and literature review

The goal of investment decision-making is to select an optimal portfolio that satis<sup>fi</sup>es the investor's objective to maximize investment returns under given constraints and limitations [28,30]. This, for instance, can be addressed through value of information analysis that assesses the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of additional information to reduce uncertainties [14,42]. Applied information economics can also be used to make cost/bene<sup>fi</sup>t optimization analyses regarding IT investments, which involve extensive mathematical models [34].

However, as Ahituv, Munro and Wand [4] pointed out, decision makers seek satisfaction and not necessarily optimization (which derives from utility theory), and tend to adopt the <sup>fi</sup>rst solution that <sup>fi</sup>ts their expectations. For instance, individuals may stick to a certain known pro<sup>fi</sup>t (from a speci<sup>fi</sup>c investment fund), which is not necessarily the maximal pro<sup>fi</sup>t, because that maximum is not known (see also [27] for more examples). Rafaeli and Raban [28] used the perceived “willingness to pay” method to show that people's use of information is irrational and apparently derives from the need to avoid risks. For instance, they noted that simply having the information “just in case” was an important thing for the users. It is a plausible that irrational thinking should be taken into account in most decision making processes [8], especially when the decision involves conditions of uncertainty, risk [19] or extensive psychological involvement [6].

The concept of evaluating DSS through its users is based on Mason and Mitroff's [23] accepted de<sup>fi</sup>nition: “An information system consists of, at least, a person of a certain psychological type dealing with a problem located in an organizational context, which the person needs evidence to solve, with the evidence being presented to her in a particular manner of presentation”. This de<sup>fi</sup>nition makes it clear that the user is the deciding factor in planning the system, since if the system does not <sup>fi</sup>t the user's needs, she will simply not use it. Hence, a vital measure of the system's success is the extent of use of the information system and the user's satisfaction. These factors were shown to be related to preliminary as well as ongoing training, especially at the managerial level. [20].

Users' ability to experience the positive outcomes of the system has also been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as a crucial factor for system satisfaction and usage. [20,22]. Lilien et al. (2004) showed that even when a high quality DSS is used, where results may thus be substantially improved, its perceived quality may not be commensurate.

## 2.2. Estimating the value of information

Ahituv [2] and Ahituv and Neumann [5] de<sup>fi</sup>ned three key approaches to evaluation of the value of information, which they termed Perceived, Realistic and Normative. The perceived value of information re<sup>fl</sup>ects the user's viewpoint and understanding of how she or he sees the advantages and disadvantages that derive from using the IS. The realistic value of information is the difference in performance (of organizations or individuals) before and after obtaining information. This is basically an empirical approach that re<sup>fl</sup>ects the organization's actual performance. The normative value of information predicts the system's value based on a theoretical model derived from normative models and from the predicted behavior of the decision maker. Under the assumption that the decision maker behaves rationally, the expected utility or expected pro<sup>fi</sup>t can be calculated.

Each approach has plus sides and shortcomings and the optimal approach to a given situation depends on the information to be assessed and the parameters of the situation. A perceived evaluation of a DSS is usually undertaken when the information quality cannot be entirely objectively measured; for instance, when the reliability of the information is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to evaluate objectively [38]. The more strategic or unstructured the decision is, the more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult it is to measure the value of information. Such decisions are usually nonrepetitive, involve numerous unknowns that are external to the organization, and may involve extensive creativity as well; altogether this can make it rather dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to assess the quality of the decision process [17]. For instance, at senior management levels only a measure of the perceived value of information can absorb real world issues such as competitive power, people skills, organization politics, etc. [2,7,15].

This is also true in the case of investment counseling, where the decision is fairly amorphous and usually extremely complex, and there is no way to work out “the best answers”. Therefore, perceived assessment of information is considered the most potent and reliable indicator and was adopted here.

Assessing the perceived value of information is feasible once the DSS is up and running since it is especially dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to carry out measures like these on planned systems that are not part of the users' or decision makers' environment. Nonetheless, with or without an existing IS, assessing the perceived value of information is not an easy task. A number of methods have been developed to measure the perceived value of information, two of which were used here. The <sup>fi</sup>rst is the AHP, that utilizes a hierarchic analytical process [29]. The second is the Neumann– Segev semantic scale (commonly employed as an odd Likert scale [14]). In both methods, the users are asked to indicate their responses to characteristics or properties of the system on a questionnaire. These methods do not yield an exact monetary value, and it is practically impossible to conduct cost/bene<sup>fi</sup>t analyses. These methods are explained in detail in Section 3.

## 2.3. Objectives of this study

The objective was to quantify the perceived impact of various information components of <sup>fi</sup>nancial DSS on the decision making processes of investment counselors. The <sup>fi</sup>rst goal was to hierarchize the contribution of a set of components of information to the decision making process. This was done by analyzing a questionnaire submitted to investment counselors in a leading bank in Israel that assessed the perceived value of information obtained by using <sup>fi</sup>nancial DSSs in the decision making process. The second goal was to determine the factors that affect the use of <sup>fi</sup>nancial DSSs. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, (a) the effect of seniority (see also [3]), and, (b) the effect of the administrative rank of the investment counselors (Investment Counselor, Team Leader, Branch Manager, etc.) were analyzed.

## 3. Material and methods

In this study, two complementary methods were chosen: the AHP method [29] and the semantic scale as used by Neumann–Segev in their method of analyzing correlations [26].

## 3.1. The AHP method

The AHP method was developed by Saaty [29] and its objective is to solve problems with large numbers of variables. The method involves creating a structured ranking system when dealing with a combination of quantitative and subjective elements. AHP can structure the decision problem into a hierarchy that re<sup>fl</sup>ects the values, goals, objectives and desires of the decision-makers [18].

The process itself is made up of the following stages. The <sup>fi</sup>rst is ‘identifying the variables’ which involves breaking the problem down into factors, and then if appropriate, in a hierarchical way. The second step is ‘quanti<sup>fi</sup>cation’ in which pairs of information components are compared. The third is ‘adjusted calculation’, the mathematical calculation of the comparisons. The fourth is ‘evaluation and recommendations’ based on the results of the adjusted calculations. The AHP method basically constitutes a measurement and comparison tool that determines the substitutability between pairs of information components involved in the decision making process. This procedure invites inconsistencies, which may result from multiple comparisons between pairs of factors (measured through an inconsistency coef<sup>fi</sup>cient). Lane and Verdini [21] dealt with this problem of inconsistency in the AHP method and recommended taking a hard line regarding the inconsistency coef<sup>fi</sup>cient when there is a deviation of over 10%. Saaty [30] suggested considering additional inconsistencies such as comprehension or typing errors, lack of information about one or more of the elements in certain comparison, and lack of concentration.

## 3.2. Implementing the AHP method

The AHP method is based on setting objective priorities, when they exist, and on setting subjective priorities, when it is impossible to determine them objectively (for instance, when there are no quantitative, concrete or <sup>fi</sup>nancial measures). Setting the priorities or calculating the perceived value of information is done in a hierarchical fashion. At the top is the primary goal, customer counseling, under which are the information components that make up this goal. Setting the order and levels of priority are done by comparing two possibilities each time. A scale of values made up of 9 grades was used here. Grade 1 denotes equality between the compared possibilities, and grade 9 denotes an absolute preference for one possibility over another. The values in between denote different levels of preference. After all the comparisons of all pairs have been done, setting priorities takes place. The move from preferences between pairs of possibilities to setting priorities de<sup>fi</sup>nes the relative priorities for all the information components. The information components that were chosen for this study are the product of the system designers' input. The system had been in operation for two years when this study took place.

## 3.3. The Neumann–Segev method

This method involves the examination of simple correlations between the primary purpose and the various information components. Although it is an accepted statistical method for measuring the perceived value of information, where the perceived value of information is re<sup>fl</sup>ected as the perceived impressions of the decision makers as regards the contribution of the information at their disposal [5] certain shortcomings have been noted.

For instance, Neumann and Segev [26] reported that they were unsure of their participants' ability to discern the difference between the various characteristics of the information they had at their disposal. Second, they suggested that participants may formulate a holistic attitude which does not allow them to isolate the various characteristics, due to extended use of the information system. They noted that their method does not refer to users' a-priori needs for information, but rather to their attitudes towards the existing system based on their experience (the perceived value of information). The data on the bank investment counselors' evaluation of the information components were obtained via a questionnaire in which they were asked to express their opinions on the contribution or the possible outcomes from the use of information.

Here, a scale range of 1 to 6 was used, where 6 indicates the highest score, and 1 indicates the lowest. Neumann and Segev [26] used Guttman's correlation coef<sup>fi</sup>cients. Here Friedman's non-parametric two-way ANOVA test for related samples within all component groups, and the non-parametric independent samples Mann–Whitney U test between groups were used.

## 3.4. Characteristics of the DSS implemented in this study

The information system that was chosen was the counseling decision support system of a leading Israeli bank. The bank is proud of its system and markets it as a source of value for its operations. The system implements advanced analytical tools to create professionally customized portfolios; these in turn are linked to data on stock indices, bonds, and funds. The system is connected to the trading systems; this makes it possible to execute transfer instructions quickly and easily.

As stated by the bank of<sup>fi</sup>cials, this information system supports three main goals that help ful<sup>fi</sup>ll the primary purpose of investment counseling; namely, customer characterization, customer counseling, and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency in customer service. The system thus includes information components that make up the stated goals. The system components are: goal and nature of the investment, investment horizon, macroeconomic parameters, customer's risk classi<sup>fi</sup>cation, and customer's investment history.

The following is a description of the system's main work<sup>fl</sup>ow process. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage, sophisticated tools characterize the customer (for instance risk-tolerance parameters), her needs and intended goals for the investment (investment horizon). In the second stage, the system generates and presents forecasts and investment options; this stage ends with mapping the most appropriate investment routes. In the <sup>fi</sup>nal stage, the system processes all the information and makes a recommendation regarding appropriate investments and budget allocation that best <sup>fi</sup>ts the customer needs; the DSS takes into account many variables from a complex set of data sources. Then, after the recommendation is presented to the customer, it is stored for future consideration, given the frequent changes in the economic climate as well as in customer requests. The full documentation of the decision process allows for future scrutiny of the reasoning behind the decisions. The customer can view past transactions and execute new ones at any given time, and from any of the organization branches throughout Israel. See Appendix A, Figs. A.1, A.2 for two illustrative screen shots that give an idea of the workings of the system.

## 3.5. Sample population

A questionnaire was submitted in several bank branches to 80 investment counselors (out of a total of 250) who differed in terms of seniority, job pro<sup>fi</sup>le and administrative rank. In the end, 40 questionnaires were returned, for a response rate of 50%. The investment counselors were recruited by research assistants in the bank branches. The participants were told that the questionnaire dealt with their opinion about the DSS. All participants were volunteers and were guaranteed anonymity.

## 4.2. Summary of the results

The two most dominant information components emerging from the results are goals and nature of investment and customer's & investment risk classi<sup>fi</sup>cation, with scores well above all other information components. An exception was found among Team counselors who ranked customer's requests higher than customer's and investment risk classi<sup>fi</sup>cation, though this exception existed only in the relative assessment and not in the absolute assessment of the same group. Conversely, the macroeconomic parameters and customer's investment history were ranked especially low. The ascending order of the perceived value of information was fairly stable in both relative and absolute assessments, thus increasing the reliability of the results.

Additionally, the results suggest that the perceived value of information provided by the DSS depends on two factors. The <sup>fi</sup>rst is the counselors' experience, in that more experienced counselors assign more importance to certain information components than less experienced counselors do. The second is counselors' administrative rank and position in that counselors at different administrative ranks attempt to gather different types of information for customer counseling. It should be noted that the participants' responses passed the inconsistency tests in all comparisons in the AHP method; consequently, the results ascribe validity to the consistency of their answers. Furthermore, the complementary Mann–Whitney U test results were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in most cases for the Neumann–Segev (absolute) method.

## 5. Discussion

The primary goal of this study was to assess the perceived contribution of a DSS to supporting the decision making of investment counselors in a banking setting. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the aim was to quantify the perceived impact of the information components of <sup>fi</sup>nancial DSS on the decision making processes as perceived by the investment counselors. This study made use of two complementary methods: the AHP method and the Neumann–Segev Correlation Analysis method. Both yielded results that were similar, signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and consistent, thus substantially con<sup>fi</sup>rming the validity and contribution of this study.

As stated above, the counselors' acceptance and belief that the information provided by the system is suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to make effective decisions is indeed crucial [13]. However, objectively improved results may not lead to changes in perception, especially at the manageria level [20]. Fellner et al. [16] found that psychological egocentric biases such as overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence and optimism lead decision makers to prefer their own portfolio to the average one, as well as to an expert's portfolio [39]. Mohsen et al. [24] noted that bank employees, especially at high managerial levels, prefer to depend on their own intuition. The results of the current study tend to reinforce these <sup>fi</sup>ndings in the sense that greater experience in managerial counseling led to regarding customer's investment history and customer's requests as less important.

This <sup>fi</sup>nding can be interpreted in several ways. Senior counselors may have given less weight to their customers' requests than junior counselors did because they considered their customers' requests to be less well informed and hence not to be acted upon (see [25] for a discussion of individuals' approach to investment). Alternatively, the strong emotional involvement in the investment decision may have come into play. Junior counselors may have been affected by emotions such as fear and regret, which might have made them rely more heavily on their client's requests, whereas senior counselors were affected by the actual risks of the investment versus the risk classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of the certain customer, and thus assigned lesser importance to their customer's requests, perhaps also as a function of professional hubris [9]. Nevertheless, the junior counselors apparently failed to recognize some of the importance of concrete risk evaluations of the customer and the investment, as compared with the other information components.

DSSs are designed to provide the user with an unbiased work<sup>fl</sup>ow or at least to lead to a certain reduction in bias [10]. The added value of the banking DSS here is that it displays the actual values of a whole set of current information components that are organized and presented well (see Appendix A, Figs. A.1, A.2). Using its current concrete information, the DSS can reduce users' bias, and as is the case in other DSSs enable the users to chart their outcomes online [22].

Reduced complexity in the decision making processes has also been cited as a vital component for reinforcing the positive perception of DSS in the banking sector [20]. The current study can provide insights into ways to reduce complexity in the investment counseling processes using DSS. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings on the relative importance of speci<sup>fi</sup>c information components (as affected as well by seniority level and administrative rank) could help develop strategies to reduce the user bias and the complexity of investment counseling.

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that more experienced, higher ranked counselors prefer relying on their own intuition rather than utilizing the information components available to them to the fullest. DSS designers could respond to this challenge by formats and prompts that could reduce the cognitive biases correlated with rank and seniority.

## 6. Limitations and future research

Several limitations should be noted. First, only the perceived value of information was examined, and not the actual performance of the decision makers. Nonetheless, each mode (realistic, normative, and perceived) has its advantages and disadvantages; if possible, system analyses should rely on a combination of modes that allow multiple complementary insights regarding the DSS [32]. Second, although two distinct assessment methods were chosen, they both have shortcomings. The AHP (relative) method allows for a certain amount of inconsistency, and the Neumann–Segev correlation analysis (absolute) method is also not error-free. The Neumann–Segev method depends on the users' ability to evaluate the components or characteristics of the system; therefore, users' cognitive and psychological limitations should also be properly addressed. It was suggested [26] that this method mainly captures users' attitudes towards the existing system in terms of their prior experience. Third, the analysis, although extensive, consisted of a relatively small sample, in which all participants held positions at the same bank.

Future research could extend the investigation of factors that affect the use of <sup>fi</sup>nancial DSSs. Understanding how a DSS is actually used (and why) cannot be achieved without a thorough understanding of the users themselves. Future studies should consider including a qualitative descriptive overview of the participants to associate with the empirical quantitative results. Future research could also deal with the assessment of the actual use of the DSS via several methods such as analyzing track log <sup>fi</sup>les and evaluating common indices that relate to repeating decision making processes. Such an assessment could be compared to the perceived assessment reported here.

## 7. Practical conclusions and recommendations for the studied DSS

There is some evidence that DSSs lead to bias reduction [10]; this paper makes some heuristics visible. DSS designers should take these data into consideration when working toward additional bias reduction, by eliminating some of the complexity of the decisions. Understanding the cognitive as well as emotional processes of decision-makers is an issue of great importance to the information system <sup>fi</sup>eld, and these concepts are invaluable to system designers [41]. DSS designers should also consider the dynamics of the situation and environment, in which many sets of portfolio alternatives exist and decisions are made at the end of the whole counseling process [12].

This study provides a unique inside view of the informational behavior of speci<sup>fi</sup>c bank counselors. For a DSS to be useful and trusted, an extremely large knowledge base is required, and this content must be kept up to date. In the absence of effective governance practices, implementation of a DSS may fail, despite the purchase or development of a sophisticated system [40]. Second, the impact of information components should be objectively monitored. Appropriate assessments could relate IT usage and performance, and identify which applications contribute most to individual performance [33]. The breadth, depth and speed of access of the information may also need to be adapted to different types of users. The level of accuracy and updating of information may be much more important when the information components involved have been ranked as essential for carrying out the counselors' tasks; therefore budget allocations may be reconsidered. Finally, DSS usage should include initial as well as ongoing training, as it makes the system more usable and trustworthy [20].
