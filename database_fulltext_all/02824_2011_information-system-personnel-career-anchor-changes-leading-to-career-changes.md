# Information system personnel career anchor changes leading to career changes

Christina Ling-Hsing Chang<sup>1</sup>, Victor Chen<sup>2</sup>, Gary Klein<sup>3</sup>, James J. Jiang<sup>4</sup>

## Abstract

Career anchors represent personal desires of employees that must be satisfied in order for organizations to attract and retain good workers. Career stages represent advancements along the paths of employment that an individual can take through an organization or chain of organizations that represent professional growth and increased value to an organization. Though research is prevalent on career anchors and career stage models, works that look at changes in one as the other changes are missing from the literature. Due to that lack, it is usually concluded that anchors reflect changes in careers as Information systems (IS) employees advance through their career stages. However, expectancy-value theory leads us to expect that the causality may be reversed, that career anchors change leading employees to value alternative positions more highly. Drawing on the career changes of 10 IS employees, we examine the changes in anchors through progressive career stages. While technical competence and security anchors are important at all stages of an IS career, managerial competence, geographic security, and autonomy become more important in the latter stages and are often adjusted prior to career movement. Management should account for these changes while designing jobs and incentives, while further research is required to confirm dominant patterns of change.

## Introduction

Information systems (IS) are a core capability of an organization in controlling critical downstream and upstream data (Chang, 2009). It is the IS professional that maintains this core capability for the organization making turnover of IS personnel costly, not only in terms of replacing staff and training new employees, but also in terms of systems development productivity and quality ( Jiang & Klein, 1999–2000; Thatcher et al., 2002–2003). In light of this, researchers argue that understanding the needs of IS employees by determining employee desires is important, because an employee’s internal wants influence selection of occupation and intention to leave an organization (Agarwal & Ferratt, 2000; Quesenberry & Trauth, 2007). Thus, organizations might be able to improve retention of IT employees by matching employee wants to what career opportunities can provide (Ramakrishna & Potosky, 2001–2002).

A career is typically viewed as a sequence of work-related positions that an individual experiences in his or her lifetime, while career management is a self-conscious activity that aligns individual goals with those of an organization (Hall, 1986). Both researchers and managers have undertaken significant steps in an attempt to understand the career development of IS personnel in order to better manage the IS function in an organization (Lee, 2002). One notable approach suggests measuring an employee’s career desires as his or her career anchors. Career anchors define an individual’s self-perception of his or her talents and abilities, needs and motives, and attitude and values. It has been hypothesized that career anchors remain stable throughout a person’s career (Schein, 1978).

The stability of career anchors, however, would tend to channel an individual through a linear career path based on established anchors. To counter this rigid view of anchors, researchers have proposed that anchors change due to changes created by market conditions and career advancement (Igbaria et al., 1999; Agarwal & Ferratt, 2000; Beecham et al., 2008). As an example, the increase in outsourcing, open source development, new technology, and light-weight development methods reflect organizational movements towards shorter, market-mediated employment relationships (Slaughter & Ang, 1996; Beecham et al., 2008) and encourages a decline in worker preference for longer-term relationships (Agarwal & Ferratt, 2000). These changes in industry conditions might be causing changes in the desires of IS employees as they face new challenges and the prospect of reduced durability of jobs (Agarwal & Ferratt, 2000; Beecham et al., 2008). Further, as employees grow from technical to management positions, anchors will change to adjust to the new conditions: a causal direction leading from change in the career to change in the anchor (Igbaria et al., 1999).

A better understanding of the relationship between career anchors and career choice is crucial. Career anchors are of paramount importance in understanding IS employees’ career aspirations. Indeed, career anchors have been related to several career indicators such as career success, career satisfaction, organizational commitment, and employee turnover rate (Igbaria et al., 1999; Jiang et al., 2001; McMurtrey et al., 2002; Hsu et al., 2003; Agarwal et al., 2007; Allen et al., 2009; Chang, 2010). Employees and the organizations that employ them share a joint responsibility in effectively planning a career path that not only suits the employees, but also helps them progress through serial career stages (Hall, 1990; Peiperl et al., 2000; Chang, 2010). Opportunities created by the organization that allow professional growth should be able to keep elite employees satisfied and motivated (Agarwal et al., 2007).

An understanding of how career anchors may vary for IS employees at various stages is lacking (Agarwal & Ferratt, 2000, 2002; Mgaya et al., 2009; Chang, 2010). Knowledge of the relationship between career stages and career anchors is expected to help employers better attract, motivate, and retain IS personnel. In light of this, in the current study, we review the literature and propose salient propositions based on case evaluations. In particular, we explore two questions: (1) Will IS employees in different career stages exhibit different career anchors and (2) what reasons induce one’s career anchors to change? As part of this, we do not limit our inquiry to career stages being the cause of career anchor changes, and instead consider the possibility that the reverse may hold in many situations. This is a timely topic, particularly because the decision to promote the best-qualified employees starts from an understanding of the IS personnel’s career anchors and the evolution of career anchors through successive career stages.

## Career anchors as drivers of career decisions

Career stage theory is rooted in the pioneering work of Super (1957). In general, the career stage model posits that an individual’s professional life can be categorized into a number of specific career stages characterized by various activities and psychological adjustments. Career researchers contend that individuals normally experience four career stages in Super’s (1957) career stage model: (1) establishment, (2) advancement, (3) maintenance, and (4) disengagement. Since individuals in the disengagement stage are gradually becoming detached from the organizational life, the first three career stages are often used to summarize professional careers (Raelin, 1985; Slocum & Cron, 1985). In variation, several career stage frameworks have been proposed and a number of methods have been used to operationally define career stages (e.g., age, tenure, organizational level, and psychological adjustment).

Another notable career stage framework is the model proposed by Dalton et al. (1977; hereafter called the DTP model). The DTP model consists of four distinct career stages among professionals and it has been used to examine the career development of professional workers over time (Carlson & Rotondo, 2001). In the apprentice stage, an individual works under the supervision of others, and the representative activities include learning, assisting others, and following directions. In the colleague stage, one demonstrates his or her capability to perform solid professional duties and to make independent contributions. In the mentor stage, one begins to get involved in work that can lead to the development of junior employees through training and interfacing. Finally, those individuals in the sponsor stage provide directions for the organization and have significant corporate power as they approach upper management level. It has been suggested that DTP is a useful tool to understand a professional worker’s career perception over the tenure of his or her career (Carlson & Rotondo, 2001). However, neither model serves to explain the movement from one stage to the next.

A different perspective of career influence is the career anchor – a multidimensional concept that represents a set of internal values that drive one’s career decisions. These self-perceptions come to guide and stabilize a person’s career because they tend to firmly attach an individual’s career to his or her underlying abilities, motives, and attitudes. As such, Schein’s (1985) career anchor measures have been widely used by researchers in representing the construct of career anchors. Schein’s early work (1978) suggests that five career anchors exist: (1) technical/functional competence, (2) general managerial competence, (3) security/stability, (4) autonomy/ independence, and (5) entrepreneurial creativity.

In light of the literature, the interaction of the individual and the organization over time reminds us that each person develops a cognitive style, a set of values and attitudes, and a set of skills that reflects their particular occupation and career history. For this reason, the long-range changes of career anchors must be embodied in the skills, values, and attitudes of the individuals (Schein, 1978). Success at career management is important because advancement (or lack of advancement) through the stages has a considerable impact on employee attitudes that may influence worker productivity and retention. These attitudes include organizational commitment, promotion stress, motivation, job satisfaction, and turnover intentions (Allen & Meyer, 1993; Peiperl et al., 2000; Carlson & Rotondo, 2001). On the other hand, career anchors are a set of career-related aspirations that an individual does not easily surrender even facing a difficult, major decision. From the perspective of human resource management, motivation theory maintains that employees will be most motivated by being rewarded with what they desire to acquire (Agarwal et al., 2007; Beecham et al., 2008).

Prior research reveals that patterns emerge in terms of the association between career anchor and some proxies related to career stage. For instance, Igbaria et al. (1995) found that age and tenure in a job are positively and significantly associated with both job- and geographicsecurity anchors. The same study also found that IS tenure is positively associated with the autonomy anchor. More recently, Igbaria et al. (1999) studied research, development and engineering (RD&E) professionals and found that age is positively and significantly associated with the managerial anchor and the job security anchor. However, Ginzberg & Baroudi (1988) believed that IS personnel have dual career paths in which dominant career anchors are either the managerial or technical anchor throughout their career life. These results are inconsistent enough to lead one to question if IS personnel in different career stages exhibit different pattern of career anchors (Agarwal & Ferratt, 2000; Chen et al., 2004).

Expectancy-value theory suggests that an individual’s choice and persistence can be explained by their expectations about how well they will do on selected tasks and the extent to which they value the traits of the tasks and their own performance of the tasks (Atkinson, 1957; Eccles et al., 1983; Wigfield, 1994). In this fashion, expectancies and individual valuation influence choice, performance, effort, and persistence. Expectancies and the way in which they are valued are shaped by individual perceptions of previous experiences and a variety of social influences. To follow the chain of logic, career and life experiences combine with social influences to alter an individual’s expectations and their valuation of outcomes, which parallel career anchors.

These revised expectations and evaluations lead to choices, including those about careers as they advance through career stages. However, unlike the literature that believes anchors change due to changes in careers, this reverses thought to individuals electing to change careers due to changes in their own expectations and how they value the newer tasks. For example, studies find that an individuals’ beliefs become more negative or positive in advanced life stages because they better understand evaluative feedback, engage with peers, and adjust to environmental changes (Wigfield, 1994; Stipek, 1996; Wigfield et al., 1996; Wigfield & Eccles, 2000). This unique perspective of causality will serve as the basis to investigate the research question of reasons for career changes and satisfaction associated with career anchors.

For simplification, only the first four career anchors are examined in career stage progress in this study. The inclusion of only four career anchors and the exclusion of the entrepreneurial creativity anchor have strong foundations. First, evidence has shown that companies adopt the model of dual career ladders, where employees involved in scientific work may consider not only a technical but also a managerial career path (Chen et al., 2004). For some professionals, career anchors are technical or functional in nature. Their primary concern in making job decisions and mapping their future careers involves the content of their work. For some other professional personnel, their career anchors might emphasize managerial competence. People in this latter group want to attain high-level management positions (Peiperl et al., 2000; Suutari & Taka, 2004). They like to analyze and solve difficult business problems, enjoy guiding others, or like exercising power.

Second, the importance of job security stems from the fact that it is related to a number of work-related outcomes. Research reveals that job security is significantly related to employee retention (Wong, 2007; Mgaya et al., 2009), employee turnover ( Joseph et al., 2007), job satisfaction (Lambert et al., 2001; Thatcher et al., 2002–2003; Chen et al., 2004), and organizational commitment (Iverson, 1996; Joseph et al., 2007). Those who are concerned with security and stability often enter a relatively large and stable company with long-term employment potential.

Third, some individuals emphasize autonomy and independence. The autonomy career anchor highlights an individual’s need to be free of constraints while seeking professional competence (Agarwal & Ferratt, 2002; Beecham et al., 2008). That is, some may want to have the freedom to make job-related decisions with no external constraints, and prefer to work at their own pace and set their own goals. The autonomy anchor is particularly relevant to IS personnel due to the high autonomy characteristic in IS jobs. In general, IS professionals seek autonomy in execution as well as unrestricted resources that will enable them to perform their jobs appropriately (Goldstein, 1989; Agarwal & Ferratt, 2002).

Finally, we find no compelling reason to consider that entrepreneurial creativity is highly relevant to IS personnel. Notably, Crepeau et al. (1992) investigated career anchors of IS personnel and found respondents do not exhibit a clear entrepreneurial creativity dimension. Though some later research studies did recognize the existence of the entrepreneurial creativity anchor, the results generally support a low importance of this anchor among IS personnel (Igbaria et al., 1999; Hsu et al., 2003). Therefore, we develop propositions related to the first four of the five career anchors.

## Methodology

In the absence of existing literatures for examining the career anchors at various career stage of IS personnel, we use a multiple case study approach to develop propositions (Strauss & Corbin, 1990; Yin, 1994; Pandit,

1996; Pare´, 2004). We started with three specifics to be explored: (1) understanding IS personnel career anchors in current and previous positions, (2) any change of career anchors during the timeframe, and (3) categorizing career anchors of our interviewees over time and root reasons for the changes. For this reason we limited the study to cases that made advancements in their careers in order to study the reasons for advancement. These cases admittedly exclude those who change anchors without advancement.

Since our objective was to explore the rationale of career anchors at various career stages for IS personnel, instead of asking them to choose from a set of options we use a grounded theory approach to ascertain reasons and their interrelationship to both change types (Strauss & Corbin, 1990). Deriving propositions and concepts from the data is a multi-step process and is outlined in Table 1 through various steps to the research (Pandit, 1996). Nine steps of grounded theory building were identified: review literature, select cases, develop data collection protocol, entering the field, data ordering, analyzing data relating to the first case, theoretical sampling, reaching closure, and literature comparison.

For the sample, cases should be selected according to the principle of theoretical sampling. Unlike the sampling done in quantitative investigations, theoretical sampling cannot be planned before embarking on a grounded theory study (Strauss & Corbin, 1990). The specific sampling decisions evolve during the research process itself. During initial data collection, when the main categories are emerging, ‘deep’ coverage of the data is necessary. Subsequently, theoretical sampling requires only collecting data on categories for the development of properties and propositions. The criterion for judging when to stop theoretical sampling is the category’s ‘theoretical saturation’ (Glaser & Strauss, 1967).

For this reason, our selection criterion for this study’s cases is IS personnel with over 10 years work experience and at least one change of job. The sampling frame had to offer enough similar aspects to exhibit comparable properties (Yin, 1994; Pare´, 2004). After contacting a number of potential interview candidates, we selected 10 IS personnel as our cases – enough to provide the data to achieve theoretical saturation. Data collection took place at the convenience of each interviewee. Table 2 summarizes the demographic data of the subjects. Table 3 shows the career path of each individual case. The primary data sources were semi-structured interviews (Myers, 1997). The interview teams consisted of two of the four authors. Interview protocols were developed and refined several times. The interviews were taped with agreement from participants. The semistructured interviews lasted on average 1 hour. The taped interviews were transcribed verbatim into text files. To understand career anchors changes through career stages, the following represent a sample of the questions that guided the interview process (follow up questions in parentheses):

\- How many years in your current job?

\- Do you want to leave? Why or Why not? (Is your desire to leave related to your family, health, colleagues, superiors or organization?)

\- Did you ever work for another company?

\- How many years did you work in each of those job(s)? (What was the job style in each company? Do you like any of those job styles? Why or Why not? Is that preference related to your professional interest or other reasons?)

\- What do you think are your most important career anchors currently? (Why? Is that related to your professional interests, family, personality, or other reasons?)

\- Do you think it is different from 10 years ago? 20 years ago? When you were an entrant? Why or why not? (Why do you think your career anchors changed through your career life?)

We also observed their physical work environments to be certain the current job matches the interviewees’ description. As an example, Case #4 stated that ‘in the hospital, one is always in a state of readiness and it makes you feel nervous and tired. I only want more available time to handle my family and my own health problems at this age’. The interviewer observed Case #4’s office climate and found that his phone always rang, and he always needed to be responding to the requests of customers, subordinates, and superiors. In this situation, the interviewer had to make another appointment outside of work hours because of the constant interruptions.

This list was refined after the first two interviews, and refined again after interviews were completed to reflect both the information gained from the interviews and the additional information of published research (Strauss & Corbin, 1990; Pare´, 2004). One researcher then coded the 10 interview files using the revised coding scheme, which provided not only structure but also flexibility for coding new or unexpected findings (Strauss & Corbin, 1990). For example, although there are many scholars asserting that the career anchors of professional workers will change through various career stages, none propose why the change occurs (Crepeau et al., 1992; Igbaria et al., 1999). In light of this, we add the category of career anchor change reasons and kept revising until all 10 cases were analyzed.

One starts from programmer or program analyst and then progresses through the position of senior programmer and systems analyst; senior systems analyst and project leader; first-line supervisor for systems, programming, or operations; system director; manager of programming; and finally IS director. They suggest that as one progresses along this career path, the importance of one’s managerial skills gradually outweigh his or her technical competence. Typically, as IS professionals progress along the career path, many move from simple technical tasks to more complex ones, from limited/local program responsibility to global system responsibility. More management duties (e.g., directing, overseeing, and leading management information systems (MIS) team/department/division members) are often required in higher career stages. Employees with strong managerial skills are thus more likely to be promoted and be given greater leadership responsibility (Chang, 2010). Indeed, professionals with a strong managerial career anchor often view their early technical positions as stepping stones to a managerial position (Peiperl et al., 2000; Chen et al., 2004; Suutari & Taka, 2004; Chang, 2010). As a result, IS personnel in their advanced/late career stage are more likely to report a stronger managerial anchor than those in their early career stage. Two interviewees stated:

Case #1: When I was an entrant I was also a sponge or a blank sheet of paper. Learning fundamental management skills was necessary to me. My career was to be improved and promoted gradually (toward management).

Case #3: When I was an entrant, in 1975, the most important thing to me was technical challenge; everything with IT, due to the application of computers at that time, was very new in Taiwan. Now managerial competence is important in my new position.

Thus, we propose:

P1: IS personnel in their late career stages will have a higher level of managerial career anchor than those in their early career stages.

To explore this pattern, we consider expectancy-value theory to surface reasons for this change to occur as opposed to merely progressing through a sequence of jobs, as held by previous researchers. According to some cases of this study, to be a manager in their late career stages is a chance to exhibit new ability in their organization; their managerial anchor has grown as has their ability. Meanwhile, they have opportunity to transfer their expertise and experience to their subordinates, showing how expectations and valuations change. One interviewee recalled:

Case #2: My career thoughts changed because my job changed and I needed to survive. You see, my colleagues had no motivation to improve their abilities and I had to be a leader to have the authority to push them and refresh their knowledge. Well, this was motivation to push me to change my career.

Therefore, managerial values increased in the recent career stage, and the managerial anchors became more important, consistent with other findings (Wigfield & Eccles, 2000). In addition, some cases indicate they emphasized the managerial anchor according to their job requirements that required contact with people, and they recognized that the technical anchor is not enough to achieve the goals of their job. Building interpersonal relationships with their project team members and users became important resulting in a change of expectations. One case stated:

Case #7: When I was an entrant, I believed technical competence the most important thing. My thoughts changed due to my job need to have contact with people.

I recognized that if only buttoned up by the technology without keeping good relationships with project members and users, it doesn’t work. It is the interpersonal relationship, the competence of management. Early I led a very successful project which confirmed the importance of those abilities. This early experience changed my thoughts about what to expect when emphasizing communication and relationships while leading a job.

## Technical career anchor

A number of studies revealed that the technical anchor was rated relatively lower than the managerial anchor (Agarwal & Ferratt, 2000; Chen et al., 2004; Beecham et al., 2008). Likewise, Igbaria et al. (1995) studied South African IS employees aged 30–44 years and found the same pattern (the mean score for technical anchor was 2.71 and the mean score for managerial anchor was 3.47). Interestingly, these results seem to suggest an imbalance in the popular dual ladder system. As many researchers pointed out, even when technical positions are designed to have the same level of prestige, salary, and status as managerial positions, the former lacks the vital ingredient of power and the technical promotions sometimes become a loyalty prize rather than true career advancement (Agarwal & Ferratt, 2002; Chen et al., 2004; Agarwal et al., 2007; Chang, 2010). Especially in the outsourcing, open-source community and internet-prevalent era, IS personnel may identify more with the entire community and align their goals accordingly, therefore, keeping stateof-the-art technical competence as a priority (Agarwal & Ferratt, 2000; Hars & Ou, 2001; Beecham et al., 2008). Because technical expertise is the base of the IS profession, however, we predict that most IS personnel will remain interested in being technical-oriented throughout their career, without any significant variation across career stages. Some of the cases recalled:

Case #1: Evan in my advanced jobs, I still need to keep learning about technology, although job stability is more important.

Case #5: I enjoy solving each problem in the technical domain. Keeping current in knowledge, addressing challenges, and experiencing variety is the most important to me. I can resolve all the technical problems by myself, and I like this kind of challenge.

Therefore, we posit that:

## P2: IS personnel will continue to hold high levels of technical career anchor throughout their career stages.

There are researchers who asserted that technical workers will change their technical anchor to managerial upon receiving a promotion (e.g., Igbaria et al., 1999). Others asserted that IS personnel have dual career paths due to having a dominant, permanent technical, or managerial anchor. However, in this study we found that IS personnel could both emphasize technical and managerial anchors in the late career stages – and other anchors described later may impact career movements as well. This proposition is due to technical competence being a fundamental competence of the IS personnel. If their technical expertise is out of date, they have no more value to the IS/IT domain and they might be replaced. Since the technical environment is always present and the employees value remaining current in the technical fields, we should not expect this anchor to disappear as careers advance since desires do not seem to change. Some interviewees stated:

Case #3: I got the master’s degree for learning the state of the art IS knowledge in my late career stage because I don’t want my IS expertise to drop behind that of my colleagues.

Case #5: I always expect to remain current in GIS expertise because I love to resolve every kind problem of GIS. I can solve problems by myself because I retain my interest.

Case #9: Learning new knowledge and keeping up my technical expertise is always an important aspect to all my jobs. Moreover, I enjoy learning new knowledge. It makes me happy.

## Security anchors

Security anchors highlight the importance of stable work status in the same organization, the same industry, or the same geographical area (Wong, 2007; Mgaya et al., 2009). Igbaria et al. (1995) found that middle-aged (30–44 years old) IS employees report an average score of 3.65 for the job security anchor and a mean score of 3.63 for the geographical security anchor, higher than the average managerial anchor and the average technical anchor. Chang (2010) asserted that the 40–49 age group of IS personnel has a higher geographical security anchor than the 20–39 age group. O’Bryan & Pick (1995) pointed out that a motivation program that neglects to offer job security often sentences the program to failure ( Joseph et al., 2007). That is, though job security has been found to be one of the most crucial components for older employees (Stanley, 2001), IS personnel in their early or mid-career stage are just as likely to report having high job security anchor as those in a later career stage. This may also be due to the linkage between job security and training opportunities where training signals the worth of an employee (O’Bryan & Pick, 1995; Agarwal & Ferratt, 2000; Mgaya et al., 2009).

With regard to the geographic security anchor, research has shown that an employee’s willingness to participate in relocation decreases as he/she moves along the career stages (Allen et al., 2009; Chang, 2010). For example, Slocum & Cron (1985) and Chang (2010) found that individuals in the trial stage (i.e., early career stage) are more willing to chase movement opportunities because of their interest in exploring different types of jobs and skills. Noe et al. (1988) found that employees in their earlier career stages tend to be more willing to consider job opportunities that require relocation. Smart (1998) examined career stages in Australian professional women, and found that women in the exploration stage (i.e., early career stage) are more willing to leave and to relocate compared to those in the establishment stage (i.e., midcareer stage). Previous studies in Taiwan also find the presence of this anchor among IS personnel (Chang, 2010). What could be said about geographical security in this study is that IS professionals gradually establish their geographic ties with the place where they reside, and they become less likely to appreciate a geographical transfer or relocation. Some cases said:

Case #3: All the reason for my turnover is due to finding a stable job. Job security is the most important thing to me. For this reason, I never change jobs after I started here. After I worked here 10 years, geographical security became more important to me.

Case #7: I stay because my current employer is a very stable company. I have experienced many kinds of job for this company, including: programmer, RD, pre-sales, customer after service, project pre-sales, project manager, and sales. I moved when I married so that my husband and I could take care of my mother-in-law.

Case #10: At the second job of mine, there were many things that seemed unreal, and would have some job problems in the long-term perspective. I felt that was not a stable environment for me, so I quit.

Therefore, we propose:

P3a: IS personnel will hold the same level of job security career anchor throughout their career stages.

P3b: IS personnel in their late career stage will hold a higher geographic security anchor than those in their mid-career stage and early career stage.

The cases of this study have increasing responsibility to family, including their parents, wife, and children. These represent changes that may impact security anchors as they progress through their careers. Changes appear more with geographic security, however, perhaps as family obligations increase. Some interviewees recalled:

Case #1: If a company could not give me an irreplaceable job, satisfy my economic needs, or has management problems, then I will go searching for another job because I have a responsibility to my family based on the stable income.

Case #8: After I graduated, I have changed jobs and both of them were short-term. Now I found a stable company that allows me to exhibit my abilities totally.

On the other hand, IS personnel hold higher geographic security anchor in their late career stages because of increased family requirements of parents and children. For this reason, they try to maintain a job close to their home. Some case statements:

Case #3: At an earlier job, it was an uncomfortable work environment. Not only was the company not stable. but I also needed to present to customers all around the island. Therefore, I discussed with my wife and decided to work for a government agency because it could satisfy both my job security and geographical security desires, and I don’t need to travel for work frequently. Meanwhile, I can take care of my family, always go to movies with my wife – it is a meaningful life.

Case #6: When I worked in Taipei, I was married and I must move to Kaohsiung to take care of my parents-in-law. Fortunately, I could transfer to a different job in the same company and work online. Marriage and family is very important because it is the source of much happiness. The geographic location of the company has top priority at my career age.

Therefore, family expectations and value placed on family change views of geographic security, but job security was always important to these individuals.

## Autonomy anchor

Schein (1996) noted that individuals anchored in autonomy more easily align themselves with ‘most organizational policies of promising only employability’ and this group of people may ‘become the role models for future career incumbents’ (p. 82). This is in line with Igbaria’s (1991) suggestion that jobs characterized by autonomy and challenging tasks are positively related to job performance (Beecham et al., 2008). For this reason, the younger workforce who need to prove themselves by obtaining high job performance evaluations may have decreased autonomy aspirations than those in the later career stages. Furthermore, ‘life style’ is considered to be part of the ‘autonomy/independence’ category (Schein, 1978). The cases in this study emphasize ‘life style’ in the middle or late career stage, not in the early career stages. Therefore, the elder workforce has relatively higher autonomy aspirations than those in the early career stages. Some of the cases recalled:

Case #2: After 13 years of working for my first organization, I was looking for a job with a chance to make decisions by myself. When the opportunity arose, I changed jobs and can now make decisions by myself. The achievement of this goal is still very important to me.

Case #4: My first position was very stable and routine which was good. Later I moved to have a chance to do something totally new by building an MIS department from the beginning. The challenge was very important to me at that later time.

Case #6: When I was younger, I lived for work and my job. But now, I feel that there is something more important than working all my life.

The above discussion leads us to believe that:

P4: IS personnel in their early career stage will hold a lower autonomy anchor than those in the middle or late career stage.

Most cases of this study assert that even if they have a management position in their organization, they still need obey the rules. They could not exert their management ability to do the job their own way, an expectancy of autonomy. Moreover, many cases believed that they should balance their job and lifestyle, showing traits of wanting more control in their lives. These changes were expressed in taking positions with higher independence. Some created their own enterprise, such as Case #2 and Case #8; some built an MIS department for a new organization (Case #4); some became consultants (Case #9 and Case #10); and others desired to balance their job and lifestyle, such as Case #6. Some supporting statements include:

Case #2: A previous position had a government environment with associated bureaucracy and class-consciousness. I had no authority to do what I thought best even though I was a manager. Therefore, I built my own company.

Case #8: As I worked I recognized I wanted to create my own business because it is the only chance to do what I want.

Case #9: When I came back from the U.S.A., I asked myself why I worked so hard. In the past I took all of the time to work and my life disappeared in the meantime.

Case #6: Lifestyle is more important now than when I was younger. My attitude and opinions changed through the years.

## Implications

IS personnel are responsible for the information systems and core data capability data of a company (Chang, 2009). Therefore, the turnover of IS personnel is costly and will affect IS productivity and quality, and an understanding of career change patterns will be helpful to management in any size organization ( Jiang & Klein, 1999–2000). In light of this, IS personnel should consider their career anchors when they take a job, but managers need to satisfy personnel anchors to reduce turnover rate. This does not contradict existing career paths within organizations, but should call for an examination of incentives and job descriptions for the variety of IS personnel at the stages in which they will reside. For managers, organizations must provide the appropriate opportunities to satisfy job anchors at each career stage, or risk losing employees. A more careful plan and set of opportunities should be effective in reducing turnover. Likewise, internal training may be better designed to match the changes in career goals as IS employees move through the organization. Senior IS personnel should be trained in executive decision-making skills before they reach the point where they are ready to shift stages. Employee tracking becomes more crucial to determine stage appropriate policies, and anchors may provide the means to help track the progress of an individual through organizational opportunities.

In view of this, career anchors that change over the careers of information system workers is new to the research arena, though others have speculated on the possibility. The cases studied indicate that career anchors of IS personnel appear stable in short term, but change over time. There is much research of career anchors of IS personnel in the past, but they did not focus on the career stage. This study shows change patterns, indicating that researchers should consider this aspect in future models. Such consideration might take the simple form of career stage as a control variable in the study of IS personnel’s career anchors, or lead to more complex models. Additionally, many cases expressed concern for commitment, organizational citizen behavior, and satisfaction, warranting further consideration.

Prior studies of IS personnel career anchors believed that IS personnel will transfer from a technical to managerial anchor in the later career stages and lead to a subsequent change in anchors. However, the present study found that the technical anchor of IS personnel is stable, instead the IS employees will develop new anchors in the middle and later career stages (autonomy, geographic security, management). IS personnel make these changes because of expectations and desires that shift over time. These shifts occur due to changes in the environment and social settings. As their expectations and values placed on traits shift, seemingly so do the anchors that drive their career desires, leading them to seek new jobs to fulfill the newer anchors of management competency, desire for geographic stability, and increased desire for autonomy.

## Concluding remarks

Career management should be viewed as a joint responsibility between the organization and the employees. Employees should consider these things when they take a job, but employers also have the responsibility to understand and resolve these problems with their employees. Communication between the organization and the IS personnel is crucial. As recruitment, development, and maintaining IS professional talent may impact a company’s performance, human resource management needs to understand IS employees’ career anchors at various career stages and act accordingly. Indeed, the human resource department may design reward systems based on the career anchors, thus normative data related to IS personnel’s career anchors at different career stages are important.

From our cases, we found that IS personnel will hold a higher level of managerial, autonomy, and geographic security anchors in their later career stages; they will emphasize technical and job security career anchors throughout their careers. They will develop new or change importance on anchors in the middle and late career stages. Four propositions were derived from interviews of ten IS professionals while considering changes to be the results of revised expectations and valuations. According to these findings, career anchors of IS personnel will at least partially evolve as the employee passes various career stages. Therefore. career stage could be a moderator effect between career anchors and turnover.
