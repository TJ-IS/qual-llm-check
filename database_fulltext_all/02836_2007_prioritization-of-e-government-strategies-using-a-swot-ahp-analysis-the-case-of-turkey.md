# Prioritization of e-Government strategies using a SWOT-AHP analysis: the case of Turkey

Cengiz Kahraman<sup>1</sup>, Nihan C¸ etin Demirel<sup>2</sup> and Tufan Demirel<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Department of Industrial Engineering, Istanbul Technical University, Istanbul, Turkey; <sup>2</sup>Department of Industrial Engineering, Yildiz Technical University, Istanbul, Turkey

## Abstract

The e-Government agenda is being pursued throughout the world to one degree or another, but it has added significance in Central Europe. The region is just beginning to emerge from a period of far-reaching political and economic transformation following the collapse of repressive communist systems. For these countries, e-Government is more than simply a new channel of delivering services; it offers an opportunity to achieve a quantum leap in transparency and efficiency of administration, which the region’s leaders have promised their citizens since the early 1990s. Turkey is currently working hard to catch this opportunity. Turkish Government has initiated the Urgent Action Plan in December 2002 to remedy long-lasting economic problems and to improve social and well-being of the country. One of the basic components of this plan is ‘e-Transformation Turkey Project’ which aims to carry Turkey into Information society. One of the two purposes of this study is to define and to prioritize the strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats (SWOT) groups and their subfactors for e-Government in Turkey. The other purpose is to determine and to evaluate the alternative strategies for e-Government applications at the national level in Turkey. We use the SWOT approach in combination with analytic hierarchy process (AHP) to achieve this task. The strategies have been prioritized and sensitivity analyses of the obtained results have been made using a software.

## Introduction

Digital technologies serve as a basic source of transformation in economies, communities, and government functions all over the world. The occurrence of technological change of the late 1990s, the result of which was the enabling of the delivery of services over the Internet, caused major and rapid transformation of governments’ functioning. Development of e-commerce and the evolution projected for the near future has encouraged consumers to demand more and more customized, rapid, and at home services. The rapid development of modern information and communication technologies is having far-reaching effects on all aspects of modern life, including government.

Academics have suggested various definitions for e-Government (Kaylor et al., 2001; Luling, 2001; Whitson & Davis, 2001). A common definition for e-Government from these is ‘implementing cost effective models for citizens, industry, federal employees, and other stakeholders to deliver government information and services online by utilizing the internet and the World-Wide-Web’.

Turkish Government has started ‘e-Transformation Turkey Project’ which aims to carry Turkey into Information society. Turkish Government has also initiated two plans to solve economic problems and to improve social and well-being of the country. These plans are Urgent Action Plan in December 2002, Short Term Action Plan (STAP) for 2003–2004. The details of these plans will be given in the following sections.

## Literature review on e-Government

Many works on e-Government have been published. Most of these works are on future development programs, and scenario planning, and some on strategic issues. Among these works, we can count Layne & Lee (2001), Chen & Gant (2001), Strejcek & Theil (2002), Gupta & Jana (2003), Cairns et al. (2004), Reddick (2004), Torres et al.’s (2005), Akman et al. (2002, 2005), Evans & Yen (2005), Ni & Ho (2005), Gil-Garcia & Pardo (2005), Moon & Norris (2005), and Mahrer & Krimmer (2005).

Carter & Be´langer (2005) integrates constructs from the Technology Acceptance Model, Diffusions of Innovation theory and web trust models to form a parsimonious yet comprehensive model of factors that influence citizen adoption of e-Government initiatives. The study is conducted by surveying a broad diversity of citizens at a community event. The findings indicate that perceived ease of use, compatibility, and trustworthiness are significant predictors of citizens’ intention to use an e-Government service.

Irani et al. (2005) seek to define the scope and role that information systems evaluation plays within the public sector. They assess whether public sector organizations might benefit from the use of established ex ante evaluation techniques, when applied to analyse the impact of e-Government information systems. This study emphasizes the importance of situated, interpretive user assessments in evaluating e-Government investments.

Gupta & Jana’s (2003) paper is directly relevant to our paper’s purpose. They suggested a flexible framework to choose an appropriate strategy to measure the tangible and intangible benefits of e-Government. In general, any approach to the evaluation of e-Government needs to have a few important characteristics including the ability for understanding and modeling complex problems, the ability to incorporate multiple views of the problem, and the ability to learn from mistakes.

The literature of e-Government offers few approaches, which have been found useful in selective evaluation. When a synthesis of the findings in the literature is made, it can be seen that most of the studies on e-Government are based on non-analytical approaches. However, some use statistical approaches like parametric tests. It can be concluded that more analytical approaches should be applied on e-Government in order to measure its worth in numerical values.

SWOT analysis is one of the most known approaches for systematic thinking and comprehensive diagnosis of factors relating to a new product, technology, management, or planning (Weihrich, 1982).

## SWOT analysis and literature review

SWOT, the acronym standing for Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, and Threats analysis, is a commonly used tool for analyzing internal and external environments in order to attain a systematic approach and support for a decision situation. The internal and external factors most important to the enterprise’s future are referred to as strategic factors and they are summarized within the SWOT analysis. The final goal of a strategic planning process, of which SWOT is an early stage, is to develop and adopt a strategy resulting in a good fit between internal and external factors. SWOT can also be used when strategy alternative emerges suddenly and the decision context relevant to it has to be analyzed (Hill & Westbrook, 1997; David, 2004).

One of the main limitations of this approach, however, is that the importance of each factor in decision-making cannot be measured quantitatively. As such it is difficult to assess which factor influences the strategic decision most. If it is used in combination with analytic hierarchy process (AHP), however, SWOT approach can provide a quantitative measure of importance of each factor on decision-making (Kurttila et al., 2000; Saaty & Vargas, 2001; Ananda & Herath, 2003). We prefer the multicriteria decision-making method, AHP since it enables decision makers to assign a relative priority to each factor through pair-wise comparisons. Shrestha et al. (2004) attempt to assess the effect of environmental, economic, and social factors related to silvopasture adoption decisions. They use SWOT approach in combination with AHP to achieve this task. The SWOT–AHP allows them to define silvopasture adoption decision process in a hierarchical structure of factors, evaluate factors in pairs, and quantify the relative importance of each factor to the adoption decision. Kurttila et al. (2000) examine a new hybrid method for improving the usability of SWOT. AHP and its eigenvalue calculation framework are integrated with SWOT analysis. AHP’s connection to SWOT yields analytically determined priorities for the factors included in SWOT analysis and makes them commensurable. The aim in applying the hybrid method is to improve the quantitative information basis of strategic planning processes.

SWOT analysis is traditionally a firm-level tool (Hackbarth & Kettinger, 2000; Martin, 2002). But it can be used for the larger levels, for example, at the national level, as long as you can determine the strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats of the problem at that level (Terrados et al., 2007).

The contribution of this paper Strategy evaluation for e-Government is problematic since there are several possible strategies and it requires a multicriteria decision-making analysis to choose the best ones. Government agencies are poorly equipped in terms of choosing appropriate strategies to fit their particular context. This paper proposes a multi-attribute decision-making-based SWOT analysis for the evaluation of alternative e-Government strategies for Turkey. The paper has two main aims: (1) to prioritize the determined main and subfactors of e-Government in Turkey, (2) to prioritize the determined e-Government strategies by a combined SWOT–AHP analysis. AHP is an approach to decisionmaking that involves structuring multiple choice criteria into a hierarchy, assessing the relative importance of these criteria, comparing alternatives for each criterion, and determining an overall ranking of the alternatives. The data were obtained by the questionnaires filled in by the experts who worked in Turkish Informatics Association (TIA) and Turkish Informatics Foundation (TIF).

The main contribution of this paper is to add a comparative analysis to the SWOT analysis on e-Government and to determine the priorities of the SWOT groups, their subfactors, and e-Government strategies. When the e-Government literature is reviewed, it is understood that a numerical tool for the evaluation of the criteria and the strategies is needed. The paper provides an analytical tool for the people who are responsible for the determination of e-Government strategies. The tool includes two well-known techniques, SWOT and AHP. Up to our literature survey, these techniques have not been used for this aim before. With the hierarchical SWOT matrix, e-Government strategies can be analytically prioritized by AHP. With this method, the sensitivity analysis helps us to view the effects of the changing parameters to the final decision.

## The steps of research methodology

The steps of our methodology in this paper can be counted as follows:

Step 1: Determine the main and subfactors of the strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats by using the reports published by the governmental organizations and obtain a hierarchical model.

Step 2: Calculate the priorities of the main and subfactors of SWOT using AHP and receiving the joint judgements of the experts who work in TIA and TIF.

Step 3: Make sensitivity analyses in order to observe the behavior of any subfactor by changing the weights of the main factors.

Step 4: Determine the alternative e-Government strategies taking care of the literature review and experts’ opinions.

Step 5. Calculate the priorities of the e-Government strategies using AHP, considering the same main and subfactors in the hierarchy.

Step 6: Make sensitivity analyses in order to observe the behavior of any alternative strategy by changing the weights of the main factors.

Strategies are systematic and long-term approaches to problems. Federal, state, and local governments are investing in the development of strategies to further their e-Government goals. These strategies are based on their knowledge of the field and the relevant resources available to them. Governments are communicating these strategies to practitioners through the use of practical guides. The guides provide direction to practitioners as they consider, make a case for, and implement IT initiatives. Gil-Garcia & Pardo (2005) present an analysis of a selected set of resources government practitioners use to guide their e-Government efforts.

## Process of Turkey’s development in e-Government

Since the beginning of 1990s, there is an increase in the efforts to transform into information society in most of the countries. Essentially, economic and social necessities bring about these efforts. Turkish Government has initiated the Urgent Action Plan in December 2002 to remedy long-lasting economic problems and to improve social and well-being of the country. One of the basic components of this plan is ‘e-Transformation Turkey Project’ which aims to carry Turkey into Information society. Some of the objectives of this Project are to facilitate participation of citizens to decision-making process; to enhance transparency and accountability for the public management; to promote ICT diffusion; and to co-ordinate e-Government investments by means of Information and communication technologies. The Minister of State and Deputy Prime Minister has the highlevel responsibility of the Project and the Project is coordinated by the State Planning Organization (SPO). In order to realize the objectives of this project and to ensure the success of the project, a new coordination unit, Information Society Department, within SPO was established. To increase the participation and the level of success, an Advisory Committee with 41 members has been established. This consulting body consists of the representatives of public institutions, non-profit organizations, and universities. The initial focal point in this project has been the STAP, which covers 2003–2004, for implementing specific tasks. e-Transformation Turkey Executive Board was also established with the same Circular that validates STAP. The Board is composed of the Minister of State and Deputy Prime Minister (e-minister), Minister of Industry and Trade, Minister of Transport, Undersecretary of SPO and Chief Advisor to the Prime Minister, and was given the responsibility of supervision of e-Transformation Turkey Project (Akman et al., 2002, 2005).

Akman et al. (2002, 2005) classify and report a list of current e-Government projects in Turkey. A classification based on the project characteristics is given as follows: Projects related to national IS and services; (1) Projects related to education, culture, youth, and sports; (2) Projects related to health, family, labor, and social affairs; (3) Projects related to finance and economics; (4) Projects related to interior affairs; (5) Projects related to justice affairs; (6) Projects related to agriculture, forestry, village, and environmental affairs; (7) Projects related to industry, technology, energy, and natural resources; (8) Projects related to communications, public work, tourism, and development and housing; and (9) Projects related to foreign affairs. From the details of the report one obtains that, majority of the projects are for processing information and hence devoted to services. The locomotives of these projects are 1, 3, and 8. However, considerable number of these systems is being developed for government-to-government (G2G) communications. Although these systems do not adopt government-to-citizens (G2C) approach entirely, the above classification provides evidence that these will constitute a sufficient base for G2C communication in the future in almost all areas of public affairs. Currently available e-Government services in Turkey are classified into three groups. Government to government (G2G) services: Those services are the ones enjoyed among public organizations electronically. Government to citizen (G2C) services: Those services are given by government organizations to citizens electronically. Government to business (G2B) services: Those services are given by government organizations to private sector.

## SWOT analysis

A scan of the internal and external environment is an important part of the strategic planning process. Environmental factors internal to the organization usually can be classified as strengths (S) or weaknesses (W), and those external to the organization can be classified as opportunities (O) or threats (T). Such an analysis of the strategic environment is called as a SWOT analysis. Figure 1 shows how SWOT analysis fits into an environment scan.

## A multi-attribute evaluation method: AHP

One of the first applications of the AHP was Saaty’s use of it to choose a school for his son (Saaty, 1980). The choice was to be made from among three possibilities: schools A, $\mathrm { B } ,$ and C. Important aspects of school quality were identified as learning, friends, school life, vocational training, college preparation, and music classes.

The AHP has been used in many different fields as a multi-attribute decision analysis tool with multiple alternatives and criteria (Kahraman et al., 2004; Yurdakul, 2004; Bertolini et al., 2006). AHP uses ‘pair-wise comparisons’ and matrix algebra to weigh criteria. The decision is made by using the derived weights of the evaluative criteria (Saaty, 1980).

After the hierarchy of the problem is constructed, the matrices of pair-wise comparisons (Eq. (1)) are obtained (Saaty, 1980). In this matrix, the element $a _ { i j } = 1 / a _ { i j }$ and thus, when $i = j , a _ { i j } = 1$ . The value of $w _ { i }$ may vary from 1 to 9, and 1/1 indicates equal importance while 9/1 indicates extreme or absolute importance.

$$
A = (a _ {i j}) = \left[ \begin{array}{c c c c} 1 & w _ {1} / w _ {2} & \dots & w _ {1} / w _ {n} \\ w _ {2} / w _ {1} & 1 & \dots & w _ {2} / w _ {n} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \dots & \vdots \\ w _ {n} / w _ {1} & w _ {n} / w _ {2} & \dots & 1 \end{array} \right]\tag{1}
$$

In the comparisons, some inconsistencies can be expected and accepted. When A contains inconsistencies, the estimated priorities can be obtained by using the matrix (Eq. (1)) as the input using the eigenvalue technique (Eq. (2)) (Saaty, 1980).

$$
(A - \lambda_ {\mathrm{max}} I) q = 0,\tag{2}
$$

where $\lambda _ { \operatorname* { m a x } }$ is the largest eigenfactor of matrix A of size n; q, is its correct eigenfactor; and I is the identity matrix of size n. The correct eigenfactor, q, constitutes the estimation of relative priorities. Each eigenfactor is scaled to sum up to one to obtain the priorities. Saaty (1977) demonstrated that $\lambda _ { \operatorname* { m a x } } = n$ is a necessary and sufficient condition for consistency. Inconsistency may arise when $\lambda _ { \operatorname* { m a x } }$ deviates from n due to inconsistent responses in pair-wise comparisons. Therefore, the matrix A should be tested for consistency using index, CI, has been constructed (Eq. (3)) (Saaty, 1980).

Figure 1 SWOT analysis framework.

$$
C I = (\lambda_ {\max} - n) / (n - 1)\tag{3}
$$

CI estimates the level of consistency with respect to a comparison matrix. Then, because CI is dependent on n, a consistency ratio CR is calculated, which is dependent of n (Eq. (4)) (Saaty, 1980).

$$
C R = C I / R I\tag{4}
$$

where CI is the consistency index; RI is random index (RI) generated for a random matrix of order $n ,$ and CR is consistency ratio (Saaty, 1993). The general rule is that CRp0.1 should be maintained for the matrix to be consistent. Otherwise, all or some of the comparisons must be repeated in order to resolve the inconsistencies of the pair-wise comparisons.

We constructed the hierarchy of the problem taking the SWOT analysis framework into account. The hierarchy in our case is composed of the goal, which is the selection of best strategy, the main factors, which are strengths (S), weaknesses (W), opportunities (O), and threats (T) and the subfactors, which are given in Figure 3, and finally the alternative strategies.

## The method for utilizing AHP in SWOT analysis

The idea in utilizing AHP within a SWOT framework is to systematically evaluate SWOT factors and commensurate their intensities. If it is used in combination with analytic hierarchy process, SWOT approach can provide a quantitative measure of importance of each factor on decisionmaking (Saaty & Vargas, 2001). The following four steps show how SWOT and AHP can be combined (Kurttila et al., 2000).

## Step 1: SWOT analysis is carried out.

The relevant factors of the external and internal environment are identified and included in SWOT analysis. When standard AHP is applied, it is recommended that the number of factors within a SWOT group should not exceed 10 because the number of pair-wise comparisons needed in the analysis increases rapidly (Saaty, 1980). Thus, the results of the comparisons are quantitative values expressing the priorities of the factors included in SWOT analysis.

Step 2: Pair-wise comparisons between SWOT factors are carried out within every SWOT group.

When making the comparisons, the questions at stake are: (1) which of the two factors compared is a greater (strength, opportunity, weakness, or threat); and (2) how much greater. With these comparisons as the input, the relative local priorities of the factors are computed using the eigenvalue method (described below). These priorities reflect the decision maker’s perception of the relative importance of the factors.

Step 3: Pair-wise comparisons are made between the four SWOT groups.

The factor with the highest local priority is chosen from each group to present the group. These four factors are then compared as in Step 2. These are the scaling factors of the four SWOT groups and they are used to calculate the global priorities of the independent factors within them. This is done by multiplying the factors’ local priorities (defined in Step 2) by the value of the corresponding scaling factor of the SWOT group. The global priorities of all the factors sum up to one.

Step 4: The results are utilized in the strategy formulation and evaluation process.

The contribution to the strategic planning process comes in the form of numerical values for the factors. New goals may be set, strategies defined and such implementations planned, taking into close consideration the foremost factors.

## SWOT analysis for e-Government in Turkey

In the following, the subfactors of the strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats for e-Government in Turkey have been determined by using the reports published by the governmental organizations. Among these, we can count e-Turkey report by Technology and Quality Management Commission, e-government: New suggestions for public services by State Planning Organization, and Information community and e-Turkey on the road of European Union by Turkish Industrialists’ and Businessmen’s Association. These subfactors have also been used in the literature (Akman et al. (2002, 2005)). These subfactors will be used in the prioritization of the e-Government strategies.

## Strengths

The three main strengths are determined as follows (Silcock, 2001; Akman et al., 2002, 2005).

Formation of supervisory and executive committees This is a strength since this formation will manage and direct e-Government activities and is a center responsible for any problem (Akman et al., 2002, 2005). The Prime Minister of Turkey made a declaration published in October 4, 2003, issue of the Official Gazette for the realization of short-term action plan (STAP) covering the years 2003 and 2004 for the e-Transformation project. The declaration specifies the tasks along with their priorities, the formation of the supervisory and executive committees, and the responsible organizations in charge of implementation. The Supervisory committee is headed by the Deputy Prime Minister and includes members from toplevel managements of public and private sector and the NGO representatives. The members of the Executive Committee are Deputy Prime Minister (Chair), Minister of Industry and Trade, Minister of Transportation, Undersecretary of State Planning Organization, and Head Advisor of Prime Minister. The representatives of nongovernmental organizations, Trade Chambers Union, Informatics Association of Turkey, TIF, The Association of Turkish IT Industrialists, and the head of Telecommunication Authority and the general manager of Turkish Telecom are also the members of this Committee. Some of the major achievements realized by the committee were the settlement of three laws, namely, e-Signature law, Knowledge Acquisition law, and Privacy law (Akman et al., 2002, 2005).

e-Transformation projects Turkish Government has started many e-Government projects as indicated in section ‘e-Government projects and services in Turkey’. These are G2G, G2C, and G2B projects. These projects are the main locomotives of e-Government in Turkey. Since almost all completed projects by now resulted in a success, we assume that the present projects are strength in our SWOT analysis.

Support from top-level management of public and private sectors This is a strength since the past experiences show that a project cannot result in a success unless the support from top-level management exist (Binbasioglu & Winston, 2003). Both public and private sectors support the e-Government projects. Many ministers from the government and many associations and foundations are the members of the e-Government committees.

## Weaknesses

The four main weaknesses are determined as follows.

Lack of access to Internet among certain sections of the population This is a weakness because of that when access to Internet among some sections of the population does not exist, the main tool of e-Government will not be provided. It is a particular problem for public sector organizations, as they cannot choose their customers. Indeed, many public services are provided specifically for vulnerable or low-income groups who are least likely to have access to the technology. The main consequence of this is that public sector organizations will have to continue to provide services through multiple channels at least in the short term to prevent excluding those who do not have access to the Internet (Akman et al., 2002, 2005).

Lack of finance for capital investment in new technologies In the U.S. and the U.K., a variety of sources of capital investment are available to the start-ups of new technologies; in the other European countries, these means are still uncommon so that the development of technology is often prevented (Giudici & Paleari, 2000). The reason is that IT was often not seen as a priority when competing for scare resources against other claims for capital investment for example new schools, roads, and so on.

Need to change common culture on electronic transaction at the national level It is a part of common cultural change issue at the national level. Another problem is with security and authentication that prevented the development of electronic transaction services. It is a specific problem with public sector organizations as the public generally saw them as being in a position of trust.

Poor economic power of citizens and businesses This weakness is different from the one in subsection ‘Lack of finance for capital investment in new technologies’ since citizens and businesses having poor economic power can not invest in new technologies even if the government’s preferences may be in favor of these technologies. While gross national product per person was U.S.\$ 3412 in 2003, it was U.S.\$ 4172 in 2004. This means U.S.\$ 348 per month for one person. It is clear that citizens having this level of economic power cannot imagine buying a computer and other softwares and hardwares.

## Opportunities

We determined five subfactors of opportunities for e-Government in Turkey. The first three are selected for the SWOT analysis since these are evaluated as the most important issues of e-Government in Turkey by the experts of TIA and TIF.

A candidate country for the European Union’s information society perspective As a candidate country, Turkey approved e-Europe þ action plan in 2001. This plan sets up priorities for an information society to comply with the European Union’s information society perspective. This action plan defines a layout to be adopted by each of the candidate countries by the end of 2003 (Akman et al., 2002, 2005).

An increase in efficiency of government business As with many information technology-related projects, one of the anticipated benefits is improved efficiency. In e-Government projects, this efficiency can take many forms. Some projects seek to reduce errors and improve consistency of outcomes by automating standardized tasks. Operational benefits from the introduction of Internet-based e-commerce, for government and public sector companies, include the reduction in paperwork, the provision of continuous service availability to customers, a reduction in response time, and a reduction in error rate. All of these factors contribute to the general increase in the efficiency of government business (Dearstyne, 2001; Seifert, 2003).

New and improved services Another opportunity promoted by e-Government supporters is the potential to improve the quality, range, and accessibility of services. Some observers suggest that, in addition to enhanced efficiency, the quality of services may be improved through quicker transactions, improved accountability, and better processes. The evolution of e-Government also creates the potential for new services. Along with the possibility of combining existing services, e-Government initiatives could contribute to a qualitative change in how government conducts business and how citizens interact with government and each other (Seifert, 2003).

Increased citizen participation A third benefit anticipated by some e-Government advocates is increased citizen participation in government. One way this could occur is by connecting people who live in remote areas of the country so that they can send and receive information more easily. A second way suggested by some observers is through increased participation in government by younger adults (Seifert, 2003).

Improved national information infrastructure A fourth possible benefit of the drive to implement e-Government initiatives is the improvement of the national information infrastructure (Seifert, 2003).

## Threats

The four main threats are determined as follows.

De-centralized Internet governance The emergence and development of the Internet technologies leads to a great leap in the technical and human ability to access, interpret, and use information. This subfactor is a global issue since it presages the advent of a national information infrastructure and a global information infrastructure in the coming information age (Kahin & Nesson, 1997). Various bodies and companies assert control over parts of the Internet and try to exercise it through technical and political means. This threatens the Internet’s stability and usability.

Copyright lawsuits Some people use the Internet to trade copyrighted works, particularly music, video, and software. Copyright owners object to this and attempt to discourage the practice through highly public legal action against participants. This subfactor is a global issue since it is a threat to public and national confidence (Kahin & Nesson, 1997).

Inadequate government IT security Poor safeguarding of personal information could damage the uptake of government services online. For example, on rare occasions personal records have been found at landfill sites, which have caused concern. If people are concerned about government security and about Internet security, they are doubly unlikely to use e-Government services.

Availability of ‘dangerous’ information Concerns are sometimes raised about the availability of ‘dangerous’ information, such as bomb-making recipes, on the Internet, and about disinformation or opinion being presented as fact. There is also a concern that information about infrastructure could be used by those seeking to threaten it. In the U.K. and the U.S., information about some government facilities and utility networks has been removed from the web in the last few years (Gauld & Goldfinch, 2006).

## Possible e-Government strategies

As Gil-Garcia & Pardo (2005) state, governments are investing in the development of strategies to further their e-Government goals. Possible e-Government strategies are the alternatives for the AHP problem above. The e-Government initiatives will deliver more and better services to citizens through more effective inter- and intra-governmental teamwork. The government will deliver these services at a lower cost, through the reduction of redundant systems and applications. The supervisory and executive committees, mentioned in the subsection ‘Formation of supervisory and executive committees’, determined the following possible e-Government strategies.

(A1) Simplify work processes to improve service to citizens at national level.

e-Government projects will be driving the migration of systems, data and processes to a common solution that better meets citizen needs. Agencies will be setting up solutions that cross traditional organization ‘silos’, based on e-business principles of ‘buy once, use many’ and ‘collect once, use many’.

(A2) Use the annual budget process and other requirements to support e-Government implementation.

There will be a continued consolidation of work plans and investments in technologies acquired by different agencies for like purposes, and external-facing transactions platforms. Agencies will continue to reduce redundant spending and improve the return on IT investments through the use of business cases, capital planning, investment and control process, and through other means, such as enterprise licensing.

(A3) Improve project delivery through development, recruitment and retention of a qualified IT workforce at national level.

Turkish government will support the efforts to analyze information resource management and personnel needs and assess and upgrade current IT training programs.

e-Government is no longer just an option but a necessity for countries aiming for better governance. People and policies play the primary role in making e-Government a success. The framework explained in this paper provides a direction about for consideration of the evaluation of e-Government strategies. The case study of Turkey provides an illustrative reference for the strategy evaluation. This model would be beneficial for evaluating any other national e-Government strategies and also comparing its priority with the other e-Government strategies.
