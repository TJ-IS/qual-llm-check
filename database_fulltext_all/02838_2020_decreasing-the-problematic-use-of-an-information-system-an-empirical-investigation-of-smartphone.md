## Abstract

Based on protection motivation theory, this study aims to identify factors that influence the intention to decrease problematic smartphone game use, including the effects of a gaming habit and subjective norms. The research model is tested with a scenario-based survey method. Players' perceived threat refers to the negative consequences (eg, reduced performance or social isolation) that are caused by problematic smartphone game use. The findings indicate that under high fear appeals, threat appraisal creates fearful emotions about suffering from the negative consequences, which further reduces the gaming habit and activates the intention to decrease use. Self-efficacy in decreasing game playing, the response efficacy of decreasing usage in avoiding the threat and subjective norms effectively promote users' intention to decrease use. Moreover, response costs reduce the intention to decrease use. The findings provide insights into how to activate users' intention to decrease problematic use, and the resulting implications are discussed.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Problematic smartphone game use has become an increasing concern with the proliferation of personal smartphone usage. Players are highly lost in a wide variety of smartphone games. For example, Candy Crush Saga, a popula smartphone game, has 500 million downloads on devices and 93 million daily players (Chen & Leung, 2016). Thes active players typically play the game at least 10 times per day (Kwon, So, Han, & Oh, 2016). In Western countries the number of addicted players of Candy Crush Saga has reached 15 million (Chen & Leung, 2016)

Following the definition of problematic information technology (IT) use (Jang & Ryu, 2016; Lee & Cheung, 2014; Lopez-Fernandez, Männikkö, Kääriäinen, Griffiths, & Kuss, 2018; Young & Rodgers, 1998), problematic smartphone game use denotes an impulse control disorder associated with dependent use of smartphone games, which causes negative consequences, such as social, work-related and academic problems. It is characterized by uncontrolled act with multiple negative consequences (Jang & Ryu, 2016). Widespread disturbing reports show that problematic us of smartphone games poses a significant threat to personal lives. Players have injured themselves, abandone housework and left their children stranded at school (Cheng Chen & Leung, 2016). Problematic game use leads to poor academic performance (Leung & Lee, 2012), social anxiety (Lo, Wang, & Fang, 2005) and a low quality of interpersonal relationships (Huang, Yang, & Chen, 2015). It has been found to harm players' health, resulting in sleep disturbances (Thomée, Härenstam, & Hagberg, 2011) and cognitive distortion (Li & Wang, 2013). Employees hav also been issued warning letters or dismissed due to their problematic use of a smartphone game named Pokémo Go (Wahab, Jamalludin, & Wok, 2017).

The negative consequences that result from problematic smartphone game use can be perceived as “threats” to players' lives. To avoid these threats, decreasing use has been considered an effective recovery strategy (Ning, Dhelim, Bouras, Khelloufi, & Ullah, 2018). In practice, measures to decrease players' certain usage have been actively sought. For example, Tencent has set up an anti-addiction system for the popular smartphone game “Honor of Kings” to limit individuals' playing time per day<sup>1</sup>. However, most players ignore the warnings and purchase goods for crack ing the system to continue playing the game<sup>2</sup>. Given the importance of decreasing use and the practical failure in controlling use, it is imperative to investigate the drivers of players' intention to decrease usage in assisting players recovery, which will contribute to the development of an effective recovery strategy.

Problematic smartphone game use is an emerging area in the information systems (IS) literature; however, th existing research predominately focuses on describing negative consequences and identifying predictors of problematic behaviours (eg, Jang & Ryu, 2016). As a result, despite growing interest from scholars, there is limited knowledg regarding these problematic behaviours, particularly recovery strategies. The drivers that prompt players to attempt to decrease their problematic use are largely unknown in the extant literature. Moreover, in the IS field, very few empirical studies have been conducted to understand how to decrease troublesome IT usage (Furneaux & Wade 2011). Thus, we expect that research on decreasing problematic smartphone game use can enrich the understandin of recovery strategies. The results are expected to provide new insights into the issue of decreased IT behaviour.

Decreasing the use of smartphone games is a protective behaviour that may enable players to prevent suffering from the threat of negative outcomes. Fundamental differences exist between decreasing behaviours (eg, those aimed at decreasing IT usage) and adoption behaviours (Elliot, 2006). As opposed to acceptance theories or models (eg, the technology acceptance model) used in previous research, a threat is considered essential for motivating user intention to decrease use because the ultimate goal is to avoid the threat (Liang & Xue, 2009) Bearing these points in mind, this study adopts protection motivation theory (PMT) rather than acceptance the ories for investigating individuals' intentions to decrease problematic smartphone game use. PMT is often regarded as the theoretical foundation that guides many extant studies on protection intentions and behaviours in the context of problematic substance use (egMilne, Sheeran, & Orbell, 2000, Pechmann, Zhao, Goldberg, &

Reibling, 2003, Xu & Chen, 2016). PMT details the threat and coping appraisals that individuals experience. These appraisals motivate individuals to undertake protective behaviours to minimize or avoid threats (Rogers & Prentice-Dunn, 1997).

Overall, we expect that this study will contribute to the IS field by using a well-established theory to elucidat the protective motivations for decreasing problematic behaviours. The article is organized as follows. We first present the theoretical background of this study. Then, we discuss the development of our research model and hypotheses. Next, we review our use of the survey method, followed by an overview of the data analysis and results Finally, we conclude our paper with discussions of the findings, implications, limitations and future research opportu nities derived from the study.

## 2 | THEORETICAL BACKGROUND

## 2.1 | Decreasing use

According to the rational addiction theory proposed by Becker and Murphy (1988), addicts can be rational and take long-term view in the sense that anticipated future consequences of current usage may trigger sensible regulation to achieve maximum lifetime utility. The rational addiction theory maintains that addicts decide on current usage with future utility in mind. It is a forward-looking paradigm, which is centred on the dynamics of current use in respons to anticipated future consequences (Kwon et al., 2016). Addiction is thus construed as a utility-maximizing and ratio nal behaviour (Kwon et al., 2016; Wang, Lee, & Hua, 2015). The essential aspect of the theory is that addicts weigh the future consequences of their actions. When they note the harm induced by their behaviours, they may adapt their current usage.

A recovery strategy has accordingly been proposed at different levels. Recovering at the physical level could b accomplished with physical exercises, rehabilitation programs and regular technology breaks. These approaches help individuals refrain from using their devices for regular periods of time to recover. At the social level, social recovery strategy centres on social support from family or friends. Cognitive behavioural therapy is regarded as an effective recovery strategy at the mental level (Ning et al., 2018). Promoting the reduction of using behaviours has been liste as a form of cognitive behavioural therapy (Kim, Han, Lee, & Renshaw, 2012). It is evident that decreasing use is an important approach in these recovery strategies and has also been widely indicated in previous research. For example, the consumption of tar and nicotine in cigarettes can be reduced by 45% and 34%, respectively, when addicts notice their harmful consequences (Ippolito, Murphy, & Sant, 1979). Verbeke and Viaene (2000) stated that con sumers who consider meat unhealthy or believe it contains harmful substances have more intentions to decrease their meat consumption, The recovery strategy in the context of alcohol is for effectively reducing hazardous alcoho use. An objective to decrease alcohol use is more likely to occur in heavy drinkers (Cook & Tauchen, 1982; Daeppen, Bertholet, Gmel, & Gaume, 2007). Hughes, Peters, Callas, Budney, and Livingston (2008) noted that decrease and cessation are both important recovery strategies for smoking, and decreasing consumption is treated as a key stag for final discontinuance. Furthermore, more participants indicate that they would like to decrease smoking, whic implies that attempts to decrease are more common than cessation (Hughes et al., 2008)

In the IS literature, Kwon et al. (2016) investigated excessive dependence on mobile social games and indicated that average users of mobile social games exhibit rational addictive behaviours. When excessive users of mobil social games are rational and forward-looking in regard to future consequences of their current usage, their weekl consumption of social games significantly reduces. Therefore, from the perspective of rational addiction theory, when smartphone game players perceive future harm of their problematic use, they may decrease their current usage. It is worth noting that because playing smartphone games also has positive aspects, such as playfulness, decreasing playing behaviours to a healthy level, as opposed to complete discontinuance, it is sufficient for avoiding the harm induced by problematic use, where discontinuance may be more necessary for problematic behaviours such as her oin, nicotine and other drug abuse. Turel, Romashkin, and Morrison (2016) proposed that to reduce or prevent problematic video game behaviours, recovery strategies, such as increased awareness of the risks of problematic video game playing, can be used to prompt users to control their own playing time. A study by Kim et al. (2012) is th first of the very limited IS research on treatments for problematic online game use. In their study, they used bupropion<sup>3</sup> and cognitive behavioural therapy to decrease online game playing time.

In non-IS fields (eg, psychopharmacology and clinical medicine), researchers have devoted studies to relevan factors for decreasing problematic behaviours. Bupropion has been demonstrated to be an effective treatment for substantially reducing excessive online game playing (Han & Renshaw, 2012; Nam, Bae, Kim, Hong, & Han, 2017; Song et al., 2016). Furthermore, Dell'Osso et al. (2008) reported that escitalopram<sup>4</sup> is effective at inducing a signifi cant reduction in the weekly hours spent online among individuals with impulsive-compulsive Internet usage disorder. A survey conducted in Brazil indicates that as parents become increasingly aware of the severe consequences of problematic game use, more and more parents are exerting efforts to reduce their children's playing time (Sioux, 2017; Spritzer & Kessler, 2018). Because decreasing game use is important for reducing the harm of problematic game use, IS researchers should put more effort into exploring how to motivate these players to reduce their usage.

## 2.2 | Protection motivation theory

PMT provides an important theoretical framework for the study of protective behaviours in social psychology. Its main contribution is the capacity to predict individual intentions to protect oneself from unwanted consequences after receiving fear-arousing information (Floyd, Prentice-Dunn, & Rogers, 2000). Fear appeal is a fundamenta assumption of PMT and denotes the use of persuasive messages about terrible consequences from unwanted behav iours; however, it also provides a path to establishing protective conduct (Boss, Galletta, Lowry, Moody, & Polak 2015). Fear appeals trigger threat appraisal and coping appraisal processes, which further shape the ensuing protec tion motivation (Bulgurcu, Cavusoglu, & Benbasat, 2010; Floyd et al., 2000).

According to PMT (Milne et al., 2000), threat appraisal refers to the process of evaluating individuals' perceived levels of a threat in response to a fear appeal. It is captured by the perceived severity of a consequence and the per ceived vulnerability of a threat, while it is also contrasted with perceived rewards from continuing to engage in a behaviour (Milne et al., 2000). Perceived severity assesses the degree to which individuals believe the consequences are serious and pertinent in their lives, while perceived vulnerability is an individual's belief in the likelihood of a threat occurring. Moreover, perceived reward refers to what needs the individual believes will be satisfied by maintaining, or even increasing, the existing behaviour (Milne et al., 2000). PMT indicates that perceived severity of, and vulnerability to, threats will increase protective intentions, whereas perceived reward will decrease protective intentions. For example, individuals' intentions to reduce cigarette consumption will be promoted when they perceive smoking causes cancer and other serious diseases (ie, perceived severity) and believe that they are more likely to con: tract the diseases (ie, perceived vulnerability). In contrast, if smokers believe, for example, that smoking brings feel ings of relaxation and happiness, their intention of smoking cessation will be reduced (Yan et al., 2014). Fear occurs when danger is perceived; thus, it is an affective response evoked by an assumed threat (Posey, Roberts, & Lowry, 2015). It plays an essential role in PMT that should not be overlooked (Johnston & Warkentin, 2010; Robert LaRose, Rifon, & Enbody, 2008) because fear is necessary for the communication of an effective fear appeal (Maddux & Rog ers, 1983).

Alongside threat appraisal, the coping appraisal process evaluates the relationship of a fear appeal to individuals assessments of their coping response to a threat. It encompasses response efficacy, self-efficacy and response cost. Response efficacy signifies the effectiveness of adaptive behaviours, while self-efficacy refers to personal beliefs about the ability to implement protective behaviours (Posey et al., 2015). Response cost refers to the losses that result from taking responsive actions (Posey et al., 2015). The likelihood of engaging in protective behaviours increases when the levels of response efficacy and self-efficacy are high, However, the perception of high response costs will weaken a protective motivation. For example, if individuals believe that (a) smoking cessation brings health benefits (ie, response efficacy), (b) they have the ability to quit smoking (ie, self-efficacy) and (c) the level of negative affection they suffer is low when they stop smoking (ie, response costs), their intention to quit smoking will be enhanced (Woon, Tan, & Low, 2005). The earliest version of PMT was proposed by Rogers (1975) in the field of preventive medicine to interpret health-related behaviours. In this version, fear appeals include components with a magnitude of noxiousness, probability of occurrence and efficacy of a recommended response. A fear appeal trigger an appraised threat severity, likelihood and belief in the efficacy of a coping response, which further activates inten tions to adopt the recommended response. A second version (Maddux & Rogers, 1983) incorporated self-efficacy and maladaptive rewards, and while this version acknowledged the idea of fear, it downplayed fear's role in PMT The third and latest version (Floyd et al., 2000; Rogers & Prentice-Dunn, 1997) reinstated the function of fear and treated it as an important mediator.

PMT has received validation in a diverse array of behaviours, such as medical treatments (Mesters, Meertens, Kok, & Parcel, 1994), environmental hazards (Weinstein, 1993) and safer sex behaviours (Tanner, Hunt, & Eppright, 1991). Predictions of PMT have also been confirmed in empirical findings within the context of problematic substance use (Milne et al., 2000). For example, Xu and Chen (2016) demonstrated that threat and coping appraisal predict a reduced tendency to smoke cigarettes. They supported the premise that PMT can be used for understand ing smoking cessation and prevention. Pechmann et al. (2003) used PMT to suggest that the content of antismoking advertising needs to enhance individual perceptions of the risk of smoking's severe social disapproval to increase nonsmoking intentions. Studies such as Yan et al. (2014), Sturges and Rogers (1996) and Macdonell et al. (2013) also treated PMT as a useful theoretical perspective for motivating smokers to reduce their daily cigarette consumption. Therefore, the value of PMT for predicting individuals' protection intentions regarding the dangers of substanc addiction is clear. Considering this, it may also be appropriate for IS scholars to adopt PMT in the emerging issue of decreasing problematic smartphone game use. In this study, the perceived threat severity refers to the degree of severity in negative consequences that players believe will occur in their lives, studies or at work due to their problematic smartphone game use. The perceived threat vulnerability refers to players' belief in the likelihood of these negative consequences occurring to them, and fear describes negative emotions associated with the anticipa tion of suffering from the consequences of problematic smartphone game use. When smartphone game users believe that the consequential harm (eg, social anxiety and academic problems) is serious or the harm will occur, their fea emotion can be activated. Perceived efficacy of decreasing use in protecting users from the negative consequences of problematic smartphone use, self-efficacy in decreasing use and fear emotion can help build intention to decreas their use. However, if users consider the cost of decreasing use to be high, their protection motivation will b diminished.

PMT has also been used in information security research to motivate individuals to engage in more secure behav iours (egBoss et al., 2015, Posey et al., 2015). Illicit monitoring, capturing, using sensitive information without the knowledge of a host and computer abuse<sup>5</sup> violate information security and privacy (Johnston & Warkentin, 2010; Lowry & Moody, 2015; Posey, Roberts, Lowry, & Hightower, 2014). PMT is thus used to identify how to encourage individuals to conduct protection behaviours. In addition to PMT's main constructs, existing research integrates wit other factors, such as security/safety habits. Appendix A lists several examples of information security studies that add habit to the PMT model. A security habit or habit of complying with IS security policies is considered to help activate protective intention or behaviour (egVance, Siponen, & Pahnila, 2012, Yoon, Hwang, & Kim, 2012). For example Yoon et al. (2012) showed that to prevent hacking, viruses and malware, a security habit has a positive impact on information security behaviours. Thus, researchers suggest that building a security habit is essential for protecting information security.

However, in contrast to information security violating behaviours, the problematic smartphone game use investi gated in this study is significantly characterized by the mismanagement of pursuing hedonic rewards. Individuals los control over playing activities and spend substantially more time than planned (Lee, Cheung, & Chan, 2014). The extant literature has widely recognized that the habit of using ITs can be an instrumental and mandatory prerequisite for the development of problematic IT use (eg, addiction) (Soror, Hammer, Steelman, Davis, & Limayem, 2015; Turel & Serenko, 2012; Xu & Tan, 2012). A strong habit results in an increased hypersensitivity to related stimuli. Conse quently, individuals may perceive a growing gap between expected and actual rewards, which, in turn, causes and strengthens problematic use to achieve the expected levels of rewards (Robinson & Berridge, 1993; Robinson & Berridge, 2003; Xu & Tan, 2012). In this case, a habit reflects repetitive and automatic counterproductive or harmfu behaviour and can be coined as a “bad” habit (Soror et al., 2015; Turel & Serenko, 2012). When individuals have attempted to avoid the harm caused by the problematic use of hedonic IT, a habit is termed a key inhibitor of taking protective actions (Turel, 2014). Therefore, this study suggests that smartphone game users' intention to decrease use will be inhibited by their gaming habits. Gaming habit here is an inhibitor of protection intention rather than a facilitator described in the information security literature.

Many recovery strategies of health behaviour change based on models similar to PMT overemphasize conscious and deliberate planning, while failing to consider the role of habit; thus, the recovery strategies often fail (de Bruijn & Rhodes, 2011; Nilsen, Bourne, & Verplanken, 2008). A bad habit is treated as an inhibitor of behavioura change (eg, smoking) (Webb, Sheeran, & Luszczynska, 2009). Thus, research has suggested that a more complet model of behaviour change should have better understandings of habits, as well as how to break the undesired unsafe habits (Nilsen et al., 2008). The literature on health behaviour change has explored approaches to reduce bad habits; however, few of these articles are empirical studies. Research has shown that reasons to quit a “bad habit” typically relate to negative outcomes caused in the long term (Jager, 2003). Providing direct and clear information on negative (long-run) consequences has been considered a recovery strategy of changing bad habits (Jager, 2003; Verplanken & Wood. 2006), However, this information is processed by individuals at a cognitive level and thus can: not have salient strong effects on habits (Jager, 2003). Hollis, Konrad, and Whittaker (2015) also contended that behaviour-change technology that only emphasizes cognitive factors often fails to maintain a long-term change and indicated that emotional factors play a crucial role in reducing bad habits and preventable behaviours (eg, smoking, overeating and alcohol abuse). Based on this concern, it is important to identify the mechanism that activates individuals to change their risky habits and recover back. This study thus takes into account the role of the gaming habit with PMT and proposes that cognitions about threat may break the gaming habit by activating fear emotion. Thi study is believed to be one of the first studies to investigate how to disrupt unhealthy habits to reduce the resistanc to protective behaviours in the IS field.

In addition to the cost-benefit analyses that PMT entails, the decision to decrease use can also be influenced by subjective norms regarding problematic behaviours. Subjective norms stress the influence of the expectations of individuals deemed important by a subject (Cheung, Chiu, & Lee, 2011). According to previous research (eg Mohamed & Ahmad 2012) threat avoidance behaviour of IT users is subiect to the effects of their social environments. Individuals learn and behave by following social rules and norms. The rationale for the influence of subjective norms is that individuals may be sufficiently motivated to implement a behaviour if they believe individuals significant to them think they should adopt that behaviour, even when subjects do not see that action as favourable or beneficial to themselves (Venkatesh & Davis, 2000). Subjective norms are not part of a cost-benefit analysis; however, they can play an independent role in activating protection behaviours (Li, Zhang, & Sarathy, 2010; Paternoster & Simpson, 1996; Sommestad, Karlzén, & Hallberg, 2015). Previous research has demonstrated that subjective norms exert powerful impacts on protective behaviours, such as the cessation of substance abuse (eg, smoking) (Ho, 1998) and intention to quit online games (Wang & Yan, 2015). The influence from their family or friends has been found to help addicts recover (Ning et al., 2018). Thus, extending PMT with social dimensions can strengthen the utility of PMT in relation to problematic use. In thi study, smartphone game players may refrain from problematic use simply because they believe other important individuals think they should do so. Overall, we expect that this study will contribute to the IS field by using a well-established theory along with gaming habit and subjective norms to elucidate the protection motivation for decreasing problematic smartphone use.

## 3 | RESEARCH MODEL AND HYPOTHESES

Drawing on the literature review, we develop our research model to extrapolate several key determinants of inten tion to decrease use (Figure 1). Problematic smartphone game use has emerged as a noteworthy concern that cause serious damages (Jang & Ryu, 2016). Studies have suggested that it can lead to health problems (Thomée et al., 2011), social anxiety (Lo et al., 2005), cognitive distortion (Li & Wang, 2013), poor academic performance (Leung & Lee, 2012) and many other serious consequences. These negative consequences can be termed “threats.” The likeli hood and severity of negative consequences of problematic smartphone game use are treated as important compo nents in the threat appraisal process. The perceived threat severity reflects the severity level of negativ consequences caused by individuals' problematic smartphone game use. The perceived threat vulnerability concern the probability that individuals will suffer from the consequences caused by their problematic smartphone game use We also follow prior studies (Floyd et al., 2000; Rogers & Prentice-Dunn, 1997) in modelling fear of suffering from undesirable consequences as an important factor between perceived threat and intention to decrease use and delv into the role of fear to observe whether it is salient in our context. Response efficacy of decreasing usage to avoid threats, along with self-efficacy in and response costs from engaging in decreasing usage, is considered in the coping appraisal process. Moreover, an inhibitor of protection intention (ie, gaming habit) and external pressure from other individuals on smartphone game players to decrease game use (ie, subjective norms) are further considered in our research model<sup>6</sup>.

## 3.1 | The effect of threat appraisals

According to PMT, a threat appraisal activates a fearful emotion (Boss et al., 2015). Inevitable events that inflict dis comfort often make individuals become scared, nervous and upset (Rogers & Prentice-Dunn, 1997), and when indi viduals assess a high threat severity along with vulnerability to the threat, they often feel afraid (Rogers, 1975). Th negative emotion of fear has been shown to be an outcome when individuals perceive threats (Shin & Liberzon 2009). Prior research in social psychology has indicated that perceptions about the severity of and vulnerability to a threat are useful predictors of fear (Floyd et al., 2000; Rogers & Prentice-Dunn, 1997). A study by Herath and Rao (2009) confirmed that fear is established as a response to dangerous situations. More recently, Boss et al. (2015) indicated that a perception of high severity in and vulnerability to a threat induced fear in subsamples exposed to high o low fear appeals regarding the use of anti-malware. Thus, this study proposes that if problematic players perceiv high levels of severity and vulnerability to a threat of negative consequences from playing smartphone games, the are more likely to fear suffering from such consequences. Therefore, we propose our first two hypotheses:

H1a. : Perceived threat severity positively influences fear.

H1b. : Perceived threat vulnerability positively influences fear.

## F I G U R E 1 Research model

PMT has demonstrated that fear influences individuals to seriously engage in protective behaviours (Rogers & Prentice-Dunn, 1997). Fear has been indicated to be a key and necessary component in the cognitive appraisal process (Eppright, Hunt, Tanner, & Franke, 2003; Tanner et al., 1991). In the later version of PMT (Floyd et al., 2000; Rogers & Prentice-Dunn, 1997), fear is situated as a mediator between threat and protection motivation, where it mediation is vital for increasing the intention to implement protective behaviours. Boss et al. (2015) and Posey et al (2015) also empirically demonstrated the direct and mediating influence of fear on protection motivation in the con text of information security. Therefore, we propose that when problematic smartphone game players fear the threat caused by their problematic use, they are more likely to develop their intention to decrease their smartphone gam use. Threat appraisal about the consequences of problematic use triggers intention to decrease use by activating th emotion of fear, leading to our subsequent hypotheses:

H2a. : Fear positively influences intention to decrease use.

H2b. : Fear acts as a mediator between threat and intention to decrease use.

As opposed to the factors that promote protective actions, habit has been treated as an important inhibitor of protective motivation (Turel, 2014). Therefore, it is important to kick harmful habits, and Reser and Swim (2011 showed that the cognitive appraisal process in PMT motivates behavioural responses, such as breaking habits Previous research develops a habit-breaking strategy to achieve a long-term reduction in race bias (Devine, Forscher Austin, & Cox, 2012). In the strategy, individuals are made aware of their biases and become concerned about th harmful consequences of these biases, and within these conditions, the threat appraisal process is useful for changin habits. Similarly, in the context of problematic smoking, Onyechi et al. (2017) proposed a group-focused cognitive behavioural health education program (GCBHEP) to break smoking habits. A GCBHEP attempts to restructure smokers' thought patterns, making them realize that smoking ruins their lives (ie, threat). It enables individuals to notice the reality surrounding smoking habits. The problematic belief about smoking is thus modified. Elliott and Tighe (1968) indicated the effect of threatened loss of money on breaking a cigarette habit and found that the habit is reduced when threat exists. They also indicated other effective approaches, including reading articles on smoking's health hazards, fear of ill-health and fear of losing money. They highlighted the imperative role of fear-inducing motives, such as smoking-related emphysema, cancers or diseased lungs, which have also been affirmed b Leventhal, Watts, and Pagano (1967). Moreover, individuals substantially differ in what is perceived to be threaten ing. When a threat stimulus is salient, individuals experience fear. Thus, fear can be used to assess the effectivenes of threats (Boss et al., 2015). Prior research recognizes fear as a mediator between threat and response (Boss et al. 2015). Considering these elements, we propose that players' fear of suffering the consequences of problemati smartphone game use mediates the influence of threat on their gaming habits. This is also consistent with the stud of Janis and Mann (1965), which investigated the effectiveness of fear in modifying smoking habits. In their experi ment, individuals are asked to play a role of lung cancer patients who receive bad news from physicians. After receiv ing negative information, high levels of fear appear to be the factor responsible for the change in the smoking habit.

H3a. : Fear acts as a mediator between perceived threat severity and gaming habit

H3b. : Fear acts as a mediator between perceived threat vulnerability and gaming habit.

## 3.2 | Effects of gaming habit

In this study, we treat gaming habit as an inhibitor of intention to decrease smartphone game use. Habit has bee recognized to be an important driver of problematic IT use (Ouellette & Wood, 1998) and is instrumental in individ uals underestimating the long-term consequences of their behaviours while simultaneously overemphasizing the current short-term thrills (Turel & Serenko, 2012). That is, the brain becomes more sensitive to using the stimulus (Robinson & Berridge, 2003). Problematic use means that behaviours are often executed repetitively in a habitual or routine manner (Yoon et al., 2012). Bandura (1998, 2004) further argued that discontinuance intentions can be damp ened by hedonic IS usage habits because a habit numbs cognitive considerations and alleviates thinking about the consequences of behaviours (Ouellette & Wood, 1998). Limayem, Hirt and Cheung (2007, p.733) stated that “if trig gered automatically, IS usage is also less prone to discontinuance.” Turel (2014) claimed that a stronger habit of hedonic IS usage is associated with a lower perceived value of discontinuance. LaRose, Lai, Lange, Love, and Wu (2005) showed that habit reduces the intention to stop illegally downloading music. Breaking a prejudice habit is found to be an effective strategy to decrease implicit race bias (Devine et al., 2012). In the context of cigarette use kicking the nicotine habit is considered to be important to help individuals stop smoking (egCummings, 1982, Fletcher, 1985, Werther & Dayal, 1988). Taniguchi and Fujii (2007) indicated that a habit prevents individuals from forming behavioural intention for behaviour change. Compared with smokers who do not try to quit smoking, individ uals who have made such an attempt show lower levels of habit (Goérecka et al., 2003). Therefore, in this study, if smartphone game players have more ingrained gaming habits, their intention to decrease problematic game use may be inhibited, leading to our next hypothesis:

H4. : Gaming habit negatively influences intention to decrease use.

## 3.3 | Effects of coping appraisals

PMT indicates that the coping appraisal process influences protective motivation. As previously noted in Section 2.2, coping appraisal includes the elements of self-efficacy, response efficacy and response costs (Boss et al., 2015), where self-efficacy refers to the degree to which individuals believe they can successfully execute a protective response to avert a threat (Maddux & Rogers, 1983). In this study, self-efficacy denotes smartphone game players perception of their ability in decreasing their game-playing behaviours. Maddux and Rogers (1983) indicated th direct effects of self-efficacy on intentions. Self-efficacy has been found to significantly predict behavioural intentions (de Vries, Dijkstra, & Kuhlman, 1988). The influence of self-efficacy on protection intention appears in diverse behavioural contexts. For example, Lee and Kozar (2005) found that when employees are more confident about thei ability to use anti-plagiarism software, they may show higher intentions to use this system to protect information security. The underlying explanation may be that if individuals have greater confidence in their ability to do some thing such as implementing a protective behaviour, they feel that the action is not difficult. They then have more motivations to apply the action (Bandura, 1977). Self-efficacy is crucial to the avoidance of threatening situations (Prentice-Dunn & Rogers, 1986). Landrum Sterling et al. (2007) stated that youth smokers who have low confidence in their abilities to avoid smoking are more likely to have intentions to continue smoking. Thus, we propose that when smartphone game players have higher perceptions of high levels of self-efficacy in regard to decreasing thei usage, they are more likely to have greater intention to decrease their use.

H5a. : Self-efficacy positively influences intention to decrease use.

Response efficacy, another variable in the coping appraisal, describes the degree to which individuals believe that the protective response will be effective (Maddux & Rogers, 1983). In this article, response efficacy refers to the per ceived effective levels of decreasing use in avoiding negative consequences of problematic smartphone game use. A PMT indicates, a positive coping appraisal requires a certain level of response efficacy (Floyd et al., 2000). Prior research has demonstrated the vital role of response efficacy in forming protective motivations (Block & Keller 1995; Boss et al., 2015; Herath & Rao, 2009; Posey et al., 2015; Wolf, Gregory, & Stephan, 1986). Similarly, Greening (1997) indicated that the likelihood of an adaptive response (ie, stop smoking) will be low if the response efficacy is perceived to be low. Individuals' intention to protect themselves from harm can be enhanced by th response efficacy of advocated behaviours (Pechmann et al., 2003; Rogers, 1983). Therefore, when smartphon game players perceive high levels of response efficacy in decreasing their usage to avoid the harm of problematic game use, they are more likely to have the intention to decrease use.

H5b. : Response efficacy positively influences intention to decrease use.

Response costs refer to expenses, whether monetary or nonmonetary (eg, difficulty or unpleasantness), associ ated with executing protective actions (Lee, 2011). In this study, it means that players perceive expenses in decreas ing playing smartphone games. For example, players may experience negative emotions, such as anxiety and unhappiness, when they reduce their playing time. As a component of the coping appraisal, response costs signifi cantly influence protective motivations (Boss et al., 2015). Individuals may hesitate or refuse to adopt protectiv behaviours if they perceive high requirements for considerable effort (Peace, Galletta, & Thong, 2003) or negativ emotions (Milne et al., 2000) in taking protective actions. Minimizing response costs can increase the chances of indi viduals adopting a recommended behaviour (Woon et al., 2005). Lee (2011) indicated that high response cost decrease faculty members' intentions to use anti-plagiarism software. The negative link between response costs and intentions to perform protective actions (eg, take security measures) has also been confirmed in other prior researc (egHsu & Kuo, 2003, Lee & Larsen, 2009, Workman, Bommer, & Straub, 2008). Campis, Prentice-Dunn, and Lyman (1989) adopted a PMT analysis and found that a high response cost significantly reduces intention to obtain a booklet and inform their children about sexual abuse. Floyd et al. (2000) also reported a meta-analysis of PMT research with regard to more than 20 health issues. They showed that decreases in response cost will increase adaptive intentions Therefore, we expect that if smartphone game players perceive high levels of response costs for decreasing tim spent playing games on their smartphones, their intention to decrease use may be weakened.

H5c. : Response costs negatively influence intention to decrease use.

## 3.4 | Effects of subjective norms

Subjective norms address the function of referent individuals or groups, and it has been proposed that individuals ar motivated to implement behaviours that are approved by the referents (Yoon et al., 2012). Prior research on healt protection demonstrates the powerful influence of social pressure on protective behaviours (Ho, 1998). The rational for the effect of subjective norms on behavioural intention is that individuals willingly perform recommended behav iours that are suggested by important referents even if they are not personally favourably inclined to the behaviou (Venkatesh & Davis, 2000). In the IS field, research has confirmed the impact of subjective norms on the implementa tion of protective behaviours. For example, Johnston and Warkentin (2010) showed that if subjects believe that important individuals think they should use anti-spyware software, they are more likely to have the intention to tak recommended computer security actions. Similarly, a study by Lee and Larsen (2009) indicated that social pressure from referents regarding adoption of anti-malware software significantly increases the intention of professionals in IT and IS to use the programs. Yoon et al. (2012) found that subjective norms activate young adults' intentions to imple ment information security practices. Turel (2016) showed that subjective norms regarding discontinuance increase intentions to discontinue the use of social networking sites. In the context of smoking, individuals who have greater awareness of subjective norms tend to have higher intentions to quit smoking (Droomers, Schrijvers, & Mackenbach, 2004). de Vries et al. (1988) also achieved consistent results and demonstrated that subjective norms predict intentions not to smoke. From this perspective, we expect that when smartphone game players perceive high levels of subjective norms, they are more likely to decrease their smartphone game usage.

H6. : Subjective norms positively influence intention to decrease use.

## 4 | METHODOLOGY

We used scenario-based surveys to validate our research model. To generate threat and coping appraisals, it is necessary to first put forth a fear appeal. Fear appeal is the message that triggers threat and coping appraisal. It not only increases threat but also increases perceptions of efficacy by providing a solving approach. Therefore, two scenario were developed to manipulate fear appeals, ranked as high fear appeal or low fear appeal. Each respondent was ran domly presented with one of the two scenarios. Participants in the high fear appeal group received more numerous and more explicit messages about the severe harm that problematic smartphone game use could cause in their lives such as sudden death, traffic accidents and violent crime, and they were also given approaches for avoiding the nega tive consequences. The messages were presented in the forms of texts and pictures. Participants in the low fea appeal group received only minimal messages, in the form of texts, regarding the importance of decreasing usage Two scenarios were reproduced in Appendix C. Boss et al. (2015) indicated that research models in which individuals with and without fear appeal manipulation are analysed together in one model can lead to convoluted results. Such model cannot recognize the key differences between effective and ineffective threat/coping appraisals, which ar the core assumptions of PMT. Thus, modelling recipients of weak and strong fear appeals separately is consistent with the tenets of PMT, which highlights that only the high fear appeal group will properly engage in threat appraisa in an adaptive manner: processing a useful degree of fear and threat, which can also kick off a useful coping appraisa process. We then conducted a pretest to ensure that these two scenarios could create high and low fear appeals. I the pretest, we invited participants to answer the questionnaire. The numbers of respondents were 29 and 19 in th high and low fear appeal groups, respectively. We determined that the high fear appeal sample consistently had higher scores for the main PMT constructs in both threat and coping appraisals than the low fear appeal sample (refe to Appendix D). Therefore, the scenarios could send out messages in two different levels of fear appeals.

We believed that it is appropriate to conduct the survey among Chinese smartphone game players because the phenomenon of problematic smartphone game use has been reported to be severe in China (Wu, Lei, & Ku, 2013). To improve the sample quality and the response rate, we secured the services of the market research firm Sojump to carry out the survey in China, and participants were recruited through invitation messages that contained the URL for the online questionnaire. Because the survey was conducted in China, the processes of translation and back translation were performed to ensure the translation quality. More specifically, we first translated the original English version of the questionnaire into a Chinese version, which was then translated back into English, and the two Englis versions were compared for inconsistencies to be resolved (Chen et al., 2017)

We followed the guidance provided by Lowry, D'Arcy, Hammer, and Moody (2016) on collecting online pane data to improve our data quality. According to their framework, the chosen context of data collection was appropri ate for using an online survey. Our topic is one of general interest to individuals who do not need special expertise and we could collect the data with assurances of anonymity. It is also convenient for reaching large groups of individ uals, likely including numerous players with excessive game use. We also applied information from Lee et al.'s (2015 study for measuring problematic smartphone game use. The construct of problematic smartphone game use was measured as a second order construct. More specifically, the score of problematic smartphone game use was calcu lated by SmartPLS. Because we used a 7-point Likert scale (from 1: strongly disagree to 7: strongly agree), if respon dents scored higher than 4, it indicated a pattern of troublesome game playing. Thus, we deleted the questionnaires with low scores (<5) for the construct of problematic use<sup>7</sup> to ensure that the samples were problematic users. Appendix E shows the scores and distributions of problematic smartphone game use in the two groups. Responses that did not achieve these scores were dropped. The numbers of dropped responses were 32 and 43 in the high and low fea appeal groups, respectively.

To increase the respondents' attention and honesty, several procedural remedies suggested in prior research (Lowry et al., 2016) were implemented. For example, the order of the questions was randomized. We explained th scientific importance of the survey and the need to pay attention, and the respondents were instructed to confirm their honesty in answering. Furthermore, Sojump helped to ensure completeness by enabling submission of onl those responses that had been completed in full. Responses that were answered within an unreasonably fast tim frame or that originated from the same IP address as other responses were also removed. Ultimately, we collecte 420 valid responses under the high fear appeal condition and 494 valid responses under the low fear appeal condi tion. The demographic profiles of the respondents in the two groups are presented in Table 1.

The measures we used were adapted from previous studies, with minor modifications to fit our context. Th items used 7-point Likert scales from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (7). The detailed measurements are listed in Appendix F.

Fear appeal manipulation was tested. The direct effects produced by fear appeal were compared (shown in Appendix G). The manipulation check indicated that the manipulation of fear appeal was statistically significant and in the correct direction. The high fear appeal subsample showed higher scores for the constructs of threat severity vulnerability, fear, self-efficacy and response efficacy. Only response costs did not exhibit noticeable differences Thus, fear appeal manipulation successfully influenced threat and coping.

In the structural model, we controlled for the impacts of demographic variables, such as age, gender, job and playing game time per day, on gaming habit and intention to decrease use. We assessed the structural models for both the high and low fear appeal groups and compared the results. Table 3 provides the results of these structural models. In the high fear appeal group, perceived severity ( =0.21, P<0.001) and perceived vulnerability (β=0.35, P<0.001), both showed significant effects on fear. In addition, fear (β=0.33, P<0.001), self-efficacy (β=0.20, P<0.001), response efficacy (β=0.11, P<0.05), response costs (β=−0.15 P<0.01), subjective norms (β=0.34, P<0.001) and gaming habit (β=−0.13, P<0.05) were demonstrated to predict inten tion to decrease use. We further assessed the mediating effects of fear by using a bootstrapping approach, which involved the evalua tion of three paths (Vance, Lowry, & Eggett, 2015). Path a is from the independent variable to the mediator, path b is from the mediator to the dependent variable, and path c is from the independent to dependent variables (when considered simultaneously with paths a and $b ,$ it is marked as path $c ^ { \prime } ) ;$ ab refers to the multiplication of the coefficient of paths a and b. If the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for ab do not include zero, the indirect effect is significant which indicates that a mediating effect exists. If the CI for $c ^ { \prime }$ includes zero, it provides evidence of a full mediation. If the CI for $c ^ { \prime }$ does not include zero, it would indicate a partial mediation (Shrout & Bolger, 2002; Vance et al., 2015). The results shown in Appendix J confirmed the mediating role of fear, which fully mediated the influences of perceived severity and vulnerability on habit. Fear was also a full mediator between perceived vulnerability on intention. With regard to the influence of perceived severity on intention, fear was a partial mediator. This study thus supported the key role of fear in PMT in the context of problematic smartphone game use. Thus, in the high fea appeal group, all hypotheses were supported.

In the low fear appeal group, perceived severity (β=0.2, P<0.001) and perceived vulnerability (β=0.27, P<0.001 showed significant effects on fear. Fear (β=0.27, P<0.001) and subjective norms (β=0.33, P<0.001) were demonstrated to predict intention to decrease use. No significant effect of fear (β=0.04) was identified on habit Self-efficacy (β=0.04), response efficacy (β=0.1), response costs (β=0.02) and gaming habit (β=0.07) did not affect intention to decrease use. According to the study of Boss et al. (2015), a high threat would be generated by fea appeals before a proper coping response can be offered. This means that threat appraisal must occur and be considered first, followed by a consideration of the coping appraisal. If the threat level is low, such as no threat detected or perceived maladaptive reward being stronger than threat perception, the coping appraisal tends to be skipped. Without a potential for threat/negative consequences, PMT will be irrelevant. To activate protection motivation, individ uals should first be aware of the need for protection. Accordingly, when smartphone game users in the low fear appeal group perceive relatively low threats, the driving effect of efficacy may become insignificant. If users think that the consequences of their problematic smartphone game use are not very serious or are less likely to occur, the may not want to decrease their use even when they have high efficacy perceptions. The results in Appendix J showed that fear fully mediated the influence of both perceived severity and vulnerability on gaming habit. The explained variances were 23% in intention to decrease use.

## 6 | DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS

The purpose of this study is to explore the key drivers of intention to decrease problematic smartphone game use The results indicate, consistent with rational addiction theory, that the decision to decrease problematic smartphone game use can be driven by sets of PMT and social-normative factors. Using PMT as a theoretical lens, we test ou research model with two different levels of fear appeals. With the influence of a strong fear appeal, the PMT mode is fully supported. However, when the level of fear appeal is low, several important relationships in PMT become insignificant. The stronger fear appeal strengthens relationships in PMT and increases the explanatory values fo intention to decrease use.

Smartphone game players who receive strong fear appeal messages in our study exhibit higher levels of threat and coping appraisals. When players perceive the consequences of problematic smartphone game use to be serious or believe there is a high likelihood of suffering the consequences, they are more likely to feel fear, which is a mediat ing stage for activating intention to decrease use. Self-efficacy and response efficacy also promote players' intention to decrease their problematic use, whereas perceptions of a high level of response costs gleaned from the coping appraisal diminish intention to decrease use. Apart from the PMT variables, subjective norms are found to exert a sig nificant influence. Players under greater levels of subjective norms show more intentions to decrease their usage. When a player considers reference others approve of decreasing usage, he/she will develop a strong intention to decrease smartphone game use. As an inhibitor of decreasing usage, the influence of the gaming habit is largely reduced by PMT factors. The rational cognition of perceived threat severity and vulnerability weaken the automatic repetition of the habit through the mediation of fear. To the best of our knowledge, this study is one of the first investigations to explicate PMT's direct influence on intention to decrease use, as well as examine its power in reduc ing the strength of protection motivation inhibition

## 6.1 | Implications

This study provides several important implications. First, this study validates that PMT contributes to the recovery strategy for problematic smartphone game use. IS research on problematic smartphone game use is currently at th stage of revealing its formation mechanisms. An investigation of recovery strategies has not been conducted. As problematic behaviours cause harmful consequences, how to motivate recovery behaviour deserves research efforts Bearing these thoughts in mind, this study proposes a recovery strategy by indicating how to motivate players to decrease their usage. This study is one of the first empirical investigations to use PMT in conjunction with gaming habit and subjective norms to explain smartphone game players' intention to decrease use. We show that when indi viduals receive high fear appeal messages, factors in the threat appraisal (ie, perceived severity and vulnerability) self-efficacy, response efficacy in the coping appraisal, fear and subjective norms can all help to activate individuals protection motivation, whereas response costs may reduce protection motivation. Our findings provide a pathway to understanding how smartphone game players can be activated to decrease their problematic game use. The findings also show that under high fear appeal, high perceptions of threat are effective in breaking the gaming habit throug the mediation of fear emotion. As an inhibitor of conducting protection behaviours, addiction research, particularly ir the substance abuse field, has continuously aimed to identify effective approaches to break the undesirable habit (Onyechi et al., 2017). This study provides insights into how PMT factors help to reduce the “bad” habit. As the unde sirable habit of problematic smartphone game use is an inhibitor of using protection behaviours, reducing the habit i thus essential for the recovery process. This study thus extends the current efforts to fill the gaps in the research lit erature on recovery strategies for problematic smartphone game use.

Second, this study contributes to the IS field by applying PMT to explain decreasing behaviours. Compared with the existing research on IT adoption, very few IS studies have been conducted to examine voluntary decreases of IT behaviours (Furneaux & Wade, 2011). Given the fundamental differences between continuance and decreasing behaviours, it may be mistaken or incomplete to apply theories or models directly from the adoption domain. Therefore, IS researchers should pay attention to decreasing behaviours and further develop a theory to explain them. Very few empirical studies (Turel, 2014, 2016) have been conducted to understand the similar issue of discontinuance. A previously discussed. both discontinuance and decreasing behaviours are recovery strategies. Compared with discon: tinuance, a decrease is more moderate and is common among users who want to recover from an online addiction. In this respect, our empirical study is timely in examining the intention to decrease use. We develop our research mode using PMT, a theory that highlights the role of averting dangers, which is the core of avoidance. PMT is more appro priate for assessing intention to decrease use in the present context than previously studied adoption models. Our results show that the drivers inherent in PMT tend to motivate intention to decrease use, even to the extent of over: riding the inhibiting influence of the gaming habit. Thus, this study further provides useful theoretical insights regard ing how to break the habit. The results show that effective threat cognition can reduce gaming habit by activating the emotion of fear. As previously discussed, reducing the gaming habit is imperative for the effectiveness of research models on decreasing problematic smartphone game use. This study extends prior research on IT discontinuance, such as Turel's (2014) work, where the inhibiting role of a bad habit is recognized, whereas how to reduce the undesirable habit is unexplored. We enrich the relevant literature by identifying a useful solution. To the best of our knowledge, this study is one of the first empirical studies on decreasing behaviours that probe into the mecha nism of breaking a habit. We believe that our findings bring new ideas to, and advance the limited knowledge in, thi research area; therefore, this study can serve as a theoretical basis for future work on decreasing behaviours

This study also provides practical implications. Individuals may want to control or discontinue their high use of smartphone games, particularly when they perceive the harm caused by their problematic behaviours. Considering the serious consequences brought about by problematic smartphone game use, it is important on a practical level to activate players' motivation to reduce usage, and this study provides contributions in this respect. Furthermore, our research provides evidence for the role of fear appeals in activating protective behaviours. Practitioners working wit individuals who exhibit problematic game use can take advantage of fear appeals to inspire coping appraisals and adaptive approaches to threats, leading to a protective response. According to our results, when helping players recover healthy usage levels, it can be effective to increase their perception of the threat severity and vulnerabilit along with their feelings of fear, perceptions of subjective norms and assessments of self-efficacy and response effi cacy. In particular, educators or clinicians can teach these users to better understand their problematic behaviours.

If players are well informed about the high likelihood of suffering severe consequences due to their problematic behaviours, their fear can be increased. This approach can not only motivate players to decrease their use but may also be effective in breaking bad habits. Users' gaming habits are disadvantageous to their intentions to decrease use The findings of this study accordingly provide an efficacious path for managers or clinicians to help players drop bad habits. Helping players learn more about problematic use, including providing information about helpful treatments and suggestions for actions to take, can improve their perception of self-efficacy and response efficacy in decreasin their behaviours. For example, clinicians can remind players of serious consequences by playing videos. The video content can be real cases or social reports about the harm induced by problematic smartphone game use. Moreover they may enable players to recall their previous experiences and describe what and how often they suffer from thei problematic smartphone game use. These approaches can ensure players have more profound and intuitive feelings In addition, clinicians should provide assistance when players meet difficulties in decreasing use. Channels for individ uals to ask for help are necessary. Examples of success could also be provided to help players build confidence. Clini cians can further explain how other individuals achieve success.

Furthermore, response costs for following through with decreasing usage should be minimized. When player reduce their playing time and compulsive behaviours, feelings of withdrawal may emerge, which can make it dif ficult to control their impulses and follow through with their intentions. In this regard, practitioners should find alternatives to distract attention and relieve negative feelings, such as participation in outdoor sports.

Moreover, given the importance of subjective norms in IS usage, players' friends, parents and other important individuals should be instructed to provide monitoring environments to help players better regulate their behaviours For example, referents may remind players when excessive usage is detected or suspected. It can also be helpful to convey their view to players about the significance of decreasing playing time in preventing severe harm. Thes approaches can help enhance a user's motivation to decrease their problematic smartphone game use.

## 6.2 | Limitations and future research

We must note the limitations of this study and point out the opportunities for future studies. First, this stud develops a research model for activating intentions to decrease use in the context of problematic smartphone game use. To enrich our understanding of decreasing IT use, future studies could use our model in other contexts, such a with problematic Internet or Facebook use. This direction of research may become increasingly pertinent as mor users present with problematic symptoms and suffer serious consequences of their behaviours.

Second, the limited explanatory values of the variances in protection intention of 44% and 23% for the high and low fear appeal groups, respectively, indicate that important factors may be missing in our model. Further research can propose additional factors from other perspectives to develop a more complete picture of the drivers that lead to intention to decrease use.

Finally, we use intention to decrease use as the dependent variable. Intention has been indicated to be an impor tant predictor of behaviours, and many studies have used it instead of actual behaviours (Hu, Chau, Sheng, & Tam 1999; Limayem & Hirt, 2003; Taylor & Todd, 1995; Van der Heijden, Verhagen, & Creemers, 2003). Nevertheless intention is not equal to actual behaviour (Delone & McLean, 2003). As our goal is to understand decreasing usage future work could focus directly on measuring actual decreasing behaviour. We hope that by taking these limitations into account, future research will provide a deeper understanding of intentional decreases in problematic behaviours
