# So close yet no agreement: The effects of threats to self-esteem when using instant messaging and audio during seller–buyer negotiations

Randolph B. Cooper ⁎, Norman A. Johnson

University of Houston, United States

## a b s t r a c t

People negotiate with the goal of reaching agreement. However, there are times when reaching agreement may be well within their grasp but it is not realized and the process ends with a loss of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts for the negotiators. We use self-esteem theory to examine the in<sup>fl</sup>uences that offers and comments have on this behavior when negotiators use an audio as opposed to instant messaging for communications. To help explain the moderating effects of these media, we use a theory on grounding. We <sup>fi</sup>nd, for example, that when using instant messaging, the inability to reach agreement though negotiator offers are very close to each other is increased by initial offers containing relatively large concessions, by violations of reciprocity norms, and by critical comments

## 1. Introduction

Many of us have been in situations where we are either a buyer or a seller in a price negotiation. After much give and take, we are very close to a mutually bene<sup>fi</sup>cial agreement, yet neither we nor our negotiating partner are willing to concede further. We argue below that threats to self-esteem can play a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in this unwillingness, and that audio as contrasted with instant messaging communication-driven decision support systems can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affect perceptions of selfesteem threats.<sup>1</sup>

The online environment provides a means for negotiators to use a variety of these systems to communicate with each other and exchange offers. Commonly used are systems in the form of synchronous textbased instant messaging (IM) and audio [6,16,32]. For example, price negotiations occur in the over-the-counter market 80% of the time using IM and 20% of the time using audio [1]. Further, as an alternate marketplace to eBay, Bonanza provides an opportunity for buyers and sellers to engage in one-on-one price negotiations using IM in order to reach agreements [13,43]. In its <sup>fi</sup>rst year of operation, Bonanza attracted about 35,000 registered users, who engaged in tens of thousands of transactions [43]; the site is currently growing at an annual rate of 60% [58]. In addition to negotiation sites, retail organizations are <sup>fi</sup>nding IM negotiations pro<sup>fi</sup>table. For example, WideStorm.com provides IM software that is implemented on organizations' websites and that enables consumers and vehicle dealers to negotiate the price of a vehicle entirely on-line [26]. This software is currently implemented on the web sites of over 300 automobile, recreation vehicle, motorcycle, and boat dealerships across the United States [25].

IM and audio are then competing forms of communication-driven decision support systems for negotiations in the global business setting [22]. We thus contrast IM and audio, and propose the following research question. How are perceptions of self-esteem in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by negotiators' communications via IM versus audio, and how do these perceptions affect the likelihood that negotiators who are very close to agreement will not be able to reach agreement? Our study contributes to decision support system theory and practice as applied to electronic commerce in a number of ways. For example, earlier studies on <sup>fl</sup>aming examined the contextual and partner-associated causes of anger and how anger can lead to <sup>fl</sup>aming [30] as well as the effects of <sup>fl</sup>aming on reaching agreement [31]. This study complements those studies by examining the how non-angry comments and offers can affect the potential for negotiators to reach agreement. In addition, our explanations of how using IM as compared to audio can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the ability to make electronic commerce sales adds to the growing decision support literature that has examined seller–buyer issues such as in<sup>fl</sup>uences of seller website dynamic display [68], seller website visual appeal [41], and online sales strategies [19].

The next section provides the theoretical background of the paper. This is followed by the development of a research model based on selfesteem that explains and predicts the outcome of getting close to agreement in negotiations using IM and audio media. Hypotheses derived from this non-agreement model are tested with experimental data, and the results are discussed. If this non-agreement model is valid, then it should be able to discriminate between dyads with close seller–buyer offers but not reaching agreement and those dyads that do reach agreement. We therefore test a variation of the non-agreement model to test this ability. The paper ends with a discussion, covering the implications of this study for theory and practice.

## 2. Theoretical background

The theory of self-esteem is the foundation of our research model. We also employ grounding theory in order to determine the ways in which the use of IM versus audio moderate the relationships we developed through the use of self-esteem theory. We begin with a discussion of self-esteem. This is followed by a description of our research context, including our level of analysis, the type of negotiations of interest, and how IM differs from audio in regard to their abilities to convey social and relational cues.

## 2.1. Self-esteem

Self-esteem is a person's overall sense of self-worth or personal value [27]; it is the impression one has of one's self, involving such feelings as con<sup>fi</sup>dence and satisfaction [44]. Self-esteem is conceptualized as a state that can have substantial variation around the trait level [9,34]. We are interested in state levels since we are examining the in<sup>fl</sup>uences of individuals' communications on the transient self-esteem of their partners.

Self-esteem has also been conceptualized as composed of several domains that are of interest unto themselves (e.g., [9]) or that can be combined to form a global value [27]. These domains are related to aspects of an individual's life that the individual feels are important, and the importance of each domain, varies among individuals as well as during different times of life for the same individual [27]. The subjects of our experiments are college students, who have been found to perceive one or more of the following category domains as important [28].

• Physical competence — consisting of athletic competence and physical appearance.

• Peer acceptance — consisting of close friendships, romantic relationships, and relationships with parents.

• Behavioral conduct — consisting of morality and sense of humor.

• Cognitive competence — consisting of scholastic competence, intellectual ability, creativity, and job competence.

Our focus is on negotiations over IM and audio among anonymous strangers, such as those negotiating using the Bonanza website. The IM and audio contexts do not allow negotiators to see each other, which reduces the potential for physical competence to be important. Since negotiators are anonymous strangers, the peer acceptance domains are also less important. Within behavioral conduct, we are not interested in morality because during negotiation typical threats to this aspect of self-esteem involve issues of fairness; and reducing such threats would involve greater concession leading to agreement rather than non-agreement. In addition, we are not interested in the sense of humor domain because we are speci<sup>fi</sup>cally examining the cognitive rather than affective aspects of comments. Finally, threats to creativity (within cognitive competence) are less important because, as described below, our focus is on distributive rather than integrative negotiations; and creative solutions are more important with integrative negotiations because of the multiple issues involved [59]. We are thus left with job competence, scholastic competence, and intellectual ability as potentially important domains. For example, during negotiations, threats to job competence could occur with the belittling of one's negotiation skills, while threats to scholastic competence or intellectual ability could occur with belittling one's ability to determine the expected value of the object of negotiation.

“[P]eople seek to maintain, enhance, and protect their self-esteem” by attempting to obtain success and avoid failure in domains they deem important [9 page 143]. People do this because they seek to validate their opinions of themselves by proving to themselves that they are successful in domains that are important to their self-esteem [11]; success then supports their beliefs that they are worthy and valuable individuals [10,53]. In addition, they seek “the emotional high associated with success in [important] domains … and strive to avoid the emotional lows that accompany failure in these domains” [10 page 200]. In this way maintenance of self-esteem serves to regulate behavior [10]. Thus, for self-validation and regulation reasons, the protection, maintenance, and enhancement of an individual's self-esteem serve to motivate his or her behavior [9].

## 2.2. Self-esteem and negotiated agreement

One way by which threat to self-esteem can be inferred during negotiations is to take into account what and how price offers are made. We use an example to elaborate what the bases are for making such inference. The example in Fig. 1 helps illustrate the relationship between self-esteem and price offers that are made between a buyer and a seller negotiating for a lottery ticket with the potential to win 30.<sup>2</sup> The range of acceptable prices for the seller is 30 to 16, and for the buyer is 0 to 22. As is typical, at any time during negotiations, not only do the buyer and seller have ranges in which they will individually accept offers, but there is also an overlap region where they will both accept [51]. In Fig. 1, assume that the buyer's <sup>fi</sup>rst offer was 4 and second offer was 7, while the seller's <sup>fi</sup>rst offer was 27 and second was 25. At this point, a matching offer (one that makes both offers equal to each other and results in agreement) by either seller or buyer is not reasonable because if the seller (buyer) matches the buyer's (seller's) offer of 7 (25) then the matching offer is outside of the seller's (buyer's) range of acceptable prices. If negotiations stop at this point (for example because both negotiators decide not to concede any more) then it is not clear to seller or buyer that there exists the potential for agreement and it is thus unlikely that the seller or buyer will view stopping negotiations as resulting in signi<sup>fi</sup>cant opportunity costs.

However, if negotiations continue to the point where the seller's offer is 22 and the buyer's offer is 16, then a matching offer by either party is reasonable, since the seller perceives receiving at least 16 as more valuable than keeping the ticket and the buyer perceives giving up to 22 is less valuable than receiving the ticket. At this point if the seller (buyer) chooses to stop negotiations it is clear to the seller (buyer) that he or she is giving up the opportunity to sell (buy) the ticket at an acceptable price.

Thus, once offers are within the overlap region there is an incentive for both the buyer and seller to make a matching offer and end negotiations with agreement. As the spread between seller and buyer offers decreases, this incentive increases for the seller or the buyer or both. This occurs because the opportunity costs increase in that the seller will be receiving more and/or the buyer will be paying less for the ticket.<sup>3</sup> Note that these incentives do not require that seller or buyer know each other's range of acceptable prices. Rather, when faced with conceding or not reaching agreement, only knowledge of their own range is required.

Fig. 1. Example offers and ranges of acceptable prices.

An individual with increasing incentives to match the last offer and reach agreement may choose not to do so if the negotiation partner's actions have threatened the individual's self-esteem. This could occur if the partner appears to be taking advantage of the individual and thereby not respecting the individual's negotiation skills [14,35]. Such threats to self-esteem motivate compensatory self-processes aimed at mitigating the threats [9]. For example, by not conceding any more, the threat to the individual's self-esteem is reduced because the individual punishes the partner by forcing the partner to concede more and thereby signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly decreasing bene<sup>fi</sup>ts that the partner might obtain from agreement [49]. Alternatively, the individual may attempt to bolster self-esteem by conveying a desired image to others [9]: reducing concession making, even to the point of offering an ultimatum, can be perceived by the individual as demonstrating negotiation prowess [49]. Finally, the individual might conclude that the task is not worthwhile and withdraw from negotiations [10]. This reduces the threat to the individual by disassociating the task from an important domain.

Punishment, presenting a desired image, and withdrawal serve the same purpose for the individual (reduce threat to self-esteem) and result in the same behavior (concessions curtailed by the individual) with the same effects on negotiations (decreased likelihood of agreement) that results in potential opportunity costs to the individual (increased likelihood of no bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from agreement).

Negotiation researchers have found that individuals are willing to take actions that result in opportunity costs in order to reduce threats to their self-esteem. For example, Leventhal and Bergman [39] found that some individuals who, after performing a task and being told by the experimenter that they performed as well as their negotiation partner, rejected a reward when they were to have received less than their partner. In addition, even more individuals rejected the unfair reward when it was accompanied by the following, which was stated by their partner (actually a confederate of the experimenters): “I'm going to keep …[the amount of money] for myself since I'm so much better at this thing than you are…..” [39 page 158]. Pillutla and Murnighan [49] conclude that this increase in rejected rewards was due to increased threats to the individual's self-esteem.

The key point for our purpose is the relationship between greater threats to self-esteem and a willingness to endure greater opportunity costs in order to eliminate those threats. Here, greater opportunity costs occur when the individuals stop making concessions even though the spread between the last buyer and seller offers (the ending spread) becomes smaller and smaller. Greater opportunity costs occur because, as described above, as the ending spread decreases, either the buyer or the seller or both will obtain greater gains by making an offer that results in agreement rather than ending negotiations without agreement. Therefore, when dyads stop negotiations without agreement, the reason for doing so increases in importance as the ending spread between their last offers becomes smaller. We focus on how greater threats to selfesteem become increasingly important and can therefore result in dyads ending negotiations without reaching agreement as the ending spread gets smaller and smaller.

## 2.3. Level of analysis

Much of the theory we employ focuses on how a dyad partner's offers and comments in<sup>fl</sup>uence an individual's self-esteem. However, we develop theory and provide empirical evidence at the dyad level. We do this because when there is a small ending spread and there are no threats to self-esteem, it is likely that the price resulting from a matching offer by the seller would be preferred by the seller to no sale and the price resulting from a matching offer by the buyer would be preferred by the buyer to no purchase. At this point, it only takes a matching offer by either negotiator in a dyad to reach agreement. If negotiations end without agreement, and there are no constraining factors, such as running out of time, then threats to self-esteem are likely to be affecting both negotiators, i.e., it is a dyad-level issue.

## 2.4. Type of negotiation

Our focus is on a distributive negotiation, involving a single issue (price) because this is what is most often encountered on electronic commerce websites, such as Bonanza and those using WideStorm.com. In addition, we are interested in two-party (dyadic) negotiation between strangers (in which one party is the buyer and the other the seller) because this is what one typically <sup>fi</sup>nds in online sales negotiations. Our object of negotiation is a lottery ticket, with opportunities to win its face value depicted by a prize wheel. In general, objects of price negotiation vary widely in terms of their characteristics. However, they share a key characteristic involving their payoff uncertainty. Perceptions of this uncertainty will vary between dyad partners, and partners will assign different utility valuations based on perceived uncertainty. This uncertainty can be associated with utility of the object itself (and its potential lack of performance) as well as from issues involved in obtaining the object. Therefore, our use of a lottery ticket as the object of negotiation represents important aspects of any object of price negotiations. Moreover, the kind of lottery tickets used in this study has been employed in many prior studies in order to examine aspects of individuals' attitudes towards and behaviors resulting from these kinds of uncertainty (e.g., [57]).

## 2.5. Media — IM versus audio mediation

There are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences between audio and IM in terms of their abilities to transfer social and relational cues, and these cues have the potential to in<sup>fl</sup>uence self-esteem during negotiation. These differences are related to grounding, which is the process by which individuals use social and relational cues to develop a shared sense of understanding and participation [7]. Audio has the following grounding characteristics, which facilitates the transfer of such cues [7,24].

• Audibility allows each party to hear the cadence and tone of speech.

• Cotemporality makes it possible for a reply to be produced without delay when a message is received and understood.

• Sequentiality enables turn taking to stay in sequence because, e.g., each person knows the moment when the other has <sup>fi</sup>nished communicating.

• Simultaneity enables message sending and receipt at the same time (e.g., when a person says ‘uh huh’ to a speaker).

In contrast, text-based instant messaging (IM) does not have grounding characteristics that can facilitate the transfer of relational and social cues [7,24].

## 3. Non-agreement model

We next describe our research model that focuses on dyads not reaching agreement (Fig. 2). Our choice of constructs covers the two major aspects of negotiation communication — offers and comments. We <sup>fi</sup>rst develop a base model depicting relationships when negotiators use IM, and the associated hypotheses are labeled with an “a”. We then determine how the use of audio instead of IM moderates these relationships, and associated hypotheses are labeled with a “b”.

## 3.1. Offers

Offers are the amounts each individual puts forward for the negotiation partner to consider accepting so as to reach an agreement. The offers' values and how much an individual is willing to change them as negotiation proceeds affect partners' perceptions of procedural fairness [5]. These perceptions can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the degree that one believes his or her negotiating abilities and skills are treated with respect, and thereby can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the cognitive competence domains of people's self-esteem [14,35,65].<sup>4</sup> This effect on self-esteem results from the looking glass self [8], in that “people see themselves or judge themselves as they think others see or judge them” [35 page 315]. Thus, for example, if a negotiation partner acts unfairly, this can signal to the individual that the partner believes the individual is a weak negotiator [35], which is felt by the individual as a threat to his or her cognitive competence-based self-esteem.

## 3.1.1. Reciprocity violation

Reciprocity is a procedural fairness norm [21] that is typical in negotiations [56], where for example, a concession by an individual (e.g., a seller providing a lower price offer) is met with a concession by a partner (e.g., the buyer providing a higher price offer). However, when violations of reciprocity occur (for example, when a negotiation partner holds <sup>fi</sup>rm at a price) this threatens the individual's self-esteem because it implies that the partner believes that the individual lacks negotiation competence and will accede to the unfair lack of reciprocation. As described earlier, these threats motivate the individual to invoke compensatory self-processes such as punishment, presenting a desired image, or withdrawal, in order to reduce the threats. Thus, concessions by the individual become less likely as the threats become greater, leading to the following hypothesis.

H1a. When negotiations cease without agreement using IM, smaller ending spreads are increasingly (decreasingly) likely with greater (smaller) violations of reciprocity.

As described above, reciprocity by a partner during negotiations will be perceived by an individual as signifying respect and thereby supporting the individual's self-esteem. However, the reduced grounding when using IM decreases negotiators' rapport with, and trust of, each other [46]. This can occur because IM is less able to convey the clues of a person's trustworthiness, such as through the pitch and cadence of his or her voice [18]. Without such clues, individuals perceive a greater potential of being exploited by their partners' unfair and unethical behaviors [23,64,67]. When negotiators are strangers, this perception is heightened because of the sinister attribution error, where individuals are more likely to attribute negotiation partners' actions to malevolent motives [59].

As a result, when compared to audio, IM communication can increase the likelihood that negotiators will have a negative bias and misinterpret behavior as being exploitive and unfair. An individual may even perceive some reciprocation by the negotiation partner as part of a manipulative strategy rather than as a sign of fairness [15]. This bias increases the likelihood that reciprocity violations will be interpreted as more threatening to self-esteem while the lack of reciprocity violations will be interpreted as being less supportive of self-esteem [64]. As a result we expect that, as compared to IM, reciprocity violations when using audio will be perceived as less threatening to an individual's self-esteem while the lack of reciprocity violations will be interpreted as being more supportive of self-esteem. This will decrease the likelihood and degree of compensatory self-processes, and thereby reduce negotiators' willingness to incur opportunity costs when using audio, leading to the following hypothesis:

H1b. Compared to IM, using audio communication will reduce the relationship between reciprocity violation and smaller ending spreads when negotiations cease.

## 3.1.2. First offer concession

Anonymity coming from the lack of grounding when using IM can lead to sel<sup>fi</sup>shness, since parties are less aware of the presence of each other and their concerns [28,29]. In turn, this can cause individuals to attribute their partners' offering of initial concessions to negotiation weakness that should be exploited during negotiations [52]. With the determination that the partner is a weak negotiator, maintenance of the individual's negotiation competence-oriented self-esteem depends on being able to capture as much concession as possible from the partner. Not being able to do so will be experienced as a threat to the individual's self-esteem and leads to compensatory self-processes (punishment, presenting a desired image or withdrawal) aimed at mitigating the threat. The individual will therefore be less likely to make further concessions and more willing to incur greater opportunity costs by not reaching agreement. This leads to the following hypothesis.

H2a. When negotiations cease without agreement using IM, smaller ending spreads are increasingly (decreasingly) likely with greater (smaller) <sup>fi</sup>rst offer concessions.

Compared to IM, grounding is increased when negotiating over audio, which facilitates the exchange of relational and social cues that increase an individual's awareness of the partner and the partner's concerns. This reduces the individuals perceptions that his or her partner's initial concession re<sup>fl</sup>ects negotiation weakness, and increases the individual's perceptions the concession indicates fairness and a readiness for cooperative negotiation [45,52]. There will thus be less impact on selfesteem and associated compensatory self-processes when using audio, leading to the following hypothesis.

H2b. Compared to IM, using audio will reduce the relationship between <sup>fi</sup>rst offer concessions and smaller ending spreads when negotiations cease.

## 3.2. Comments

Critical comments are a natural part of the negotiation process. When these comments convey social disapproval, such as criticism regarding an individual's competence, an individual's self-esteem is threatened [36,42], with more criticism resulting in a greater threat [34,36], and motivate compensatory self-processes involving punishment, presenting a desired image, or withdrawal. The individual therefore will be less likely to make further concessions and more willing to incur greater opportunity costs by not reaching agreement, leading to the following hypothesis.

H3a. When negotiations cease without agreement using IM, smaller ending spreads are increasing likely with the communication of more critical comments.

Comments can also communicate accord that can support and validate an individual's competence resulting in the maintenance or enhancement of self-esteem [10,11]. This should reduce the likelihood of compensatory self-processes and leads to the following hypothesis.

H4a. When negotiations cease without agreement using IM, smaller ending spreads are less likely with the communication of more accord comments.

As described above, when compared to audio, IM communication is less effective in the support of grounding, which reduces negotiators empathy, rapport, and trust of each other. This results in negotiators being more likely to attribute each others' actions to malevolent motives, and increases the likelihood that communicated critical comments will be interpreted more negatively and that communicated positive comments will be interpreted less positively [33], such as perceiving positive comments to be damning with faint praise. Given the relationships between self-esteem and negative and positive comments described above, we therefore propose the following hypotheses:

H3b. Compared to IM, using audio will reduce the relationship between critical comments and smaller ending spreads.

H4b. Compared to IM, using audio will increase the relationship between accord comments and smaller ending spreads.

## 4. Method

For brevity, details associated with the participants, the task, and the procedure are presented in the Appendix A. A summary of the Appendix A is provided next, followed by construct operationalizations.

## 4.1. Participants, task, and procedure

Each participant was randomly assigned to one of 74 negotiating dyads and to the role of either seller or buyer. The dyads were randomly assigned to begin negotiating with either IM or audio. Though dyad pairs were kept constant across media, the identity of each dyad partner was unknown to the other, and students were told that their dyad partners would be different when they negotiated again using the alternate medium. We did not employ any other experimental manipulations; this enabled natural interactive dynamics to occur, making our study more relevant to real-world negotiations [17].

The negotiation object was a lottery ticket that was displayed on each participant's computer at the beginning of negotiations. Participants in each dyad negotiated the price of the lottery ticket in terms of points. Each participant in a dyad was given \$10 for participating in the experiment and was told that at the end of it that he or she had a chance to receive an extra \$10, depending on the number of points earned as a result of the outcome of the negotiation and the lottery draw for the ticket's value. At the beginning of the negotiations, participants saw a prize wheel that displayed two potential pairs of lottery ticket values: 0 and 12 points (on the left side of Fig. 3) and 0 and 34 points (on the right side of Fig. 3). Participants were told that after negotiations ended, there was a 50% chance that the prize wheel spun for them would be the 0–12 value pair and a 50% chance that the prize wheel spun for them would be the 0–34 value pair. If the seller decided to keep the ticket and won the lottery (the prize wheel landed on the shaded area), he or she received points equal to the actual ticket value: 12 or 34 points; if the seller lost (the prize wheel landed on the unshaded area) he or she got zero points. In either case, the buyer received zero points. If the seller sold the ticket, he or she received points equal to the selling price, and the buyer received points equal to his or her lottery winnings (determined as described above for the seller) less the amount he or she used to purchase the ticket.

## 4.2. Operationalization of first offer concessions

A seller's (buyer's) <sup>fi</sup>rst offer may contain a concession in that it is relatively low (high) because of beliefs that this will increase the likelihood that the buyer (seller) will come back with a relatively high (low) counter offer [37]. Because an individual's reservation price tends to change throughout negotiations [54], we cannot determine the degree of concession by comparing an individual's <sup>fi</sup>rst offer with his or her reservation price. Therefore, we employed a measure to help tease out such values. The measure assumes that the negotiation strategies of most buyers are similar to each other (either relatively cooperative or competitive) and that the negotiation strategies of most sellers are similar to each other (either relatively cooperative or competitive). We then measure each seller's level of concession by the difference between a seller's <sup>fi</sup>rst offer and the average of all sellers' <sup>fi</sup>rst offers. Similarly, we measure each buyer's level of concession by the difference between a buyer's <sup>fi</sup>rst offer and the average of all buyers' <sup>fi</sup>rst offers. This measure was calculated for the <sup>fi</sup>rst person in the dyad to make an offer as the difference between the person's offer and the <sup>fi</sup>rst offer average. This average took into consideration the negotiator role (buyer or seller) and lottery ticket expected value (see Appendix A).<sup>5</sup>

## 4.3. Operationalization of reciprocity violation

Concession making is the change of an offer in support of another party's interests with a reduction of bene<sup>fi</sup>t to one's self [52]. Unilateral concessions are offered by an individual without an explicit expectation of a subsequent concession by the dyad partner, but with the hope that the partner will reciprocate with a concession [38]. Reciprocal concessions are those made to pay back unilateral concessions [4]. These types of concessions are illustrated in Table 1, where the seller initially offers 25 and the buyer counters with 13. The seller's next offer of 23 represents a unilateral concession of 2. When the buyer holds <sup>fi</sup>rm at 13, the seller offers 22, which represents another unilateral concession that is equal to 1. The buyer's counter of 15 represents a reciprocal concession of 2. When the seller holds <sup>fi</sup>rm and the buyer agrees at 22, this represents reciprocal concession by the buyer of 1.

Total reciprocal concessions cannot exceed total unilateral concessions because a concession by an individual that goes beyond paying back a unilateral concession by his or her partner is part reciprocal concession (the payback amount) and part unilateral or matching (the amount beyond the payback if agreement is reached). Therefore, violations of reciprocity are measured for each dyad by doing the following. The unilateral concessions made by seller and buyer during negotiation are added together resulting in the total unilateral concessions for the dyad. The reciprocal concessions made by seller and buyer during negotiation are added together resulting in the total reciprocal concessions for the dyad. Total unilateral concessions minus total reciprocal concessions is then the measure of reciprocity violation. This number has a lower bound of zero, when reciprocal concessions equal unilateral concessions. As this number gets larger, the degree to which unilateral concessions are reciprocated becomes smaller, i.e., reciprocity is violated.

## 4.4. Operationalization of communicated criticism and accord

$\mathrm { H } _ { 3 a }$ and $\mathrm { H } _ { 3 \mathrm { b } }$ require the coding of dyad communication in order to identify instances when comments indicate criticism. Categories 10 and 11 in Bales [3] interaction process categorization scheme identify ways in which one individual can communicate criticism to another. Category 10 is coded when a response appears to be skeptical, dubious, hesitant, critical, suspicious, and/or distrustful. Category 11 is coded when a response demonstrates impatience, restlessness, agitation, surprise, or alarm. The coding process is as follows.

The IM messages were randomly divided into three sets and audio messages were randomly divided into three sets. Each set of data was made up of messages from about 25 dyads. Three coders were assigned to each set of data; these coders read or listened to each message and assigned it to Bales' categories. All coders were blind to the research hypotheses and worked independently of each other on coding the messages. The average inter-coder reliabilities for categories 10 and 11 were 0.93 and 0.91, respectively.

The relative amount of communicated criticism was determined in the following manner for each message. Each coder assigned a value of 1 to a message the coder believed included criticism according to Bales and zero otherwise. The coders' evaluations for this message were then averaged. For example, if the three coders all believed one communication included category 10 criticism while only two of the three believed another communication included category 10 criticism, then the <sup>fi</sup>rst communication would have a value of 1 for category 10 while the second would have a value of 0.67 for category 10.

$\mathrm { H } _ { 4 a }$ and $\mathrm { H } _ { 4 \mathrm { b } }$ require the coding of dyad communication in order to identify instances when comments indicated accord. Category 3 in Bales [3] interaction process categorization scheme identi<sup>fi</sup>es ways in which one individual can communicate accord to another. For example, category 3 is coded when the partner appears to concur, follow, and/or yield. Coding process described for category 10 was followed for category 3. The average inter-coder reliability for category 3 was 0.85.

## 4.5. Operationalization of ending spread and communication media

We measure the ending spread for a dyad by taking the absolute value of the difference between the seller and buyer offers when negotiations cease. A single variable was employed for communication media in which IM was represented with a value of 0 and audio was represented by a value of 1.

## 4.6. Operationalization of controls

Unilateral (and therefore reciprocal) concessions are sensitive to the spread between the initial buyer and seller offers. For example, when the seller initial offer is lower and the buyer initial offer is higher, the beginning spread between offers is lower and there is less negotiating room before agreement is reached. As a result, there is less opportunity for unilateral and associated reciprocal concessions. A dyad's concession making process could also be sensitive to whether the dyad's lottery ticket had a high or low expected value. Finally, the IM versus audio media effects examined in the model are interactions that require the statistical control of the media main effect. These issues could be controlled by adding control constructs into the model. However, doing so would in<sup>fl</sup>ate the resulting model R<sup>2</sup>. In order to control for these issues without in<sup>fl</sup>ating the $\begin{array} { r } { \bar { \mathsf { R } } ^ { 2 } . } \end{array}$ , the dependent variable was regressed on the beginning spread, the lottery ticket expected value, and medium. The residuals from this regression were captured and used as the dependent variable in the PLS analyses.

5.3. Alternatives to the relationship between self-esteem threats and smaller ending spreads

We do not claim that threats to self-esteem are the only cause of smaller ending spreads when agreement is not reached. However, it appears that some of the more obvious candidates that might confound this relationship are not signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. For example, if negotiators decided to quit negotiations because they were no longer interested in the task, we could not infer that individuals stopping negotiations when offers were within the parties' overlap regions of acceptable prices were incurring opportunity costs. However, this is not likely here because all participants were volunteers, and were attracted by the potential to earn money. In addition, as described earlier, their ability to earn more money depended upon good negotiation performance. Further, we examined the transcripts for evidence that participants had become disinterested in the task. Only 1 instance was found where one party or the other wanted to discontinue negotiations. A portion of the dialog between the parties was:

Buyer: HUNGRY!!!

Seller: actually let hurry up I wanna get the f\*\*k out

Seller: yea me too

Buyer: me too- me too

It thus appears that the vast majority of participants was reasonably interested in the negotiations and would therefore experience opportunity costs if they caused negotiations to cease when ending spreads were very small.

Another potential problem involves dyads running out of time. If negotiations ended with buyer and seller offers close to each other but with no agreement because they had no more time to negotiate, then we could not infer the link between threats to self-esteem and smaller ending spreads. However, in our pilot study dyads were allowed as much time as they needed, and the maximum time taken by any dyad was 3 1/2 min. We therefore allowed each dyad in the current study 3 1/2 min to complete their negotiations. The actual maximum time taken by any dyad in the current study was 3 min, so it appears that dyads did not run out of time.

## 5.4. Subject anonymity

We proposed that the differences found between IM and audio contexts were due in large part to differences in trust that result from the two media. However, if the participants had a prior history, they would be more likely to recognize that they knew each other when they used audio as opposed to IM, which could account for some of the differences. However, the participants were from a population of 17,000 freshmen through senior college students, who volunteered in response to posted <sup>fl</sup>yers as well as class announcements. As participants arrived at the experiment, they were randomly assigned to different rooms: one for sellers and one for buyers. When a group of students who knew each other arrived together, they were all assigned to the same room (either buyer or seller), so that they would not have each other as dyad partners. The participants in the buyer room were then randomly assigned to those in the seller room in order to form negotiation dyads. This process reduced the chance that dyad partners had a common history, or if they did have a common history, that they recognized each other. In addition, we examined the negotiation transcripts looking for indications that the partners recognized each other. There were no dyads in which partners appeared to identify each other without an introduction. There were three dyads when members introduced themselves, and only one of these three dyads did not reach agreement. The following portion of dialog from that dyad is typical of the other two introductions that occurred.

Seller: Hi.

Buyer: What's your name?

Seller: My name is Harry.

Buyer: And mine is Ariel.

Seller: Hi, Ariel.

The negotiations then continued in this dyad without any reference to prior history, such as classes, events, people, etc. that they had in common. We are therefore con<sup>fi</sup>dent that the differences we found between IM and audio are not due to differences in recognitions of shared histories.

## 6. Agreement model

Our examination of dyads not reaching agreement supports our contention that smaller ending spreads can be caused by greater threats to negotiators' self-esteem. We next examine a context with the dependent variable changed to agreement, which allows us to determine the nomological validity of our self-esteem based model [12]. Theoretical rationales provided for the antecedents in the non-agreement model should apply for this agreement model, because they describe how threats to self-esteem result in fewer concessions, and because concessions are necessary for agreement [51]. Therefore, based on the theoretical development in the non-agreement model and with agreement as the dependent variable, we offer the following hypotheses.

H11. Reciprocity violation will decrease the likelihood of reaching agreement.

H12. First offer concessions will decrease the likelihood of reaching agreement.

H13. Critical comments will decrease the likelihood of reaching agreement.

H14. Comments indicating accord will increase the likelihood of reaching agreement.

We include only IM for hypotheses testing because we found that threats to self-esteem have a more consistent impact with IM. The dependent variable is binary, indicating whether there is an agreement (1) or no agreement (0). The data set includes all 34 that reached agreement and the 6 that ended negotiations with buyer– seller offers within 1 point of each other. PLS results are depicted in Fig. 5, with descriptive statistics in Table 5. The procedure described earlier was used to <sup>fi</sup>nd path standard error estimates. Three of the hypotheses are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant $\left( \mathsf { p } = . 0 5 \right)$ and in the correct direction, with 35% of agreement explained; this provides substantial support for the agreement model. Rerunning the model and adding dyads with ending spreads of 2 and 3 provided results generally consistent with that illustrated in Fig. 5.

## 7. Discussion

Our research question asked “How are perceptions of self-esteem in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by negotiators' communications via IM versus audio, and how do these perceptions affect the likelihood that negotiators' who are very close to agreement will not be able to reach agreement?” We found that, when using IM, self-esteem-based antecedents signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affect the likelihood of ending negotiations without agreement when the ending spreads are very small. We also found that, when using IM, these antecedents were able to distinguish between dyads reaching agreement and dyads ending negotiations without agreement but that had small ending spreads. These two tests provide substantial support for our model and in the nomological validity of our self-esteem based conceptualization.

## 7.2. Limitations

The contributions of this study are constrained by its limitations. One potential limitation concerns our use of students as negotiators. However the students were relating to how each other framed their concerns and research shows that such framing has similar effects on both students and professional negotiators [47]. For this reason, Neale and Northcraft [47] conclude that results from negotiation research relying solely on student subjects can be generalized to the real world of negotiation. In addition, students make up a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant portion of the population purchasing online through, for example, Bonanza.

## 7.3. Implications for practitioners

Independent of whether IM or audio is used for negotiations, violations of the reciprocity norm by an individual appear to threaten the partner's self-esteem and thereby increase the likelihood that negotiations will end without agreement even though the spread between negotiators' offers is very small. Therefore, individuals should be very careful when employing negotiation strategies that do not reciprocate concessions of their partners.

Independent of whether IM or audio is used for negotiations, <sup>fi</sup>rst movers should be careful not to concede too much with their <sup>fi</sup>rst offers because this may be interpreted as negotiation weakness. When such weakness is observed, their partners become less willing to offer concessions even if it means ending negotiations without agreement.

When using IM, critical comments that accompany offers and counter offers can substantially affect the likelihood of not reaching agreement even though negotiators' offers have become very close to each other. We therefore suggest that comments appearing to be skeptical, dubious, hesitant, critical, suspicious, and/or distrustful not be communicated. In addition, comments demonstrating impatience, restlessness, agitation, surprise, or alarm should not be communicated. Rather, when making a counter offer, merely come back with the new price and do not include any critical comments. In contrast, when using audio, such comments do not appear to have a negative in<sup>fl</sup>uence on reaching an agreement.

When using IM, the likelihood of not reaching agreement even though the negotiators' offers are very close to each other is reduced when comments demonstrate accord by appearing to concur, follow, and/or yield to suggestions, directions, or information provided by a negotiation partner. This is not to suggest that negotiators are required to manufacture an overabundance of accord comments. For example, with IM dyads reaching agreement, accord comments were only 18% of comments made.

With the support for our self-esteem based model described above, we next expand on our <sup>fi</sup>ndings and offer tentative negotiation guidance using our <sup>fi</sup>ndings as a foundation, but based largely on the self-esteem literature itself. In general, when using IM individuals should do what they can to elevate their negotiation partners' self-esteem. To that end, individuals should make offers and comments indicating that they are acting fairly [35]. This was demonstrated in our study by negotiators following the reciprocity norm. In addition, fairness can be demonstrated by an interest in outcome equity [66]. Note that equity is not the same as equality. While equality refers to an equal sharing and exact division of resources, equity takes notions of justice and fairness into consideration when determining the division of resources. Therefore, an individual might be able to reduce his or her reciprocity and still maintain the partner's self-esteem by justifying the reduction in terms of equity. For example, the individual might indicate that he or she has a greater need for the resource.

We found that a negotiator's self-esteem is sensitive to the communication of critical, and accord comments. Following on this, individuals' self-esteem has been found to be negatively (positively) affected by impolite (polite) comments [35]. Therefore, an individual can increase the partner's self-esteem by the communication of politeness rituals, such as the communication of greetings (e.g., “Hello”) and sympathy (“I can see how you feel”) [3 page 177].

Finally, an individual's competence-based self-esteem is enhanced when he or she feels in control [42]. We found this might be the case when we examined the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of critical comments. An individual can enhance the partner's self-esteem in this regard by asking him or her for suggestions regarding the negotiation process, such as “What do you suggest?” or “What can we do about this?” [3].

## Appendix A

One hundred and forty-eight students (40% female) in a large university participated in an experiment designed to test our hypotheses. These students were asked to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll roles as negotiators in a price negotiation for a computer-generated virtual lottery ticket. The ticket represents an asset because its ultimate value converts to cash for the person who makes the best trade on it. The monetary units over which the virtual ticket is negotiated are points. This type of ticket has been employed in many prior studies in order to examine individuals' attitudes towards and behaviors resulting from uncertainty (e.g., [A1, A3]).

Each student was randomly assigned to a dyad and to the role of either seller or buyer. We thus started with 74 dyads, each of which was randomly assigned to begin negotiating with either IM or audio. Though dyad pairs were kept constant across media, the identity of each dyad partner was unknown to the other, and students were told that their dyad partners would be different when they negotiated again using the alternate medium. We did not provide any other experimental manipulations. This enabled natural interactive dynamics to occur, which makes our study more relevant to real-world negotiations [A2]. We next describe the experimental task and procedure, followed by operationalizations of the constructs.

## Task

The negotiation object was a lottery ticket that was displayed on each participant's computer at the beginning of negotiations. Participants in each dyad negotiated the price of the lottery ticket in terms of points. Each participant in a dyad was given \$10 for participating in the experiment and was told that at the end of it he or she had a chance to receive an extra \$10 if his or her negotiation was randomly chosen and if he or she had more points than the negotiation partner.

At the beginning of the negotiations, participants saw a prize wheel that displayed two potential pairs of lottery ticket values: 0 and 12 points (on the left side of Fig. 3) and 0 and 34 points (on the right side of Fig. 3). Participants were told that after negotiations ended, there was a 50% chance that the prize wheel spun for them would be the 0–12 value pair and a 50% chance that the prize wheel spun for them would be the 0–34 value pair. In addition, participants were told that after the value pair was randomly determined, the prize wheel representing that pair would be spun in order to determine if the holder of the lottery ticket received 0 or more than 0 points. For example, if the 0–12 value pair prize wheel was randomly selected, there was a 74% chance that the result of spinning the wheel would be 12 points (the shaded portion in Fig. 3 on the left) and a 26% chance that the result would be 0 point. If the 0–34 value pair prize wheel was randomly selected, there was a 98% chance that the result of spinning the wheel would be 34 points (the shaded portion in Fig. 3 on the right) and a 2% chance that the result would be 0 point. The participants were not provided with these percentages; rather, they estimated the percentages by examining the prize wheels. After negotiations ended, this resulted in the ticket holder having a 37% chance of winning 12 points (.5 ∗ .74), a 49% chance of winning 34 points (.5 ∗ .98), and a 14% chance of winning nothing (.5 .26 + .5 .02). This results in an expected value of about 21 for the lottery ticket.

Two different kinds of lottery tickets were used, in which the number of points were the same on each ticket, but the chances of winning differed. Sellers in half of the dyads received the lottery ticket and associated prize wheel described above with an expected value of 21 points. Sellers in the other half received a lottery ticket and associated prize wheel with the same points (12 and 34) but with chances of winning reduced to result in a lower expected value of 3 points. This expected value is calculated as follows. The chance of winning 12 points was 1% and winning 34 points was 17%. With this second lottery, the ticket holder therefore had a 0.5% chance of winning 12 points (.5 ∗ .01), an 8.5% chance of winning 34 points (.5 ∗ .17), and a 91% chance of winning nothing (.5 ∗ .99 + .5 ∗ .83).

We used this combination of two different winning amounts (one relatively high and one relatively low) and two different sets of chances to win (one risk averse and one risk seeking) in order to increase the generality of our experiment. The winning amounts and the sets of chances were determined based on a methodology developed and validated by Berg et al. [A1]. First, the winning amounts of 12 and 34 points were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by two random draws. Next, these two amounts were associated with a risk averse utility function, which resulted in the risk averse lottery ticket described above having a 74% chance of winning 12 points, a 98% chance of winning 34 points, and an expected value of 21 points. Finally, the two amounts were associated with a risk seeking utility function, which resulted in the risk seeking lottery ticket described above, having a 1% chance of winning 12 points, an 8.5% chance of winning 34 points, and an expected value of 3 points. Each dyad received either a risk averse or a risk seeking lottery ticket, with a 50% chance to spin for 12 points and a 50% chance to spin for 34 points, as suggested by Berg et al. [A1].

At the start of negotiation the seller was the owner of the ticket. If the seller decided to keep the ticket and won the lottery (the prize wheel landed on the shaded area), he or she received points equal to the actual ticket value: 12 or 34 points; if the seller lost (the prize wheel landed on the unshaded area) he or she got zero points. In either case, the buyer received zero points. If the seller sold the ticket, he or she received points equal to the selling price, and the buyer received points equal to his or her lottery winnings (determined as described above for the seller) less the amount he or she used to purchase the ticket. At the end of all negotiations, a negotiation was randomly chosen to receive an extra \$10. Based on the number of points for that negotiation as described above, either the seller or buyer received the \$10 bonus.

## Procedure

As subjects arrived at the experimental site, they were randomly assigned as buyer or seller and escorted to one of two locations, depending on that role. There they received instructions about the experimental task via PowerPoint, and completed three practice rounds of negotiation with an instructor. During these exercises, subjects were encouraged to ask questions if they were uncertain about the game procedures and objectives. In the practice rounds, they were told the following. (1) The experiment began when a lottery ticket, a prize wheel, a timer, and instructions appeared on their computer screen. (2) After negotiations ended, the appropriate prize wheel would be spun in order to determine the number of points the ticket holder had won. (3) Dyad negotiation partners were in different locations but would see the same information at the same time on their computer screens. (4) Anyone could initiate negotiation by contacting their dyad partner via the assigned medium, either by text using instant messaging or orally using a computer supported audio. (5) They had three and a half minutes within which to complete their negotiations. (This time limit was based on a pilot study where subjects engaged in the same task as the current study, but in which dyads had no time limit. The maximum time taken by any dyad was about 3 1/2 min. The maximum time taken by dyads in the current study was 3 min.) (6) Before the time elapsed, the seller had to indicate via the PC what the parties agreed on in terms of the decision to sell or not to sell the ticket. If the seller did not make such an indication and the time elapsed, both the seller and buyer would receive no points.
