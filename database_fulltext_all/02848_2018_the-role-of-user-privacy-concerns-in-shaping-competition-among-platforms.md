# The Role of User Privacy Concerns in Shaping Competition Among Platforms

Esther Gal-Or,<sup>a</sup> Ronen Gal-Or,<sup>b</sup> Nabita Penmetsa<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Katz Graduate School of Business, University of Pittsburgh, Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania 15260; <sup>b</sup> D’Amore-McKim School of Business, Northeastern University, Boston, Massachusetts 02115; <sup>c</sup> David Eccles School of Business, University of Utah, Salt Lake City, Utah 84112 Contact: esther@katz.pitt.edu, http://orcid.org/0000-0002-5988-8839 (EG-O); r.galor@northeastern.edu (RG-O); nabita@eccles.utah.edu (NP)

Received: July 22, 2015 Revised: March 5, 2016; October 27, 2016; March 24, 2017 Accepted: May 1, 2017 Published Online in Articles in Advance: April 3, 2018

https://doi.org/10.1287/isre.2017.0730

Copyright: © 2018 INFORMS

Abstract. We study the efect of user privacy concerns on competition between online advertising platforms. Online platforms attract advertisers by ofering capabilities to reach audiences likely to be receptive to their ads in a timely and accurate manner. However, the collection and processing of user information required for targeting of ads may lead to privacy concerns. We model the competition between two platforms as a two-stage game where platforms announce their targeting capabilities in the first stage and advertising fees in the second stage. The presence of heterogeneity in the user and the advertiser populations with respect to their preferences for targeting leads to diferentiation between platforms. While one platform ofers the minimum level of targeting feasible, the other platform ofers a strictly higher level of targeting. The extent of diferentiation in targeting levels depends on the intensity of competition between the platforms on the user side. When competition on the user side is relatively low, the extent of diferentiation is higher. Such competition for users may decline, when users are less concerned about loss of privacy or when they choose to double home. Higher targeting diferentiation allows platforms to charge higher advertising fees and earn higher profits. We also consider the case where platforms can reduce the privacy concerns of users by ofering them greater control over their personal information. We demonstrate that awarding user control leads to reduced targeting diferentiation between platforms and lower advertising fees. Last, we derive the equilibrium targeting levels for platforms that use a subscription-based business model instead of an advertising-based business model.

## 1. Introduction

Digital advertising has been experiencing tremendous growth in the past decade and has surpassed other adsupported media such as television and newspapers in terms of revenue and growth. The Interactive Advertising Bureau (IAB) reported that Internet advertising revenues in the United States reached \$49.5 billion in 2014 representing a 16% increase over the revenue reported in 2013. In addition to a shift in the viewing patterns of users, a major factor contributing to the growth of online and mobile advertising is the ability of platforms to match advertisements to interests of users and to measure the efectiveness of advertising campaigns. Digital platforms have the ability to collect user data and track user online behavior such as browsing patterns, shopping preferences, and social interactions. They use this information to build a profile of the user and deliver ads to users who are likely to be interested in the product or service being advertised, a process that has been termed targeted advertising (Tucker 2012, Goldfarb 2014). Targeting can improve the efectiveness of advertising by delivering ads to the desired user segments and by reducing the number of wasted impressions.

Digital advertising platforms such as search engines, browsers, social networks, and email service providers depend on advertising as a major source of their revenues. Platforms can charge higher advertising prices by investing in better targeting technologies and increasing the efectiveness of their ads. Firms such as Google, Facebook, Yahoo, and AOL that operate the leading advertising platforms have been improving their ad-serving and targeting capabilities by making huge investments in technology infrastructure and acquiring advanced analytics and intelligence capabilities. The acquisitions focus primarily on specialized ad-technology companies such as Double Click, Live Rail, and Brightroll. While such eforts help platforms to compete efectively for advertising dollars, they may have a negative influence on the platforms’ appeal to users. Tracking and collecting data about users to target advertising can raise privacy concerns among users. Although users may benefit from exposure to relevant ads that match their interests rather than random ads, the resulting loss of privacy may discourage users from adopting the platform. Empirical studies on targeting and privacy concerns (Xu et al. 2012, Tucker 2014) in online advertising provide ample evidence that such concerns can lead consumers to avoid ads that seem intrusive. Disclosure of data collection practices by firms, in response to regulatory pressures and media coverage (Wall Street Journal series on privacy<sup>2</sup>), have further heightened privacy concerns among users and attracted the attention of regulatory authorities and consumer advocacy groups. The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) has been pushing for industry selfregulation and standards such as the Do Not Track (DNT) feature to provide some control to users over their information. However, regulation alone cannot achieve a comprehensive plan to protect user concerns without causing damage to the industry (Tang et al. 2008 and Chellappa and Shivendu 2007). Therefore, the FTC is encouraging competition between platforms to protect consumer privacy as evidenced in the statement of the FTC commissioner above.

Because digital advertising platforms have to compete both for users and advertisers, it is important to understand how the trade-of between advertising efectiveness and user privacy considerations afects the behavior of competing platforms. On the advertiser side, platforms compete both in terms of the superiority of the advertising technology that they ofer and in terms of the ad prices they charge. On the user side, platforms usually ofer their services free of charge, and competition among the platforms relates to the attributes of the primary service that they ofer. Platforms may invest, for instance, in new functionality or in providing a unique user interface. In addition to competing based on the attributes of the primary service, however, platforms can also gain competitive advantage by positioning their platform as being less intrusive or by limiting the amount of data that they use for targeting advertising messages. The growing popularity of privacy apps and services such as

Snapchat, Cyberdust, and DuckDuckGo indicates that users are embracing privacy tools to protect their personal information. Studies such as Tsai et al. (2011) and Tucker (2014) show that platforms can gain by ofering users increased control over their information. In fact, there are recent examples of platforms that have emphasized higher protection of user privacy by limiting targeting on their platforms. For example, Microsoft announced that it would not use the content of emails for targeting ads in a move to compete with Google’s email service that regularly uses the content of emails to target ads.<sup>3</sup> It also introduced a DNT feature as the default option in the more recent versions of Internet Explorer (versions 9 and 10),<sup>4</sup> thus improving privacy protection on its browser and related products. By contrast, Facebook has recently announced that it plans to launch ads within Messenger by allowing advertisers to send ads as messages to people who previously initiated a chat thread with them.<sup>5</sup> In addition, even though Facebook Messenger has the option of end-to-end encryption in special private messaging mode, this option is not the default option, and most users do not turn it on.<sup>6</sup> These examples highlight the fact that platforms difer in the manner in which they evaluate the trade-of between higher efectiveness of ads and improved user privacy protection. The examples in Table 1 illustrate that it is usually the bigger platform in a given service category that tends to choose in favor of higher efectiveness of advertising.

Empirical studies and industry reports (Turow et al. 2009, Goldfarb and Tucker 2011) suggest that not all consumers are equally averse to targeted advertising and to sharing of personal information. There are large variations in the manner in which users react to targeted ads. Some consumers prefer to see relevant rather than random ads, even at the expense of sharing personal information. Similarly, not all advertisers benefit equally from targeting and difer, therefore, in their willingness to pay for improved targeting. Firms selling niche products that cater to specialized segments of users derive greater benefit from high levels of targeting, to reach the specific segments interested in their products. By contrast, firms selling products that appeal to a larger spectrum of diferent consumer segments benefit to a lesser extent from targeting. Existing literature (Chen and Stallaert 2010) provides evidence for the presence of such heterogeneity among advertisers. Because there is heterogeneity in consumers’ attitudes toward targeting and in advertisers’ willingness to pay for targeted ads, we characterize the nature of competition in targeting levels that diferent platforms ofer. In particular, we investigate whether platforms choose to diferentiate in terms of the level of targeting capabilities that they ofer to advertisers, which in turn, also influences the user side.

We model the competition between platforms as a two-stage game where platforms announce their investment in targeting capabilities in the first stage and advertising prices in the second stage. Both users and advertisers make their platform choices based on the targeting capability ofered by the platform. For advertisers, higher targeting capabilities can increase the efectiveness of ads by raising the probability of a click or a sale. Although targeting also benefits users by allowing them to view relevant ads, it may also inflict disutility on users by exposing them to the potential negative consequences of sharing their personal information. We refer to the disutility inflicted on users as the privacy cost of utilizing a higher targeting platform. To illustrate the potential existence of such privacy costs, consider, for example, Google’s strategy of tapping ofline data to improve the efectiveness of mobile ads. This requires integrating online activity data that Google collects about a user with in-store data provided by third-party data vendors such as Acxiom and Datalogix. Improved targeting can be achieved by matching the cookie information on a user’s phone or other mobile device to a user’s email account registered with a retailer. However, this improved targeting introduces increased risk to users for potential breach of privacy.

Assuming that both the user and advertiser markets are fully covered, we first consider an environment where both users and advertisers adopt a single platform, namely, they both single home, using terminology introduced in the literature on platform competition. We also assume that the users’ choice between platforms depends solely on the trade-of between privacy and relevancy of ads. We find that the existence of significant diferentiation between platforms is crucial to ensure the coexistence of both in the market. Such significant diferentiation arises only when the extent of heterogeneity in the advertiser and user populations is suficiently large and the marginal cost of achieving improved efectiveness of ad targeting is not excessively high. At such equilibrium, one platform ofers the minimum possible level of targeting and the other platform chooses a strictly higher level of targeting. The extent of targeting diferentiation between the platforms depends on the intensity of competition for users, with stifer competition leading to reduced targeting diferentiation. Competition for users intensifies with heightened user privacy concerns. When diferentiation in targeting levels is feasible, both the advertiser and user populations are segmented. Advertisers who benefit more from ad targeting and users with greater appreciation to viewing relevant ads choose the platform that ofers the higher targeting level. Advertisers and users of the opposite profiles choose the platform that ofers minimum targeting. At such a segmenting equilibrium, the platform that ofers the higher targeting level commands larger market shares in both the advertiser and the user markets, charges higher prices, and earns higher profits. Relaxing the assumption that the advertiser market is fully covered reduces the extent of diferentiation between the platforms, as the low targeting platform raises its targeting level above the minimum to attract additional advertisers that otherwise would stay out of the market.

We extend the analysis by considering the possibility that platforms ofer users control over their personal information. In this modified environment, each platform chooses its investment in the maximum level of targeting that it can ofer to advertisers, but it also provides privacy tools to users that allow them to personalize the level of targeting to fit their tastes. When a user chooses a platform, she compares her desired level of targeting with the maximum level ofered by the platform. If this maximum level is higher than that considered optimal by the user, the user utilizes the privacy tools ofered by the platform to lower the extent of targeting to the level she considers optimal. If the maximum level ofered by the platform is lower than the optimal level desired by the user, the user does not use any extra privacy precautions. We show that, in this setting, the high targeting platform reduces its investment in the targeting technology and the low targeting platform raises its investment in targeting. With reduced targeting diferentiation between platforms, advertising prices and the profits of the platforms decline.

Next, we consider an environment where users or advertisers may choose the services of both platforms, namely, may decide to double home. Double homing of users has two counteracting efects on the incentives of the platforms to invest in the targeting technology. Because double homing of users implies that competition for users is weaker, the high targeting platform can raise its targeting intensity to improve its appeal to advertisers without significant concern over losing users. This leads to a stronger incentive to improve its targeting technology. On the other hand, advertisers may derive less benefit from advertising if most of the platform’s users double home because these users are likely to spend less time on the platform, and therefore, less likely to be exposed to the advertisers’ message. With reduced benefit from advertising, advertisers have a lower willingness to pay for improved targeting and the high targeting platform has an incentive to cut its investment in the targeting technology. Hence, double homing may lead to either intensified or reduced diferentiation between the platforms contingent on the extent to which advertisers discount the double homing segment of users in assessing the benefit they derive from advertising with a given platform. This ambiguity regarding the extent of targeting diferentiation also leads to ambiguity regarding the efect of double homing of users on advertising fees. We also investigate the efect of double homing by advertisers. We demonstrate that it reduces price competition on the advertiser side of the market and, therefore, reduces the platforms’ incentives to diferentiate in terms of their targeting levels. In particular, circumstances may arise under which the low targeting platform raises its targeting level above the minimum. We find that in this case the degree of diferentiation and the average investment in the targeting technology decline when advertisers choose to double home. Similar to the environment with single homing, platforms remain diferentiated in this environment as well, with the higher targeting platform commanding higher fees and earning higher profits.

Our analysis provides insights to some of the dynamics that we can observe in the online advertising industry. Among the cases we mentioned in Table 1, the high targeting platforms, such as Google and Facebook have emerged as dominant players having the larger market share among both users and advertisers. This allows them to charge higher advertising prices when compared to competitors. Low targeting platforms such as Microsoft emphasize privacy protection by building better privacy features into their services. It is interesting though that the privacy protection strategy of Microsoft has evolved over time as its market share in the browser market declined. In 2008, when Microsoft introduced Internet Explorer (IE) 8, it had the biggest share of the browser market (about 60%). At this time, developers wanted to design IE8 to automatically thwart common tracking tools, unless a user deliberately switched to settings afording less privacy. However, executives in Microsoft’s ad business objected to making the DNT feature the default option and prevailed in the debate with product planners. Microsoft did include the DNT feature as the default option when it introduced IE9 and IE10. The market share of Microsoft in the browser market at the time these versions of the product became available has declined significantly, to around 40%. This behavior is consistent with the prediction of our model, that it is the smaller platform that tends to diferentiate itself by ofering greater privacy protection to users.<sup>7</sup>

To understand further the unique characteristics of the advertising-based business model, we extend our formulation to also consider platforms whose main source of revenues is from subscription fees of users. Platforms may continue to compete in terms of their targeting capabilities in this case as well to provide better content to users. For instance, Netflix and HBO can use improved targeting capabilities to make compatible recommendations for movies to their subscribers. We find that platforms will continue to be diferentiated in this case as well to alleviate competition on subscription fees. However, in contrast to the advertising-based equilibrium that predicts dominance and higher profits to the high targeting platform, in the subscription-based equilibrium, the low targeting platform may be the more profitable one. The reason for this diferent result is that revenues now accrue from a population that has mixed preferences regarding higher levels of targeting. Hence, unlike in the advertising-based model, the high targeting platform does not have a natural advantage anymore in the population that generates revenues for the platform.

## 1.1. Literature Review

Our work contributes to the emerging literature on targeting in online advertising. There are diferent categories of targeting in online advertising. They include keyword, contextual, and behavioral targeting. Using keyword targeting, search platforms display ads related to the keywords used by a user. Contextual advertising matches advertisements to the content of the page that a consumer is currently viewing. For example, a consumer who visits a sports site may see advertisements for golf clubs or baseball tickets on that site. Behavioral advertising matches advertisements to the interests of the consumer as determined over time. By tracking user online behavior and collecting demographic data, platforms build a profile for the user, which they use for targeting advertisements. With the exception of the paper by Chen and Stallaert (2010) who study targeting in the context of behavioral advertising, most of the current literature on targeting by platforms focuses on keyword and contextual advertising (De Corniere and De Nĳs 2016, Jerath et al. 2011). These studies consider the interaction between platforms, advertising firms that are competitors in the same product market, and users. The studies provide insights on how keyword-targeting impacts firm advertising strategies, product prices, and welfare of users. The relationship between targeted advertising and the extent of competition in product markets has also been investigated when advertisers themselves (and not the platforms) choose the extent of targeting (see, for instance, Iyer et al. 2005 and Gal-Or et al. 2006). In the context of behavioral advertising, Chen and Stallaert (2010) investigate whether a platform intermediary benefits from ofering behavioral targeting and how its choice afects competition between advertisers and user propensity to click on ads. In this paper, there is a single platform, and therefore, the paper does not address the issue of competition between platforms. Even though De Corniere and De Nĳs (2016) consider targeting competition between platform intermediaries, as we do, the focus of this study is on predicting the efect of targeting on product prices. By contrast, we focus on understanding the efect of competition among platforms on the level of advertising fees platforms can charge and the precision of the targeting technology that they choose to invest in, when services are ofered to users free of charge.

The issue of user privacy has been studied in the context of personalization of web services (Pavlou 2011 provides a summary of the information systems (IS) literature on information privacy). For example, Chellappa and Shivendu (2006 and 2010) analyze how user privacy concerns and advertiser marginal value of customer information influence the service levels and strategies of a personalization vendor in a monopoly setting. Similar to our modeling, in these papers as well, users have heterogeneous preferences in the manner in which they evaluate the benefits and costs of personalization, the benefit being the improved services personalization ofers and cost being the increased cost of privacy breaches. Casadesus-Masanell and Hervas-Drane (2015) have recently addressed the efect of privacy considerations in shaping two-sided market competition among platforms. In this paper, users choose how much information to share with platforms and platforms choose how much of this information to disclose to others. Similar to our setting, when users share information with a platform, they face both benefits and costs. In contrast to our formulation, however, where revenues from advertising facilitate ofering services to users free of charge, in this previous paper, platforms charge users for the services they provide. Moreover, in Casadesus-Masanell and Hervas-Drane (2015), the revenue generated from selling information of users is an exogenous function of the degree of information disclosure selected by the platform. By contrast, in our setting, platforms choose the prices they charge advertisers, and advertisers self-select on which platform to place their ads. Hence, in our model, both sides of the market served by the platform (both users and advertisers) strategically choose their desired platform, whereas the previous paper models only the strategic choice of users.

On the conceptual front, our work relates to the literature on two-sided markets. While the pioneering works of Caillaud and Jullien (2003), Rochet and Tirole (2003), Parker and Van Alstyne (2005), and Armstrong (2006) provide the analytical framework for studying pricing in two-sided markets, subsequent studies have focused on understanding the role of cross-group network externalities and nonprice variables in shaping the pricing and competitive strategies of platforms. For example, Bhargava and Choudhary (2004) show how an intermediary providing matching services can use versioning to improve profitability. They assume heterogeneity among agents on both sides of the market, as we do, and arrive at a similar segmenting equilibrium where ofering vertically diferentiated variants of a service leads to segmentation of both sides of the market and higher monopoly profits. Hagiu and Spulber (2013) investigate how a platform can use a nonprice variable, in their case, investment in first-party content, to improve coordination between the two sides and fight competition. Anderson et al. (2015) show that in a content-driven two-sided market, higher investment in platform performance may not always yield the competitive edge. Because of the presence of cross-group network externalities, platforms have to assess the objectives of users and third-party content developers to achieve higher profits. Focusing more specifically on media markets, Fan et al. (2007) consider the choice digital media companies face between selling programs online and using an advertising strategy instead to generate revenues. They find that the quality of the program and the cost users face to access programs online determine the optimal choice. Gal-Or and Dukes (2003) and Anderson and Coate (2005) model competition for advertisers among media platforms. They demonstrate the possible existence of equilibria with either maximal or no content diferentiation among the platforms. Building on this framework, studies on media competition (see, for instance, Anderson and Gabszewicz 2006, Peitz and Valletti 2008, and Reisinger 2012) developed models of horizontal diferentiation between competing platforms to analyze the efect of diferentiation in the user market on equilibrium advertising levels. While the above papers consider heterogeneity only on the user side, Gal-Or et al. (2012) also consider heterogeneity among advertisers. They investigate the efect of heterogeneity among advertisers on the extent of horizontal diferentiation (polarization or bias in news media) on the user side. We contribute to the above literature by investigating the manner in which competing platforms balance the competing interests of users and advertisers in their attempts to appeal to both sides of the market they serve. In contrast to the above studies, where both sides of the market served by the intermediaries are modeled as being horizontally diferentiated, in our model the advertiser side is modeled as being vertically and the user side as being horizontally diferentiated.

## 2. The Model

There are two platforms i <sup></sup> 1, 2 competing for users and advertisers in an online advertising market. As is common in many online markets, the platforms ofer free services to users and raise revenue by selling advertising space. We assume that the two platforms ofer identical services to users with the exception that they may choose to diferentiate in terms of the extent of precision of the targeting capabilities that they ofer to advertisers.<sup>8</sup> Thus, while Gmail and Hotmail ofer very similar emailing services, they may ofer advertisers diferent capabilities to reach targeted segments of the population of users. Let $t _ { i }$ and $p _ { i }$ designate the targeting capability and the price per ad<sup>9</sup> (referred to in the industry as cost per thousand impressions) chosen by platform i. Both variables are restricted to be nonnegative. The variable $t _ { i }$ indicates the number of targeting options ofered by the platform. For example, Facebook allows advertisers to target ads based on location, age and gender, demographics, behavior, interests, and connections. Within each of these targeting options, there are multiple ways for an advertiser to identify its audience. For example, using location as a targeting option, an advertiser can further classify users based on users who live in a specific location, users recently in this location, and users traveling in this location. Ofering a larger number of targeting options allows advertisers to define their target audience more precisely. Therefore, the variable $t _ { i }$ also represents the precision with which the platform can match an ad to a user who may be interested in the product or service advertised. As users interested in the product advertised are more likely to respond to the ad, more precise targeting can improve the efectiveness of an ad. Platforms typically do not share consumer data with advertisers. An advertiser interested in advertising on the platform defines the characteris tics for her target market. The platform uses its targeting technology to find users with characteristics similar to those defined by the advertiser. Advertisers can observe the performance of an ad through metrics such as click through rates (CTRs), website visits, or purchases. Ofering better targeting capabilities requires the platform to collect and process a large number of details about a user and divide the user population into finer segments (Johar et al. 2014). Platforms incur costs to collect and process information about users, and better targeting requires, therefore, higher investments in storage solutions, data processing capabilities, pow erful analytics technologies, and third-party relations. We designate the cost of generating the precision level t by $c ( t )$ , and assume that $c ( t ) = c \bar { t } ^ { 2 }$ . Hence, the cost is increasing at an increasing rate.<sup>10</sup> Both platforms have access to the same cost function.

Advertisers difer by the benefit they derive from improved targeting. Depending on the nature of the product or service advertised and the objective of the advertising campaign, advertisers may either look for a narrow or broad audience. For instance, a seller of organic baby diapers can define his target audience as parents of 0-3 year-old children, with more than \$100,000 in household income, read natural health blogs, and who have a record of shopping in luxury stores. By contrast, an online retail store such as Zappos may define its target audience as men and women in the age group of 18–35 years old. Similarly, an auto manufacturer such as Ford or GM running a brand awareness campaign may define its target audience more broadly compared to an auto dealer in San Jose, California advertising a sales promotion. We use the parameter $\gamma$ to designate the specificity of an advertiser’s target audience, namely, the number of criteria required to define an advertiser’s target audience. We assume that $\gamma$ is uniformly<sup>11</sup> distributed over the support $[ \gamma , \bar { \gamma } ]$ with $\underline { { \gamma } } > 0$ . A higher $\gamma$ indicates an adver-<sup>¯ ¯</sup>tiser with a more specifically defined target audience, who can benefit more from a bigger number of targeting criteria. A lower $\gamma$ indicates an advertiser with a broadly defined target audience who does not require a large number of targeting options to identify this audience. The seller of organic baby diapers will have a higher γ compared to an online retailer. Similarly, an auto dealer trying to advertise his special promotion will have a higher $\gamma$ compared to an automaker running a brand awareness campaign.

Platform i charges an advertiser the price $p _ { i }$ for a fixed number of impressions. We interpret this fixed number of 1,000 as a proxy for the number of viewers (eyeballs) who see the ad. The net payof that the advertiser derives from this payment is equal to

$$

where the variable $n _ { i }$ designates the size of the population of users who subscribe to platform i.

The first two terms of the payof function measure the gross expected benefit to the advertiser from the likely business impact<sup>12</sup> that advertising on the platform might generate. This expected benefit comprises two components. The first component captures the fixed benefit an advertiser derives from advertising on the platform, using the basic (nontargeting) functionality ofered by the platform. Because $r ^ { \prime } ( \gamma ) < 0$ , this benefit is the highest for advertisers that require the least specificity in targeting customers (i.e., type γ) and the <sup>¯</sup>lowest for advertisers that require the most specificity $( \mathrm { i . e . , t y p e } \bar { \gamma } )$ . For example, an advertiser may create and promote a page on Facebook to build social presence and connect with customers. Even when the advertiser does not utilize any targeting options ofered by the platform, the advertiser may gain some value from his sheer presence on the platform, when a randomly chosen audience among the platform’s followers view the page. This value will be lower for a business with a narrowly defined customer base. On the other hand, an advertiser like Procter & Gamble can capture a higher value from its presence on a platform, even without using any targeting options, because of its appeal to a broader customer base. Even a randomly chosen group of users is likely to engage with the ad and generate the desired impact, such as liking the page or becoming customers. Because the first term captures the benefit from random placement of the 1,000 impressions among the users of the platform, it does not depend on the total number of users, active on the platform. Any random choice of 1,000 users from the total yields the same fixed benefit.

The second component of the net payof function is the additional benefit advertisers derive from utilizing the targeting features ofered by the platform. The product $n _ { i } t _ { i }$ in (1) measures the efectiveness of targeting in identifying and reaching the right audience. Success in identifying the right group of users depends both on the level of targeting selected by the platform and the number of users who choose platform i. The platform finds the right group of users by employing targeting technologies that rely on data generated by user activity to identify groups of users who exhibit similar patterns. Because of network efects, a bigger number of users on a platform can generate more user activity, thus leading to superior data in terms of both quantity and quality. Better quality of user data can improve the performance of targeting technologies. For example, an advertiser can reach new customers on Facebook by promoting his business page to users who are similar to his own customers, using the lookalike audience feature. Having a bigger number of users allows the platform to infer more accurately the characteristics of its users that are likely to be consistent with the advertiser’s customers. The γ measure of the advertiser multiplies the second term of the net payof function. Namely, the benefit from targeting depends on the number of criteria required to identify the advertiser’s target audience.

Moreover, for any fixed number of selected targeting criteria, when a platform has many users it can aford to be “picky” in the manner in which it chooses the target audience of 1,000 users who satisfy the criteria. It can rank, for instance, users according to how strongly they satisfy the criteria to ensure that those selected are more likely to purchase the advertised product. For the example of the seller of organic baby diapers, the platform can improve the odds of purchase by selecting users who read natural health blogs very frequently, rather than only occasionally, or who have a regular, rather than a scant, record of shopping in luxury stores. If w designates the probability of a user who satisfies a certain number of targeting criteria to actually purchase the product, then the above discussion implies that the platform can increase w when it has many users. With many users, it can pick viewers with a stronger record of satisfying the criteria. As a result, the fixed 1,000 impressions/viewers that the price $p _ { i }$ buys yield higher benefit to the advertiser because, in expectation, the ad yields a bigger number of new potential customers (i.e., 1,000 <sup>×</sup> w).

Users difer in the benefit they derive from targeted ads. While some consumers prefer to see relevant ads instead of random teeth whitening ads, other users are concerned about the privacy implications of targeting. Similarly, outdoor enthusiasts may benefit from exposure to targeted ads from outdoor brands such as Patagonia and REI. The utility of the user consists of three components. The first component a is the benefit the user derives from the basic service (email or search services) ofered by the platform. This benefit is common to all users and is determined independent of the targeting level chosen by the platform. The second component αt measures the added utility users derive when being exposed to ads more consistent with their preferences. Greater consistency in the delivery of ads is feasible when a platform invests more resources in its targeting technology $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ chooses a bigger value of t). The added utility from viewing relevant ads varies in the population of users. Specifically, users having more particular needs (those having higher values of the parameter α) benefit more from improved consistency between their preferences and the ads delivered to them. The last component of the utility function in (2) $- \beta t ^ { 2 }$ reflects privacy concerns of users. Users are aware of the fact that when a platform chooses to invest in higher precision of its targeting technology, it becomes more likely that privacy breaches might occur, thus reducing the expected benefit they derive from the services ofered by the platform. The coeficient $\beta$ is common to all users and measures the overall privacy concerns in the population.<sup>17</sup>

If the parameter a is suficiently large, it follows from (2) that all individuals derive positive utility from subscribing to a platform, and as a result, the market of users is fully covered. This assumption is consistent with the reality that, by now, almost all consumers use email, search, or social network services, especially because platforms ofer these services free of charge to consumers. From (2), the utility of individuals with higher α values is higher both in total (because $u _ { \alpha } > 0 )$ and in the margin (because $u _ { t \alpha } > 0 )$ Hence, individuals with more particular needs benefit more from improved targeting of ads. Note that, even though advertisers may difer in the marginal benefit they derive from improved targeting, they all have an unambiguous preference for improved efectiveness of targeting users who are more likely to purchase their products. By contrast, users difer according to what they consider an optimal level of ad targeting. The heterogeneity in the advertiser and user populations in our model captures these diferent preferences for ad targeting of the two sides of the market that the platforms serve. On the advertiser side, we assume that advertisers all value improved efectiveness of targeting ads, albeit at diferent levels of valuation (an assumption typical of models of vertical product diferentiation). On the user side, we assume that each user has a diferent targeting level that they consider optimal (an assumption typical of models of horizontal product diferentiation). Because users receive services from the platforms for free, it is essential that they difer in what they consider to be an optimal level of targeting to support equilibrium in which both platforms can command positive market shares among users. If users had preferences typical of models of vertical product diferentiation, where they would all agree that higher relevancy of ads is beneficial regardless of privacy issues, or greater privacy protection is beneficial regardless of excessive exposure to irrelevant ads, then only one platform would survive. We believe that the assumption we make is realistic because users tend to appreciate both ads more consistent with their tastes and greater privacy. They are aware of the fact that there might be a trade-of between these two attributes of online advertising, and diferent consumers evaluate this trade-of diferently (Chellappa and Sin 2005). For instance, according to a survey conducted by the IAB, 58% of users in the age group of 16–24 are happy to see targeted ads even if it means that their browsing history is tracked. On the other hand, only 38% of users in the age group of 55<sup>+</sup> prefer to see relevant ads, based on browsing history, over random ads.

We assume that each user chooses to subscribe to only one platform, and similarly, each advertiser chooses to place an ad with only one platform. The literature on two-sided markets has referred to this choice as single homing. In the context of our environment, users choose the services of only one email service provider (Gmail or Hotmail) or only one search engine (Google or Bing). This behavior may be the result of hassle costs of utilizing multiple platforms, once an individual gains familiarity with one of them. It can also be the result of network externalities in the use of social networks. Single homing of advertisers may be the result of limited advertising budgets. In Section 5, we relax the assumption of single homing and allow each side to double home. In addition, for simplicity, we also assume that while an advertiser’s gain from using a platform depends on the number of users on the platform, the utility of a user does not vary with the number of advertisers on the platform. As users see a fixed number of ads per session because of limited advertising slots, the utility of users only depends on the relevancy of those ads. Using terminology of two-sided markets, such an environment has been characterized as one-sided network externality (advertisers care about the size of the opposite side but users do not).

We model the environment as consisting of two stages. In the first stage, platforms simultaneously choose their levels of targeting capabilities, in the second stage they simultaneously choose their advertising fees, and users and advertisers choose one of the platforms after observing their fees and targeting capabilities. The design of a targeting technology with high precision requires technological information technology (IT)-investments of an irreversible nature. To capture this feature, we model the environment with platforms committing to the precision of their targeting technology in stage 1 prior to choosing their fees in stage 2. We wish to characterize the equilibrium where platforms choose to diferentiate in terms of the level of targeting capabilities they ofer to advertisers. With $t _ { 2 } > t _ { 1 } ,$ , platform 2 ofers improved targeting of ads in comparison to platform 1. As a result, users who prefer better targeting even at the expense of lower privacy (bigger values of α<sup>)</sup> and advertisers that can benefit to a greater extent from improved targeting (bigger values of γ<sup>)</sup> are likely to prefer platform 2 over platform 1. While such a deviation would establish platform 1 as the platform that ofers the more precise targeting technology, it would also lead to intensified competition on advertising fees between the platforms. Note that when segmentation fails because one of the conditions in part (ii) of Lemma 1 is not satisfied, platforms become completely homogeneous. In the absence of diferentiation, platforms compete fiercely in setting advertising fees and the Bertrand outcome of marginal cost pricing (zero in our case) arises, thus eliminating any profitability from investment in the targeting technology. However, this outcome cannot correspond to equilibrium, as each platform would have an incentive, in this case, to slightly increase its targeting level to alleviate price competition. Hence, if the conditions of part (ii) fail, the equilibrium cannot support two active platforms and a single platform dominates the entire market. The literature on platform competition (Damiano and Li 2008) illustrated that achieving segmentation of the two populations served by the platforms is dificult in platform competition because of cross-group network externalities. In our paper, because advertisers care about the size of the user market served by the platforms, each platform has a strong incentive to dominate the entire market. Only when diferentiation between the two platforms is significant, as implied by the conditions stated in part (ii) of Proposition 1, coexistence of two platforms is possible.<sup>20</sup>

Note that, as in the traditional quality diferentiation model of Shaked and Sutton (1982), in our environment as well, platforms choose to diferentiate in terms of the levels of targeting they ofer to advertisers to allow them to compete successfully in the market. However, when establishing diferentiation in the traditional one-sided market, firms consider only the preferences of one population (consumers in the quality competition model of Shaked and Sutton 1982). By contrast, platforms have to appeal to two diferent populations when making their investment decisions in the targeting technology (users and advertisers in our case). Those two populations may have conflicting preferences regarding the platforms’ choices, whereas in the traditional quality competition environment, consumers all agree that higher quality is better. To illustrate how these conflicting interests of the two populations afect the platforms’ choices, consider Equa tion (7). The strategic efect included in this equation is similar to that in the traditional quality competition model. In both environments, each firm adjusts its investment, downward for the low-quality (targeting) firm and upward for the high-quality (targeting) firm, to induce the competitor to raise its price. The diference between the two environments relates to the direct efect included in the equation. The expressions for ${ { \partial \gamma } ^ { * } } / { { \partial { t _ { 1 } } } }$ and ${ { \partial \gamma } ^ { * } } / { { \partial { t _ { 2 } } } }$ in $\hat { ( 7 ) }$ do not only capture the preferences of the paying population (advertisers) but also the preferences of the nonpaying population (users). In traditional one-sided markets, only the preferences of the paying population (consumers) matter. Incorporating the preferences of the nonpaying population in our environment leads to moderation in the direct incentives of platforms to diferentiate in comparison to competitors in one-sided markets. Consider, for instance, the direct efect of a decision by platform 1 to raise its targeting level. Such a decision improves its appeal both to advertisers and to users because the threshold user who is indiferent between the two platforms considers its level of targeting insuficient. Hence, the nonpaying segment further increases the marginal benefit of improving targeting for platform 1. By contrast, when platform 2 increases its investment in targeting, the platform loses its appeal to the nonpaying segment of users because the threshold, indiferent user considers its level of targeting excessive. As a result, the preferences of the nonpaying segment lowers the marginal benefit platform 2 derives from improving its targeting technol ogy. The fact that users matter to the platforms in our two-sided market environment implies, therefore, reduced incentives to diferentiate between the platforms in comparison to traditional competitors in onesided markets. The equilibrium in our environment has platform 1 still choosing the lowest level of targeting feasible because of the strength of the strategic efect that pushes the investment of this platform downward to alleviate price competition with platform 2. However, platform 2 chooses a lower level of targeting than in an environment where only the preferences of one side of the market (advertisers) matter in its choice.

## 7. Suggestions for Empirical Testing of the Model

In this section, we suggest how to empirically test some of our assumptions and predictions. The first tests relate to the results we report in Proposition 1. This proposition predicts asymmetry in the targeting strategy of competing platforms, with the bigger platform investing greater resources in its targeting technology and the smaller platform ofering greater privacy protection to users. The diferent policies lead to asymmetry in the advertising fees that the platforms can command. To empirically test the implications of this proposition, we propose to evaluate how changes over time in the market shares of Google and Microsoft in the browser market afected the advertising fees that they could command. Proposition 1 predicts that advertising rates would be higher for Microsoft over the period 2008–2011 when it dominated the browser market and higher for Google over the past five years, now that it dominates the browser market. We also propose to evaluate the impact of the new privacy features introduced in IE9 and IE10 on market metrics such as new downloads and installs of the software and on advertising fees that Microsoft could command. Proposition 1 predicts that advertising fees would decrease following this browser upgrade.

Proposition 1 also predicts that platforms may attract diferent types of advertisers, with the high targeting platform appealing to advertisers of niche products and the low targeting platform appealing to advertisers of products that have a broader customer base. To test this prediction, we propose to compare the profile of advertisers on Google and Microsoft and evaluate whether Google indeed attracts advertisers that have a narrower customer base than Microsoft. To assess how narrow the customer base of a given advertiser is, we can use diferent proxies for the extent of heterogeneity among the advertiser’s customers in terms of income, geographic location, interests, social interaction online, and so on.

Similarly, Proposition 1 predicts that, in the case of single homing, platforms may attract users of diferent profiles, with the high targeting platform attracting users less concerned about protecting their privacy. Given that studies have documented that younger individuals are less concerned about privacy infringement than older individuals, it may be interesting to test whether there is significant diferences in the age of user groups that subscribe to competing platforms, and whether such diferences are related to the extent of targeting and/or privacy protection ofered by the platforms.

We also predict that privacy concerns of users reduce diferentiation between platforms by forcing the larger platform to lower its targeting. We can test this prediction by observing how news about privacy breaches or reports uncovering new privacy threats impact market metrics such as market shares or advertising prices of competing platforms.

In Proposition 2, we assert that ofering users new privacy controls may afect the efectiveness of the targeting technology, and therefore, the advertising fees that platforms can charge. We can test this assertion by evaluating the efect of Google’s or Facebook’s introduction of a centralized dashboard for privacy settings on advertising performance metrics such as clicks and page views, and on advertising prices.

## 8. Managerial and Policy Implications

Our analysis provides a deep understanding of the efect of privacy concerns on competition between online advertising platforms. Ofering diferentiated targeting capabilities allows platforms to alleviate price competition on the advertiser side and achieve higher profits. However, the presence of privacy concerns on the user side limits the extent of diferentiation achieved at the equilibrium. Specifically, the high targeting platform lowers its targeting level when considering user privacy concerns to gain a suficient share of the user market. This reduces diferentiation between platforms and intensifies price competition. It follows, therefore, that platforms can gain by focusing on developing horizontally diferentiated primary services so that users rely less heavily on privacy features and more heavily on the service features themselves when choosing between the platforms. Platforms can also minimize the impact of user privacy concerns by educating users about the benefits of targeting and providing greater control to users over their personal information. We also find that double homing by users can sometimes have a beneficial efect on platform profits as it reduces the intensity of competition on the user side.

We show that diferentiation also leads to asymmetry between the platforms in terms of their market shares and profits, with the high targeting platform gaining a bigger market share and higher profits. Our analysis indicates, however, that the manager of the smaller platform should resist the temptation to match the targeting capabilities of its competitor. Even though the smaller platform earns lower profits than the larger platform, its profits would decline even further if it decided to imitate the targeting capabilities of its competitor. Our analysis also indicates that the preference of the platforms in favor of targeting diferentiation persists even when user subscription fees is the source of revenue for the platforms. However, in contrast to the advertising-based business model, in such a subscription-based model, the low targeting platform may end up being the dominant and more profitable platform.

We also demonstrate that the dominance of the higher targeting platform among users is not the result of users lacking options to choose from or because users are unaware of the increased risk for privacy breaches that high targeting may inflict. In fact, at the equilibrium, users can choose the competing platform that limits targeting to the minimum level feasible. In spite of the availability of this option, most users still choose to obtain services from the high targeting platform. Hence, receiving relevant ads is suficiently important to a big portion of the population of users so that they are willing to tolerate an increased risk for privacy breaches. The insistence of some policy makers, therefore, on eliminating tracking of users’ behavior online may be inconsistent with the revealed preference of consumers, as implied by their choices.

## 9. Conclusion

The digital revolution has led to the growth of data intensive services that are simplifying and improving various aspects of our lives. Driven by the convenience that these services ofer, consumers are sharing information ranging from their interests and opinions to more personal details such as who their friends are, where they are at any given moment, and with whom. Firms with access to this wealth of data are using information about users to improve the efectiveness of ads served on their platforms. Efectiveness of ads improves by finer segmentation of the user population that requires collecting and analyzing information about users. However, such practices may expose users to unforeseen risks such as data breaches or leakages. Therefore, users face a trade-of between privacy and the benefit they gain from sharing information. Diferent users may evaluate this trade-of diferently based on their backgrounds and preferences. In this paper, we attempt to understand the efect of this trade-of on the competition between online advertising platforms. We find that the presence of heterogeneity among users and advertisers leads to diferentiation in the levels of targeting ofered on competing platforms. In an environment where both the user and advertiser markets are fully covered, one platform ofers the minimum level of targeting possible and the other ofers a strictly positive level of targeting. The platform that ofers the lower level of targeting ends up with a smaller share of the user and the advertiser markets and earns lower profits when compared to the platform that ofers the higher level of targeting. We also find that privacy concerns lead to lower targeting levels and intensified price competition between platforms. As a result, platforms can alleviate the intensity of competition and achieve higher profits by encouraging users to consider other factors, such as the attributes of the primary service when choosing between the platforms, and by encouraging users to patronize multiple, competing platforms.

User privacy in digital platforms is an evolving subject and future research may further our understanding of this issue. Our analysis may be limited by the assumptions we make to simplify the analysis and identify the main forces arising because of the conflicting interests of advertisers and users. There are several directions to extend the present work to gain a deeper understanding of the role of user privacy in the digital platform ecosystem. Some examples for future research include issues related to advertiser bargaining power, more complex ad pricing strategies, and the role of diferent players in the advertising value chain. When the advertiser and $. / \mathrm { o r }$ user markets are not fully covered, it may also be interesting to evaluate the evolution of these markets over time as competing platforms adjust their investment in the targeting technology to attract new advertisers and users. Privacy is poised to take center stage in firm strategy and policy discussions as we move to the era of connected devices. As tracking and collecting of sensitive personal data becomes more common, including data related to health history and real-time monitoring of homes, the trade-of between privacy and convenience is likely to influence consumer choice. We hope that our study serves as an impetus for future analytical studies on the role of privacy in digital marketplaces.
