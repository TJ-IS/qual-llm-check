## Abstract

The introduction of a large-scale image processing system at United Services Automobile Association (USAA) required both external and internal partnerships. The USAA-IBM external partnership demonstrates how the traditional arms-length relationship between a vendor and a customer evolved into a close relationship of mutual benefit with blurred boundaries between buyer and seller. Two internal partnerships, one within USAA and the other within IBM, illustrate the importance of internal partnerships in making an external partnership successful. This paper discusses the “mosaic” of relationships and the “squiggly lines” of responsibility that characterized the internal and external partnerships from the perspective of senior information systems management. The article also provides conceptual frameworks that help in generalizing from the partnership arrangements within USAA’s environments to those of other organizations.

Keywords: Partnership, strategic alliances, image processing, user involvement, executive support, large-scale project management

## Introduction

We started with three sandboxes—IBM, the USAA Information Services Division, and the operations people within the insurance policy services organization at USAA. We ended up with one sandbox. Beyond some core knowledge and expertise, there was never any formal partitioning of the work among the three groups. There were just some squiggly lines and a tremendous commitment for getting the job done.

Partnerships $^{1}$ between a firm's information systems group and either its suppliers or its internal customers can play a critical role in the effective acquisition and management of new emerging technologies. At United Services Automobile Association (USAA), a financial services firm headquartered in San Antonio, Texas, the development of a large-scale image processing system required the use of both internal and external cooperative relationships. In addition to senior-level partnership relationships between IBM and both technical and user management, USAA established a living laboratory to promote operations-level partnerships with the firm's service representatives. The system, which drew on resources from many IBM divisions, also required partnership relationships within IBM. This paper examines the structure and content of these partnerships.

## USAA

Founded in 1922, United Services Automobile Association provides a broad range of investment, security, and quality of life (e.g., retirement, travel, and purchasing) products to American military officers, former officers, and their dependents. With \$20 billion in owned or managed assets, USAA is the fifth largest personal car insurer and the sixth largest home insurer in the United States. Selling directly to its members by mail or telephone, the firm is the largest mail order company in the U.S. and operates the largest automatic call distribution telephone system of any site in the world. Every day, USAA receives over 100,000 letters and mails over 250,000 items.

## The image project at USAA

By early 1990, after two previous pilot tests, USAA completed the roll-out of about 1,500 IBM image terminals to over 2,000 users in the policy service and underwriting organization of its property and casualty insurance business. Property and casualty insurance (P&C) represents about 80 percent of USAA's total business, and the service and underwriting function (as distinct from claims) represents about half of the paper-intensive effort. In the policy services organizations service representatives take customer calls and process mailed requests for new policies, policy changes, and the like. Each day in this group, 15,000 letters are received (consisting of about 35,000 pages) and over 50,000 outgoing letters and policies are entered into the image system.

The documents are indexed on the IBM 3090 mainframes and then are scanned to create a digital electronic picture of the incoming mail. Paper originals never leave the mailroom and are eventually destroyed. The imaged documents can then be accessed from anywhere in the company for processing with the existing business applications that are part of USAA's automated insurance environment. Special high resolution terminals are used for displaying and printing the image documents. Because 85 percent of the work is done by telephone, it is practical for two representatives to share an image terminal.

The image system incorporates a work management system that establishes priorities for documents, tracks the backlog of work, and assigns documents to employees based on their training and experience and the assignments of their supervisor. The system provides the supervisor with a summary of each type of work awaiting processing in each department or work group. It also provides the supervisor with the ability to reassign work among employees and to access any document and its processing history. The image system is also accessible by representatives in large field offices in Tampa, Sacramento, and other cities via high speed communications lines.

The impact of the image system at USAA has been widely felt. In 1988 the firm used 39,000 square feet of office space just to store the active paper files. About 80,000 boxes held inactive files in a warehouse. Over 160 people were required to sort, deliver, and file those documents. The same information stored on optical discs on shelves requires just 100 square feet of floor space. The image system reduces the support cost of clerical staff, space, equipment, and supplies amounting to millions of dollars per year. In addition, the system has allowed the professional staff to be more efficient while providing faster, better quality, and more timely service to the members.

The second image processing application at USAA, scheduled for release in 1993, will provide services to the Property and Casualty Insurance claims processing operations. This application will require 2,500-4,500 additional image terminals. A study has been conducted for a third image application—for credit card applications and correspondence at the USAA bank. Image is also an integral part of a second partnership arrangement signed between IBM and USAA in October 1990 for the development of an integrated workstation.

The functionality and the underlying technology of USAA's image system have been described elsewhere (e.g., Elam and Sviokla, 1990; Plesums and Bartels, 1990; Trammel, 1989). Little has been reported, however, about the partnerships that were struck both within and between USAA and IBM. These cooperative relationships illustrate the key ingredients of managing an ongoing partnership: clear end objectives, a willingness to change direction to meet those objectives, benefits for all parties, complementary resources and capabilities, incentives to share knowledge and skills, powerful champions and sponsors, strong teamwork, a sense of trust, and commitment of senior management. The partnerships also exemplify the value of a vision and personal participation of senior executives in cooperative relationships. The image project further provides insights into both systems integration and large-scale project management.

Finally, this project is an illustration of emerging information technology being successfully used to re-engineer work in today's rapidly evolving office environment.

## Partnerships Within the Image Project

The image processing project was a major innovation for USAA and IBM. Although both firms had been studying image technology for some time, neither had previous experience with a major commercial implementation. Successfully developing the application, while simultaneously permitting IBM to develop an image product solution, required a complex intertwining of the two organizations. Three partnership arrangements, depicted in Figure 1, brought the image solution to life.

The first partnership arrangement, shown in the middle column of Figure 1, was the one forged between IBM and USAA. The two organizations structured partnership arrangements to orchestrate the design, planning, ongoing management, and implementation of the image processing project. These relationships, begun through USAA planning and IBM marketing, were consummated by divisional presidents of both organizations, with the ultimate personal concurrence of the chairmen of USAA and IBM.

The second set of partnerships, shown at the right of Figure 1, involved personnel within USAA. Every Information Services Division project within USAA includes sponsorship by a user executive officer. In the case of the image project, this partnership extended to the managers and users in the Central Region, who used three different image systems between 1984 and the IBM production roll-out in 1988 to determine requirements and demonstrate the feasibility of the new technology. The partnership arrangements between USAA's Information Services Division and users were expanded from the Central Region to incorporate a systems working group with representatives from all the areas where image was implemented.

## The USAA-IBM partnership

The most obvious partnership arrangement was between IBM and USAA. USAA's requirements were compatible with IBM's—they wanted a general image solution that would be applicable to all areas of USAA, a solution that would become a standard-supported IBM product, not a custom system. Although IBM's Insurance Industry staff had been convinced of the potential of the image technology, they had not succeeded in “selling” their management. Senior management of USAA played a major role in bringing the necessary vision to life within IBM.

## A Vision From the Top

Robert McDermott, a retired Air Force brigadier general, joined USAA in mid-1968, and was appointed president and CEO in January 1969. During his orientation he became aware of, and chagrined by, the piles of correspondence and member folders that littered the hallways and desks of the service representatives. $^{2}$ In his first management meeting as CEO, McDermott challenged USAA to move to a paperless environment. He would later refer to this as his impossible dream. McDermott declared that investments in information technology were to be viewed as strategic assets rather than expenses and encouraged the use of information technology.

In the 1970s two microfilm projects were aimed at creation of the paperless office, but neither proved “worth the effort” because paper handling procedures had improved. In the mid 1970s, the first key business application (auto insurance)

was redesigned to take advantage of online and database technology. In 1980-81, a long range system plan (LRSP) was developed to move other property and casualty applications to a more integrated “one stop” automated insurance environment. Two of the 80 projects in the LRSP focused on image processing, although this was still a laboratory curiosity of the time.

In October 1982, an information systems R&D group was formed and staff were hired to study image and other emerging technologies. "Unfortunately," a USAA executive recalls, "there wasn't anyone we could turn to help us learn about image processing. We had to create the knowledge and overcome the barriers." Nonetheless, speaking in December 1988, the chairman of USAA declared, "In partnership with IBM, we have virtually achieved this paperless environment in our insurance operations."

## Selling the Vision to IBM

Including image processing in USAA's 1981 system plan was a long way from making it happen. Although USAA's senior management was generally supportive of image technology, the system planners could not buy, or even determine the cost of, an image system from any vendor. Custom-built systems were considered and rejected on the basis of cost, maintainability, expandability, and reconfigurability. Only a few vendors, primarily from the microfilm industry, were ready to start product development.

USAA's first full-time employee for image was hired in October 1982. Early in 1983, a USAA R&D team began work with 3M on a prototype system. For almost two years the 10-person team struggled to establish the external requirements for the system. Users had difficulty visualizing how the system would work or how it could be used. The prototype was ready for use in late 1984. Through 1985 the prototype was refined, and a second (FileNet) system was installed in early 1986. USAA's management was, however, concerned that major vendors such as IBM had still not entered the field.

Traditionally vendors sell and customers buy. But for prospective strategic partnerships, the role may be reversed. IBM had no complete off-the-shelf image product that met USAA's requirement. Instead, IBM would have to invest constrained resources to develop a new solution.

Getting those resources for the project would require broad sponsorship within IBM.

USAA executives used every opportunity to convince senior IBM executives and other vendors of the potential of image processing. USAA's chairman had early discussions with IBM and later served as a member of IBM's customer advisory council. A video demonstrating the 3M image system in use at USAA was provided to IBM and other vendors. USAA executives hoped to establish competition, and eventually standards, in the field.

By 1983 optical storage was starting to emerge from the R&D laboratories. This technology provided large amounts of relatively low speed storage at a low cost. The initial technology was Write Once-Read Many (WORM). $^{3}$ Vendors did not perceive a large market for such storage systems, especially because they could not be used with existing software. Information systems executives within USAA realized, however, that erasable storage was not required, and a non-erasable WORM storage media could provide distinct advantages when explaining the system in court. Information systems management at USAA realized that someone had to convince the vendors of this unrecognized market opportunity.

To sell IBM on the WORM storage concept, General Lasher, president of USAA's Information Services Division (ISD), visited the president of IBM's General Product Division in 1986. The General Product Division was in charge of storage technologies. Later that year, USAA's head of ISD also visited one of the division's plants in Tucson, Arizona, to address an IBM storage planning conference. USAA's other executives also lobbied IBM for image, explaining why imaging was critical to USAA and the insurance industry. IBM executives began visiting San Antonio and were shown the customer service environment and the existing pilot projects. IBM executives started sending their superiors to USAA, and the USAA lobbyists found themselves speaking to ever-higher levels of management within IBM.

USAA continued to look for a long-term partner. IBM was not the only target of USAA's lobbying efforts, although some prospective vendors seemed more enamored with the technology than marketing a viable product. The head of USAA's ISD, however, expressed a concern that if IBM should later enter the market, IBM's image product was likely to become the defacto standard. If that happened and USAA had gone with another vendor, USAA could be left with a unique, non-integratable solution. The IBM account manager assigned to USAA, along with other IBM representatives, continued to assure their customer that IBM should be USAA's partner. The IBM marketing team also lobbied IBM headquarters to join USAA in an image processing initiative. By the time USAA issued a formal request for information for a full-scale production system in February 1987, IBM was eager to respond. In the third quarter of 1987, an agreement between USAA and IBM was signed.

## Provisions for the Partnership

Successful cooperative relationships require that each side bring valued resources to the partnership. An IBM account executive recalled a conversation with another customer concerning an image opportunity: "The customer told me that he was sold on IBM's image solution and that he was now only waiting for IBM to make him the same kind of deal that they had made with USAA. I responded that IBM was very interested in working closely with customers in partnerships where there could be mutual benefit. I then asked him what it was that he would bring to such a partnership."

USAA supplied ample resources to the image partnership. They brought McDermott's "paperless office" vision, evidence that they were successfully pursuing it, and a willingness to commit additional resources. They brought management commitment from all levels of users, management, and information services. They also brought valuable experience, both user and technical. USAA had been experimenting with image since 1982 and had learned a great amount from its many studies and two very different pilot installations. USAA came to the table with a proof of concept that demonstrated both the technical and operational feasibility of image systems using WORM storage. Further, USAA believed that its requirement was not unique. Instead, it reflected an industry-wide need, thus ensuring IBM of a broad market for a generalized solution. Also, as a large insurance company, USAA offered IBM the opportunity to experiment with a new technology in a challenging and highly visible environment. The nature of USAA's applications suggested that later there would be opportunities to deal with color photographs, sound recordings, integration with other information systems, and very high volumes of transactions.

USAA also brought a management team that was familiar with and experienced in the development of advanced information technology solutions. The user executive sponsor had been responsible for the development of the long range systems plan. The head of ISD had ample experience managing consultants, information technology R&D, and systems integration activities. USAA, as a niche player focused on providing financial services for military personnel, was less concerned than some firms might have been with IBM's interest in later selling the product to competitors within the insurance industry.

IBM provided a complementary set of assets to the project. Its financial viability, quality field service, long-term relationship with, and knowledge of USAA were compelling strengths. IBM could be counted on to meet its commitments, even if unexpected hurdles were encountered. It also desired quick introduction of a product in the marketplace and could mobilize the resources and expertise to get the job done. Through its Federal Systems Division, IBM brought systems integration skills to put together a solution from a myriad of existing pieces drawn from IBM's various product divisions, and if necessary, from outside of IBM.

Another criteria favoring the partnership was the similarity of culture within IBM and USAA. Both were strongly committed to customer service, respect for individual employees, and continued pursuit of excellence.

## Building and Managing the Development Team

After IBM's bid was accepted, a development team of five IBMers came to San Antonio to build the system. The head of IBM's development team had previously worked on image processing within the Insurance Industry Division of IBM. In

October of 1987, IBM appointed a seasoned executive as the director of account development in charge of the USAA project. Together, this IBM executive and his counterpart within USAA provided a focal point for the image project. A single project plan provided a common set of objectives for the project participants. Within this single sandbox, team members pursued their varied tasks.

Because of the importance of the project to both companies and its high visibility, participants tended to be very experienced and qualified. As one team member stated, “We were using very senior people, but everyone was expected to be able to do the mundane as well as the difficult.” The team met with early success and pulled ahead of schedule. These early successes helped establish commitment for the project.

The two firms met some challenges in the initial interactions between USAA and IBM's Federal Systems Division (FSD). The FSD was used to dealing with its primary customer, the federal government, on a fixed price contract basis. But for this partnership, flexibility during development was essential; only approximate specifications could be determined prior to contract agreement. One IS executive at USAA recalled his first meeting with counterparts from FSD, "I went into the meeting and said we needed a new feature that was not in the requirements. They didn't know how to respond." Over time, the initial contractual agreement came to largely symbolize both organizations' commitment to building a workable product. Arms-length negotiations were replaced by cooperation and camaraderie. As the relationship grew more tightly knit, organizational boundaries began to blur. When an IBM product division representative informed IBM's director of account development at USAA that a certain request was impossible to meet, the IBM manager told his colleague, "It better not be." On another occasion the IBM team was stumped trying to meet a contractual response time requirement. The team was close, but couldn't quite meet the specification. After learning of the problem, USAA expressed the satisfaction with the near miss.

## A Living Lab

A user group from the Central Region had been the target of the two previous image prototypes.

This same group of 28 service representatives worked closely with the IBM-USAA development team as the design and implementation proceeded. According to one IBM participant, the pilot group felt it was blazing a trail for the company and the industry to follow. These users met for two hours a week with the development team. However, according to the executive heading the Central Region, “The participating IBMers were interested in more than just the reactions of the service representatives. They wanted to know how the supervisors felt about the system, how I felt about it, and on up the chain of command.”

## Post Implementation Partnership

By March 1990 the imaging system was in use by over 2000 operators within USAA. Although the development portion of the project has ended, the image processing partnership between IBM and USAA continues. In the first quarter of 1991, increased transaction volumes produced challenging performance degradations on the system. One participant stated, "There was no basis for us to forecast that this problem was going to occur or when it would occur. Nor had anyone ever experienced it before. But, we worked closely with IBM to find a mutual solution. Although such problems are painful for us, they provide IBM with a real working environment for testing an evolving technology."

## Partnerships within USAA

User involvement and line management support are often prescribed cures for ill-defined systems projects. Although there is considerable enthusiasm for user involvement in systems development and some evidence for its utility (Franz and Robey, 1984; Ives and Olson, 1984; Markus, 1983; Rockart, 1988), far less has been written about the specifics of effective involvement. Within USAA a variety of mechanisms and traditions ensure that ownership for developing systems is established early in the process. These partnership mechanisms extend to operating personnel within the client organization as well as to client management. For the image project, like many others, USAA also required effective mechanisms to ensure transfer of the technology from the test site to the remainder of the firm.

## Partnerships With Client Management

At USAA, effective systems development meant signing up a senior client executive to sponsor the project. For the image project the sponsor was the senior vice president of P&C Operations. Standing close behind him was USAA's chairman, who continued to take an active interest in the image project throughout the development process.

The sponsorship commitment was deeply ingrained throughout the client management. The head of the Central Region had participated years before in the investigation of microfiche. He saw the potential of image technology, and was willing to volunteer his group to host the two image prototypes and then to serve as the sponsor for the IBM-USAA living lab.

Within USAA there were a variety of coordinating mechanisms—the Information Services Council, Architectural Review Board, executive steering committees for systems, and systems working groups. These all ensured continual input from user groups at all management levels. As one executive within ISD stated, “Broad user sponsorship translates to less creeping project scope, less disagreement among users, and strong emphasis on joint application development in the early phases of the information systems projects.”

## Partnerships With Operations Staff

The operational level users were closely involved in the image project from the beginning. Two employees from the Policy Services Division were assigned to work full time on the image R&D team in 1983. The users were an integral part of the "marathon" sessions in trying to establish the initial requirements for the image system. The prototypes developed were sent out to other users for testing. The use of the same user group for the 3M prototype, the FileNet pilot, and later for the IBM pilot provided continuity and learning from one test to the next.

The image system provided some dramatic changes and improvements in the work design of the service representatives (SR) (see Table 1) and their supervisors. Most of the changes were initiated by the SRs, who were encouraged to use the opportunity to improve their jobs. The head of the Central Region described the atmosphere within the living lab, "It would be intellectually impossible for us to design an environment in which the work becomes less satisfying for the worker. That's not the way we operate at USAA. We know that the people who do the work best understand how it can be redesigned. Using a process that is primarily bottom-up, we go through iterations of job design. It takes considerable up-front time to ferret out the knowledge and intuition to make things better, but that time is well spent."

## Partnerships With Other Regions

The system eventually had to be implemented throughout the South Central Region and then in the entire Policy Services Organization. The head of the Central Region saw this as an important task: "At USAA the user sponsors have a big responsibility to help get the rest of the user community on board. Everyone takes their lead from your group. You have a tremendous responsibility for disseminating information and for maintaining a positive project atmosphere."

A variety of organizational mechanisms were used to facilitate this transfer. SRs and supervisors were brought in from other regions, first to participate in the design, and then to see the system in operation. Telephone conferences were held with representatives from throughout the organization, and hard copies of screen designs were provided to SRs from other regions. Despite the involvement of the many other regions, one IBM participant, not surprisingly, found that these other groups were not quite as tolerant of problems as were the initial pilot users.

## Partnerships within IBM

USAA was one of many valued customers seeking attention from IBM. With no product in IBM's catalog that could immediately satisfy its needs, USAA became just another voice competing for IBM's research and development funds. To convert USAA's request into a successful partnership required IBM to first see a compelling market opportunity and then to select USAA as its customer partner. Once the partnership with USAA was formed, IBM's size and diversity, as well as the complexity of the imaging product, forced IBM management to develop its own set of internal partnerships.

## Gathering Internal Commitment

As with any large firm, priorities within IBM vary over time and from one division of the company to the next. Moreover, with so many operating units, it would not be surprising to see one division unaware of what another division is working on. But the matrix structure within IBM ensured that USAA's lobbying efforts would receive an audience; IBM had resources and management focused on both products and customer industries. Working through industry councils, the industry divisions sought to influence the efforts of the product divisions. During 1985, IBM's insurance industry group was lobbying hard to promote image technology within the company. IBM had relatively few image-related products at that time. The Insurance Advisory Council's image meeting in 1985 provided a vehicle for further promoting image technology within IBM product divisions.

In 1985, image technology was the responsibility of the Communications Products Division within IBM. However, the president of IBM's General Products Division, who was in charge of storage technologies, became intrigued by USAA's interest in image. If USAA executives were correct, image would lead to dramatic growth in the optical storage business. But the WORM technology USAA required was not currently receiving high priority within IBM's storage technologies group. The USAA tape on image technology started to circulate at IBM. "By the end of 1986," according to one observer within IBM, "that tape had been seen by most of our senior executives." Within a year, IBM had initiated several major studies investigating the potential of image processing technology.

Resources began to flow toward image technology when John Akers, IBM's chairman, declared image technology to be one of five key technologies in IBM's future. Opportunities for partnerships with customers began to be considered. USAA emerged as a choice for a partnership arrangement. This was due largely to the efforts of the IBM account managers assigned to USAA, the lobbying of USAA executives, and the combined efforts of the director of IBM's Insurance Industry Division and the director of the Image Market Development Group.

IBM had declared 1987 to be the year of the customer. Part of the goal of that year was the rapid movement of new products to market. In the past, products were typically designed over a five-year period in a laboratory and sometimes received a cool reception by the time they reached the marketplace. By using customer requirements and design, a product might be ready in a year (as was image at USAA). What the first release lacked in generality would be offset by an excellent match to the requirements of at least a portion of the customers.

In 1987, IBM also announced the formation of its new Systems Integration and Professional Services organization. This spin-off from the Federal Systems Division signalled IBM's commitment to systems integration in the commercial sector. The USAA image system, with its integration requirements, provided IBM with an opportunity in this important market segment. Further, IBM would no longer force systems to be built exclusively from IBM components. The image system included components manufactured by Bell & Howell, FileNet, Laser Magnetic Storage, and others. Other vendors' components were also held in reserve in case emerging IBM components were not available when needed.

Over time, the sponsorship of the USAA project was elevated within IBM. The management responsibility for the USAA project was moved from the Federal Systems Division to the Image Market Development Group. In 1986, IBM had formed this new development group $^{4}$ that reported to Services Sector Market Development, headed by the senior vice president in charge of all IBM's U.S. market activities. The IBM chairman reportedly also became involved in oversight of the USAA image project and subsequent follow-up activities. In December of 1988, he attended the systems inauguration held in San Antonio.

## Building the Mosaic

Responding to the USAA request for proposal in 1987 required the involvement of many IBM divisions. By the time the image project was completed, 17 IBM laboratories and product divisions were involved. Among the more important of these were IBM's branch offices at USAA, the Insurance Industry Division, the General Products Division, the Communications Division, Entry Systems Division, the Federal Systems Division, and the Data Systems Division. From the various IBM organizational tiles, a mosaic was designed to manage the image project and the evolving partnership within IBM. The mosaic consisted of a variety of organizational roles, structures, and social networks. This represented a new way of doing business and a major break with past policies.

Monthly status meetings ensured communication among the various IBM units. This internal coordination team, made up of the directors for each impacted unit within IBM, presented status reports and sought to identify future problems. According to one IBM observer, the coordination team did not eliminate all the problems: "All of the IBM groups viewed each other as peers. If there was disagreement among these peers it could bring the project to its knees till the disagreement was resolved."

The many faces of IBM also presented some potential confusion for USAA. An IBM director of account development was assigned responsibility to serve as the tie breaker within IBM as well as the senior IBM contact person for USAA. In these roles, he served, in a sense, as an internal customer for the other IBM divisions. He was given a project budget that permitted him to fund some resource requirements within the various IBM business units.

Frequent interpersonal communication kept IBM's developers and engineers in close contact. Many IBMers from the laboratories and product divisions visited USAA to see the working environment and the prototype. Electronic mail linkages between the various IBM groups, including those assigned to USAA, ensured rapid dissemination of specifications, test results, and other news. This interaction provided expertise to the USAA implementation team and simultaneously helped guide the development of the imaging solution within IBM's product and development divisions.

The USAA-IBM partnership also demonstrates how the traditional arms-length contractual relationship between a vendor and a customer turned over time into a relationship of mutual benefit, but with blurred boundaries concerning who is doing the selling and who is doing the buying.

The two internal partnerships, one within USAA and the other within IBM, illustrate the key role that internal partnerships play in making the external cooperative relationship successful. Modic (1988) strongly argues that for external partnerships to be successful, middle and operational management must be involved from the beginning because they are the ones who “really understand what it will take to make things work” (p.47). “Too often,” an executive quoted by Modic (1988) states, “alliances are negotiated at the highest level and become too blue-sky” (p.47). The knowledge about the service representative work demands and work environment resided outside USAA’s Information Services Division. Using USAA’s internal user partnerships, that knowledge was successfully elicited and communicated to IBM development people in a timely fashion. The internal user partnerships at USAA also ensured strong executive user sponsorship and later, smooth diffusion of technology to other parts of the organization.

On the IBM side, a web of relationships among IBM laboratories and product divisions was necessary to leverage as well as integrate the existing products and know-how in order to release a workable image product. Here again, executive level sponsorship was critical in focusing the diverse groups on a common goal. Without successful internal partnerships, the external partnership could not have delivered the joint product, at least not in a timely manner.

Certain key roles provided the organizational lubrication to bring the partnerships to life. These roles are described in Table 3. A fine balance must be maintained on the assignment to, versus assumption by, such roles to individuals. As Zmud (1988) states, “[formal mechanisms] can break the sense of commitment so critical to effective partnership relationships,” whereas “[relying on merely informal mechanisms and roles] can result in partnership relations that atrophy over time as individuals move through managerial positions” (p. 67). Managers intending to experiment with partnerships might wish to map their own situation into the roles described and assess the potential implications of personnel changes.

## Keys to success

What does it take to build a successful partnership? The growing literature on partnerships and strategic alliances provides insight for establishing and managing partnerships. In Table 4 we have mapped these insights into five phases: establishing a purpose, finding a partner, defining the partnership, maintaining the partnership, and institutionalizing the partnership. The USAA-IBM partnership has nearly reached the fifth phase and, as the above description illustrates, exhibits the characteristics identified under various phases in Table 4.

The USAA-IBM partnership suggests that the most critical ingredient for a successful partnership is trust. At USAA this trust was built on an established relationship with IBM and a similarity of cultures. The trust increased over time as the partnership matured. Other success factors include a common goal, executive leadership, clearly established success criteria, and a distinct organizational unit that manages the partnership on a day-to-day basis. This organizational unit must employ individuals who are capable of deeply penetrating into the other organization's fabric (Kanter, 1989).

In conclusion, while a formal contractual agreement might legally define the external partnership, the partnership has to be embedded within the mosaic of each participating organization. Partnerships emerge slowly and often after a lengthy bargaining and negotiation process. Although a partnership might be initially crafted based on personal relationships between a few key individuals, a long-term partnership must be supported and executed at all levels of the organization. All parties of the partnership must understand and work for mutual benefit, not just individual benefit.

# Executive Overview

The software bottleneck continues to plague most organizations. Demands for greater functionality and integration of applications call for larger and larger programs. These demands far exceed our ability to deliver. As a result, the software crisis deepens. There is actually room for optimism, though. We are seeing encouraging progress along several avenues that stands to achieve a tenfold increase in software productivity and quality over the next few years.

There appears to be a common thread to almost all of the promising approaches to boosting the effectiveness of application development: the reuse of system components. Whether an application is built in a high-level specification language or a traditional third-generation language like COBOL, we cannot afford to continue reinventing the wheel. We must learn how to assemble big systems out of standard components if we have any hope of dealing with the growing software demands.

This article discusses an approach that one company, Celite Sales Corporation, found to be very successful in increasing the productivity and quality of the software development process. It is called the application software factory (ASF). Modelled after the disciplined process long practiced in product engineering, it has been applied by a number of software development groups. Not surprisingly, most of the successful applications of the ASF concept have been achieved in Japan, where process engineering has been honed to a high degree.

What are the essential characteristics of a disciplined engineering process as practiced, say, in the automobile industry? Each component is designed with simplicity, reliability, and maintainability in mind. Designers can then put together with confidence high-quality products from the standardized set of perfected components. This is basically the approach taken by Celite in the development of application software. The ASF rests on the following foundation: (1) use of standard methods and development tools, (2) exploitation of available automated support products, (3) disciplined planning, analysis, and project control, and (4) extensive reuse of standardized, interchangeable components.

The approach begins with the development of components designed from the ground up with reuse in mind. This calls for the identification of common functional requirements, and then mapping the requirements into a minimal set of standard modules (or “shells,” as Celite terms them). Attempts to add frills or capricious variations are rigidly resisted. Each component is highly parameterized in order to accommodate legitimate differences in requirements. The process of identifying and designing the components is much like the process of designing a high-level specification language, and it has the same purpose: to give application developers a relatively small set of powerful “primitives” that can be combined to form a larger system.

The approach has paid off handsomely for Celite. Two applications have been successfully developed already, and others are in the process of development. Of the two, a quality control application for a process manufacturing operation achieved the most dramatic results. Consisting of 250,000 lines of code, the application was developed with about 1.5 person-years of effort. No bug was found during the system testing phase, and one was found thereafter during the first 90 days after conversion. About 99 percent of the code was generated from reusable components. (The remaining one percent of customized code accounted for the single bug that was found.)

It is being increasingly recognized by IS management that the productivity and quality of software development is not a mere matter of operational efficiency; it brings about fundamental strategic changes in the way an organization can meet its growing software demands. Achieving such a major gain calls for a paradigm shift in the way we think about application development—from an expensive, inflexible approach to one that can adapt to changing needs. It also requires rethinking about how development teams are organized and staffed. It is by no means clear which approaches will prove most effective in a given organization, but we can certainly be completely confident that major improvements in software development are on their way. This article provides some helpful guidance on how progress is likely to be made.
