# Online customers’ cognitive differences and their impact on the success of recommendation agents

Hui-Chih Wang <sup>a,1</sup>, Her-Sen Doong b,

<sup>a</sup> Department of Information Management, National Chung Cheng University, 168 University Rd., Min-Hsiung, Chiayi 621, Taiwan <sup>b</sup> Department of Management Information Systems, National Chiayi University, 580 Sinmin Rd., Chiayi 60054, Taiwan

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 4 March 2008 Received in revised form 23 July 2009 Accepted 28 November 2009 Available online 18 January 2010

Keywords: Recommendation agents Cognitive traits Situational involvement Adaptive-innovative style Online purchase

## A B S T R A C T

RAs (recommendation agents) have become a major way to assist customers make online purchase decisions. However, do customers consider an RAs’ advice to be as important as managers expect? Which customers, if any, use RAs more frequently? Although these questions are crucial to Website management, sparse knowledge of the answers was found. Based on 316 randomly selected customers, we empirically demonstrated that customers’ deliberations were not determined by a single cognitive trait, as examined in past IS studies; their decision was more due a function of innovativeness and involvement. More-involved users were found to adopt an RAs’ advice. Managerial implications of these findings are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

Recommendation agents (RAs) are software agents that offer purchase advice to customers who have limited knowledge of B2C e-commerce products and suppliers. Leading Websites have established product-brokering RAs to help customers overcome this challenge and information overload in using B2C Websites [7]. By providing additional advice based on current customers’ purchase choices in terms of item-to-item correlation or sequential data patterns [14], RAs play a similar role to that of a salespersons in a retail store [6]. Amazon has an RA to provide customers with advice on books or music that may be of interest to them; saying ‘‘Customers also bought XX after purchasing this item’’. Lands’ end utilizes an RA to offer customers purchase advice, such as ‘‘You might also like. . .’’ depending on the specific items they are browsing. These practical cases demonstrate the fact that managers see the RA as a useful system to boost Website sales, entice customers into buying more products, and, eventually, increasing company profits.

However, setting up a successful RA is not straightforward [3] and understanding how customers make decisions based on an

RAs’ advice is essential: if online customers are unwilling to take the RAs’ advice into consideration, the efforts expended in developing RAs could be in vain, or could become too expensive. However, few studies have examined the issue of how customers’ cognitive diversity may shape their deliberation of the RAs’ advice. In particular, ‘‘Do all or some customers deliberate on the RA’s advice?’’ And if yes, ‘‘who are these customers?’’ Answers to these questions, remain under-investigated.

Individual differences in user’s cognitive traits, their innovativeness and involvement levels have often been cited as significant for the success of an IS. However, our study was designed to analyze and thus deepen knowledge of user involvement and its impact on system usage by introducing the concept of situational involvement. This is the importance that a user attaches to the RA’s advice while purchasing on the Website; however, it quickly, disappears when the user leaves the Website. The influence of situational involvement on customers’ IS use has been ignored in the past.

The objectives of this study are thus to:

1. Broaden the personalized implications of RAs from a technology to the strategic marketing level.

2. Show that customers’ deliberation on RAs’ advice is not determined by a single trait but by the interaction of their adaptive-innovative style and involvement level, thus including situational involvement.

## 2. Theoretical background and hypothesis development

## 2.1. The recommendation agent

This provides online customers with advice on products or services that, based on previous or current actions, seem to match the customer’s needs, preferences, and interests. Such advice is intended to motivate customers’ further purchase desire and help them decide in a self-service e-commerce context [12]. As more and more e-tailing Websites build their own RAs, their acceptance and adoption have attracted researchers and practitioners; e.g., customer trust of RA was studied by Wang and Benbasat [17], who found that positive knowledge-based, interactive, calculative, and dispositional factors resulted in higher trust, while negative calculative and interactive reasons resulted in lower trust. In another study, they found that the use of different types of explanations enhanced trusting beliefs: ‘‘how’’ explanations increased the RA’s apparent competence and benevolence, while ‘‘why’’ explanations increased benevolence belief and ‘‘when’’ trade-off explanations increased feelings of integrity [16].

The user interface design is also an important aspect of RA design. Al-Natour et al. [1] investigated two types of perceived similarity (i.e. personality and behavioral) between customers and RAs; their findings indicated that the design of RAs can be used to manifest desired personalities to gain customer preference for a website. Gretzel and Fesenmaier [4] also found that relevance, transparency, duration, and required effort of the elicitation process were important cues of how customers perceived the value of RAs. Investigation of the methods used to develop RAs was another popular issue; Liu and Shin [11] demonstrated an approach combining group decision-making and data mining techniques to: determine the relative weights of RFM variables (recency, frequency, and monetary) in evaluating customer lifetime value; and subsequent development of a rule based data mining approach that provided product recommendations. Xiao and Benbasat [19] conducted an extensive review of RA studies to answer two questions:

\- How does RA use, its characteristics, etc. influence customer decision-making and outcomes? and

\- What is its effect on users’ evaluation of RAs?

## 2.2. Adaption-innovation theory

Psychologists refer to cognitive style as the manner in which individuals make decisions and solve problems [8]. Individual innovativeness is a cognitive style that has been considered important in social science, psychology, and business, because groups and organizations rely increasingly on the success of new product introductions to stimulate their growth. User’s innovations in the IS discipline have been investigated in order to explain individual acceptance of or intention to use systems (e.g. [13]). Individual innovation theory, as developed by Rogers in his Diffusion of Innovations Theory, noted that diffusion of ideas was a complex cognitive process affected by prior adoption and re-use of technology; Kirton’s adaption-innovation theory has been applied in IS studies to reveal the relationship between individuals’ creativity and behavior (e.g. [2]).

## 2.3. Personal involvement theory

Individual involvement has played a significant role in customer behavior studies. This trait has been used to explain customers’ responses to advertising, products, purchase decisions, brands and technology (e.g. [9]). Apart from product and purchase-decision involvement, enduring and situational involvement have also been widely discussed in the literature as dimensions as delineating the cognitive state of an individual. Enduring involvement is the ongoing personal concern with a product, purchase and brand exhibited by an individual. Situational involvement is the temporary relationship between a customer’s interest and the product/situation, which can be influenced by product attributes (e.g., price, design, and quality) and environmental factors (e.g., promotion, purchase for company use, etc.). It will diminish as the interest is satisfied or as time passes. Thus a customers’ involvement in the RA advice at a Website is likely to be situational because the customers’ need for RA’s support only occurs while they are browsing for a specific item and becoming interested in it; and the need for advice diminishes as soon as the customer leave the Website.

Prior studies have tended to define user involvement as participation in the system development process by representatives of the target user group, but user involvement also includes situational involvement and its effect on information use behavior is sparse.

## 2.4. Style/involvement model

Building on adaption-innovation theory, studies were made to ascertain whether innovators actually adopted more new methods than others. In studies of computer use, it was found that early innovators included adaptors. In verifying and explaining individuals’ innovation adoption behavior, innate innovativeness apparently interacted with interest in product categories, resulting in personal involvement as an explanatory variable that affected why adopters rather than innovators used computers more frequently. This resulted in the style/involvement model (see Fig. 1), which suggested that an individual’s problem-solving and decision-making process was shaped by his/her cognitive style and involvement. Consistent empirical findings across studies have shown that this model can effectively explain and predict individuals’ use of technological innovation.

As computer usage for different applications can still be perceived as a new venture by many customers due to increased technical complexity and expense, researchers have asserted that computer usage is a suitable product for the investigation of customers’ innovation adoption behavior, and a series of studies were conducted to verify style/involvement. The first examined current computer users and their usage. The second investigated current computer users’ use-innovativeness in home-computing software applications. The third tested graduate business school students’ actual use of home-computing software applications; these students were from three different graduate programs, which make different demands vis-a\`-vis computer use. Consistently, these studies showed that both innovative cognitive style and a high level of personal involvement with computing were significantly related to the number of software packages used. Further, adaptors and innovators were represented equally in both low and high involvement with computing.

## 2.5. Research framework and hypotheses

The studies demonstrated that the style/involvement model may be an important addition to IS studies because it provided empirical evidence that individuals’ involvement levels of computer use interacted with their cognitive styles and subsequently impacted their use of innovations. Decision-makers with different cognitive styles prefer different information and these dynamically varied sets are incorporated into their decision-making processes. Recently, Yi et al. [20] reported that personal innovativeness not only directly influences intentions to use an IS innovation, but also indirectly influenced their perception of the IS innovation. These led us to postulate the hypothesis:

H1. Four segments of online customers with their own adaptiveinnovative styles and involvement levels – that is, more-involved innovators, more-involved adaptors, less-involved innovators and less-involved adaptors – will differ significantly in their deliberation of the RA’s advice while purchasing from the e-tailing Website.

As considering an RA’s advice is similar to using an IS in an online environment, the style/involvement model, which can explain a diverse range of customers’ adaptive-innovative traits and their subsequent use of an innovation was appropriate in providing insight into customers’ acceptance of the RA’s advice. Thus, we proposed a research framework as depicted in Fig. 2.

Recently, Wang et al. [18] extended the style/involvement model to fit an e-marketplace environment and investigate how customers decided to adopt online purchasing. Based on a sample of 1044 customers from a famous international Website, they found that customers’ underlying adaptive-innovative styles and involvement levels could be divided into four parts that differed significantly in their actual online purchase frequency, perceived risk of buying on a Website, and online loyalty towards the Website. In particular, more-involved adaptors were found to be responsible for the highest purchase frequency, followed by more-involved innovators, less-involved innovators, and lessinvolved adaptors. Thus, in the context of online purchasing, they showed that individual involvement might have a greater impact on customers’ online purchase frequency than adaptive-innovative style. This was consistent with suggestions that, while making decisions, more-involved individuals tend to process relevant information in detail. Consequently, we hypothesized that:

H2. More-involved adaptors will deliberate upon the RA’s advice more often than more-involved innovators, less-involved innovators and less-involved adaptors while purchasing from the e-tailing Website.

## 3. Research methodology

## 3.1. Measurements

We investigated three constructs in our study: personal innovativeness towards the RA (the tendency to learn about and adopt the suggestions of an RA), personal involvement towards the RA (an individual’s perception of the relevance of the suggestions, based on inherent needs, values, and interest), and consideration of the RA’s advice (the frequency with which an online customer used the RA’s recommendations in deciding on a product).

The survey questionnaire was constructed from those found in studies reported in the earlier literature. Semantic scales were used in both the Personal Involvement Inventory and deliberation on the RA’s advice; a seven-point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree was used as a measure of the domain specific innovativeness scale. Table 1 provides a summary of the constructs studied and measure items.

The initial version of the survey instrument was pre-tested for content validity by eight customers and two professors, who served as expert judges. The wording of each item was examined and unclear items were reworded on the basis of this feedback; e.g., the term recommendation agent was replaced by recommendation mechanism because the expert judges suggested that the latter would be more easily understood by respondents.

## 3.2. Our sample

The Website of the subsidiary of a major, well-known international e-tailing company was selected as the survey target. Its name has been withheld due to a non-disclosure agreement we executed with this company. This Website has utilized techniques such as statistical summaries, a basket analysis algorithm, and an item-to-item correlation algorithm to mine their purchase data. Purchase recommendations based on the item that customers have put into the Website shopping cart are generated and provided to customers via the RA; e.g., if the customer puts a digital camera into the shopping cart, the RA makes a recommendation such as ‘‘We recommend the Kamera camera bag’’ or ‘‘Customers who bought the Canon IXUS 95IS digital camera also bought the Kamera camera bag’’ after using the basket analysis algorithm.

Our sample frame consisted of 1200 individuals who had been randomly selected from the Website’s customer database. Email invitations containing a hyperlink were sent to each, inviting them to participate in our study. The hyperlink enabled potential respondents to connect directly to the questionnaire Web page. To motivate customers to join the study, the sponsor offered a US\$3 electronic coupon that could be used when they next made a purchase from the Website.

To determine whether participants had the knowledge or experience necessary to participate in the study, the introductory section contained one screening item to which they had to reply ‘Yes’ or ‘No’: ‘‘I am aware of the recommendation mechanism built within the company’s Website to help me with decisions regarding which product to purchase.’’ Data collection was completed in two weeks and 360 responses were received. Forty-four replies from customers who answered ‘No to the screening item were removed from the set of responses, resulting in a total of 316 valid responses and a response rate of 26.3%. Just under three-quarters of respondents were male (71.2%) and 84.5% were employed. The average age of respondents was 31.4 years (standard deviation was 7.7); 80.7% were university-educated; and their average Internet usage experience was 4.5 years (standard deviation was 0.8).

## 4. Research results

## 4.1. Data validation

Exploratory factor analysis using principal components extraction with varimax rotation was applied to extract three latent constructs from the 17 measure items. Table 2 shows the factors with eigenvalues greater than 1.00. As it indicates, all the scale items loaded as expected, indicating that they were unidimensional. All of the items loaded on their respective factors with values greater than 0.7; each loaded higher on its associated factor than on any other construct and there were no cross-loadings higher than 0.4; and the three latent constructs collectively explained 72.0% of the variance. Thus, construct validity was confirmed and they also demonstrated adequate reliability. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.95 for the Revised Personal Involvement

Inventory, 0.82 for domain specific innovativeness, and 0.91 for deliberation of the RA’s advice.

We employed the Harman one-factor test, to examine the risk of common method variance. Such risks are minimized if the onefactor test results reveal more than one factor or when no individual factor explains more than fifty percent of the variance. In our study, no individual factor explained more than fifty percent of the variance and the Harman one-factor test revealed more than one factor. Hence, this analysis helped us to confirm that there was no common method bias.

## 4.2. Segmentation and hypotheses testing

Respondents whose domain specific innovativeness scale scores were higher than the mean were categorized as innovators with the others as adaptors. Similarly, respondents with a Personal Involvement Inventory score higher than the mean were categorized as more involved with the others as less involved. The sample was then split into four groups which were cross-tabulated and the respondents were thus divided into more-involved innovators (103, 32.6%), more-involved adaptors (62, 19.6%), less-involved innovators (65, 20.6%), and less-involved adaptors (86, 27.2%). Levene’s test of equality of error variances was not significant (F (3, 31) = 1.2, p = 0.3). Thus, ANOVA was further applied to examine whether the four segments’ cognitive styles and involvement levels were associated with significant differences in their deliberation upon the RA’s advice. Table 3 shows that the effect of segment type $( F ~ ( 3 , ~ 3 1 ) = 3 3 . 4 , ~ p < 0 . 0 1$ , power = 1.0) was significant. Thus, hypothesis 1 was supported.

Since the ANOVA yielded a significant result, Fisher’s LSD test pairwise comparisons were conducted to investigate differences among the four segments in terms of their consideration of the RA’s advice. Table 4 indicates that differences between the segments were all significant. More specifically, more-involved adaptors were found to deliberate upon the RA’s recommendations more often than the other three segments. Thus, support was found for hypothesis 2.

## 5. Discussions and conclusions

Statistical results were strongly supportive of our study’s goals: (1) both customers’ adaptive-innovative style and involvement level were found to be significantly positively related to their deliberation on the RA’s advice; (2) these two traits interacted with each other and shaped customers’ consideration of the RA’s advice to four significantly different extents; and (3) more-involved adaptors were found to consider the RA’s advice more frequently than the other segments. More-involved innovators had the second highest frequency, followed by less-involved innovators, and finally less-involved adaptors. This supports Hess et al. [5], who reported that more-involved individuals tend to be the most engaged with decision aids.

Past studies have investigated adaptive-innovative style and involvement levels and their resulting outcomes separately, but enduring involvement differs from the situational involvement, which is of temporary importance when considering the RA’s advice while purchasing from an e-tailing Website. The theoretical contributions of our study are three-fold: it has:

1. Empirically demonstrated that customers’ deliberation on the RA’s advice was not determined by a single cognitive trait.

2. Introduced the concept of situational involvement to information system research. Given that packaged software (COTS) has replaced customized software and become the major practical solution for companies, the importance of understanding situational involvement and its outcome cannot be overlooked.

3. Broadened knowledge of RAs, by developing a theoretical basis for understanding customers’ cognitive traits and their influence on their deliberation of the RA’s advice.

## 6. Managerial implications and limitations

Our findings have several implications for managers. They can help executives to recognize that not every customer benefits from the advice of an RA to the same extent. Customers’ consideration of the RA’s advice was shown to be diverse as a result of the interaction of their adaptive-innovative styles and involvement. In practical terms, managers can neither stimulate customers’ involvement in system building nor can they keep track of variations in customers’ levels of innovativeness. However, they can influence customers’ situational involvement, since it can be influenced by environmental factors, such as promotions.

Few e-tailing Websites have developed personalized RA services. Liang et al. [10] reported that personalized RA services can reduce information overload and lead to higher user satisfaction. Specifically, as more-involved customers come to recognize that customized services are offered by a specific Website, their satisfaction with its RA service increases. RA services may be more useful if customers search for specific products rather than browsing without a purpose. Su et al. [15] found that novice customers are more satisfied with an interactive RA than expert customers.

As in most research, our study had limitations. First, it employed a survey method to collect data rather than using a controlled laboratory experiment. Consequently, we could not control for the effect of other factors. Second, the analysis of data was based on mean values and did not include any covariates. Third, as the majority of those who responded to the empirical survey were male and the empirical results were based on only one e-tailing Website and one customer group, caution is necessary when drawing conclusions.
