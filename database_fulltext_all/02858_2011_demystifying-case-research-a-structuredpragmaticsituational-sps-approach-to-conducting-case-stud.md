# Demystifying case research: A structured–pragmatic– situational (SPS) approach to conducting case studies

Shan L. Pan <sup>a,1</sup>, Barney Tan <sup>b,</sup>⁎

## a b s t r a c t

Despite an abundance of prescriptions and examples for the conduct of case research in the literature, the fact that most prescriptions tend to (1) articulate general principles/guidelines that are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to translate into speci<sup>fi</sup>c, actionable steps, (2) hold only under idealized conditions and may be unworkable in the <sup>fi</sup>eld, and (3) emphasize the need to be <sup>fl</sup>exible without explaining how <sup>fl</sup>exibility can be achieved, is creating a steep learning curve. To address these gaps, a structured– pragmatic–situational (SPS) approach to conducting case research is proposed with detailed instructions provided for each of its eight steps. The eight steps include (1) access negotiation, (2)conceptualizing the phenomenon,(3) collecting and organizing the initial data, (4) constructing and extending the theoretical lens, (5) con<sup>fi</sup>rming and validating data, (6) selective coding, (7) ensuring theory-datamodel alignment, and (8) writing the case report. With its prescriptions, the SPS approach introduces a number of conceptual innovations, integrates the different recommendations of some of the most frequently cited works on the case research method into a coherent whole, and suggests resolutions for a number of common issues that confront case researchers.

## 1. Introduction

Over the years, the prevalence of case research in the academic discipline of Information Systems (IS) has grown (Mingers, 2003, p. 475) to the point where its validity as a research method is now rarely, if ever, questioned (Lee & Hubona, 2009). The mainstream acceptance of case research has resulted in an upsurge of demand for materials on how to conduct case studies (Walsham, 2006). Consequently, works that contain prescriptions (e.g. Klein & Myers, 1999; Walsham, 1995; Yin, 2003) and examples (e.g. Markus, Steinfeld, Wigand, & Minton, 2006; Walsham & Sahay, 1999) for the conduct of case research regularly feature among the most widely cited or ‘best papers’ in the <sup>fi</sup>eld today.

Yet, despite the abundance of references for the conduct of case research in the literature, over the course of conducting a number of case study seminars across the globe, we continue to encounter researchers and students who report dif<sup>fi</sup>culties in conducting actual case research on a fairly frequent basis. The root of the problem, in our view, does not stem from a lack of enthusiasm or knowledge of the seminal works on the conduct of case research (e.g. Klein & Myers, 1999; Walsham, 1995; Yin, 2003). Instead, we would contend that their dif<sup>fi</sup>culties derive from a number of gaps in the literature.

First, among the works that contain prescriptions for the conduct of case research, a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant majority are centered on elucidating the overarching principles (e.g. Klein & Myers, 1999) and guidelines (e.g. Dube & Pare, 2003) for the conduct of case studies. However, while these studies are informative and have proved to be immensely useful in de<sup>fi</sup>ning what constitutes overall quality in case research, at the intended level of an overview, they do not translate easily into speci<sup>fi</sup>c, actionable steps (Seale, 1999).

Second, of the works that provide more detailed prescriptions, many of them tend to hold idealized assumptions about the research setting, which result in a proliferation of prescriptions for the conduct of case research that may be at times impractical or simply unworkable. For example, in one of the most widely-cited accounts of the case research method (approximately 39,716 citations on Google Scholar as of July 10, 2011), Yin describes a procedure for screening and selecting a case organization out of “a score or so (20–30) of possible candidates” (Yin, 2003, p. 78), and hints at abandoning case studies if a discovery emerges that leads to the “reconsidering one or more of the study's original theoretical propositions” (Yin, 2003, p. 50). While these prescriptions may certainly be relevant for a very select group of highly regarded scholars with ready access to a long list of organizations, it is unimaginable for the majority of us who do not, and even more so for aspiring case researchers who lack the credibility that might persuade any organization to grant access. Prescriptions that only hold under idealized conditions are particularly problematic for the less-experienced. This is because when conditions are less than ideal, they may lack the experience to <sup>fi</sup>nd a practical workaround, which leaves them in a void in terms of practical discretion.

Finally, among the existing prescriptions for the conduct of case research, the need to be <sup>fl</sup>exible and adaptive to the research setting is often heavily emphasized (e.g. Eisenhardt, 1989; Walsham, 2006). However, while the need to be <sup>fl</sup>exible and adaptive when conducting case research is unquestionable, the question of ‘how one can be flexible’ has yet to be addressed in a substantive way. Perhaps this is re<sup>fl</sup>ective of the highly tacit nature of case research, which many consider a craft that is “in large part learned ‘on the job,’ through apprenticeship, experience, trial, and error rather than by studying general accounts of method” (Seale, 1999, p. 475), but there is a pressing need to at least make explicit the <sup>fl</sup>exibility-enabling techniques and practices that can be explicated. This is so that when contingencies occur, case researchers can utilize this knowledge to adapt their approach accordingly.

In tandem with the fact that most published case studies devote “little (of their allocated space) to their own research conduct and method” (Walsham, 2006, p. 321), and hence provide little guidance for the conduct of case research, these gaps may account for the dif<sup>fi</sup>culties faced by many researchers in their initial adoption of the method. Towards addressing the three identi<sup>fi</sup>ed gaps, the purpose of this article is to propose an approach to conducting case research that is characterized by three corresponding attributes (refer to Table 1). First, the proposed approach is structured in that it conceptually divides the process of conducting case research into eight systematic steps that are speci<sup>fi</sup>c, detailed and can be easily replicated. Second, the proposed approach is pragmatic in that it is infused with techniques and workarounds to simplify and ensure the viability of its prescriptions that does not sacri<sup>fi</sup>ce the rigor required of an academic study. Third, the proposed approach is situational in that it facilitates <sup>fl</sup>exibility and adaptiveness by incorporating techniques that enable researchers to detect and respond to contingencies and the emergence of surprising case data.

The proposed structured–pragmatic–situational (SPS) approach to case research represents the culmination of what we have learnt over the course of conducting more than 30 case studies and publishing over 60 journal papers in a variety of well-regarded IS outlets over the last decade. We do not claim that it is the best approach to case research, but we are certain that it is a workable approach, and, in its simplicity, can be picked up fairly quickly. Attesting to this, we note that many of the PhD students trained in this approach (including one of the co-authors of this paper) have gone on to become productive case researchers that have published in the very best journals of our <sup>fi</sup>eld (e.g. Ravishankar, Pan, & Leidner, 2011; Tan, Pan, & Hackney, 2010c). In the spirit of creating a “consultable record” (Geertz, 1973, p. 30) for the conduct of case research, it is hoped that this article can serve to advance the state of existing knowledge by addressing the gaps in the existing literature and reduce the learning curve for aspiring case researchers. The latter is especially important because lowering the barriers to participation potentially increases the number of adherents to the craft, with corresponding implications for the legitimacy of the research paradigm, quantity and quality of case studies produced, opportunities for research collaboration and the allocation of resources for the conduct of case research (Pfeffer, 1993) within the IS discipline.

This article is organized in the following way. The <sup>fi</sup>rst section has established the motivation and purpose of the paper. In the second section, we will provide an overview of the SPS approach to conducting case research, list its key steps, and discuss how the steps transition from one to another. The remaining sections will be devoted to a detailed account of each step of the SPS approach in which we will describe our prescriptions, explain how they are corroborated by the existing literature, and whenever possible, provide illustrative examples based on the case studies we have conducted. This is followed by some remarks on the limitations of the proposed approach, as well as its contributions, in the concluding section of the paper.

## 2. A structured–pragmatic–situational approach to case research: an overview

Before we delve into the speci<sup>fi</sup>cs of our approach, we would like to clarify the type of case research for which our proposed approach is appropriate. Conventional wisdom has it that a case study can be descriptive, exploratory or explanatory (Dube & Pare, 2003; Yin, 2003). However, we would contend that an exploratory case study is the safest option because it plays to the strength of the case research method (Siggelkow, 2007), by which we are referring to the inherent richness of case data (Miles & Huberman, 1994). A case study that is purely descriptive would be too easy to dismiss for a lack of theoretical contribution (Siggelkow, 2007), while the test of a causal theory by means of an explanatory case study (Dube & Pare, 2003) is usually far less convincing as compared to other available means of statistical validation. Although exploratory case studies can be used to motivate research questions or illustrate conceptual arguments (e.g. Beath & Ross, 2007a, 2007b), one of the most effective ways of using them is in the inductive derivation of new theories (Eisenhardt, 1989). Accordingly, our discussion of the proposed SPS approach will focus on this form of use so as to limit the scope of the paper.

The proposed SPS approach to case research consists of eight steps (refer to Fig. 1) and begins with access negotiation. After access to an appropriate case organization is gained, the process enters what we term the ‘framing cycle’. The framing cycle begins with the gathering of background information on the case organization and the phenomenon of interest (Strauss & Corbin, 1998), as well as a review of potentially relevant theories (Walsham, 2006) so as to construct a mental concept of the phenomenon before the commencement of data collection. Based on this mental concept, an overview interview (e.g. Hallen & Eisenhardt, 2008) is conducted to validate the researcher's perspective of the phenomenon under study, and the initial data are organized and structured to facilitate a preliminary stage of theorizing. The result of this preliminary stage of theorizing is the construction of an initial theoretical lens using the constructs and propositions from an appropriate theory that is meant as a “sensitizing device” (Klein & Myers, 1999, p. 75) to guide subsequent data collection and analysis. Based on the initial lens, the mental concept of the phenomenon is re<sup>fi</sup>ned and more data are collected. The additional data are then iteratively used to develop the initial lens further as additional categories in the form of new constructs, propositions, or contingent conditions are included. The framing cycle continues until the state of what we term ‘theoretical confidence’ is reached. Theoretical con<sup>fi</sup>dence is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the stage where the researcher is suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently con<sup>fi</sup>dent that the theoretical lens, when corroborated by the eventual case data, is both an accurate representation of the empirical reality, and can make an adequate contribution to both theory and practice.

Following the framing cycle, the process enters what we term the ‘augmenting cycle’. The augmenting cycle begins with the collection of additional data with the aim of obtaining corroborating evidence that transforms the theoretical lens into a full-<sup>fl</sup>edged theory. The earlier data, as well as each piece of additional data, are then systematically coded, organized and examined using a number of strategies for qualitative data analysis (Langley, 1999). The emergent model is then validated by ensuring that it is congruent with both the empirical data and the existing literature (Klein & Mvers. 1999). Following this. the emergent model is validated with the informants (Neuman, 2005) and additional data are collected and coded. This process of iterating between the emergent model, existing theories and data (Eisenhardt, 1989) continues until the point of theoretical saturation (Glaser & Strauss, 1967) is reached. Theoretical saturation refers to the state where the inductively derived model can comprehensively account for the case data and “incremental learning is minimal because the researchers are observing phenomena seen before” (Eisenhardt, 1989, p. 545). Finally, following the augmenting cycle, the SPS approach concludes with the writing of the case report. The following stream of reporting will now discuss each step of the SPS approach in greater detail.

## 3. Step 1: access negotiation

The predominant school of thought with regards to the commencement of case research is that the researcher should begin by formulating a comprehensive research design (Yin, 2003), or at the very least, decide on the research questions (Eisenhardt, 1989). There is nothing wrong with this approach. In fact, by asking the questions of import to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c literature, the chances of making a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant contribution to both theory and practice is probably higher (Walsham, 2006), assuming that the researcher is indeed able to gain access to a suitable organization. However, from our experience, devising impactful research questions and selecting a pertinent research topic is the easy part. Gaining access to an organization that does justice to your research intention is far more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult. Hence, in the interest of pragmatism, we would instead suggest that case researchers begin with the search for an interesting case. An interesting case could be that of an internationally-renowned organization (e.g. Tan, Pan, Lu, & Huang, 2009), an extreme case (e.g. Weick, 1993), or a dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to access phenomenon (e.g. Pan, Pan, & Devadoss, 2005).

Searching for an interesting case is a more viable starting point because the means of gaining access to an organization that perfectly matches a predetermined research criteria almost always takes the form of unsolicited contact (Yin, 2003). This is barring a fortunate coincidence, such as having a friend or family member who holds a position of in<sup>fl</sup>uence in the target organization. On the other hand, by being open to possibilities and adopting an approach that is best characterized as “planned opportunism” (Pettigrew, 1990, p. 274), the probability of gaining access is much higher. Planned opportunism means letting research interests, funding strategies and explicit opportunities for network building shape the long-term plan for case selection (i.e. delimiting a set of potential case types out of an in<sup>fi</sup>nite array of possibilities). But the decision to approach a speci<sup>fi</sup>c organization should be governed by shorter-term parameters that may include “forethought and intention, chance, opportunism, and environmental preparedness” (Pettigrew, 1990, p. 274) so that the selection of a speci<sup>fi</sup>c site becomes an informed choice (with a higher likelihood of being granted access) based on resource and opportunity considerations.

In addition, another advantage of starting with an interesting case is that it is easier to sell, and can sometimes compensate for a lack of theoretical and practical signi<sup>fi</sup>cance. Siggelkow's (2007, p. 20) analogy of a talking pig supports this view. A talking pig may have little theoretical or practical relevance but if there was a case study of a talking pig, imagine the interest it will stir! He argues further that “it is much harder to make a paper interesting whose findings or conclusions only address theory. A paper should allow a reader to see the world, and not just the literature, in a new way” (Siggelkow, 2007, p. 23). As such, with all things being equal, we contend that an interesting case will always be more attractive to potential readers than one that is not.

Regardless of the starting point of case research, it is probably a good idea to avoid making unsolicited contact (see Yin, 2003) with the target organization when negotiating for access. By our count, out of every ten unsolicited requests for access made, nine of them will be <sup>fl</sup>atly ignored, and the one response received is usually that of a rejection. As mentioned earlier, the probability of access tends to be higher if you have an endorsement or referral from an in<sup>fl</sup>uential benefactor; who could be a friend, a family member, an alumnus, an executive student, or an invited speaker. If the target organization is from another country, it is usually more effective to look for a local collaborator with ready access. It also helps if you are endorsed by or in collaboration with an institution that is perceived to be legitimate. For example, when we had the opportunity to collaborate with a research out<sup>fi</sup>t sponsored by the government of Singapore, we found that we could access a variety of interesting organizations in the Singapore public sector (e.g. Tan, Pan, & Cha, 2010a), even those that had rejected our unsolicited approaches earlier.

Access negotiation, in itself, is relatively straightforward. Good social skills are a must (Walsham, 2006), but beyond that, it is also important to spell out clearly the objectives of the case study, the resources required (in terms of the number of interviews and possible secondary sources of data), and to introduce the investigators that will be participating in data collection (Yin, 2003) during the <sup>fi</sup>rst access meeting. Although access is usually granted based on goodwill, it often helps to offer something to the case organization in return; such as a teaching case based on the case data, or a summary presentation of the lessons learnt. This is because access is seldom one-off, and you never know when you might want to study another aspect of the organization in the future. Most importantly, there is a need to identify an in<sup>fl</sup>uential gatekeeper to assist with making the relevant introductions, refer informants that possess the required information, and scheduling the interviews. Unless the case study is being conducted on a very tight timeframe, it is also a good practice to offer to schedule the interviews at the convenience of the informants so as to minimize disruptions to the informants' routines.

## 4. Step 2: conceptualizing the phenomenon

Once access has been gained, the researcher should then turn his/her attention to preparing for data collection by developing a mental concept of the phenomenon. This can be done at two levels. First, the researcher can turn to “non-technical literature” (Strauss & Corbin, 1998, p. 52) such as mass-market books, biographies, letters, newspapers, electronic articles, or even the oft-vili<sup>fi</sup>ed Wikipedia, to gather important background information about both the organization and the phenomenon. The information gleaned from these sources can enhance the researcher's sensitivity towards the unique aspects and pertinent issues of the phenomenon or organization under study, provide the researcher with an idea of what to expect during data collection, and serve as the basis for formulating the questions for the initial interviews (Darke, Shanks, & Broadbent, 1998). It is always good practice for a case researcher to gather as much background information about the organization and the phenomenon as possible, regardless of whether he/she begins with case access (i.e. the planned opportunism approach) or with a research topic (i.e. the conventional approach). If the former approach is selected, then such information is naturally vital as the researcher may not have a concrete idea of what he/she intends to study to begin with. Even if the latter approach is selected, however, the information would be just as useful as the researcher may often <sup>fi</sup>nd while in the <sup>fi</sup>eld that the phenomenon of interest is not what he/she imagined it to be (Meyer, 2001). When that happens, the information may enable them to switch to a new research topic immediately (Eisenhardt, 1989) so that the opportunity for access will not be put to waste.

Second, based on the insights gained on the organizational context and the phenomenon of interest, we would propose that the researcher begin to “read widely on different theories” (Walsham, 2006, p. 325) so as to identify a number of candidate theories whose constructs and propositions are pertinent to the issues and aspects of the phenomenon under study. While there is some controversy with regard to the conduct of a review of prior theories before data collection (Halaweh. Fidler. & McRobb. 2008). like a number of other qualitative researchers, we are of the view that the ideal of a clean theoretical slate (Glaser & Strauss, 1967) is impossible (Eisenhardt, 1989). We believe that the human mind is <sup>fl</sup>exible and independent enough to read about theories without being trapped in them (Walsham, 1995), and more importantly, familiarity with theories can enhance the researcher's “sensitivity towards subtle nuances in data” (Strauss & Corbin, 1998, p. 49) and serve as “a complicated sensing device to register a complicated set of events” (Weick, 2007, p. 16) that a case study invariably is. Nevertheless, the researcher should constantly evaluate if theoretical preconceptions are leading him/her to conjure (Strauss & Corbin, 1998), or inhibit the acceptance of emergent data (Walsham, 1995). The rule of thumb is that if the emergent theoretical model feels detached from reality, or if process of theory building feels ‘forced’ at any time, then they probably are.

The case study we conducted of Alibaba in China (see Tan et al., 2009) serves as a good illustration of how to develop a mental concept of a phenomenon. After access was negotiated, we began preparing for data collection by gathering secondary data that would provide us with background information on the organization. These secondary data include a variety of books documenting the history and strategies of Alibaba; there were plenty of these books available on the mass market as Alibaba is one of the largest and most successful B2B portals in the world and the source of much national pride, a number of Internet articles, teaching cases that were predominantly written in Chinese, and internal publications provided by our gatekeeper, a Vice-President of Research and Training at Alibaba. Based on the secondary data, we were able to construct a chronological timeline of the key business initiatives launched by the organization over the years, which led to the realization that the strategic objectives of Alibaba were distinct across three temporal phases. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst phase (1999–2004), its strategies appeared to be centered on leveraging <sup>fi</sup>rmspeci<sup>fi</sup>c capabilities and its intimate knowledge of the Chinese Small and Medium Enterprise (SME) market. In the second phase (2005–2006), its strategies appeared to be focused on relentless innovation and capability development. Finally, in the third phase (2007–2009), its strategies appeared to be centered on developing the capabilities of the members of its trading platform.

This insight, in turn, led to the review of a number of different literatures. As the strategies of the <sup>fi</sup>rst phase appeared to echo the prescriptions of the Resource-Based View (RBV) of the <sup>fi</sup>rm (Barney, 1991) and the Dynamic Capabilities Approach (Teece, Pisano, & Shuen. 1997), we reviewed the literature on those theories. Similarly, we found traces of the prescriptions of the theories on Hypercompetition (D'Aveni, 1994) and IT-enabled enterprise agility (Sambamurthy. Bharadwai, & Grover. 2003) in phase 2, and those of the Complexity theory (Brown & Eisenhardt, 1997) and the literature on Business Ecosystems (Iansiti & Levien, 2004) in phase 3. Consequently, our attention was directed to the relevant literatures accordingly. While not all of the reviewed theories were useful in the end, by the end of this step, we had a clear mental concept of the phenomenon under study, we were keenly aware of the potential theoretical constructs and propositions of interest, and we were ready to begin data collection.

## 5. Step 3: collecting and organizing the initial data

Interviews form the primary source of data for case research because they provide the case researcher, who is external to the organization, with access to the views and interpretations of the informants (Walsham, 1995). As detailed treatments of the craft of conducting interviews are available elsewhere in the existing literature (e.g. Myers & Newman, 2007), we will not repeat them here, but we would like to point out that the focus of the initial interviews and that of the latter interviews should be very different. The <sup>fi</sup>rst interview, in particular, should be with an informant who can provide an overview of the phenomenon under study. This allows the researcher to validate and, if necessary, modify his/her mental concept of the phenomenon at the earliest available opportunity. While the <sup>fi</sup>rst informant does not need to have detailed knowledge of all aspects of the phenomenon under study, he should have a good idea of who the researcher can interview subsequently to obtain the required pieces of information. We would like to make the point here that “snowball” or “chain referral” sampling (Biernacki & Waldorf, 1981) is probably the only feasible means of identifying informants in case research because the researcher rarely has enough inside information to identify the right informants independently. However, as the informants may not be selected objectively, the researcher has to be sensitive to “possible ‘biases’ and systematic ‘distortions’” (Klein & Myers, 1999, p. 72) in the accounts provided by the informants.

After the <sup>fi</sup>rst interview, the researcher should already begin to organize the data to prepare for subsequent analyses and theory building. At this point, we would suggest that the strategy for organizing the data should depend on the ‘general shape’ of the data collected. For example, if the data appear to be anchored on an evolutionary process; such as the transformation of an organization's IT strategies or system implementation initiatives over time, we <sup>fi</sup>nd it useful to adopting a temporal bracketing strategy (see Langley, 1999) to conceptually distinguish between the various phases of evolution. For this type of data, we would also recommend the use of a visual mapping strategy (see Langley, 1999) to construct a chronological timeline of the key events, activities and decisions that transpired at the focal organization with regard to the phenomenon of interest (for an illustration of the visual mapping strategy, see Pan, Pan, & Newman, 2009). The timeline will clarify the delineation between the different evolutionary phases, provide a clear overall picture of the evolutionary process under study; which is especially useful if multiple investigators are involved, and make it easier for the researcher to clarify his/her interpretation of what happened with the study's informants (Klein & Myers, 1999). On the other hand, if the data appear to be centered on a unique organizational routine (e.g. Pan, Pan, Chen, & Hsieh, 2007), a series of inter-related factors (e.g. Kim & Pan, 2006), or some other form of recurrent phenomenon that is independent of time, the data can be organized through “open coding” (Strauss & Corbin, 1998, p. 101) in which the data are broken down into themes (Walsham, 2006), examined, compared for similarities and differences, and categorized (for a review, refer to Strauss & Corbin, 1998).

To illustrate using a case study that we conducted of Taiwan Teleservices and Technologies (TT&T), a large organization providing call center outsourcing services in Taiwan (see Pan et al., 2007), our <sup>fi</sup>rst interview in the organization was with the Deputy Director of Development and Planning, who also happened to be the gatekeeper that granted us access to the organization. The interview provided us with a broad overview of the operations of the call center, which led to the realization that what was particularly unique about TT&T was its capability for switching seamlessly to provide call center support for a variety of clients. Based on this insight, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the information we required of TT&T's operations, strategies and routines and passed this on to the gatekeeper, who then directed us to the relevant stakeholders within the organization who could provide us with the required information. More importantly, after the <sup>fi</sup>rst interview, we realized that the phenomenon of interest in this study was a recurrent operational routine that did not involve the dimension of temporality. Consequently, we used the techniques of open coding to break down the data from the initial interviews into a set of themes and issues, which was used to facilitate our subsequent theorizing from the case data.

## 6. Step 4: constructing and extending the theoretical lens

Following the initial collection and organization of data, the researcher is ready to embark on a preliminary stage of theory building. Might it not be better to collect more data so that the theorist can have more empirical materials to work with? The answer, in our view, is no. We believe that the process of theory building should begin as early as possible so as to take full advantage of the <sup>fl</sup>exibility that the case research method affords (Eisenhardt, 1989). Theory building begins with the selection of an appropriate guiding theory (Walsham, 1995). While the choice of theory is a subjective one, it is important to choose one that is at the appropriate level of analysis (Yin, 2003), insightful (Walsham, 2006), and whose concepts and propositions represent a close <sup>fi</sup>t with the empirical reality (Eisenhardt, 1989). From a pragmatic standpoint, it is also not a good idea to choose a theory that is (1) dated, as it may be perceived to be no longer relevant, (2) immature, as the theory will not provide a strong theoretical foundation for building new theories, (3) overused, as it makes it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to justify a contribution, or (4) overly practitioneroriented, as it may be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to translate to the academic vernacular.

After a guiding theory has been chosen, the next step consists of breaking down the selected theory into its component constructs and propositions to create a set of categories. This set of categories forms the initial theoretical lens, which will subsequently be used to guide latter iterations of data collection and analysis (Klein & Myers, 1999). The initial lens may be thought of as an incomplete data table. To illustrate using a simpli<sup>fi</sup>ed example, if we select the RBV (Barney, 1991) as our guiding theory, then based on its fundamental proposition that <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c resources and capabilities lead to the attainment of competitive advantage, the proposition itself, as well as the component constructs ‘firm-specific capabilities’ and ‘competitive advantage’, will form the categories of our initial lens (i.e. the left column of Table 2). The initial lens can then be used to guide the next iteration of the framing cycle: The identi<sup>fi</sup>ed categories can be used to indicate theoretical constructs and propositions of interest to re<sup>fi</sup>ne the researcher's mental concept of the phenomenon (Step 2), and used as the basis for formulating the questions for subsequent interviews (Strauss & Corbin, 1998) and organizing the data (Step 3), which, to reuse our earlier example, is akin to <sup>fi</sup>lling out the incomplete table with empirical evidence that substantiates the conceptual categories (i.e. the right column of Table 2).

However, it is insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient to simply complete the table or the study will be reduced to an explanatory case study that validates the original propositions of the selected theory (Yin, 2003). Instead, when surprising data that cannot easily <sup>fi</sup>t within the existing schema emerge as they invariably do (Weick, 2007), the researcher can extend the initial lens by including additional categories in the form of new constructs, propositions, or contingent conditions. When this happens, the theoretical lens is no longer a static, theory-bound set of categories, but a dynamic, generative structure that can be used to support the construction of theory.

The case study we conducted of one of the largest global IT services and consulting companies from India (see Ravishankar et al., 2011) provides a good example of how to construct an initial lens and subsequently extend it. The phenomenon of the study was the implementation of a Knowledge Management System (KMS) in three distinct business units within the organization. While the phenomenon itself may be relatively well-studied in the IS literature, we felt that it was rarely examined from the perspective of alignment. despite the importance of alignment to the success of KMS implementation (Schultze & Orlikowski, 2004). Consequently, our initial theoretical lens was developed from the constructs and propositions from the alignment literature (e.g. Sabherwal, Hirschheim, & Goles, 2001). However, with subsequent iterations of data collection, the emergent data revealed that beyond alignment at the organizational level (e.g. Chan, 2002), alignment at the sub-cultural level was also an important determinant of successful systems implementation. Consequently, we began to incorporate constructs and propositions from the literature on organizational subcultures (Martin & Siehl, 1983) to extend our theoretical lens, which served as the foundation for our subsequent theorizing.

With each iteration of the framing cycle, the theoretical lens is incrementally extended until the point of theoretical con<sup>fi</sup>dence is reached. At this point, the researcher should be suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently con<sup>fi</sup>dent that the extended lens has captured the essence of the phenomenon under study (i.e. the included constructs and propositions provide an adequate depiction of the empirical reality). This can be ascertained by sketching the constructs and propositions of the theoretical lens diagrammatically, and validating the diagram with the relevant informants within the case organization (Neuman, 2005). In addition, the researcher should also be con<sup>fi</sup>dent of the contributions of the theory that can be derived when the lens is fully corroborated by the case data. While it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to specify a set of objective evaluation criteria for the contributions of a study, we would suggest three questions that should be foremost in the researcher's mind. First, ‘What is my contribution to the literature on the phenomenon?’ Second, ‘What is my contribution to the literature from which I derived my theoretical foundation?’ And <sup>fi</sup>nally, ‘How can a practitioner benefit from the reading of my work?’ If the researcher is able to <sup>fi</sup>nd satisfactory answers to each of these questions, then he/she is ready to enter the augmenting cycle of the proposed SPS approach.

## 7. Step 5: con<sup>fi</sup>rming and validating data

With a structure provided by a validated lens in place, the <sup>fi</sup>rst step of the augmenting cycle is probably the most straightforward step of the proposed SPS approach. At this point, if the conditions of theoretical con<sup>fi</sup>dence have been aptly met, it would be reasonable to expect no further radical changes to the set of categories that comprise the theoretical lens, and the focus of data collection can turn to (1) gathering the evidence to transform the theoretical lens into a full-<sup>fl</sup>edged theory, and (2) ensuring the validity of the data collected.

For the <sup>fi</sup>rst objective, the researcher has to ensure that there is suf<sup>fi</sup>cient data. Although suf<sup>fi</sup>ciency is most commonly associated with the point of theoretical saturation in many accounts of the case research method (e.g. Eisenhardt, 1989; Glaser & Strauss, 1967), we would like to add two important caveats. First, although there are no <sup>fi</sup>xed prescriptions for the number of interviews to be conducted for a case study (Patton, 2003), it is generally dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to claim that the state of theoretical saturation has been reached when the number of interviews conducted is very small. The same can be said for the number of unique informants. Although the appropriate number of interviews or unique informants for a case study depends on a number of factors; such as the size of the organization, the phenomenon of interest, as well as the scope and timeframe of the study, we would generally recommend conducting a minimum of 15 interviews, with no repeat informants, for each case organization studied to ensure the representation of “a variety of voices” (Myers & Newman, 2007, p. 22), and to avoid controversy. Second, besides having an adequate number of interviews or unique informants, it is also important to ensure that the interview data to be included in the eventual case report does not come from only a small subset of the informants. While certain informants may be particularly informative or effusive, having ‘dominant voices’ in the case report is something to be avoided as the study may be subjected to criticisms of biased reporting.

For the second objective, we would suggest that the researcher applies the principles of suspicion and multiple interpretations (see Klein & Myers, 1999) to ensure the validity of the data collected. To do this, the researcher has to ensure that every piece of evidence that he/she intends to use in the construction of the case study is triangulated by at least two sources of data (Yin, 2003). However, if the con<sup>fl</sup>icting interpretations of the same phenomenon can all be corroborated by additional sources, then the researcher has to look for an additional data source that can objectively mediate between the con<sup>fl</sup>icting accounts. To cite a quick example, in the case study we conducted of Hardwarezone, a highly successful e-commerce startup that revolutionalized the IT publications industry in Singapore, we wanted to reconstruct the story ofits performance and growth over the vears (see Tan. Pan, & Hackney. 2010c). However, in our interviews. the informants from the organization and those from its competitors provided two con<sup>fl</sup>icting pictures; naturally, the <sup>fi</sup>rst group was inclined towards a more positive view while the second group was inclined towards a more negative view. To resolve the conflict. we conducted additional interviews with two informants who were working for an advertising <sup>fi</sup>rm and were familiar with the happenings within the IT publications industry. We perceived the information from these sources to be objective as it stemmed from neutral sources external to the industry in question with no vested interests in supporting either of the con<sup>fl</sup>icting views. This enabled us to make an informed choice about the data we wanted to include (Klein & Myers, 1999).

## 8. Step 6: selective coding

A theoretical lens becomes a quali<sup>fi</sup>ed theory only when all of its conceptual categories are corroborated and integrated with the case data in a process that some scholars term “selective coding” (Strauss & Corbin, 1998, p. 143). In simpler terms, if a theoretical lens is conceptualized as an incomplete data table (e.g. Table 2 extended with new constructs and propositions as additional categories), then selective coding can be understood as the process of <sup>fi</sup>lling in the table with at least two sources of data (Yin, 2003) for every category. While the existing prescriptions for selective coding and the inductive derivation of theories in the literature may range from the scienti<sup>fi</sup>c (e.g. Miles & Huberman, 1994) to the artistic (e.g. Walsham, 2006), our personal preference is a ‘middle-of-the-road’ approach that combines the structure of the scienti<sup>fi</sup>c approaches with the elegant simplicity of the artistic ones.

To begin the process of selective coding, we would <sup>fi</sup>rst adopt a narrative strategy (Langley, 1999). Typically, at this point in time, a sizeable amount of data would have already been collected, and the purpose of the narrative strategy is to condense the voluminous amount of primary and secondary data to a more manageable form so as to avoid “death by data asphyxiation” (Pettigrew, 1990, p. 281). The essence of the strategy is to summarize the data collected in the form of a story, but a notable difference between our approach and the approach advocated by Langley (1999) is that we are suggesting a progressive form of story development, with new pieces of information incorporated into the narrative incrementally with each iteration of the augmenting cycle. The purpose of the narrative strategy is to help the researcher visualize the order of events that unfolded. as well as to clarify the association between decisions made. activities carried out, and their consequences. Moreover, assuming that the narrative is written well, the entire narrative can be ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently ported to the eventual case report with minor changes.

Next, we would also supplement the narrative with plenty of data summary devices like tables and diagrams that are <sup>fi</sup>lled with quotes from the interviews conducted. To illustrate, we would like to attach one such diagram (refer to Fig. 2) that we have developed for a case study of Shanghai Tobacco Corporation (see Tan, Pan, Chen, & Huang, 2010b); the largest state-owned tobacco <sup>fi</sup>rm in China (for an example of the table of quotes we alluded to earlier, please refer to Tan, Pan, & Hackney, 2010c). These data summary devices bring about a number of important advantages. First, by capturing the key constructs and propositions of the theoretical lens of the study, the devices can serve as a means of organizing thought, even if they are not included in the eventual case report (Pratt, 2009). Second, by indicating which section of the model under development requires more data (as denoted by the question marks in Fig. 2), the devices can provide a guide for subsequent data collection. Finally, a familiar predicament for case researchers is that we almost never seem to have enough space to present the empirical evidence needed to substantiate our theoretical arguments. As compared to the conventional method of presenting interview data in a separate, indented paragraph (e.g. Pan & Leidner, 2003), these summary devices provide the means of displaying the supporting evidence concisely, which enables the case researcher to present more empirical data within an allocated space. With each iteration of selective coding (Strauss & Corbin, 1998), the skeletal structure provided by the theoretical lens is a step closer to becoming an empirically-supported theory. But with each step of theoretical development, there is a need to ensure theory-data-model alignment (Klein & Myers, 1999), which forms the next step of the proposed SPS approach.

## 9. Step 7: ensuring theory-data-model alignment

After selective coding, the new developments in the emergent model should be checked for theorydata-model alignment before the next iteration of the augmenting cycle so that the researcher can adapt his/her subsequent data collection strategy accordingly. As its names suggests, the process of ensuring theory-data-model alignment involves recursively iterating between existing theories, data, and the emergent model (Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007) to ensure that the three dimensions are aligned. In othe words, theory-data-model alignment can be conceived to consist of three types of alignment; (1) theorydata alignment, (2) data-model alignment, and (3) theory-model alignment. Ensuring each of the three types of alignment confers the attributes of simplicity, accuracy and generality to the emergent model respectively. These are attributes that characterize an ideal theory (Sutton & Staw, 1995; Weick, 1979), and we will explain how each of the three types of alignment brings about the corresponding attribute in turn.

The essence of theory-data alignment is best captured by the question ‘Can the case data be explained by existing theories?’ Given that any theory is inevitably a simpli<sup>fi</sup>cation of a complex reality (Pettigrew, 1990; Weick, 2007), the purpose of ensuring theory-data alignment is to <sup>fi</sup>nd a succinct and elegant theoretical explanation for the case data that helps in “preventing the observer from being dazzled by the full-blown complexity of natural or concrete events” (Hall, Lindzey, & Campbell, 1998, p. 13). To achieve this, the researcher must constantly compare the emergent data with the competing explanations for the data that are proffered by alternative theories (Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007) and, assuming equivalent validity, pick the most pointed explanation. This is important because constructing a model with parsimonious theoretical arguments has positive implications for its overall comprehensibility (Bacharach, 1989) and, from a practical perspective, enhance the probability that it will be picked up, used and cited in future research.

On the other hand, data-model alignment is best represented by the question 'Does the data support the emergent model?’ Since an inductively derived model is invariably a product of the researcher's mind (Walsham, 2006), the objective of data-model alignment is to ensure that the constructed model is an accurate depiction of empirical reality, which is of course, the basic aim of theory building (Eisenhardt, 1989). To check for data-model alignment, the researcher can use the data summary devices described in the Step 6 to conduct a ‘test of inclusion’; by ensuring that the interview data to be included in the case study corroborate the constructs and propositions of the emergent model. In addition, the researcher can re-examine the data that have been left out from the case study in a ‘test of exclusion’; by ensuring that the data that have been excluded does not contradict or extend the fundamental arguments of the emergent model.

Finally. the concept of theorv-model alignment may be rendered as the question 'Do existing theories support the emergent model?’ Given that a theory must be generalizable in order to be useful, and that statistical generalization is impossible from case studies, the alignment between the emergent model and the existing theories in the literature is crucial as it provides a different means of establishing generality (Lee & Baskerville, 2003). In particular, if the emergent model, which is essentially a stylized representation of the empirical reality, resonates with previously developed theories, then generality may be claimed on the basis of the logic of replication (see Yin, 2003). To ensure theory-model alignment, we would suggest that the researcher systematically decomposes the emergent model into a set of propositions and tries to explain each of the propositions based on the theoretical arguments of prior theories. In order to claim to have generalized from the emergent model to theory (Walsham, 1995), there should be theoretical explanations for all of the propositions of the emergent model, even for the most surprising <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

When the developments in the emergent model for the present iteration of the augmenting cycle have been validated for theory-data-model alignment, the researcher has to decide if the point of theoretical saturation (Eisenhardt, 1989) and data suf<sup>fi</sup>ciency (refer to Step 5) has been reached. In particular, if the researcher is experiencing signi<sup>fi</sup>cant overlaps in the data collected, or <sup>fi</sup>nding it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to extend the emergent model in a meaningful way (Strauss & Corbin, 1998), then he/she is probably ready to exit the augmenting cycle and move on to the <sup>fi</sup>nal step of conducting case research: the writing of the case report

## 10. Step 8: writing the case report

In our view, a <sup>fl</sup>air for writing, although helpful, is not a pre-requisite for the ability to publish case research. A case study that addresses research issues of great academic and practical signi<sup>fi</sup>cance remains just that, even if it is expressed in a more mundane form. If a paper is rejected, it is more likely that the researcher was not able to put forth his/her theoretical arguments in a coherent or convincing way, which requires the demonstration of a clear chain of logic (Klein & Myers, 1999), and the ability to express oneself clearly.

To establish a clear chain of logic, we would prescribe a highly structured approach to reporting the case study. This is because a structured approach to writing helps to (1) ensure that nothing important is left out, (2) strengthen the linkages between the various sections of the case report, and (3) enhance the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of writing. Although a number of approaches for writing case research has been described in the literature (for a review, see van de Blonk, 2003), we recommend the pragmatic approach advocated by Pratt (2008) for two reasons. First, as this approach adopts “the general formatting of a quantitative paper” (Pratt, 2008, p. 496), it is designed to evoke less controversy and is likely to be more palatable for a range of reviewers. The format consists of six standard sections: (1) Introduction, (2) Literature review, (3) Research method, (4) Results, (5) Discussion, and (6) Conclusion (Golden-Biddle & Locke, 1997; Yin, 2003). Although minor variations exist, the critical components that are generally expected in each of the sections are listed in the following table (refer to Table 3).

Second, as compared to more traditional approaches that advocate the separation of data and theory (see van Maanen, 1979), this approach meshes case <sup>fi</sup>ndings and theoretical interpretation in the results section (e.g. Chua, Wareham, & Robey, 2007). This enhances ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and comprehensibility as it removes the burden of connecting the theoretical points of a conventional case description section, where data is presented without theory (e.g. Pratt & Rosa, 2003), from the ensuing discussion section. The integrated presentation of data and theory is especially appropriate for papers involving multiple cases as there is rarely enough space to present multiple case descriptions (Pratt, 2008), Moreover, it might be unreasonable to expect the reader to plough through all of them before a theoretical interpretation is offered.

While the ability for expressing oneself clearly is often associated with language pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency, from our experience in coaching case researchers from China and Taiwan, we found that even for non-native speakers of the English language, it is possible to convey theoretical ideas with clarity through the use of organizing tables and diagrams. Organizing tables and diagrams may be used in all the sections of a case research paper and can serve to depict methodological procedures, display a chain of evidence, illustrate complex analyses, summarize <sup>fi</sup>ndings (e.g. refer to Step 6), or explain the process of inductive derivation (Pratt, 2009). More importantly, the use of these devices carries two additional advantages. First, it reduces the burden of language as there is less of a need for elegant and <sup>fl</sup>owing prose. Second, it captures the essential information succinctly, such that the reader does not have to pore over every line and every word in order to grasp the author's intention. This does not mean, however, that the use of these devices should be taken to the extreme — a balance between the presentation modes (i.e. text and summary devices) is needed. These devices should be used to summarize, complement or substantiate, but not completely replace, textual paragraphs.

## 11. Conclusion

## 11.1. Limitations, caveats and future directions

The article is not without its limitations. First, the steps of our approach are presented procedurally with the aim of providing the less experienced with a <sup>fi</sup>rm guiding hand throughout the entire research process. The fact that our prescriptions are presented procedurally, however, does not mean that we are advocating a single, absolute path to conducting case research. While our conviction in the utility and ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of the proposed approach is <sup>fi</sup>rm, we must acknowledge that much of the knowledge on how to conduct case research remains tacit (Seale, 1999), and we strongly urge our readers to constantly re<sup>fl</sup>ect on the proposed steps in relation to their speci<sup>fi</sup>c research context and adjust the prescribed approach when necessary (Klein & Myers, 1999). This may require that one iterates recursively between some of the steps and modify their conceptualization of the phenomenon, or the methods and techniques of data collection and analysis. In other words, adhering to steps one to eight of our “workable” approach can and should result in the production of a case study, but for optimal results, our prescriptions are best approached as a general guideline that is to be adapted or augmented as research progresses.

A second limitation is that the focus of our approach is limited to a single, albeit the most common (Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007), form of case research (i.e. inductive, exploratory type case studies aimed at theory building). As indicated earlier, we opted to focus on this form of case research as it takes full advantage of the intrinsic strengths of the method (Siggelkow, 2007), but it must be acknowledged that our prescriptions may not be universally and invariably applicable for all research objectives. Although it is certainly impossible to exhaustively describe all the possible ways in which case research can be conducted within a single paper, future work can certainly be directed at developing similar guides for other forms of case research. These may include descriptive case studies (e.g. Lacity & Fox, 2008), explanatory case studies (e.g. Sambamurthy & Zmud, 1999) and exploratory case studies aimed at deeply examining a speci<sup>fi</sup>c causal relation (e.g. Beath & Ross, 2007a, 2007b).

## 11.2. Theoretical and practical contributions

Although this article was primarily motivated by a practical need (i.e. addressing the common difficulties that confront many case researchers), we nevertheless contend that our work has provided a number of theoretical contributions. For methodology researchers, this study has introduced a number of conceptual innovations (e.g. framing cycle, augmenting cycle, the state of theoretical con<sup>fi</sup>dence, etc.) and variations to the existing prescriptions for the conduct of case studies (e.g. alternative approaches to case selection, differentiated initial and latter interviews, the need to ensure theory-data-model alignment, etc.). More importantly, by integrating the prescriptions of some of the most frequently cited works on the conduct of case research (e.g. Klein & Myers, 1999; Walsham, 1995; Yin, 2003) within a coherent research approach, we have essentially constructed a process model for the conduct of case research (represented by Fig. 1). In doing so, we hope that the process model can serve as a common base of communication and a signpost for future research on the case research methodology, so that in turn, clarity and further theoretical advancement may be achieved.

In terms of practical contributions, the utility of this article lies in its speci<sup>fi</sup>c, actionable, and more importantly, viable prescriptions, as well as the substantive recommendations on how to be <sup>fl</sup>exible and adaptive when conducting case research (e.g. selecting a case based on a “planned opportunism” (Pettigrew, 1990, p. 274) approach, reading of theories to enhance sensitivity to subtle nuances in data, initiating theory building at the earliest available opportunity, etc.). For experienced case researchers, this provides a different vantage point to a process with which they are familiar, and this article may serve as a useful point of reference that provides them with the basis of evaluating and re<sup>fl</sup>ecting on their own work. In addition, by providing an account of the case research method that is structured, pragmatic, and situational, aspiring researchers can use this article as a step-by-step guide to conducting and reporting case research. In doing so, it is hoped that the proposed SPS approach can serve to reduce their learning curve and consequently, facilitate their induction into the world of case research.
