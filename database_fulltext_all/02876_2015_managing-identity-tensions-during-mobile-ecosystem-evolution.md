## Abstract

The idea of an ecosystem suggests a holistic framing of how heterogeneous actors relate to one another and of the dynamics of their relationships. Because of the dynamics some relationships will become uncertain, posing signi<sup>fi</sup>cant challenge to the identity of participating organizations. Unfortunately, the Information Systems (IS) literature has not examined how organizations develop and negotiate their identities during ecosystem evolution. We <sup>fi</sup>l this void by exploring identity challenges that Swedish Road Administration (SRA) faced while implementing the Radio Data System – Traf<sup>fi</sup>c Message Channel (RDS – TMC) traf<sup>fi</sup>c information service. Through a longitudinal case study we follow how SRA’s inherited expectations, guiding norms, and standards of sense-giving about its identity prevented it from becoming a <sup>fl</sup>exible service provider within an emerging mobile ecosystem. We record a constant clash – the identity tension – between the old inherited identity of a public road administrator and the aspiring new identity of a digital service provider. To enact a successful identity change, SRA had to engage in a series of change episodes whereby it deliberately implemented new routines that forged novel relationships with actors within the ecosystem. This permitted SRA to gradually align its identity to the evolving needs of the RDS-TMC service ecosystem. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that deliberate attempts to implement innovative mobile services – especially those involving public-private partnerships – trigger intriguing identity ambiguities and role dilemmas, and future research should therefore focus on effective strategies to identify, manage, and resolve inherent identity tensions. ## Introduction

riven by mobility, convergence, and mass-scale (Lyytinen<sub>and</sub> <sub>Yoo,</sub> <sub>2002;</sub> <sub>Yoo</sub> <sub>et</sub> <sub>al.,</sub> <sub>2012),</sub> <sub>today’s</sub> <sub>Information</sub> and Yoo, 2002; Yoo et al., 2012), today's Information Technology (IT) solutions provide new and varying information services to a wide range of nomadic users (see e.g., DHenfridsson and Lindgren, 2005; Mathiassen and Sørensen, 2008; Henfridsson and Lindgren, 2010; Yoo, 2010; Hedman et al., 2013). These services share a great deal with common consumer services by being heterogeneous, intangible, and perishable (Barrett and Davidson, 2008; Williams et al., 2008), and their use often evolves and varies unexpectedly (Andersson and Lindgren, 2005, 2010; Henfridsson and Lindgren, 2005; Lindgren et al., 2008; Henfridsson and Lindgren, 2010). One reason for this is that these services integrate co-creating consumers, producers, and other stakeholders around an evolving ecosystem (Tiwana et al., 2010; Vargo and Lusch, 2011; Ghazawneh and Henfridsson, 2013; Selander et al., 2013; Wareham et al., 2014; Eaton et al., 2015).

A growing stream of research has investigated how mobile ecosystems emerge and grow and how the services become coordinated through standards (e.g., Tilson et al., 2010; Tiwana et al., 2010; Lindgren and Holgersson, 2012). Less attention, however, has been paid to how participating organizations need to adapt their structures, processes, behaviors, or sense-making and sense-giving to successfully participate inservice delivery. Recently, Basole (2009) and Basole and Karla (2011) pointed out that mobile services often spark tensions (Lewis, 2000) over what the services mean for participating actors and consequently how they should be delivered. The service providers must therefore <sup>fl</sup>exibly adapt themselves as the ecosystem evolves to balance the tensions by orchestrating capabilities, changing processes, and modifying structures and meanings that underpin the service (Andersson et al., 2008; Gal et al., 2008; Basole, 2009; Edwards et al., 2009; Ribes and Finholt, 2009).

One of these necessary adaptive responses is how the partnerships are rede<sup>fi</sup>ned and how they change the roles and identities of involved actors (Kwak et al., 2009), i.e., how participation in the service delivery affects the identity of the participating organization. Organizational identity, in general, can be de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a set of mutually shared and enforced expectations, norms about what is the ‘core’ of an organization and what consequently constitutes a range of legitimate behaviors for that organization (Corley and Gioia, 2004). When calls for identity renewal emerge – such as those experienced while participating in a new ecosystem – we note an identity tension – an inconsistency in how the participating organization continues to de<sup>fi</sup>ne its identity and de<sup>fi</sup>nes its relationships to the environment when, in fact, its salient relationships with that environment have changed (Tripsas, 2009).

Participating in a mobile ecosystem (Basole, 2009; Basole and Karla, 2011) is therefore likely to question the participating organization’s identity (Yoo et al., 2006; Boland et al., 2007; Tripsas, 2009). It is consequently important to understand how organizations navigate and reconcile successfully emerging identity tensions. Extant Information Systems (IS) literature, however, provides little guidance on how organizations effectively manage and resolve such tensions. Most studies of identity in the IS context focus on the effects of IT use on individual user identity or the interactions between organizational identity and ways of using internal IT (Whitley et al., 2014). To our knowledge no prior research has examined the role of identity change in designing, implementing, and delivering information services in (mobile) ecosystems. We next address this gap by examining the following question: How can an organization manage and resolve its emerging identity tensions when it participates in a mobile ecosystem to deliver information services? By addressing this question our study thus addresses recent calls for studies of emerging mobile ecosystems and their dynamics (Basole, 2009; Basole and Karla, 2011 Tilson et al., 2010; Sørensen, 2014).

To answer our research question we engage in an exploratory longitudinal case study of the Swedish Road Administration’s (SRA) successful endeavors to implement and manage the delivery of a worldwide traf<sup>fi</sup>c information service called Radio Data System - Traf<sup>fi</sup>c Message Channel (RDS-TMC). The service is currently the only language-neutral (mobile) traf<sup>fi</sup>c information service in Europe. It is founded on two vertical standards – Alert-C and Location Code – that specify the functional nexus of the service. They offer a vertical ‘grammar’ for communication between multiple actors and systems about location-based traf<sup>fi</sup>c events and states (cf., Damsgaard and Truex, 2000; Markus et al., 2006; Bala and Venkatesh, 2007; Malhotra et al., 2007; Andersson et al., 2008). Given the undeveloped state of the research in the <sup>fi</sup>eld, we engage in theory building around identity dynamics in mobile ecosystems by exploring over a 13-year period how SRA set out to transform core elements of its identity in response to unexpected identity tensions. In short, during this period SRA had to forge a new identity whereby its members and external constituents altered their deeply held assumptions and beliefs about its legitimacy and mission.

The remainder of the paper will proceed as follows. First, we review the related literature that has scrutinized information services, innovation ecosystems, and identity tensions. In the next section, we describe the research context and detail the study method. Thereafter, we present <sup>fi</sup>ndings from our longitudinal case study. We conclude with implications for future research on organizational strategies to deal with identity tensions during mobile ecosystem evolution.

## Theory review

## Mobile ecosystems for service innovation

In the service research, IT is commonly recognized as a medium that leverages service innovation (Bitner et al., 2000; Michel et al., 2008). For example, technology can serve as a repository and delivery mechanism for customer information or as a process tool that caters for customer involvement during co-creation (Bitner et al., 2000). Technological choices and the capacity to successfully implement services therefore have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on a service organization’s ability to create value-adding services (Michel et al., 2008). However, IT-enabled service delivery raises fundamental questions about the underlying service concepts and their logics (Vargo and Lusch, 2008). One of these concerns how such delivery needs to be organized and what roles different actors play during the service delivery. Indeed, despite calls for research on the nature of service ecosystems that enable novel value cocreation, detailed studies of such phenomena remain largely absent (Vargo et al., 2008; Vargo and Lusch, 2011).

Not surprisingly, IS researchers have recently turned to service ecosystems that offer novel research opportunities to address such questions. These opportunities spring largely from the increased digitalization and resulting growing complexity of services (Barrett and Davidson, 2008; Williams et al., 2008). As early as a decade ago, Lyytinen and Yoo (2002: 378) envisioned that computing services were about to change and would become increasingly mobile: ‘In the past, computing services were always provided in a stationary location. Accordingly, users had to come to the physical site to receive the service. For example, a user of a desktop computer needs to come to his or her of<sup>fi</sup>ce to use it. In a nomadic information environment, however, all this will change: services will come to the users whenever and wherever they are needed’. Given that such settings change constantly, their inherent dynamism transcends multiple use contexts (Lindgren and Wiberg, 2000; Henfridsson and Lindgren, 2005; Sørensen and Pica, 2005; Ferneley and Light, 2008; Kietzmann, 2008; Lindgren et al., 2008; Henfridsson and Lindgren, 2010) and creates unprecedented levels of generativity calling for constant modi<sup>fi</sup>cation of services (Tilson et al., 2010), often through industry-level ‘socio-technical engineering’ and standard setting (Andersson et al., 2008; Lyytinen et al., 2008; Grøtnes, 2009).

We de<sup>fi</sup>ne a mobile ecosystem as an extensive and complex network of heterogeneous actors (both public and private) who interact with each other, directly or indirectly, to deliver mobile products and services to customers (Basole, 2009). Such an ecosystem consists of resource-integrating actors who exchange services in order to co-create service (Tiwana et al., 2010; Vargo and Lusch, 2011; Ghazawneh and Henfridsson, 2013; Selander et al., 2013; Wareham et al., 2014; Eaton et al., 2015). Coordination among partners is characterized by simultaneous cooperation and competition that follow the new ‘rules’ of the game (Adner and Kapoor, 2010). Due to the ‘coopetitive’ relationships, service ecosystems are also characterized by high levels of ambiguity (Lyytinen et al., 2008), which makes it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to predict the range of consequences that speci<sup>fi</sup>c technological solutions have for different participants. Recent research suggests that participating in such complex service systems is also likely to re-de<sup>fi</sup>ne the roles and identities of involved actors (Kwak et al., 2009). Simply put, as the stakes grow higher the identity of the participating organizations becomes uncertain.

## Research on organizational identity

According to Whitley et al. (2014: 19), ‘organisational identity is generally understood to be the collective understanding of members of an organisation of the features that are presumed to be central, distinctive and relatively permanent about the organization’. Identity, in a way, provides its members a normative territory, which its members can identify with (Kogut and Zander, 1996). The identi<sup>fi</sup>cation de<sup>fi</sup>nes and ‘economizes’ the rules by which members coordinate their actions, behavior, decision making, sense-making, and sensegiving. It also sets out the process by which individuals continue to learn, leading to the formation of convergent expectations about who they are and what they are for. Over time, the members will identify with a special type of organizational identity and related legitimate behaviors.

In essence, identity is rooted in a deep cultural level of the organization (Gioia et al., 2000); it resides in interpretive schemes that organizational members construct to provide meaning to their shared history, experiences, and activity (Ravasi and Schultz, 2006). It consists of two key elements: internal identity, which guides the actions and interpretations of organization’s members; and external identity, which captures how outside audiences such as institutional actors, customers, suppliers, or complementary producers view the organization and relate to it (Tripsas, 2009).

Most researchers agree that identity is reproduced through ongoing communicative activities that take place within and across people and organizations. Thus, IT is likely to transform the way organizations communicate and it affects the way individuals in organizations think about individual and organizational identity (Gal et al., 2008). Yet, while research on organizational identity has raised a growing interest in a diversity of <sup>fi</sup>elds, e.g., organization studies, corporate communications, social and organizational psychology, personnel and human resources, and strategy and marketing, the notion of organizational identity has not been prominent in IS research. It has been used sporadically as a lens through which to understand and explain individuals’ use of systems, organizational changes associated with system implementation, or ways of design ing systems (Whitley et al., 2014). There is a dearth of research that uses organizational identity as a lens through which to understand the interplay between organizational identity and strategic uses of IT. No study has examined the role of (external) identities in designing and delivering information services in (mobile) ecosystems. At the same time, Whitley et al. (2014) note, in particular, the need to focus on such identity processes at the level of organizations. This is what we will focus on next.

## Identity tensions in mobile ecosystems

Organizational identity stabilizes core aspects of ‘who we are’ and ‘what are we for.’ Thus, both insiders and outsiders will ascribe speci<sup>fi</sup>c characteristics to organizational identity and form speci<sup>fi</sup>c expectations and codes about how the organization should act. In cases where an organization starts to deviate from these ‘rules’, it is likely to spawn ambiguity both internally and externally around the actual identity of the organization (Tripsas, 2009). Constant search for questions, ‘Who are we? and ‘How do others know who we are?’ reveals the surge of new identity tensions (Whitley et al., 2014). These tensions manifest inconsistency in how an organization continues to de<sup>fi</sup>ne its identity when its salient relationships with the environment have changed (Tripsas, 2009).

To reconcile this tension either the identity must change or the deviant behaviors must go away. The tension may polarize, however, if the organization must continue to engage in both and cannot do either of them. Such a situation can, for example, emerge when the organization has agreed to engage in a new strategic innovation by provisioning information services in a mobile ecosystem (new deviant behavior). New technologyenabled capabilities will recon<sup>fi</sup>gure the organization’s operations and rede<sup>fi</sup>ne its relationships with external stakeholders and reshape how it sees itself. But this contradicts its past identity as an administrator of physical roads. In a situation like this the continued delivery of the service will question the dominant identity, and it becomes increasingly dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for organizational members to answer the simple question: ‘Who are we?’ That is, inherited institutionalized referents that have helped in the past to de<sup>fi</sup>ne identity lose relevance (Corley and Gioia, 2004). Incongruities also spark constant questions as to how insiders and outsiders perceive the mission and nature of the organization (Tripsas, 2009).

Overall, the presence of such an identity tension implies the availability of multiple plausible interpretations of which core features de<sup>fi</sup>ne the organization (Corley and Gioia, 2004). Indeed, when multiple alternatives are present with regard to ‘who we are going to be’ the organization enters a transitory context calling for an identity change. Deciding which interpretations can prevail is necessary to create a new ‘common ground’ (Zammuto et al., 2007) through an ‘identity clearance (Corley and Gioia, 2004). An organization also needs to resolve the tension as continued confusion will lead to gradual erosion of sense-making, values, and commitments, and the organization will face growing dif<sup>fi</sup>culties in legitimating and giving sense to its operations. That is, it must either take steps to undergo the identity change or lose its ‘organizational mind. Given the inability of (established) organizations to overcome organizational inertia (Gilbert, 2005), however, the necessary, explicit efforts to shift identity so as to effectively accommodate identity-challenging technology are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to carry out (Tripsas, 2009). They threaten deep-seated expectations and behavioral habits and therefore often play out incrementally. But changes can also be discontinuous and sweeping (Corley and Gioia, 2004).

To change its identity the organization must engage in a series of deliberate attempts to negotiate new roles and establish new relationships that will mobilize the interests of its members, but are also seen as legitimate and valuable to outsiders. At the same time, the organization must align with the operational and identity requirements dictated by the ecosystem (Van de Ven, 2005; Adner and Kapoor, 2010; Wareham et al., 2014). Thus, identity construction becomes intertwined with the evolution of the ecosystem: the garnering and removal of capabilities, tinkering with procedures that in<sup>fl</sup>uence the organization’s members as well as external constituents. We follow this idea of identity change (Corley and Gioia, 2004; Tripsas, 2009) next to analyze how a key actor – SRA – in a growing mobile ecosystem – the new RDS-TMC service – struggled to change its identity.

## Research design

## Research method

Because identity change is not well understood, we engaged in a longitudinal, <sup>fi</sup>eld-based exploratory case study (Pettigrew, 1990). Such a research design is deemed well suited for articulating emergent theory (Yin, 2010). In particular, given the explorative nature of the research question – the study of a poorly understood and ambiguous research phenomenon through the lens of multiple meanings people assign to it (Orlikowski and Baroudi, 1991) – we examined SRA’s implementation of the RDS-TMC service as an interpretive case study (Walsham, 1995). We focused on the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of key events that in<sup>fl</sup>uenced identity change and related meaning construction by following SRA’s and other stakeholders’ efforts to create and make sense of a RDS TMC service while struggling to align this service with the current understanding of what the organization is expected to do (Langley, 1999). Due to the longitudinal, process focus, we adopted narrative and grounded theory strategies (Langley, 1999) in making sense of <sup>fi</sup>eld data and generalizing toward theory (Pettigrew, 1990).

The study period ran from fall 2001 until summer 2013 – from the launch of the service to its current delivery model. The long time span allowed us to explore the emergence and reconciliation of the identity tension and related meaning making. We examined how SRA orchestrated efforts to continue service delivery while trying to rede<sup>fi</sup>ne and renegotiate its identity (see Figure A1). In particular, we scrutinized SRA’s deliberate attempts to articulate its new routines and roles and forge relationships that would align its operations better with a new mobile ecosystem.

## Study setting

SRA was chosen as a study setting because SRA’s implementation of the RDS-TMC service represents a prime example of public and private collaboration to deliver a complex mobile service ecosystem. The service has been in place for over a decade, and it was deemed likely that identity tensions created by SRA’s interactions and change responses with its environment would be accessible to detect and study. SRA’s endeavors to organize and manage the delivery of the RDS-TMC service were thus considered an appropriate setting in which to examine our research question. Indeed, given the substantial efforts of change that SRA embarked upon, it eventually managed to transition from a traditional road administrator to a more innovative traf<sup>fi</sup>c information service provider.

SRA’s key mission as public governmental (regulatory) entity is to build a safe, environmentally sound, and genderequal road transportation system by cooperating with multiple stakeholders. Because SRA is responsible nationwide for traf<sup>fi</sup>c planning and control, it recognizes new traf<sup>fi</sup>c-related services integral to its mission. It has participated in developing traf<sup>fi</sup>crelated information services such as RDS-TMC, and views them as an important means to offer drivers and the business community useful traf<sup>fi</sup>c information that will improve transport quality and safety and decrease environmental impact. It has also designed and built systems with which to offer such services. $\mathsf { S R A } ^ { 2 }$ is currently the sole RDS-TMC service provider and delivers the service through a collaborative public-private effort.<sup>3</sup> The service is supported by two vertical standards (Damsgaard and Truex, 2000; Markus et al., 2006; Bala and Venkatesh, 2007; Malhotra et al., 2007; Andersson et al., 2008), Alert-C and Location Code. Functionally, the system consists of the Location Code Manager, the TRISS system, the RDS-TMC Editor, and their databases and interfaces, including software links to various sources of real-time traf<sup>fi</sup>c information (e.g., emergency centers, <sup>fi</sup>re departments, police). For RDS-TMC service delivery SRA has the responsibility (i) to ensure that RDS-TMC receivers are available in the marketplace, (ii) to verify that the RDS-TMC equipment sold in Sweden complies with the standards, and (iii) to represent Swedish national actors in the TMC Forum. Accordingly, SRA has been actively involved in RDS-TMC service delivery as well as standard development activities at the international level.

The primary role for SRA in RDS-TMC service is to organize and coordinate the production of RDS-TMC messages,<sup>4,5</sup> which are carried out by operators at traf<sup>fi</sup>c information centers.<sup>6</sup> To identify, analyze, encode, and then communicate potential traf<sup>fi</sup>c situations, the operators utilize the traf<sup>fi</sup>c information sources. They also draw on other information streams available at traf<sup>fi</sup>c centers (traf<sup>fi</sup>c <sup>fl</sup>ow data, incidents, weather etc.) generated by traf<sup>fi</sup>c monitoring systems. An increasingly important source of input is the information provided by motorists themselves through their mobile devices. ## Data collection

Data covering the lifespan of SRA’s delivery of the RDS-TMC service were gathered from seven sources<sup>7</sup>: (i) direct observations of traf<sup>fi</sup>c message production at centers; (ii) <sup>fi</sup>eld notes made during standard and service development meetings at SRA and European Committee for Standardization (CEN); (iii) semi-structured interviews at SRA and among other stakeholders; (iv) e-mail and telephone conversations with strategic and technical service managers at SRA; (v) regular informal meetings with the strategic service manager at SRA; (vi) participation at system and technology demonstrations; and (vii) reviews of documentation about the service. Additional archival data were acquired from the TMC Forum covering documents that described strategies and plans for the implementation and use of the standards in different countries. Overall, capturing multiple voices was deemed critical for data triangulation and theory development (Klein and Myers, 1999), and the multiple data sources increased our ability to triangulate data and validate the emerging theory. Table A2 summarizes collected data sets and how they were acquired over the four phases between 2001 and 2013.

Our primary data set was generated over a 13-year period and consisted of testimonies from both individual and group interviews. These interviewees were chosen so that they covered critical actors in SRA’s implementation and adaptation of the Alert-C and Location Code standards. Within SRA, we interviewed 15 people occupying different roles, including the head of service management, strategic and technical service managers, standardization experts, system administrators, and operators/coordinators at traf<sup>fi</sup>c information centers. Multiple interviews were conducted with the head of service management (three), as well as with the strategic (<sup>fi</sup>ve) and technical (<sup>fi</sup>ve) service managers. The technical and strategic managers had also been deeply involved in the work of the RDS-TMC Forum and knew the measures that the organization had taken to support the implementation of the service in Europe and around the world. We used a snowball method, where key informants at SRA helped identify other potential interviewees.

We also conducted interviews with four telematics managers at Volvo and one navigation system designer at Volvo Cars, all of whom had been extensively involved in SRA’s research and development projects. In addition, we interviewed <sup>fi</sup>ve consultants who actively participated in the standardization, one R & D consultant who was involved in open service projects between SRA and Volvo, one standardization expert on traf<sup>fi</sup>c information, and four people (two academics and two practitioners) who had extensive knowledge about SRA’s new Information Technology Service (ITS) action plan. We conducted a total of 41 interviews with 31 different interviewees from eight different organizations (see Table A3).

The semi-structured interviews lasted a minimum of an hour, with a few extending to a half-day. They were designed to help us understand how Alert-C and Location Code were implemented and used to leverage a successful multi-contextual service delivery. In particular, the interviews sought to capture salient aspects related to identity ambiguity and change. Key questions on these topics were followed by additional drill-in questions that depended on the answers. We tried to mitigate tendencies to offer our interpretations by conducting the interviews in an open-minded and <sup>fl</sup>exible manner and sought to avoid any over-directing,<sup>8</sup> which would potentially trigger testimonies that depicted presuppositions of the researcher. The interviews were taped and transcribed (more than 250 pages) to facilitate further analysis and validation. The transcribed interviews were sent to the interviewees, followed by an e-mail to elucidate unclear points. Indeed, the careful validation of data and the adding of new data sources were deemed important for triangulating results and validating the emerging theory.

## Data analysis

Given our focus on identity change (Corley and Gioia, 2004; Tripsas, 2009), we sought to generate and validate empirical categories that would capture essential aspects of the <sup>fi</sup>t between the operational logics of SRA and the new RDS TMC service and how these were interpreted as something that SRA should be involved with. Concurring with the hermeneutic circle (Klein and Myers, 1999), new empirical categories were generated while working with the data in an iterative fashion. Armed with an increased understanding of the phenomena, the categories were continually re<sup>fi</sup>ned to better re<sup>fl</sup>ect service delivery practices, identity tensions, capability con<sup>fi</sup>gurations, and subsequent responses to achieve identity clarity (Strauss and Corbin, 1990).

Through cycles of deepening insight, our analysis gradually geared toward classifying and generalizing the responses orchestrated and implemented to resolve the identity tension. Both alternative expressions of identity tensions and related responses have been reviewed for accuracy by interviewees and other people at SRA. We also wrote a teaching case study about SRA’s delivery of the RDS-TMC service with particular emphasis on how these responses were orchestrated and implemented. This teaching case was circulated and discussed among our colleagues, thus helping us to re<sup>fi</sup>ne and revise original interpretations, and offering external validation for our conclusions.

## Findings

We report the <sup>fi</sup>ndings chronologically following the key shifts in identity and associated emergence and mitigation of identity tensions. These key phases are illustrated in Figure A1 (see Appendix) and reported in the following section.

## Implementation success

To deliver the RDS-TMC service SRA had to erect the sociotechnical structure around the Alert-C and Location Code standards and build a mobile ecosystem. That is, SRA had to con<sup>fi</sup>gure a socio-technical infrastructure within its realm that generated the speci<sup>fi</sup>ed service and conformed to the new standards. Since the start of the initiative (early 1990s), SRA had been successfully engaged in the (i) creation of organizational accountabilities and structures that coordinate and manage the service, (ii) invention of organizational routines that integrate the production of linguistic messages across the whole ‘production’ pipeline to deliver the service, and (iii) development of new IT systems that offer the storage, processing, and data communication capabilities required by the service. Hence, SRA has over time internally garnered and integrated necessary capabilities for the RDS-TMC service to grow.

The number of RDS-TMC service installations, i.e., the instances of receivers and applications in mobile devices that use the service, has grown fast and exponentially. After the <sup>fi</sup>rst year of use, in 2001, there were 40,000 receivers, while the number was 80,000 two years later (there are approximately 8 million vehicles in Sweden). In 2007, the number of installations had surged to 500,000 installations, and in 2010 the number had increased to approximately 3,000,000. Indeed, the successful implementation of the RDS-TMC service in Sweden has not only improved the quality of traf<sup>fi</sup>c information, but also created demands for new service innovation. The strategic manager of the RDS-TMC service made the following comment when re<sup>fl</sup>ecting upon the delivery of digital traf<sup>fi</sup>c services:

One of the strongest aspects of the successful RDS-TMC service implementation is that we’ve clearly improved the quality of traf<sup>fi</sup>c information. There’s no other service that requires such high quality to be useful, and the user at the location is evaluating the veracity of messages delivered. This has led to a situation where the expectations for and requirements of high quality traf<sup>fi</sup>c information have increased signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly.

The implementation of the RDS-TMC service demanded establishing close collaboration with other traf<sup>fi</sup>c administration bodies across Europe. It also required collaboration with new industry partners that provided navigation and telematics products for a global market. By forging and leveraging all these relationships, SRA played a central role in the creation of a successful RDS-TMC ecosystem populated by many previously unconnected public and private actors. Indeed, the Alert-C and Location Code standards have proved pivotal for creating and sustaining this ecosystem, as the technical manager of the RDS-TMC service commented:

Technology vendors (Philips, Siemens, Blaupunkt etc.) as well as car manufacturers (Volvo and BMW) were really pushing navigation systems … their agenda combined with the standards Alert-C and Location Code contributed signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly to the change of the map industry. Navigation served as a trigger alongside GPS (actors like Garmin) and we had our standards. The map industry could therefore start to sell digital maps containing our basic RDS-TMC information, and this was requested by industries such as the automotive industry. Decision makers in the map and navigations industries decided to jointly exploit this venue … it was like the fall of the Berlin wall given that these industries historically represented two separate worlds. For us (SRA), this created relationships with totally new actors.

The fact that new organizations have joined the RDS-TMC ecosystem with their technical capacity, untapped knowledge bases, and new markets has sparked complex exchanges and interactions that spanned the boundaries of allparticipating organizations.

## Innovation inertia

At the same time, SRA has not been successful in using the RDS-TMC service and related ecosystem as a powerful lever for continued service innovation. The head of service management at SRA admits this when he discusses the need for a digital infrastructure supporting service improvements:

We’ve accomplished the RDS-TMC service and that went well largely because of the FM channel … but its capacity is not high enough to cater for the precision a navigation system requires to change its route planning …. the big problem is the communication between the mobile parts and our digital infrastructure … we’ve not been able to resolve it. As long as such a structure is not in place it will be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to create a service delivery process that integrates the entire value chain …

Further service innovation due to SRA’s new role of providing traf<sup>fi</sup>c information has been undermined by several measures:

Opportunities with additional information sources in the form of sensors have been managed neither systematically nor professionally. We’re able to demonstrate further developments … under limited test conditions. To do it at a larger scale has not been possible. No decisions have been made by the government to support the implementation of infrastructure changes following our speci<sup>fi</sup>ed requirements. No agreements have been reached with the wireless communication industry as to complement the original RDS-TMC approach [broadcast via the FM channel].

The main reason for this is meager support from the government, virtually non-existent collaboration with the telecom industry (which was capable of leveraging the infrastructure), and lack of commitment from <sup>fi</sup>rms (such as car manufacturers, truck companies, and IT consultancy <sup>fi</sup>rms) operating within the ecosystem. As one interviewee noted, ‘someone <sup>fi</sup>nally must be willing to pay for the service being developed … for this type of service to really <sup>fl</sup>y <sup>fi</sup>rms ought to do more.

## Identity tensions and role dilemmas

## Identity tensions

The inability of SRA to productively leverage the RDS-TMC ecosystem for continued innovation can be traced in part to problems of establishing the internal procedures and practices necessary to go beyond the RDS-TMC service that took hold around 2004–2005 when the basic service was stable. The growing strains and challenges in understanding the identity of SRA and how it relates to other actors in this ecosystem are a key challenge for continued innovation. The inherited expectations, norms, and rules of the organization hampered continued service innovation and eventually created an identity tension. It was deemed a serious challenge for many stakeholders that SRA continued as the main service provider, because of its historical role as a public government authority responsible for physical road infrastructure. The strategic manager of the RDS-TMC service noted:

SRA is an old-fashioned authority primarily focusing on road construction and maintenance … service delivery is not our core business. We’re not the appropriate organization, and we don’t have the knowledge and competence required. This all has created uncertainty about our function and role. Someone else with a service-orientation would be more suitable.

In fact, both its members and external constituents questioned emerging assumptions and beliefs about what the organization stands for. A telematics manager at Volvo argued:

I don’t think it’s suitable for SRA to function as a service provider. They did <sup>fi</sup>ne initially … but then it hasn’t worked our very well. We’ve clearly communicated that we’re not happy with the way the service been delivered. The core business needs to be delivery of traf<sup>fi</sup>c information. SRA’s core business is something else. They focus on cement and asphalt … that roads and physical infrastructure are built correctly. SRA’s mission as an authority binds them, but I know they worked a lot with <sup>fi</sup>nding a different identity and creating new organizational processes.

The head of service management at SRA, however, asserted that the industry wrongly blamed SRA for misplaced identity, simply because actors in the automotive industry like Volvo, for example, tend to struggle with their own identity:

You cannot simply criticize SRA’s structure and identity … in the automotive industry they experience the same problem. Those in charge are still people who are experts on cylinders, pistons, varnish and what have you. People working with electrical systems, IT applications, and digital infrastructures have become increasingly powerful though. Car manufacturers realize that sensor technology is a means for avoiding collisions and increase traf<sup>fi</sup>c safety … we can see that a breakthrough is coming.

These growing identity ambiguities following RDS-TMC delivery created a situation where multiple actors’ roles became unclear and linkages between members of the ecosystem began to worsen.

## Role dilemmas

A navigation system designer at Volvo Cars observed that the collaboration between key actors was initially intense, but gradually faded away:

Early on Volvo Group Companies like Cars and Technology collaborated intensively with SRA. However, it has weakened over time though … this goes for SRA’s interactions with other actors too. The weak relation with external actors has negatively affected the evolution of the service. There’re other potential sources like people re-coupling feedback using their phones, embedded sensors in the road, and what have you … SRA should have tried to incorporate such additional sources as to get the service more effective. This can be traced to the quality assurance procedures performed at the traf<sup>fi</sup>c information centers. It’s simply not suf<sup>fi</sup>cient that someone out there just calls and reports … everything has to be checked and veri<sup>fi</sup>ed. There has been a clash between public policy and private values … they’ve a picture of their role as an authority and it’s dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for them to see that the information can be used for new things and that everything doesn’t need to be speci<sup>fi</sup>ed in detail.

The head of service management at SRA traced this to the lack of a viable service arrangement in which the roles of different actors and their relationships were appropriately de<sup>fi</sup>ned:

What is missing here is the business model … simply how the value chain should look like to allow actors making money … or what you’re supposed to gain from it. Clearly, there’s a potentially win-win relationship between the government and the industry. Nowadays a huge challenge is that the development of new services and systems is seldom governed by a single actor only but rather involves a multiplicity of players … this represents a new way of doing business. You must reach out and seek opportunities fo collaboration, which inevitably makes it much harder …

A telematics manager at Volvo acknowledged that this emer ging complexity within the ecosystem was also challenging:

It would have worked better if a public-private partnership organization had been created at the outset. This type of mobile service requires a freedom to collect information and data from diverse sources, and a mix of actors is a necessity for creating and maintaining quality. Maybe there must be multiple service providers as to create competition and leverage dynamism … you can collect data from individual drivers as well as commercial actors. We need more automated systems such as <sup>fl</sup>oating car data and sensors embedded in the physical traf<sup>fi</sup>c infrastructure that continually produce and report. … The rules of the game have to be de<sup>fi</sup>ned.

The head of service management at SRA con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that such public-private service arrangements can be extremely valuable for providing traf<sup>fi</sup>c information and are necessary for a stable ecosystem:

In Great Britain they’ve this system called Traf<sup>fi</sup>cmaster, which originally was the RDS-TMC service in Great Britain. Industry actors thought that the Department of Transport was way too slow. The technology was in place, but there was no dynamism. Department of Transport simply didn’t deliver any information because they thought it belonged to them. Traf<sup>fi</sup>cmaster found a loophole in the law allowing them to install infrared sensors on viaducts. They installed a hell of a lot of sensors on viaducts and those sensors collect data to the Traf<sup>fi</sup>cmaster system. So this organization was born out of anarchy because they didn’t accept that the M25 and M5 were congested due to the Department’s reluctance to deliver traf<sup>fi</sup>c information.

The growing uncertainty around public-private relationships ultimately led SRA to engage in a deliberate search for new identity. SRA had to orchestrate processes to negotiate new roles for itself within the ecosystem and forge new relationships within the ecosystem that would help mobilize interests of outside members in that they would see SRA as legitimate and valuable to other actors within the ecosystem.

## Relationship orientation

In 2008, SRA decided not to continue as the main service provider and to clear its identity as a pure administrator in the world of atoms. Soon after this decision SRA and the government decided that an independent traf<sup>fi</sup>c service provider called Destia would replace SRA. Even though Destia’s primary task in the past had been to deal with customer relationships and service agreements, it soon became clear that it would have a hard time <sup>fi</sup>nding a viable business model for its RDS-TMC service. In fact, it was unclear whether Destia would promote the RDS-TMC service alone, or whether it would bundle its other services together, thus removing the service as public good. Destia’s ability to maintain the quality and reliability of the RDS-TMC service across the country was also questioned. The head of service management at SRA said:

People within SRA were skeptical about the initiative to hand over the service to Destia … it was not clear at all how they would <sup>fi</sup>nd a market for the RDS-TMC service and be able to maintain quality and reliability. It was also unclear whether they would have just the RDS-TMC service or if they would pursue a multi service approach. The negotiation with Destia was initiated three years ago and there was an agreement on Destia’s take over. However, we’ve now reclaimed the service because of the uncertainty we felt about their business plan.

SRA had already reclaimed the service in 2009, as it was now successful and popular, with improved legitimacy. At the same time, this decision signalled that SRA adopted a new, fully committed approach toward digital traf<sup>fi</sup>c information services that called for clearance of its new identity. A designer of navigation systems at Volvo Cars noted this new identity change:

SRA decided to open up and supply their information to other actors … … The argument behind this approach is to replace authority and control as driving forces with incentives that motivate other actors to plunge in and help them to realize their goals. This can be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for SRA especially given their history as a dominating actor required to make political decisions about technology investments. I believe Alert-C and Location Code have a large installed base, but what is required is that SRA ties things together in a new way. This would require a model of governance (access, certi<sup>fi</sup>cation, quality assurance etc.) and service agreements that allow other actors to take responsibility ….

However, SRA soon realized that enacting the new espoused identity orientation was easier said than done. To undergo such identity change it had to orchestrate new supporting capabilities and structures that would help its members to understand SRA in the context of new relationships with a variety of external stakeholders within the mobile ecosystem.

## Identity change

In 2010, simultaneously with the new identity clearance, SRA launched an action plan for its IT strategy with strong backing from the government. The goal was to create new capabilities through which SRA could carry out unprecedented public private coordination and engage in innovation necessary for the advancement of the RDS-TMC service. The head of service management at SRA explained that this initiative was aligned with the service innovation programs pursued by Ertico – ITS Europe so as to improve the RDS-TMC service as well as to expand the role of the international TMC Forum.

What we need is a business plan that involves besides ourselves actors from industries like automotive, IT, and wireless communication. Because without a joint business concept we cannot discuss data quality issues.

Accordingly, SRA participated in several TMC Forum initiatives to further develop the Alert-C and Location Code standards. These initiatives would identify new potential actors for service delivery and stipulate how the relationship between them would be organized within the ecosystem. The head of service management at SRA argued that such improvements would make it easier for SRA to deal with the complexity created by the new technological landscape (e.g., broad bandwidth, location-based services, sensors):

TMC Forum initiatives … develop the underlying standards so that they better communicate what actors should be part of service delivery … they must also more clearly describe the relationships between them. Such improvements would make it easier to understand how to deal with emerging technologies.

Consistent with the ITS action plan, SRA was now actively involved in new R & D initiatives. It had to <sup>fi</sup>nd ways to collaborate with private companies and third-party developers. One major initiative involved creating an app store for traf<sup>fi</sup>c-related services. This was advanced through several open platform projects:

What is needed is some kind of app store that comprises several services. And of course the associated business relationships need to be de<sup>fi</sup>ned …. Instead of serving as the broker we can supply information that someone else can then package for subsequent delivery through various media like displays in cars, motorcycle helmets or what have you.

This positioned SRA as a kind of data broker and market maker creating new types of relationships between other actors within the ecosystem:

ITS services are to be offered via an app store and it shouldn’t make a difference for the customers whether the services are being developed by Volvo or someone else. As a portal for traf<sup>fi</sup>c information, Volvo Online Service Store can become a marketplace for ITS innovation. It will resemble the logic of a double-sided market or rather a multi-sided market. SRA won’t offer anything directly to end customers. They’ll rather provide content like RDS-TMC or Floating Car Data to third party developers seeking to use SRA’s resources for developing customer apps. SRA is responsible for assuring the quality of the data they supply to third party developers, but cannot control how the data sources are then used to create customer offerings and services. If SRA would dictate the terms of usage the incentives would soon be long gone. This is virtually the only way we can go to allow for commercial use of SRA’s data. We want to create an ecosystem or ecology where various actors complement each other.

(SRA consultant)

Indeed, given that SRA handles a large amount of data and related applications (such as road incidents, travel times, and traf<sup>fi</sup>c <sup>fl</sup>ows), it could be expected to provide better services and implement its mission for safer and more environmentally friendly traf<sup>fi</sup>c by becoming involved in these new service collaborations. By creating such novel public-private business models SRA was expected to supply information freely to others. As soon as the arrangement was seen to contribute to the ful<sup>fi</sup>llment of the general policy goals, however, it became a part of SRA’s portfolio being <sup>fi</sup>nanced by the government. These goals were deemed central to creating legitimacy for the new SRA identity. Yet, it proved dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to create a constructive dialog between SRA and other business collaborators (such as Volvo), because of their disparate goals. The head of service management at SRA notes:

We don’t know much about the relationship between costs for ITS investments and the potential good side of fewer accidents and saved lives. So it’s not really clear how much money we can invest to secure the delivery of high quality information to Volvo.

Overall, SRA remained uncertain about the relationships between its new level of ITS investment and its societal bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and how these initiatives would actually legitimate its new identity.

To further strengthen its new identity orientation SRA established a horizontal advisory board for ITS. This new organ turned out to be a powerful means to transform ideas into concrete and observable actions within the ecosystem:

SRA has created an ITS secretariat that collaborates with the government’s ITS council to organize and guide the development of ITS in Sweden … it has different companies and institutions coupled to it to cater for better public-private cooperation. As a consequence, ITS services have eventually become a central concern for us. For many years there were only a few people who had any understanding of our service agenda, but there’re many instances of an authority that need to understand what it takes to accomplish the societal collaboration necessary for ITS service delivery.

(Head of service management at SRA)

Overall, SRA had to make substantial efforts to facilitate the transition from road administrator to an innovative traf<sup>fi</sup>c information service provider. These efforts were all deliberately designed to change the ways in which insiders as well as outsiders would perceive the mission and nature of SRA, and were not pursued in isolation, but constituted part of a broader organizational change agenda that eventually helped SRA to embrace an identity transition.

## Summary of key <sup>fi</sup>ndings

Our longitudinal study of SRA allows us to capture the history of the RDS-TMC service and narrate a story of the genesis of a large and successful mobile service. Our analysis indicated, however, that relatively early on the success did not emerge without struggle. One key challenge was related to the very identity of the organization that orchestrated the service: SRA’s new role and new types of relationships within the ecosystem (cf. Tripsas, 2009). That is, the implementation of new information services in mobile ecosystems would eventually put pressure on the very identity of SRA and also on other actors. In our case, one issue was about radical reshaping of the relationships between public and private organizations (Kwak et al., 2009).

We also observed that the identity tension emerged within SRA because the inherited expectations, norms, and rules of SRA became highly problematic and/or unacceptable while it continued to deliver the RDS-TMC service. From a historical perspective, the organization’s mission was building and maintaining physical roads under the regulatory sway of the government. New types of public-private partnerships had to be formed as RDS-TMC service was created (cf. Kwak et al., 2009) and its continued evolution heightened this tension. Addressing this tension proved to be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult. In other implementations of RDS-TMC throughout Europe this turned out to be even more fatal. The fate of Traf<sup>fi</sup>cmaster, which was the original RDS-TMC service provider in Great Britain, is a case in point. Because of the UK Department of Transport’s reluctance to deliver requested traf<sup>fi</sup>c information to outsiders, a group of private industry players found a loophole in the regulations that allowed them to collect the needed information by installing their own sensors on viaducts. The related service, Traf<sup>fi</sup>cmaster, was born out of poor understanding of the new roles of traf<sup>fi</sup>c administration and exhibited poor public-private collaborations. In Sweden, the SRA has from the inception of its service tried to organize and execute the service delivery as dictated by the standard. The initial success in delivering the RDS-TMC service eventually broke down its traditional role as a traf<sup>fi</sup>c administrator as it necessitated more extensive public-private cooperation. This put pressure on the clarity of its identity (Pentland and Feldman, 2008) and ultimately highlighted growing differences between its mission, role, and routines as a public authority and what it was actually doing (Caudle et al., 1991).

As a result, other participants in the RDS-TMC ecosystem struggled to de<sup>fi</sup>ne and negotiate their roles. For example, Volvo’s core business has traditionally been car and truck manufacturing. As of now new IT-based traf<sup>fi</sup>c services are becoming increasingly important (Len<sup>fl</sup>e and Midler, 2009). Yet, the uncertainty about who should take on the role of the main service provider also negatively affected Volvo’s role within the ecosystem and service innovation with RDS-TMC. While new information sources made the service potentially more dynamic and attractive, the heightened ambiguity around SRA’s identity resulted in only a few service improvements. This also strained relationships with different participants including Volvo. The expansion of the RDS-TMC service required SRA to adopt a new identity, yet SRA remained reluctant to see itself as a main information service provider. To resolve this problem, in 2008, SRA made an unsuccessful effort to outsource the RDS-TMC service. However, SRA realized that offering such service constituted part of its new public mission to improve road safety and environmental impact, and as the government’s new policies emphasized innovation and new types of traf<sup>fi</sup>c services, it began to promote RDS-TMC service.

At the information infrastructure level, SRA had to start rede<sup>fi</sup>ning its role as a platform and data owner and operator. The ambition was now to become a new kind of transportation authority that not only builds and maintains physical roads, but also innovates and develops digital services within transportation to become a data broker. In this regard, ITS became a critical instrument in promoting new policy goals of SRA that included general traf<sup>fi</sup>c ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, safety, and sustainability. SRA had to forge new relationships with its environment by establishing an external board of advisors from key technology and service providers. It also developed a strategic action plan for such services and articulated new tactics for collecting and managing traf<sup>fi</sup>c data as well as rede<sup>fi</sup>ning ownership and service delivery conditions (Hargrave and Van de Ven, 2006; Kwak et al., 2009). One result of this agenda is SRA’s involvement in an open platform project – a deliberate attempt to stimulate a multi-sided market of information services (Eisenman et al., 2006) involving heterogeneous actors, platforms, and services (Adner, 2006; Adner and Kapoor, 2010). In this arrangement, by becoming a data supplier to Volvo, the digital content that SRA provides can now be delivered in other ways to customers. However, whether such a public-private business model has the potential to become viable and will actually work remains to be seen.

At the standard development level, the TMC Forum launched several initiatives to establish viable public-private alliances that enable ITS innovation worldwide. While these new service innovations primarily address the business side of digitalization of transportation, the standardization networks they have brought together also deal with key technical challenges. The RDS-TMC service is seen here as the most successful ITS project and therefore it serves as a reference case for further innovation.

## Discussion

The emergence of standards-based mobile services calls for studies of mobile ecosystems and their evolution (Tilson et al., 2010; Sørensen, 2014). A wide range of issues may affect service delivery and continued development of mobile ecosystems (Basole, 2009; Basole and Karla, 2011). One such issue is that participation in a mobile ecosystem is likely to question the participating organization’s identity (Yoo et al., 2006; Boland et al., 2007; Tripsas, 2009). Investigating how organizations navigate and reconcile emerging identity tensions thus represents a worthwhile research endeavor. We will next discuss our key <sup>fi</sup>ndings from this study in relation to prior work on ecosystems and associated identity tensions, and then develop implications for future research.

There is a growing literature on governance of platformcentric ecosystems (Tiwana et al., 2010; Yoo et al., 2012). These studies focus primarily on digital platforms (Tiwana et al., 2010) and related boundary resources (Ghazawneh and Henfridsson, 2013), and are primarily concerned with how focal (Eaton et al., 2015) and non-focal actors (Selander et al., 2013) can innovate within a service ecosystem. These studies recognize the presence of varying roles such as platform owners and third-party developers and their speci<sup>fi</sup>c relationships. Recent literature has investigated the locations of activities and capabilities within service ecosystems and their effects on ecosystem evolution (Basole, 2009; Adner and Kapoor, 2010; Basole and Karla, 2011). We depart from this literature by explicitly exploring how organizations often have to change their identity by crafting new roles and establishing novel relationships to address service requirements, even though the desired future identity may remain unclear. This challenge to identity has not been recognized in the past, thoughorganizational identity transformations have been well understood in the context of mergers and acquisitions and/or radical innovation (Corley and Gioia, 2004; Tripsas, 2009). We also found that no prior IS studies had examined the role of identities while designing and/or delivering information services. So far, according to Whitley et al. (2014), identity research in IS has mainly explored the effects of IT use on individual user identity or the interactions between organizational identity and speci<sup>fi</sup>c ways of using internal IT.

We also note that a contributing factor to the slow innovation of the RDS-TMC service was the absence of a viable public-private business model and the reluctance of both the public and private sector actors to assume new identities. However, all actors also shared the vision that taking the next step requires that the RDS-TMC service be organized differently by articulating it as an ecosystem (cf. Adner, 2006; Adner and Kapoor, 2010). While this will inevitably require that public authorities and private companies cooperate in new ways, there was no past model or reference to do so. The solution emerged through negotiation and tinkering but remains still at some level unresolved. The main reason for the establishment of the new policy was radical change in the institutional environment and government policy that provided incentives and support for the search for a new identity. In this regard, our study suggests that successful digitalization of traditional infrastructures (roads, railroads, airports, terminals, etc.) will demand the creation of novel and unseen public-private relationships. Such relationships will constitute one of the key building blocks of ecosystems associated with related advanced information services. Indeed, the Volvo Online Service Store is one example of attempts to realize this vision through institutional tinkering.

Our study calls for institutionally focused studies of multi sided markets (Eisenman et al., 2006) to understand the enabling organizational and institutional mechanisms for multi-party information service delivery. We need to understand how and why decisions about technical infrastructure call for the articulation of new roles and relationships. For example, the creation of a multi-sided market structure that relies on information service delivery requires that <sup>fi</sup>nancial and intangible incentives and related performance measures are aligned and are relevant for different actors. We need to understand under what conditions such mechanisms may facilitate or impede the igniting or evolution of service ecosystems. Given that the role of publicprivate partnerships in ecosystems (Kwak et al., 2009) has remained largely unexplored, future IS studies need to heed the strategic implications of how such partnerships are created and governed.

All past analyses of organizational change reveal that identity change takes time (Corley and Gioia, 2004; Tripsas, 2009). Accustomed as we are to the ‘information revolution’, ‘ubiquitous services’, and the fast pace of the ‘24/7’ life, we rarely step back and ask what changes have been occurring at a slower pace (Edwards et al., 2007). The standards development that made the RDS-TMC service possible and formed the embryo of the mobile ecosystem started in the 1980s. SRA took part in test activities at the beginning of the 1990s before becoming the service provider in 1997. The identity change that followed can be described as slow and winding. Instead of taking a step back and assuming only the traditional role of a public authority, however, SRA has over the years successfully taken actions that have helped it innovate with new digital traf<sup>fi</sup>c services and thereby become an important partner in the ITS ecosystems. In particular, SRA was able to eventually overcome routine rigidity (as a bureaucracy focused on road planning and maintenance) by enabling new organizational routines to con<sup>fi</sup>gure the service routines necessary to deliver the RDS-TMC service. However, ironically enough, because it created such capabilities its identity came into question and one reason for the routine rigidity (and related inertia) was exactly that they questioned the identity of the organization. This is a topic that is largely overlooked in the literature on organizational inertia (see e.g., Gilbert, 2005), and therefore more detailed studies of the intricate relationships between routines and identity are needed.

Forging a new organizational identity depends also on the legitimacy of the organization in the eyes of its partners (Tripsas, 2009; Whitley et al., 2014). In this regard, identity construction is constantly intertwined with the legitimate evolution of the ecosystem in adapting to new service requirements, and the capacity of the organization to adopt appropriate roles within the ecosystem while balancing this with the internal culture and politics. This can occasionally be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult because this calls for changing norms, values, and power structures. Such identity changes are therefore often affected and supported by strategic decisions from the top, for example the reorganization of the SRA by the government, which gave it a new mission. Future studies therefore need to examine how internal organizational decision making helps spur identity construction by backing or legitimizing assumptions and beliefs supportive of new roles in the ecosystem.

## Conclusions

Mobile ecosystems like the one enabled through the RDS-TMC standards will continue to multiply. They will invite new implementation strategies that must travel across time and space. SRA’s history with the RDS-TMC service provides some initial insights into why not only public but also private organizations will <sup>fi</sup>nd delivery of such services challenging. Organizations that wish to provide such mobile services will inevitably face pressure to adjust their identity by adopting new roles and forging new relationships vis-à-vis business logic, data ownership, and revenue sharing. Indeed, these organizations will be increasingly involved in dynamic networks that comprise the complex mobile ecosystem. It is likely that in such ecosystems identity tensions will continue to energize or inhibit the emergence of innovative services.

Our study advances our understanding of how generative mechanisms within mobile ecosystems create identity-breaking opportunities for service innovation. In this regard, IS researchers are well positioned to take a proactive role in generating knowledge that underlies these services and their social and institutional dynamics. The account offered in this paper can serve as an initial template for future research on institutional and organizational aspects of mobile ecosystems and their service innovation. We believe this piece will add to the cumulative research on the implementation of mobile services that evolve through the mine<sup>fi</sup>eld of heterogeneous technologies, con<sup>fl</sup>icting demands, and competing institutional logics that shape organizational identities.

## Appendix

This section is structured as follows. We <sup>fi</sup>rst outline our approach to study identity ambiguity and change at SRA before detailing the components of the RDS-TMC service delivery process. We then present the main characteristics of the service and explain key steps that operators at the traf<sup>fi</sup>c information centers undertake to produce service messages. Finally, we offer information about our data collection and analysis procedures.

The RDS-TMC<sup>9</sup> service is currently the only standardized language-neutral and dynamic traf<sup>fi</sup>c information service in Europe. It offers mobile users <sup>fi</sup>ltered real-time information for immediate route selection in their preferred language. When the service was delivered initially in 1997, it was intended to cover at least the ‘Trans European Road Network’, and be available 24 hours a day, 7 days a week, 365 days a year. SRA offered the <sup>fi</sup>rst global RDS-TMC service in Sweden. The service is available (free or for low cost) in most West European countries and a number of East European countries. It is now also offered in Australia, Brazil, Canada, China, New Zeeland, Russia, South Africa, and the US, and has been demonstrated in Chile, India, Saudi Arabia and several other countries worldwide. Indeed, the RDS-TMC service is becoming a truly global technology. The number of users has also steadily increased, and there are currently 3 million installations of RDS-TMC receivers in Sweden alone. SRA customer surveys indicate that users <sup>fi</sup>nd the traf<sup>fi</sup>c information delivered useful for planning routes and avoiding problems.

The RDS-TMC service forms the <sup>fi</sup>rst truly multi-contextual traf<sup>fi</sup>c-based information service, because it provides drivers dynamic information about accidents, congestions, construction work, and road conditions across countries and multiple driving situations on heterogeneous receivers. Any willing national, regional, or local broadcaster can transmit RDS-TMC messages. Through current agreements, national service providers can also broadcast cross-border messages for long-distance/international drivers.

Each RDS-TMC message contains <sup>fi</sup>ve basic information items: (i) event description (details of the traf<sup>fi</sup>c problem); (ii) location (the area, highway segment, or point location affected); (iii) direction and extent (adjacent segments or point locations affected and the direction of traf<sup>fi</sup>c affected); (iv) duration (how long the problem is expected to last); and (v) diversion advice (whether or not drivers are advised to <sup>fi</sup>nd an alternative route). The message is transmitted via a ‘silent FM data channel, which allows users to listen to music or news broadcasts without the interference from RDS-TMC transmissions. RDS-TMC can support up to 300 messages/h, and messages can be re-broadcast as long as they remain relevant. Delivered traf<sup>fi</sup>c messages are displayed immediately, meaning that users are thus not required to wait for scheduled traf<sup>fi</sup>c news bulletins or to listen to speci<sup>fi</sup>c programs.

Traf<sup>fi</sup>c messages can be received and decoded by a TMCequipped car radio system, or an RDS-TMC-enabled navigation system. To decode traf<sup>fi</sup>c messages, receivers need to (i) have the appropriate software to handle prede<sup>fi</sup>ned traf<sup>fi</sup>c events and locations (which are identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by numbers) and (ii) be entitled to use the RDS-TMC service broadcast. The messages are then translated according to the ‘receiver’ language chosen in the receiver application. This software-enabled mechanism makes the service language-independent, i.e., whichever country users are in they can understand traf<sup>fi</sup>c situations in their preferred language.

Alternative receivers allow the driver to display the traf<sup>fi</sup>c information either as a text description or as icons on the navigation map. Systems may have a full map display or a smaller display showing basic turn-by-turn directions. Most RDS-TMC products today are embedded in a GPS-based navigation system that offers dynamic route guidance by alerting the driver of a problem and calculating an alternative route (see Figure A3).<sup>10</sup>

The RDS-TMC service is based on two anticipatory vertical standards: Alert-C (ISO 14819-1: 2003, ISO 14819-2: 2003) and Location Code (ISO/TS 14819-3: 2000). Standardization started in 1982, and the <sup>fi</sup>rst full standard was released in 1995.

The development work was organized and coordinated by CEN<sup>11</sup> (European Committee for Standardization). Alert-C and Location Code were rati<sup>fi</sup>ed as standards by ISO in 2004. Between 1995 and 2007, the standards were maintained by TMC Forum,<sup>12</sup> which merged into TISA (Traveler Information Services Association)<sup>13</sup> in 2007.

As for Alert-C, the standard document ‘ISO 14819-1: 2003’ speci<sup>fi</sup>es the protocols that de<sup>fi</sup>ne the message structure and how messages are transmitted and received. The standard document ‘ISO 14819-2: 2003’, in turn, describes both the data structure and the content of the event list. With regard to the Location Code standard, the document ‘ISO/TS 14819-3: 2000’ speci<sup>fi</sup>es rules for how places (related to the road network) should be coded and identi<sup>fi</sup>ed. This includes guidelines for de<sup>fi</sup>ning location categories and managing the location table (stored in the RDS-TMC receiver application).

The rationale behind the Location Code standard is that it should provide a structure as well as grammar for constructing Location codes. Because it would be impossible (or at least too expensive) to develop receivers that could handle multiple database models, a standardized reference data model for location data has been created for the standard. This location table contains a country-speci<sup>fi</sup>c set of location codes for the strategic European road network.

While the event list is the same for all European countries, it is translated into different national languages. In addition, Alert-C provides guidelines for how the RDS-TMC service should be implemented, and presents a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of actors that guides the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and establishment of roles and relationships necessary for service delivery. These roles include broadcast operators, car manufacturers, map suppliers, network operators, public authorities, receiver vendors, and service providers.
