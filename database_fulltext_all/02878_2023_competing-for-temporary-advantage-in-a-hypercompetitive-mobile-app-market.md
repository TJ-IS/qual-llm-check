# COMPETING FOR TEMPORARY ADVANTAGE IN A HYPERCOMPETITIVE MOBILE APP MARKET<sup>1</sup>

Tabitha L. James Department of Business Information Technology, Pamplin College of Business, Virginia Tech, Blacksburg, VA, U.S.A. {tajames@vt.edu}

Zhilei Qiao Department of Management, Information Systems and Quantitative Methods, Collat School of Business, University of Alabama at Birmingham, Birmingham, AL, U.S.A. {qiaozl@uab.edu}

Wenqi Shen and G. Alan Wang Department of Business Information Technology, Pamplin College of Business, Virginia Tech, Blacksburg, VA, U.S.A. {shenw@vt.edu} {alanwang@vt.edu}

Weiguo Fan Department of Business Analytics, Tippie College of Business, University of Iowa, Ames, IA, U.S.A. {weiguo-fan@uiowa.edu}

Hypercompetitive mobile app stores are characterized by rapid innovation and intense competition. App firms must vie for temporary competitive advantage through competitive actions such as releasing product improvements. We study how competitive indicators influence a particular competitive action— app updates—in a mobile game app market. Our results reveal that app firms take action to improve or sustain their temporary competitive advantage, updating their apps when there are opportunities to capitalize on popularity (e.g., rank and rating volume are increasing) and when their apps’ advantages are threatened (e.g., customer ratings are decreasing). We also find that app updates are released in response to competitors’ actions—specifically, when competitors update their apps and new competitors enter ranking lists. Moreover, our findings show that app firms release app updates when an app’s customer rating volume is increasing or when an app firm’s portfolio of game apps is less diverse, relative to its competitors. We conduct additional analyses that show that older app firms are responsive to more competitive indicators than younger ones and major updates are primarily used to respond to serious threats to apps’ competitive positions. Overall, our results indicate that app updates are competitive actions used to improve or sustain temporary advantage when competitive indicators reveal opportunities to improve or threats to apps’ competitive positions.

Keywords: Mobile applications (apps), mobile app updating, apple app store, mobile game apps, competitive dynamics, competition, rivalry, temporary competitive advantage, hypercompetition

## Introduction

The study of competitive dynamics focuses on competitive moves (i.e., actions and responses) through which companies create temporary advantage (Chen & Miller, 2012; Chen & Miller, 2015; Smith et al., 2006). Competitive actions and responses include product improvements and price and service adjustments (Miller & Chen, 1996). Software updates are product improvements that can be used to compete by providing customers with better-quality apps that have enhanced functionality. The software industry can be described as a hypercompetitive industry “characterized by intense and rapid competitive moves, in which competitors must move quickly to build advantage and erode the advantage of their rivals” (D’Aveni & Gunther, 1994, p. 217- 218). In fact, from 1979 to 1991, the competitive response time in the software industry went from 34 days to 1 day (Grimm et al., 2006).

A mobile app (hereafter, app) is a software application designed to run on a mobile device such as a smartphone or tablet. Today, customers download most apps from the Apple App Store or Google Play (formerly the Android Market) (Statista, 2021b), which are the app stores for the two major platforms. A platform or ecosystem can be defined as “any combination of hardware and software that provides standards, interfaces, and rules that enable and allow providers of complements to add value and interact with each other and/or users” (Teece, 2018, p. 1375). The iOS (Apple) ecosystem is an example of such a platform, where Apple defines and controls the hardware, operating system, store, and rules for operating within the ecosystem for complementors who add value by producing products (e.g., apps) that power users’ experiences. In this study, we examine competition in an app market, specifically game apps offered on the Apple App Store.

High-technology industries, such as software, are emerging growth industries that are dynamic and uncertain because of high rates of innovation, and that are also characterized by many new entrants and low entry barriers (Smith et al., 1989; Smith et al., 1992). Ecosystems such as Apple’s have low entry barriers; the cost of app development is low and platform access to offer an app for download is equitable (Boudreau, 2012; Lee & Raghu, 2014). Low entry barriers and the perceived potential of a high-growth industry lead to many new market entrants (Smith et al., 1992). In March of 2022 alone, 580 new game apps were submitted to the Apple App Store (Statista, 2022b). Of the more than 4.7 million apps available on the Apple App Store, the games category is the largest, with more than one million game apps available in 2022 (Statista, 2022c). From 2007 to 2018, the mobile gaming market saw double-digit growth every year and it is forecasted to become a 139.5 billion USD market by 2026 (Global Industry Analysts, 2022; Wijman, 2018).

Traditionally, emerging growth industries have been characterized by incomplete, uncertain, and unreliable information about competitors and their actions (Smith et al., 1992). However, on app stores, information visibility is controlled and curated by the platform provider as a condition of participating in the ecosystem (Grover & Kohli, 2012) and has resulted in “a considerably more transparent market for deployment of software systems compared to more traditional software markets” (Al-Subaihin et al., 2021, p. 2). In fact, quality (e.g., ratings) and price information is available about all of a store’s apps in a standard form controlled by the platform. Standard descriptive information about each app and app update (e.g., release dates) is also curated by the platform. Such store data is intended to help customers easily compare and shop for apps, but it also provides app firms with easily accessible information from which to determine competitive opportunities and threats. This data obviously has competitive value because there are companies (e.g., SensorTower and Apptopia<sup>2</sup>) that app firms can pay for sophisticated analytical displays on and data dumps of data mined from app stores. App stores also maintain ranking lists of top apps that can serve as leaderboards of the top competitors in an app market. These ranking lists provide de facto lists of competitors. Because comparing competing apps is easier, there is more direct competition than in similar markets such as music (Lee & Raghu, 2014). Firms competing in app markets thus have access to more complete and reliable information about their competitors and their competitive position than is typical in emerging growth industries.

In hypercompetitive industries, companies make frequent and bold competitive moves to topple industry leaders (Ferrier et al., 1999; Lee et al., 2010; Smith et al., 2001). Companies compete on price and quality (Matsubayashi, 2007). Prices for mobile apps are low; the average iOS game app price in March of 2021 was \$0.49 USD (Statista, 2021a). Many apps are offered for free and generate revenue through advertisements or in-app purchases. These microprices generate significant revenues through millions of downloads (Kimbler, 2010), which indicates that quality considerations to stimulate downloads are important to profitability. App firms can easily change their product (i.e., update an app) to improve its quality in an attempt to gain more customers (i.e., stimulate downloads) (Lee & Raghu, 2014; Yi et al., 2019). App updating is part of the software development lifecycle; after its initial release, an app must be updated to improve its quality and keep users engaged (Abran et al., 2004; Al-Subaihin et al., 2021). In fact, Apple advises that “regular app updates can help [an app] stay competitive on the App Store, as each new release is an opportunity to reengage existing users and attract new users” (Apple, 2020, p. 1). An app update can thus be considered a competitive action that can be taken to improve the competitive position of an app, or as a response to threats such as other app firms’ competitive actions to sustain an app’s competitive position.

Competitive dynamics is the study of how a firm’s moves or actions affect its competitors, competitive advantage, and performance (Smith et al., 2006). The moves (actions) and countermoves (reactions) create competitive dynamics within an industry and reflect the pursuit of profit through innovation (Smith et al., 2006). The foundation of competitive dynamics is the concept of creative destruction proposed by Schumpeter (1950), which describes a process through which companies take competitive actions in the pursuit of market opportunities to overtake the market leader and reap greater profits. The process continues as competitive actions are taken to dethrone the next leader; thus, creative destruction describes the eventual decline of firms as a result of competitive action and reaction (Chen & Miller, 2012; Smith et al., 2006). Competitive dynamics research has three distinguishing characteristics: it (1) focuses on specific, real market actions, (2) emphasizes the competitive context in which the actions take place, and (3) explains both the drivers and consequences of market actions (Smith et al., 2006). Grounding our study in competitive dynamics research, we examine the drivers of a specific, real market action (i.e., app update) in a novel competitive context (i.e., an app store). Our research question is: How do competitive indicators shape app update competitive actions?

Competitive actions are taken because a company observes an opportunity to improve or perceives a threat to its competitive position. Competitive dynamics thus describes a longitudinal process that is repeated over the lifetime of the product (Ferrier, 2001). This process can be summarized as: (1) an opportunity to improve or a threat to competitive position is observed (i.e., cause), (2) a competitive action is taken to capitalize on the opportunity or counteract the threat, (3) which, if successful, results in the competitive position being improved or sustained (i.e., consequence). Although competitive dynamics research aspires to explain both the causes and consequences of competitive actions, researchers often focus on the performance consequences of competitive actions (Smith et al., 2006). Similarly, information systems (IS) and management researchers have studied factors that affect performance in app stores (Lee & Raghu, 2014; Yi et al., 2019) and have shown that updating an app can result in performance benefits (Comino et al., 2019; Lee & Raghu, 2014). However, few studies have focused on the drivers (i.e., causes) of competitive actions, and such studies are especially lacking in the software industry context (Turner et al., 2010; Young et al., 1996). To fill this gap, our study focuses on the drivers of a specific competitive action—an app update—rather than on the performance consequences of this competitive action. We seek to understand how firms leverage competitive information (i.e., competitive indicators) made visible by the platform provider to determine when to execute a competitive move (i.e., app update), which is key to understanding how firms can be more competitive and thus more successful in hypercompetitive markets characterized by high information visibility. We use competitive dynamics as our theoretical foundation to examine how competitive indicators prompt firms to leverage a specific competitive action to act on opportunities or react to threats. The ability to leverage competitive actions quickly in this context is facilitated by the visibility of competitive indicators (e.g., rank, rating, competitors’ updates) characteristic of platform environments.

We study a specific competitive action, an app update, in a specific context, an app store. Packaged software updates have rarely been studied through the lens of competitive dynamics (Turner et al., 2010), and to the best of our knowledge, app updates in a platform environment have not been studied at all. There are key differences between packaged software and apps, most notably the enhanced competitive information visibility characteristic of an ecosystem. We consider three types of competitive information made available by the platform provider reflecting opportunities or threats that may drive competitive actions: (1) changes in the app’s competitive position, (2) competitive actions taken by the app’s competitors, and (3) changes in the app’s competitive position relative to its competitors. Although some of the general logic of these drivers has been tested in other industries, we are among the first to provide a comprehensive examination of the drivers of this specific competitive action in this context. We thereby contribute to the literature on competitive dynamics by providing a robust examination of the drivers of a novel competitive action in a unique hypercompetitive context characterized by high information visibility. Studies have considered how some changes in the app’s marketplace indicators (e.g., lagged downloads or initial review variance) influence app firms’ update management strategies (Allon et al., 2022; Comino et al., 2019), but to the best of our knowledge, none have provided a holistic examination of how competitive indicators shape the app update competitive action through a competitive dynamics lens. Therefore, we also extend research on app update management (Allon et al., 2022; Comino et al., 2019) by applying a competitive dynamics lens to consider how three types of competitive information drive app update competitive actions.

We found evidence of offensive competitive actions in the game app market that follows the logic of competitive dynamics such that apps are updated when they can capitalize on their upward momentum to improve their position (i.e., dethrone competitors). Specifically, when past performance indicators suggest that an app is experiencing popularity such as increasing rank or buzz (i.e., customer ratings volume), app firms update their apps. We also found that defensive competitive actions are taken by app firms in response to competitive threats. Specifically, when apps’ competitors update, new competitors emerge, customers become unhappy with apps (i.e., customer ratings fall), or app firms compete across fewer game app subcategories than their competitors, app firms update their apps as a defensive move to protect their temporary advantage.

## Theoretical Background

We use competitive dynamics as the theoretical foundation for our study. In the following sections, we first discuss temporary advantage in hypercompetitive industries. Then, we provide background on competitive dynamics in the software industry before introducing our model.

## Temporary Advantage in Hypercompetitive Markets

Baum and Korn (1996, p. 256) state that “firms constantly take offensive and defensive actions in their quests for competitive advantages vis-à-vis competitors.” Hypercompetitive industries are characterized by rapid and intense competitive actions taken to obtain temporary advantage over rivals (D’Aveni et al., 2010; D’Aveni & Gunther, 1994). It is argued that in disruptive environments characterized by fast-paced competitive actions among rivals, sustaining competitive advantage may not be possible (D'Aveni et al., 2010). The software industry is an example of a hypercompetitive industry that is characterized by technological change, highvelocity innovation, and market share turbulence (Lee et al., 2010). Hypercompetition is explained by the notion of the gale of creative destruction that proposes a constant state of disequilibrium arising from innovation-based competition (Lee et al., 2010; Schumpeter, 1950). The foundations of competitive dynamics draw from the concept of creative destruction which suggests that firms undertake competitive actions to capture market opportunities, eventually dethroning the market leader, and continuing the cycle as new leaders emerge (Ferrier et al., 1999; Smith et al., 2006). It explains the eventual decline of firms as they are dethroned by competitors (Chen & Miller, 2012; Ferrier et al., 1999; Smith et al., 2006).

It has been argued that high-tech, disruptive environments do not reach maturity but rather “self-reproduce, cannibalize, innovate, and self-perpetuate by incessantly innovating, reviving, and reinitiating the initial stages of different waves of industry and product lifecycles” (D’Aveni et al., 2010, p. 1372). Such environments necessitate a dynamic maneuvering strategy of acting and reacting to achieve temporary competitive advantage that lasts only until the firm is outmaneuvered by competitors (D’Aveni et al., 2010). The literature on software development has similarly argued that “timely responsiveness and rapid and flexible product innovation are needed in circumstances with critical time to market, rapid technology change, and difficult-todetermine future competition and markets” (Cao & Ramesh, 2007, p. 42). To be competitive, software firms must therefore continuously scan their environment for opportunities and threats and take innovative action based on these indicators in hopes of improving or sustaining temporary competitive advantage.

A product market can be defined as “a set of goods and services that serve similar functions, are created with the use of similar technology, and are used by similar users” (Abell, 1980, p. 17). Following this definition, game apps on Apple’s App Store are created with similar technology, serve similar functions, and are used by similar users. Hence, we refer to our context as a game app market. Game app markets are the epitome of hypercompetitive markets because game apps face fierce competition and have very short product lifecycles; the product lifecycle of a game app was 54.84 days in 2012 (Yi et al., 2019). It is important for game apps to quickly attract a large user base and to sustain their initial boom for as long as possible (Yi et al., 2019). Quality perceptions from customer ratings are crucial to attracting users and achieving higher rankings as the game builds a user base (Yi et al., 2019). Game apps that are able to hold their rank can benefit from informational cascades as customers seek out popular games (Yi et al., 2019). It is thus important for a game app to have high customer satisfaction and capitalize on its initial popularity to achieve the highest rank possible. Moreover, it must sustain that rank for as long as possible to prolong its product lifecycle.

A firm’s competitive position is the power it has relative to its direct competitors, and it determines its performance (e.g., profits) (Simmonds, 1986). Creative destruction describes a race where firms jockey for competitive position (Ferrier, 2001), which in the game app market is formalized by rank on the ranking lists maintained by the platform provider. Ranks change as game apps vie for customers’ attention to generate downloads, which is key to improving or sustaining their ranks (Yi et al., 2019). Ferrier (2001, p. 858) states that “especially in hypercompetitive markets, leading firms are relentlessly pursued by existing and unforeseen challengers that aggressively find new ways to satisfy customers.” Releasing an app update is a competitive action that can be undertaken to vie for market position by finding new ways to satisfy customers. App updates may fix problems identified in the app (e.g., bugs) or may be functional improvements to make the app more appealing. However, regardless of the quality improvements being made, app updates are intended to satisfy customers. On app stores, gauging customer satisfaction is simplified because the platform provider curates and publicly provides customer feedback data. In fact, this feature of the app store allows app firms to obtain customer satisfaction data on their own apps and their competitors’ apps. It is the app update competitive action that is the focus of our study. In particular, we are interested in examining which observable competitive indicators prompt this competitive action. For our analysis, we observed the app updates of 2,306 ranked game apps on the Apple App Store over a 298-day period and collected the store data on those apps prior to the updates.

## Competitive Dynamics in the Software Industry

According to Chen and Miller (2012, p. 137), “competitive dynamics is the study of interfirm rivalry based on specific competitive actions and reactions, their strategic and organizational contexts, and their drivers and consequences.” A competitive action is a move such as a price cut, product improvement, advertising campaign, new product introduction, or entry into a new market that a firm takes to improve or defend its competitive position (Chen & Miller, 2012; Miller & Chen, 1996; Smith et al., 1992). A competitive response is a counteraction that a firm takes in response to the actions of one or more of its competitors, also to defend or improve its competitive position (Chen & Miller, 2012; Smith et al., 1992). Competitive actions “position firms in their competitive environment” (Li et al., 2010, p. 639). Therefore, an app update, which is a product improvement, may be a competitive action or response that positions the app in its competitive environment.

Competitive dynamics research has shown that firms that carry out more competitive actions and execute them at greater speeds experience increased profitability and market share (Ferrier et al., 1999; Smith et al., 1992; Young et al., 1996). In a study of the software industry, Young et al. (1996) found that as firms’ competitive actions increase, firm performance increases, which supports the argument that in hypercompetitive environments, the advantage from a move quickly erodes. Moreover, they also found that the negative consequences of competitor rivalry do not outweigh the benefits of competitive moves, which suggests that competitive actions are critical to performance when faced with intense competitive rivalry (i.e., high levels of competitive activity) (Young et al., 1996). These findings indicate that in the face of intense rivalry in the hypercompetitive game app market, app firms should frequently execute competitive actions to increase performance.

For new software ventures, introducing product refinements and extensions at a greater intensity than one’s rivals is associated with increased performance (Zahra & Bogner, 2000). Frequent product upgrades are a key element of a company’s strategy in dynamic environments such as the software industry because they can be used to gain market share, increase profitability, maintain consumer loyalty, and improve competitive position (Zahra & Bogner, 2000). In the software industry, updating is an important part of firm strategy (Allon et al., 2022; Bala & Carr, 2009; Li et al., 2017; Mehra et al., 2012; Zhang & Seidmann, 2010). Studies on app stores have alluded to the criticality of app updates to performance. For example, Lee and Raghu (2014)

empirically demonstrated that app updates on the Apple App Store could result in a threefold increase in survival in the top-300 chart. App updates are critical to an app’s performance because they increase visibility and user engagement and stimulate downloads (Apple, 2020; Comino et al., 2016, 2019).

Competitive dynamics suggests a longitudinal process in which (1) observed competitive information (e.g., past performance, competitors’ actions) indicates an opportunity or threat at time t-1, (2) which stimulates a competitive action (e.g., app update) at time t, (3) and which, if successful, results in improved future performance at time t+1. In other words, competitive dynamics aspires to study both the drivers (i.e., indicators of opportunities or threats at time t-1) and performance consequences (i.e., performance effects at time t+1) of competitive actions (Chen & Miller, 2012; Smith et al., 2006). As previously described, research has found that an update at time t can increase future performance (e.g., survival on the top charts) at time t+1; therefore, the impact of software updates on performance has been addressed (e.g., Comino et al., 2019; Lee & Raghu, 2014; Zahra & Bogner, 2000). However, less attention has been paid to the drivers of software updates, and studies that have emphasized this part of the longitudinal process have rarely done so through a competitive dynamics lens which has limited the types of drivers examined (Allon et al., 2022; Comino et al., 2019; Turner et al., 2010). The focus of this study is on steps (1) and (2)<sup>3</sup>; that is, determining the competitive indicators a time t-1 that drive the competitive action (i.e., app update) at time t. Our study can thus help firms understand how to better leverage the information visibility characteristic of the app stores to inform their competitive action decisions in pursuit of improving or sustaining the temporary competitive advantage that is ultimately key to maximizing performance.

There are established differences in competitive actions in stable versus hypercompetitive markets where gaining temporary advantage is key (Chen et al., 2010). However, the preponderance of competitive dynamics studies have been conducted in mature markets such are airlines (Chen et al., 2010). Only a few studies have explored competitive strategy in the packaged software industry, and these studies have often focused more generally on the competitive environment (e.g., market concentration) (Turner et al., 2010; Young et al., 1996; Zahra & Bogner, 2000). These studies do provide necessary insight into how game app markets may function. Game app markets are highly concentrated and Turner et al. (2010, p. 854) showed that in the packaged software industry, “increasing industry concentration raises the stakes surrounding market positions and leads to greater interdependence of innovation strategies in an industry.” In the 1990s packaged software industry, firms were more responsive to competitors’ innovations (i.e., software releases) as industry concentration increased (Turner et al., 2010), despite not benefitting from the internet-enabled platform delivery systems for product improvements available today. Such findings suggest that in hypercompetitive app markets, app firms may use app updates as a response to competitive threats.

Competitive dynamics theory posits that it is necessary for firms to be aware, motivated, and capable of competitive actions to respond (Chen & Miller, 2015). Platforms provide a unique level of visibility about competitive position on the app store that can aid awareness (Al-Subaihin et al., 2021; Grover & Kohli, 2012; Lee & Raghu, 2014). Product improvements are key to performance, which can provide motivation (Comino et al., 2019; Lee & Raghu, 2014). The internet-based delivery mechanism for app updates is efficient, which can assist capability. These characteristics empower game app firms to compete for temporary advantage through app update competitive actions. Specifically, they enable an environment in which app firms can observe competitive indicators in real time to inform their app update competitive actions. When competitive indicators suggest an opportunity to improve or a threat to an app’s competitive position, an app can be updated to capitalize on the opportunity or ward off the threat. In this hypercompetitive platform environment, we thus study the drivers of app update competitive actions.

## Research Model and Hypotheses

Porter (1980, p. 17) states that “rivalry occurs because one or more competitors either feels the pressure or sees the opportunities to improve position.” Our interest in this study is examining how competitive indicators shape app update competitive actions.<sup>4</sup> Our dependent variable is thus app update competitive actions. As shown in Figure 1, we test the influence of three types of competitive indicators on the release of app updates in the game app market.

Although competitive dynamics researchers have studied factors influencing competitive actions in traditional industries (Smith et al., 2006), few competitive dynamics studies have focused on the software industry (Turner et al., 2010; Young et al., 1996). Turner et al. (2010) studied how market concentration moderates the relationship between competitors’ innovations and the likelihood of a new software release, whereas Young et al. (1996) examined how cooperation influences competitive activity. Our study extends these studies by examining how competitive indicators observable by app firms due to the high information visibility of the ecosystem shape app updating competitive actions. We study a larger set of indicators than these prior studies because the information visibility characteristic of the ecosystem context enables the examination of competitive indicators that are not as readily available in packaged software contexts.

Research on update management practices has provided some insight into how indicators of an app’s past performance can affect update timing. Comino et al. (2019) found that on iTunes, app updates are more likely when downloads of apps decline. Examining operational agility in versioning-based app development, Allon et al. (2022) showed that app firms update their apps when facing quality threats from poor initial review variance and that app firms with small product portfolios update more frequently. These studies did not focus on competition indicators, although Allon et al. (2022) did examine the effect of the number of active competitors in a genre at the time an app was launched on the quality decay rate. Rather, these studies emphasized how firms manage their updating decisions based on information about their own app or firm characteristics. Our study extends this research by examining app updating as a competitive action, and thus through the lens of competitive dynamics. Rather than considering only one or two indicators of the app’s past performance, we examine three types of competitive indicators that can be observed from data made visible by the platform. Specifically, we provide a robust examination of the influence of an app’s own competitive position (i.e., past performance) on app update competitive actions. Notably, we also consider the influence of a robust set of competitive indicators (i.e., apps’ competitors’ actions and indicators of an app’s relative competitive position) on app update competitive actions.

We draw our independent variables from the two literature bases described above: competitive dynamics and software update management. First, past performance as a driver of competitive action has been theorized and tested in the competitive dynamics literature (Ferrier, 2001; Smith et al., 2006; Young et al., 1996). Ferrier (2001, p., 862) states that “despite the common use of performance as an outcome variable, the important issue for organizations is how past performance impacts preparedness and motivation for future action.” Performance metrics prior to the app update provide the app firm with a real-time gauge of the app’s competitive position that can be used to inform decisions regarding app update competitive actions. Indicators of past performance curated by the platform that may drive an app update competitive action include rank, average star rating, and star rating volume. Although the general logic of the influence of past performance on competitive actions has been tested (Ferrier, 2001; Smith et al., 2006; Young et al., 1996), to the best of our knowledge, our exact measures of past performance in the game app market have not been studied in the competitive dynamics literature. Second, although not theorized as past performance using a competitive dynamics lens, the update management literature has shown that indicators of past performance such as lagged downloads and initial review variance are drivers of app updates (Allon et al., 2022; Comino et al., 2019). Together, the two literature streams suggest that indicators of an app’s past performance (i.e., competitive position) may influence app update competitive actions. Therefore, one type of competitive indicator we examine in this study is past performance— specifically, indicators of an app’s competitive position: rank, average star rating, and rating volume.

Although outside the scope of update management research (e.g., Allon et al., 2022; Comino et al., 2019), competitive dynamics research provides general guidance regarding predictors of competitive actions beyond past performance (e.g., Chen & Miller, 2012; Smith et al., 2006; Turner et al., 2010; Zucchini et al., 2019). First, that competitors’ actions will elicit competitive responses is a central tenet of competitive dynamics theory (Smith et al., 2006). In the packaged software industry, Turner et al. (2010) found that software firms are increasingly responsive to their competitors’ updates when market concentration increases. Moreover, studies have shown that a build-up of rivals actions creates competitive pressure that prompts firms to take action (Hsieh & Hyun, 2016; Zucchini et al., 2019). The number of an app’s competitors’ app updates provides an aggregate of the competitive actions taken by the app’s competitors. Although to the best of our knowledge it has not been studied in our context, it is logical to propose that an accumulation of competing apps’ updates may stimulate app update competitive actions.

Second, competitive dynamics researchers have argued that high entry barriers decrease intense competition (Smith et al., 2006). However, entry barriers are low on app stores (Boudreau, 2012; Lee & Raghu, 2014), which means that app firms will experience competitive pressure from new entrants (Smith et al., 2006). On app stores, new competitors are most visible as new entrants on ranking lists. Although to our knowledge, it has not been studied in our context, it is logical to suggest that competitive pressure will build as new competitors enter the ranking lists, increasing the likelihood of an app update to ward off the competitive threat. We thus propose that two observable indicators of an app’s competitors’ actions may stimulate app update competitive actions: competitors’ updates and new competitors.

Third, Chen and Miller (2012, p. 138) state that “relativity is an essential premise—the notion that a firm’s strategy and market position must be examined within the context of and vis-à-vis its competitors’ strategies and positions.” Perceived relative quality involves assessing external perspectives on quality relative to competitors (Gale & Buzzell, 1989). It has been shown that relative market quality positively affects business performance through its effects on market position (Phillips et al., 1983). It is thus logical to suggest that relative consumer ratings prior to the release of an update (i.e., relative past performance) may be a driver of app update competitive actions. Rating volume can represent consumer interest in a product (i.e., buzz). Luo and Zhang (2013) found that both a firm’s own and its rivals’ buzz can influence firm value; specifically, rivals’ buzz volumes decrease a firm’s returns. Therefore, relative consumer rating volume prior to the release of an update may also be a driver of app update competitive actions. To our knowledge, these relative competitive indicators have not been studied in our context. However, the general logic of examining relative competitive position is supported by the competitive dynamics literature (Chen & Miller, 2012).

Management scholars have classified common competitive strategies on a continuum from prospectors to defenders (Miles & Snow, 1986; Slater & Narver, 1993). Prospectors seek new product and market opportunities and defenders attempt to aggressively protect their product and market (Slater & Narver, 1993). Each game app can list up to two game subcategories (e.g., Puzzle), and an app firm may produce multiple games in different subcategories. App firms with games in more subcategories than their competitors are more likely to be prospectors, and prospectors are less likely to aggressively defend their product, which suggests they may update less. Conversely, app firms with games in fewer subcategories are more likely to be defenders, and defenders aggressively protect their app’s competitive position, which suggests they may update more. Some support for this premise is provided by Allon et al. (2022), who found that app firms with small product portfolios are more likely to update. Moreover, app firms that have apps in multiple subcategories are more likely to compete with the same competitors in multiple subcategories, and multi-market contact decreases competitive actions because competitors have increased opportunities to retaliate (Gimeno & Woo, 1996). To the best of our knowledge, the influence of relative portfolio diversity on app update competitive actions has not been studied, but the general logic is supported in the competitive dynamics and update management literature. We thus propose that three observable indicators of an app’s relative competitive position may stimulate app update competitive actions: relative star rating, relative rating volume, and relative portfolio diversity.

## Observable Indicators of an App’s Competitive Position

Competitive dynamics describes a process by which firms jockey to improve their competitive positions (Ferrier, 2001); thus, competitive actions are often taken when conditions are opportune to overtake competitors (Ferrier et al., 1999; Smith et al., 2006). Firms attempt to improve their competitive positions because there are advantages to being a market share leader (Ferrier et al., 1999). On app stores, the advantages of market leadership, designated by a high position on ranking lists, have been quantified by research showing that the performance benefit is greater the higher the app’s ranking (Garg & Telang, 2012; Ifrach & Johari, 2014). Specifically, research has shown that an app that moves from the 20th position to the first position on a ranking list can experience a doubling of demand (Ifrach & Johari, 2014) and top-ranked apps are downloaded up to 150 times more than an app with a rank of 200 (Garg & Telang, 2012). It has also been demonstrated that top apps have better utilities than apps that rank lower on the ranking lists (Carare, 2012). Lee and Raghu (2014) found that higher initial rank influences sales and therefore the survival of apps on the top charts of the Apple App Store. It has been argued that the temporary advantage of rank for game apps stems from informational cascades that result in a steady stream of new users who help prolong the app’s product lifecycle (Yi et al., 2019). Firms are thus incentivized to take competitive actions to improve an app’s position on the platform’s ranking lists. An app firm that observes that its app’s rank is increasing (i.e., positive past performance) may capitalize on this opportunity to move ahead of competing apps on the ranking lists by leveraging an offensive competitive action to boost the app’s upward momentum. Specifically, this suggests that when the app’s past performance indicator of rank increases, so does the likelihood of an app update competitive action.

The competitive dynamics literature has argued that good past performance can make a firm complacent and decrease competitive actions (Ferrier, 2001; Smith et al., 2006). Contradictorily, in the software industry, results show that good past performance increases competitive actions (Smith et al., 2006; Young et al., 1996). This suggests that indicators that an app is doing well (e.g., moving up on ranking lists) will likely increase competitive actions in the app game market. The logic for this association stems from research in competitive dynamics that describes a battle to dethrone the current market leader through competitive actions that improve challengers competitive positions (Ferrier et al., 1999). Researchers have also found that firms that compete aggressively and quickly to carry out competitive actions are less likely to experience dethronement (Ferrier et al., 1999), which suggests that aggressive competitive actions when past performance is good could help an app sustain its current rank, if not improve its rank further. There are thus incentives to quickly and rapidly update when an app is doing well to achieve a better competitive position and ward off competitors seeking to dethrone the app.

Updating when experiencing success is also a strategy used in traditional software development, where researchers explain that once a software product becomes accepted by the market, firms attempt to capitalize on this advantage by improving the software’s quality and integrating enhancements (Grady, 1987). Zahra and Bogner (2000, p. 142) state that “in a dynamic environment, frequent product upgrades and extensions are necessary to capitalize on the changes in a firm’s industry and achieve profitability.” There are large numbers of competitors on app stores, and app firms must fight for placement on the ranking lists (Datta & Kajanan, 2013; Garg & Telang, 2012). It has been empirically demonstrated that updating an app in the Apple iTunes Store will not only increase the chances of an app entering the ranking lists but also prolong an app’s success on them by approximately 1.3 weeks per update (Kajanan et al., 2012). Therefore, the software development literature also alludes to the use of app updates when apps are experiencing success as offensive competitive actions to further improve or sustain their competitive positions.

The advantages of market leadership in terms of rank on the ranking lists are clear. This suggests that rank is a key competitive indicator, and it would thus behoove app firms to observe and react to rank changes. Specifically, the logic of competitive dynamics suggests that leveraging an app update competitive action when an app’s rank is increasing (i.e., past performance is good) may ultimately provide a boost that could help the app dethrone its competitors from their market positions. It is useful to repeat that the logic of competitive dynamics suggests a longitudinal process in which an indicator of past performance (e.g., increasing rank) at time t-1 prompts a competitive action (e.g., an app update) at time t, which hopefully improves future performance (e.g., rank increases further) at time t+1. That prior studies have illustrated the performance benefits of being a market leader (i.e., high rank) (Carare, 2012; Ifrach & Johari, 2014) showcases the importance of observing this indicator of past performance and basing app updating decisions on changes to it. Following the logic of competitive dynamics, we propose that when an app’s position on the ranking lists improves (i.e., its past performance is good), the app firm will release an app update.

H1a: As the rank of an app increases, the likelihood of an app update competitive action increases.

Another key indicator of past performance is the star rating of an app, which reflects how the app is performing with its customer base. Customer feedback on the app store is a realtime gauge of customer satisfaction (Liu et al., 2014), and customer satisfaction is a key source of competitive advantage (Müller, 1991). A firm’s success depends on satisfying it customers, and firms that cannot do so “are likely to lose market share to rivals who offer better products and service at lower prices” (Simon & Gómez, 2014, p. 371). Negative word of mouth (WOM) also increases the difficulty of obtaining new customers (Samson, 2006) and negative ratings have been shown to have a stronger impact on market share than positive ones (Chevalier & Mayzlin, 2006). App firms that experience customer satisfaction issues therefore risk losing market share and can be more easily dethroned by their competitors. Frequent incremental product upgrades can help firms compete in hypercompetitive environments by quickly addressing customers’ needs (Zahra & Bogner, 2000). In fact, Allon et al. (2022) found that when faced with intense competition, apps that do not frequently update face a greater quality decay rate, which suggests that in hypercompetitive markets, updating is critical to sustaining quality perceptions (e.g., ratings and reviews). In a hypercompetitive environment, it is thus crucial to observe an app’s ratings and reviews (i.e., past performance) and quickly take defensive action by releasing an app update when customer satisfaction falters.

Online WOM is widely used by customers to inform purchase decisions (Gu et al., 2012), and it is therefore critical to manage it well. Customer feedback, in the form of ratings and reviews, has been found to be an important driver of the success of games (Cox, 2014; Liu et al., 2014; Yi et al., 2019). For digital games, maintaining positive customer feedback is crucial because potential customers can use WOM to form an initial perception regarding a game’s fit with their preferences (Chellappa & Shivendu, 2005; Liu et al., 2014). Yi et al. (2019, p. 9) state that “the high satisfaction level of other consumers may reduce the uncertainty related to the game’s quality, which encourages a continuous flow of new users and higher rankings.” Digital games are considered experience goods (Chellappa & Shivendu, 2005; Mudambi & Schuff, 2010), and WOM is a critical determinant of the long-term success of experience goods (De Vany & Walls, 1999; Dellarocas et al., 2007). Positive user feedback can lead to an increased demand for apps (e.g., downloads) (Ghose & Han, 2014), and therefore, can help the app survive on the ranking lists (Lee & Raghu, 2014). Conversely, negative customer feedback can have a deleterious impact on the ability of an app to break into the ranking lists and, notably, can reduce the presence of an app on the ranking lists by approximately four days (Kajanan et al., 2012). For game apps, it may thus be advisable to monitor customer feedback for signs of trouble and take defensive competitive action to avoid being overtaken by competitors.

It is important to guard against negative customer feedback because it is easily spread and can lead to customer loss (Blodgett et al., 1993). It is possible to respond to negative WOM; however, failing to adequately respond to it can have a negative impact on a firm (e.g., decrease repatronage intentions or increase negative WOM) (Blodgett et al., 1993). Quickly addressing customer complaints in ways that customers regard as fair is key to reducing damage to the firm (Blodgett et al., 1993; Mattila & Mount, 2003). Research has demonstrated that customer agility can lead to better sales, but there are limits because overresponding to customer demands can lead to decreased performance (Zhou et al., 2018). Additionally, there is a benefit to highly positive consumer sentiment (i.e., customers that love rather than like the firm), which indicates that carefully attending to WOM can have advantages (Clemons et al., 2006). It is therefore critical to quickly and adequately respond to negative customer feedback to ensure future performance, and one possible response in our context is to release an app update.

Zahra and Bogner (2000, p. 146) recommend that a new software venture should “upgrade its products by quickly modifying or extending its existing offerings … [because] upgrades can enable the venture to maintain customer loyalty.” App firms can easily respond to customer feedback by releasing product updates (Lee & Raghu, 2014; Lee & Raghu, 2016), and developers are willing to incorporate customer feedback into their software update decisions (Di Sorbo et al., 2016; Ko et al., 2011; Nayebi et al., 2016). In one study, 51% of respondents stated they pay attention to user reviews on the app stores and incorporate user feedback into their update decisions (Al-Subaihin et al., 2021). The proposition that app firms strategically update in response to customer feedback is also supported in Lee and Raghu (2016). More agile versioning (i.e., updating) is associated with higher market risk, which is defined as the variance of the initial reviews of the app (Allon et al., 2022). This finding suggests that firms will update when their customer ratings or reviews indicate a risky market position. Correspondingly, we propose that when an app’s average star rating<sup>5</sup> declines (i.e., its past performance is poor), the app firm will release an app update.

H1b: As an app’s average star rating decreases, the likelihood of an app update competitive action increases.

People who download an app are not required to assign it a star rating or leave a comment. Consequently, the count of customers who do leave feedback serves as a gauge of interest (i.e., buzz). Therefore, another key indicator of past performance is the buzz generated by the app (i.e., rating volume). Increased rating volume has been associated with better future performance of apps. For example, higher volumes of user comments increase survival on the ranking lists (Lee & Raghu, 2014) and positively influence the demand for mobile apps (Ghose & Han, 2014). Moreover, studies in other contexts demonstrate that the volume of user feedback can be more important to performance (e.g., sales) than the product evaluation (e.g., star rating) (Duan et al., 2008; Gu et al., 2012; Liu, 2006). One explanation offered for the importance of customer feedback volume is that the more users comment on a product, the more awareness of that product is generated (Berger et al., 2010; Duan et al., 2008; Liu, 2006). The product is being talked about, so even if the customer feedback is negative, the buzz surrounding the product can lead to better performance (Han et al., 2020). The volume of customer feedback can thus be an important goal for app firms that wish to stand out in an overcrowded market. These findings suggest that monitoring buzz (i.e., past performance) and capitalizing on improvement opportunities through app updates when customers are interested in the app may improve future performance.

It is important for companies to obtain buzz because it signals awareness of and affection for the company or brand to others (Chevalier & Mayzlin, 2006; Li & Hitt, 2008; Luo & Zhang, 2013). It has been empirically demonstrated that buzz can affect firm value and performance (Li & Hitt, 2008; Luo & Zhang, 2013). Notably, research has illustrated that “a positive shock in a firm’s own buzz rating and volume … will help attract more customer interest in the firm” (Luo & Zhang, 2013, p. 232). Therefore, competitive actions, such as app updates, that can be strategically deployed to increase an app’s buzz can be beneficial. Researchers have found that companies do deploy tactics to help generate buzz (i.e., encourage WOM). For example, a firm’s information release or advertising strategies can be manipulated to increase buzz (Campbell et al., 2017). Online WOM can also be influenced by press releases and personalized responses to individual customers (Hewett et al., 2016). Other suggested tactics include firms engaging in product redesign to reduce the influence of negative reviews or offering price discounts to generate more reviews to minimize the impact of negative bias (Li & Hitt, 2008). Product redesign, which can be accomplished through app updates, can thus be used to manipulate buzz. By adding new features or fixing defects, app firms can leverage app updates to keep customers engaged with and talking about the app.

The volume and speed of managerial responses to customer reviews increase the future volume of customer reviews (Sheng, 2019). In her study of online reviews, Sheng (2019) found that firms that are more responsive to customers reviews generate a larger review volume than less responsive firms. Moreover, it was revealed that the speed of response is important to generating larger review volumes (i.e., more buzz). Chen et al. (2019) also found that managerial responses increase the volume of subsequent customer reviews. The managerial response considered in these studies is a written reply to a customer comment, but an app update can also be deployed as a response to customer feedback. An app update can provide fixes that respond to customer requests or complaints and may also provide new functionality that can drive user engagement (Fleischmann et al., 2016). Quick and plentiful firm responses can thus help generate higher volumes of future reviews (Sheng, 2019). Consequently, updating apps when they are generating buzz can help app firms capitalize on market opportunities to generate future buzz, which may improve their competitive positions (Duan et al., 2008; Gu et al., 2012; Liu, 2006). Star rating volume is thus a variable that should be continuously monitored and increases to it should prompt offensive competitive action. Correspondingly, we propose that when the volume of an app’s feedback increases (i.e., its past performance is good), the app firm will release an app update.

H1c: As an app’s volume of ratings increases, the likelihood of an app update competitive action increases.

## Observable Indicators of an App’s Competitors’ Actions

Li et al. (2010, p. 639) state that “in their pursuit of competitive advantage, firms make offensive and defensive competitive moves” and that these actions “position firms in their competitive environment.” Hypercompetitive environments are characterized by aggressive competition in which firms exploit new market opportunities and preempt competitors’ efforts to improve their competitive position (Smith et al., 2006). In fact, firms that carry out more competitive actions experience market share gains and improved performance (Young et al., 1996) and are better able to fend off challengers (Ferrier et al., 1999). App firms in the game app market are thus likely to aggressively fend off competitors to defend their competitive position.

Some competitors’ actions are highly visible on the app store, such as when competitors update their apps or new competitors enter ranking lists. The literature has suggested that visibility regarding competitors’ actions is crucial to competitive responses (Chen & Hambrick, 1995; Chen & Miller, 1994). Visibility has been described as “the amount of information available about a competitive move,” where “highly visible moves tend to elicit competitive responses” (Chen & Hambrick, 1995, p. 458). Chen and Miller (1994) found that high-visibility and low-difficulty competitive moves were likely to induce rival responses. Releasing an app update is not an excessively difficult competitive move. Consequently, app firms may respond to competitors’ app updates by releasing their own. Similarly, the appearance of a new competitor on the ranking lists is highly visible, and it is not excessively difficult to respond to this threat by releasing an app update to defend the app’s competitive position.

An app update falls under what Gnyawali et al. (2010, p. 605) term “product innovation,” which includes “moves or actions by the focal firm focused on innovation and improvement on a particular product or service, including inventions, development, improvements.” Such competitive actions are perceived as threats and can trigger competitive responses (Aboulnasr et al., 2008; Chen & MacMillan, 1992; Smith & Grimm, 1991). Firm innovativeness has been shown to have a positive effect on firm performance most dramatically under conditions of high competitive intensity and market turbulence (Tsai & Yang, 2013), which suggests that there is a benefit to innovating when competition and customer preferences change rapidly. Moreover, there is evidence that innovation-related competitive actions are key to the survival of software firms (Li et al., 2010). In the desktop software market, the timing of the release of generational software versions has been shown to be responsive to competitor innovation in concentrated markets (Turner et al., 2010). Specifically, at high levels of market concentration, software companies are more likely to respond to a major software version release by releasing their own (Turner et al., 2010).

Much of the competitive dynamics research has focused on action and response dyads; that is, a firm taking a competitive action and the response of a competitor to that action (Chen & Miller, 2012; Hsieh & Hyun, 2016; Zucchini et al., 2019). Hsieh and Hyun (2016, p. 2189) posit that “it is more reasonable to view that a firm responds to the aggregate impact of multiple competitors’ actions.” Similarly, Zucchini et al. (2019, p. 659) propose that “as firms observe rival actions accumulating they perceive increased ‘competitive pressure’ to respond to protect their competitive position.” As the number of competitors’ actions increase, firms are more likely to take their own competitive action in response (Hsieh & Hyun, 2016). Moreover, the more competitive actions taken by rivals since a firm’s last competitive action can also increase the magnitude of its next action (Zucchini et al., 2019). Correspondingly, we propose that as the number of an app’s competitors that have released an app update increases, the likelihood of an app update competitive action increases.

H2a: As the number of an app’s competitors that have released an app update increases, the likelihood of an app update competitive action increases.

One of Porter’s (1979) five competitive forces is the threat of new entrants. High entry barriers and retaliation from incumbents lessen the threat of new entrants (Porter, 1979). Market entry can provide an approximation of rivalry intensity; that is, “the higher the entry rate, the more intense the rivalry” (Baum & Korn, 1996, p. 257). App firms in the game app markets face intense competition from new market entrants. For example, 580 new game apps were submitted to the Apple App Store in March of 2022 (Statista, 2022b). The app stores have been described as overcrowded, such that new apps can get lost before being given a chance to prove their worth (Siegfried et al., 2015; Yi et al., 2019). Consequently, new apps that achieve rank are visible, looming threats. New competitors that encroach upon an app’s position on the ranking lists visibly threaten the app’s competitive position and may stimulate a competitive response.

Economic growth is characterized by existing firms innovating to improve existing products while new firms offer original innovations to the market in attempts to displace the incumbents (Acemoglu & Cao, 2015). Incumbents that do not respond to new competitors risk falling into a “death spiral” in which they lose customers and market share (Clemons et al., 1996, p. 66). Firms that do not continuously innovate and respond slowly to competitive threats may find it difficult to defend their market positions (Gatignon et al., 1997). In highly competitive markets, firms innovate to escape the competition, and competition drives innovation because technological advancement is necessary to stay in contention (Qian & Wang, 2017). A first-mover or market pioneer advantage can stem from, for example, shaping customer preferences early, gaining a reputation as a market leader, or technological leadership (Kuester et al., 2000). However, later market entrants can benefit from incumbents educating customers about the product category and from observing customers’ responses to incumbents’ products (Soberman & Gatignon, 2005). Although later entrants must steal market share from incumbents (Ketchen et al., 2004), innovative late-market entrants may pose more significant problems to incumbent firms because they may have higher market potential (Kuester et al., 2000; Shankar et al., 1998). Therefore, app firms must innovate and retaliate to escape new competitors in the hypercompetitive app stores because barriers to entry are low and competition is intense. Consequently, we propose that as the number of new competitors the app faces increases, it becomes more likely that the app firm will release an app update.

H2b: As the number of new competitors increases, the likelihood of an app update competitive action increases.

## Observable Indicators of an App’s Relative Competitive Position

One of the key determinants of competitive strategy according to Porter (1985) is the relative competitive position of a firm in an industry. Relative competitive position can be characterized in many ways, including relative market share, relative perceived quality, or relative technological leadership (Oliva et al., 1988; Varadarajan & Jayachandran, 1999). It has been argued that assessing relative competitive position is critical to improving a firm’s position (Oliva et al., 1988). Not understanding the firm’s relative competitive position can lead to underresponding, which can result in the firm losing market position, or overresponding, which can result in wasted resources (Oliva et al., 1988). Luo and Zhang (2013) argue that although firms may exercise more control over their own metrics, it is critical to consider competitors’ metrics to understand their influence on firm value. An app firm may thus benefit from comparing its app against competing apps to determine if there is an opportunity to improve the app’s market position, or conversely, to correct course if the comparison suggests the app’s competitive position is threatened.

In arguing hypothesis H1b,<sup>6</sup> we illustrated the importance of maintaining positive consumer sentiment, and quickly responding to customer complaints. Similar logic would suggest that if an app’s ratings relative to its competitors fall, the app’s market position will be threatened and defensive action should be taken. Gale and Buzzell (1989) introduced the concept of relative perceived quality, which describes external quality perceptions (i.e., customers’ views of the quality) of a product, relative to its competitors. Relative product quality has been found to affect firm performance indirectly through its positive effect on relative market position (Phillips et al., 1983). This suggests that it is important to correct negative external quality perceptions before they can damage the product’s competitive position. Peteraf and Bergen (2003, p. 1031) state that “what drives consumer choice is not simply whether or not a product can satisfy a given need, but how well it can do so.” Customers make product comparisons based on quality; consequently, it is critical for an app to receive customer ratings on par with or better than its competitors to maintain and grow its user base and stay competitive. Relative star rating is thus a variable that should be continuously monitored and decreases in it should prompt defensive action. Correspondingly, we propose that when an app is experiencing decreasing ratings relative to its competitors (i.e., poor past performance relative to its competitors), an app update will be released.

## H3a: As an app’s relative average star rating decreases, the likelihood of an app update competitive action increases.

A large number of reviews is an important social cue that indicates that a product is popular with others (Powell et al., 2017). Customers sometimes demonstrate a preference for products that have a large volume of reviews even when the ratings indicate those products are of poor quality (Powell et al., 2017). Studies have consistently found that review volume is important to sales (Babić Rosario et al., 2016; Chevalier & Mayzlin, 2006; Ho-Dac et al., 2013; Sahoo et al., 2018). Consumer buzz refers to user-generated comments or reviews and is an important predictor of firm value (Luo & Zhang, 2013). In fact, even negative buzz can increase both awareness and purchase intent for some types of products (Han et al., 2020). Therefore, to be competitive, it is important for app firms to increase or sustain the buzz surrounding their apps.

Both the firm’s own buzz and its rivals’ buzz can affect firm value, with the former having a positive effect and the latter a negative one (Luo & Zhang, 2013). Luo and Zhang (2013, p. 232) noted that “a positive shock in a firm’s own buzz rating and volume (as well as a negative shock in rival buzz) will help attract more customer interest in the firm and its products.” Customers have limited cognitive resources, and thus, when attention is paid to one product (e.g., app), fewer resources are available for others (Luo & Zhang, 2013; Yoo et al., 2019). Research has indicated that responding quickly and frequently to customer feedback can increase buzz (Chen et al., 2019; Sheng, 2019), which is desirable because increased consumer interest can improve an app’s downloads and position on the ranking lists. The findings of Luo and Zhang (2013) indicate that this effect may be even more prominent when the app’s buzz is increasing and its competitors’ buzz is decreasing because it may be easier to attract customers’ attention to the buzzworthy app. Relative rating volume is thus a variable that should be continuously monitored and opportunities to capitalize on the popularity of an app should prompt offensive competitive action. Correspondingly, we propose that an app will update when its relative rating volume increases (i.e., its past performance is good relative to its competitors).

H3b: As an app’s relative rating volume increases, the likelihood of an app update competitive action increases.

In an environment of imperfect competition, there are two primary marketing strategies: product differentiation and market segmentation (Smith, 1956). Product differentiation is an attempt to create or highlight the distinctiveness of the firm’s product vis-à-vis its competitors, whereas market segmentation involves playing to the specific needs of subsets of customers in a market. Smith (1956, p. 5) describes the difference as “the differentiator seeks to secure a layer of the market cake, whereas one who employs market segmentation strives to secure one or more wedge-shaped pieces.” Competing based on differentiation means that customers perceive a firm’s product to be unique or believe that it offers additional benefits that competitors’ products do not, and differentiation strategies are closely linked to innovation (Santos-Vijande et al., 2012). There were 18 game subcategories on the Apple App Store at the time of our data collection. The game subcategories represent types of games (e.g., Puzzle), and can be considered app game market segments. A maximum of two subcategories can be assigned to an app. App firms that compete in multiple game subcategories with one or more apps can be considered to have more diverse portfolios than firms that compete in fewer subcategories.

An app firm that competes in fewer subcategories than its competitors will be more reliant on customers in fewer market segments. When firms compete in multiple markets, basic concerns include how to leverage position in one market to improve position in another and how to use one investment to account for another one being attacked or collapsing (Aaker & Mascarenhas, 1984; McGrath et al., 1998). Moreover, app firms that compete in many subcategories are more likely to engage in multiple-point or multimarket competition (Chellappa et al., 2010; Gimeno & Woo, 1996; McGrath et al., 1998) in which they competitively vie for position in multiple subcategories against the same competitors. Notably, multimarket competition decreases rivalry intensity (Gimeno & Woo, 1996; Young et al., 1997). Although we do not measure multimarket competition directly, there are only 18 subcategories; thus, the more subcategories app firms compete in, the more likely it is that they will have overlapping subcategories. Therefore, app firms that compete in fewer game subcategories than their competitors will be both more dependent on the focal app and less likely to engage in multimarket competition.

Competitive strategies have been classified on a continuum from prospectors to defenders (Miles & Snow, 1986; Slater & Narver, 1993). Although their performance is similar, the way that prospectors and defenders operate in their competitive environments differs. Prospectors seek out new product and market opportunities, whereas defenders aggressively protect their product market (Slater & Narver, 1993). App firms with games in fewer subcategories are more likely to be defenders that aggressively protect their apps. Moreover, app firms that lack portfolio diversity relative to their competition need to focus their efforts on product differentiation (e.g., adding features to their apps) to remain competitive. Research has demonstrated that “firms favor product innovations when the degree of competition is intense and the innovation costs are low” and argued that “the rationale behind this result is firms’ desire to escape competition by introducing a distinct product” (Weiss, 2003, p. 311). Allon et al. (2022) showed that app firms with smaller product portfolios update more frequently, which they attribute to the scale economies that can be attained by firms with larger portfolios coordinating development. App firms that compete in fewer subcategories than their competitors may thus release app updates more frequently to differentiate their apps from competitors’ apps, thereby intensely and nimbly defending the competitive positions of their smaller portfolio of apps. Correspondingly, we propose that as the relative portfolio diversity of an app firm decreases, the likelihood of an app update being released will increase.

H3c: As an app’s relative portfolio diversity decreases, the likelihood of an app update competitive action increases.

## Method

## Research Setting

Our sample consists of apps from the games category<sup>7</sup> of the Apple App Store. The games category is the largest on the Apple App Store, with more than 1 million game apps available 2022 (Statista, 2022c). Game app offerings made up more than 13% of the Apple App Store in 2022, with the next closest category capturing only 10% (business) and all other categories capturing 9% or less of the market share (Statista, 2022a). The games category thus provides the largest sample of apps in the same market. We focus on the game apps that appeared on one of the 18 subcategory<sup>8</sup> ranking lists during our data collection. Our interest is in examining how observable competitive indicators shape app update competitive actions, and rank can be used to define competitors. Therefore, using apps that have achieved rank at least once during our observation period provides the desired sample. Our approach is similar to prior literature focusing on a single store or app category or on ranked apps (e.g., Garg & Telang, 2012; Lee & Raghu, 2014; Liu et al., 2014; Yin et al., 2014).

To examine the effect of the app’s competitors’ actions and the app’s relative competitive position, it is necessary to define a set of competitors of an app. We define an app’s competitors as those apps that appear within 10 positions (i.e., +/-10) of the app’s rank on any of the subcategory ranking lists on which the app appears that day.<sup>9</sup> Therefore, the competitors of an app are those apps that share at least one subcategory in common and are ranked close (i.e., +/- 10) to the app.

## Data

To collect our data, we crawled the top free, top paid, top game, and each of 18 the subcategory ranking lists for the period of February 4, 2015, to November 29, 2015. There were 100 ranking positions in both the top free and top paid charts, but these 200 positions could be filled by mobile apps in any category. We excluded any non-game apps obtained from the top free and top paid charts to ensure we had apps that were similar in nature. The top game chart had 240 positions, and each subcategory ranking chart also had 240 positions, for a total of 4,560 positions per day available only to apps that listed “game” as one of their two category choices. Game is a category on the Apple App Store and the 18 subcategories are the types of games (e.g., Action, Puzzle, Card). A game app can list up to two subcategories. A single game can thus appear on multiple charts (e.g., the top game chart and a subcategory ranking chart).

On each day of the 298-day observation period, we collected the data required to calculate the variables used in our analyses for each app appearing at least once during our observation period on any of the 21 charts. We retrieved additional data from SensorTower to calculate firm age and obtain the number of categories in which the 1,155 app firms in our sample had apps. Our data collection resulted in data for 4,885 game apps. Of these, 2,293 did not have any updates during our observation period and were thus excluded from our analysis. We also excluded 286 apps that had extensive missing values or had both free and paid versions to control for potential correlation between the two versions of the same app. The final number of apps included in our dataset was 2,306. The 2,306 apps updated an average of 3.5 times during our observation period, and these 2,306 apps represented 80% of the downloads during this period.

## Operationalization of Variables

## Dependent Variable

The objective of our analysis was to examine how observable competitive indicators influence app update competitive actions. Therefore, we defined the dependent variable, Update, as a binary variable that, for each day, is 1 if the app was updated $( U p d a t e _ { t } ~ = ~ 1 )$ on day t and 0 otherwise $( U p d a t e _ { t } = 0 )$ Specifically, for each app every day of our data collection period, we scraped the field “CurrentVersionReleaseDate.” If CurrentVersionReleaseDate<sub>t</sub> was not equal to CurrentVersionReleaseDate<sub>t-1</sub>, we set $U p d a t e _ { t } ~ = ~ 1$ We examined this binary decision variable, which considers whether the app firm updated given the set of independent variables.

## Independent Variables

We defined three observable competitive indicators of an app’s competitive position (i.e., past performance) as independent variables in our model: rank (Rank), rating (VersionRating), and rating volume (VersionRatingVolume). Competitors were defined as any app that appeared 10 spots above or below the focal app that day on any of the subcategory lists on which the focal app appeared. As a robustness check, we provide model results with a second competitor definition in the Appendix. We defined two observable competitive indicators of the app’s competitors’ actions as independent variables in our model: number of new competitors (NewCompetitor) and number of updates by competitors (UpdatesOfCompetitors). We also defined three observable competitive indicators of the app’s relative competitive position as independent variables in our model: relative rating (RelativeRating), relative rating volume (RelativeRatingVolume), and relative portfolio diversity (RelativePortfolioDiversity). The relative measures were calculated by taking the difference between the app’s variable (e.g., number of ratings) and the average of its competitors’ variables $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , the average of the competitors’ numbers of ratings). The relative portfolio diversity variable considered the difference between the number of game app subcategories the app firm competed in and the average number of its competitors’ game app subcategories.

We formally define each of the independent variables used to test our hypotheses in Table 1. Table 2 reports the summary statistics for all variables. We provide the correlation matrix for the variables in the Appendix. The correlations between the variables are reasonably low. We developed three independent variables to test H1a-H1c. For H1a, we used Rank<sub>ijt</sub>, which is the natural log of app firm j’s app i’s average subcategory rank at time t. For H1b, we computed VersionRating<sub>ijt</sub> as the average customer star rating since the last version of app firm j’s app I calculated at time t. For H1c, we defined VersionRatingVolume<sub>ijt</sub>, which is the natural log of the count of the number of customer star ratings since the last version of app firm j’s app i calculated at time t. Competitive positions of the current version of the focal app (i.e., past performance) informed the next app update decision. Therefore, we used only ratings that customers left for the focal app between the last app update and the current app update to calculate VersionRating<sub>ijt</sub> and VersionRatingVolume<sub>ijt</sub>.

We developed two independent variables to test H2a and H2b. For H2a, we defined UpdatesOfCompetitors<sub>ijt</sub> as the average number of updates over all competitors of app firm $j ^ { \circ } \mathbf { s }$ app i at time t. For H2b, we defined NewCompetitors<sub>ijt</sub> as the number of new apps to which the competitor definition applies at time t that were not previously labeled as a competitor of app firm j’s app i prior to time t. We developed three independent variables to test H3a-H3c. To calculate the relative variables, we first averaged the star ratings and the count of the ratings for the current releases of all competitors of the focal app at time t. We also averaged the count of the game subcategories of all competitors of the focal app at time t. For H3a, we defined the variable RelativeRating<sub>ijt</sub> as the difference between app firm j’s app i’s current version rating and the overall average current version rating of all its competitors at time t. We defined the variable RelativeRatingVolume<sub>ijt</sub>, for H3b as the difference between app firm j’s app i’s current version rating count and the overall average current version rating count of all its competitors at time t. For H3c, we defined the variable RelativePortfolioDiversity<sub>ijt</sub> as the difference between app firm j’s subcategory count and the overall average subcategory count of all its competitors at time t.

## Control Variables

We included a set of variables to control for other exogenous effects that may impact an app firm’s app updates. We included two possible platform changes. First, we considered updates to the operating system (iOS), which may force an update to an app. iOSUpdate<sub>t</sub> is a dummy variable that equals 1 if the iOS is updated at time t and 0 otherwise. An app may also need to be updated to be compatible with new hardware devices (Arora et al., 2010; Choudhary & Zhang, 2015; Temizkan et al., 2012). Therefore, we included NumberOfSupportedDevices<sub>ijt</sub>, which counts the number of supported hardware devices for each app i at time t to capture the release of new hardware devices. We controlled for the age of the app, ProductAge<sub>ijt</sub>, which we computed as the natural log of the number of days since app firm $j ^ { \circ } \mathbf { s }$ app i’s initial release date at time t. We also included the natural log of the square of the product age as a control for a potential nonlinear relationship between product age and app update release, ProductA $g e ^ { 2 } { } _ { i j t }$ (Wang et al., 2018).

## Model Development

We estimated app firms’ app update competitive actions using a discrete choice model to examine the influence of the independent variables on the app update release. Variations of discrete choice models have been widely used in fields such as IS and marketing to study user or consumer decisionmaking processes (e.g., Berry, 1994; Ghose & Han, 2011; Murthi & Sarkar, 2003). In our setting, an app firm decided whether to release an update for app i in each time period t. We considered app update release competitive actions from a binary choice set (i.e., whether or not to release an update). Therefore, we utilized a utility-based multilevel mixedeffect logit model with binary choices. For app firm j’s app i, we specified the latent utility it received at time t as a function of the above-defined variables as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} U _ {i j t} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \text {Rank} _ {i j t - 1} + \beta_ {2} \text {VersionRating} _ {i j t - 1} + \\ \beta_ {3} \text {VersionRatingVolume} _ {i j t - 1} + \\ \beta_ {4} \text {NewCompetitors} _ {i j t - 1} + \beta_ {5} \text {UpdatesOfCompetitors} _ {i j t - 1} + \\ \beta_ {6} \text {RelativeRating} _ {i j t - 1} + \\ \beta_ {7} \text {RelativeRatingVolume} _ {i j t - 1} + \beta_ {8} \text {RelativePortfolioDiversity} \\ + a X _ {i j t} + \xi_ {i} + \zeta_ {j} + \delta_ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i j t}, \end{array} \tag {1}
$$

where X<sub>ijt</sub> is a vector of the control variables and the error term $\varepsilon _ { i j t }$ is from the extreme value distribution. The indirect utility from not releasing an update at time t is ${ \cal U } _ { i j 0 t } = \varepsilon _ { i j 0 t } .$ Because an app firm may have multiple apps that require updates, the odds of one of its apps releasing an update could be correlated with the odds of another of its apps releasing an update. Therefore, we used a two-level nested logit model to accommodate the nature of the dataset. A two-level nested logit model allows for correlation among the update release odds of apps from the same app firm, as well as correlation among the update release odds for the same app over time. In addition, we considered a mixed-effect model at different levels to best accommodate time-invariant unobserved effects from apps and app firms. Such unobserved effects may include the quality of the apps, market tastes, and/or app firms’ development experience or capabilities, which could affect app update release decisions and are unobserved to the researchers. In Equation (1), $\xi _ { i }$ and $\zeta _ { j }$ are the app and app firm-level random effects, while δ<sub>i</sub> is the app-level fixed effect that allows for correlation with the independent variables (Jabr et al., 2014).<sup>10</sup> All independent variables and control variables were time-lagged to control for potential endogeneity among independent variables and the outcome variable. Our model specification provides more flexibility than a traditional logit model because it accounts for correlation among alternatives and accommodates latent multilevel effects for apps and app firms. To assess the importance of adopting a nested logit model over a traditional logit model, we conducted likelihood ratio (LR) tests to compare the models (Gu et al., 2012). Our result suggests that a nested logit model significantly improves the model over a traditional logit model with a p-value smaller than 0.0001 in all the LR tests. To test for potential multicollinearity among independent variables, we computed the tolerance and variance inflation factors (VIFs) for all the models. The VIF values for all variables in our models are below two, which suggests no multicollinearity problems in our results (O’Brien, 2007).

Our results indicate that app firms may be capitalizing on the opportunity to maximize position on the ranking lists by releasing an app update to boost downloads when an app’s rank increases (i.e., good past performance) to improve future performance (e.g., more downloads or higher rankings). This result is not strictly intuitive because research has linked app updating to a decrease in past performance—specifically, a decline in the number of downloads, using a sample from a European app store (Comino et al., 2016, 2019). We posited that app firms would be more likely to release updates as rank increases because it is known that higher-rank positions carry financial advantage (Carare, 2012; Garg & Telang, 2012; Ifrach & Johari, 2014), which combined with the short product lifecycles characteristic of game apps (Lui et al., 2014; Yi et al., 2019), may make it opportune to jockey for competitive position while the app is doing well instead of waiting until it is experiencing signs of trouble. Indeed, our results indicate that when app firms see an opportunity to improve their app’s future performance, they take it.

We also found support for H2, which indicates that as customer ratings decline (i.e., past performance is poor), an app update competitive action becomes more likely. App firms have the novel opportunity to monitor real-time customer feedback and quickly release app updates in response to customer concerns (Al-Subaihin et al., 2021; Lee & Raghu, 2014; Lee & Raghu, 2016). Negative WOM is a threat to the success and longevity of an app because it can spread quickly on the internet (Pfeffer et al., 2014; Ravichandran et al., 2015) and lead to the loss of customers and market share (Blodgett et al., 1993; Chevalier & Mayzlin, 2006). It is thus critical for app firms to quickly respond to falling customer ratings to reverse the trend and improve future performance. Our results indicate that app firms are indeed responsive to this competitive threat. Finally, we found support for H3, which reveals that app firms take app update competitive actions in response to increasing volumes of customer feedback. This can be interpreted as an attempt by app firms to boost the buzz surrounding their apps. It is important to keep people talking about an app to increase awareness of it (Berger et al., 2010; Duan et al., 2008; Liu, 2006; Luo & Zhang, 2013). Providing enhancements through app updates is one way to stimulate buzz and increase user engagement (Apple, 2020; Comino et al., 2019). Our results indicate that app firms capitalize on the opportunity to boost buzz by releasing app updates when the volume of customer feedback increases.

Second, we examined two observable indicators of app competitor actions: competitors’ updates (H2a) and new competitors (H2b). We found support for H2a, which indicates that app update competitive actions are taken when competitive pressures from an app’s competitors’ app updates build. Firms innovate to improve products, and such competitive actions are common among technology firms, are perceived to be competitive threats, and can trigger competitive responses (Aboulnasr et al., 2008; Chen & MacMillan, 1992; Gnyawali et al., 2010; Smith & Grimm, 1991; Turner et al., 2010). Our results indicate that app firms take app update competitive actions in response to competitors’ app update releases in an active attempt to defend against the threat of their competitors’ product innovations. We also found that app updates are released when the number of new competitors increases, which supports H2b. New competitors encroaching on an app’s ranking is highly visible, and highly visible competitor actions are more likely to elicit competitive responses (Chen & Hambrick, 1995). In hypercompetitive environments, firms need to continuously innovate and quickly respond to competitive threats in order to defend their market positions (Gatignon et al., 1997; Qian & Wang, 2017). Our results suggest that app firms do respond to the threat of new competitors by releasing app updates.

Third, we examine three observable indicators of an app’s relative competitive position: relative rating (H3a), relative rating volume (H3b), and relative portfolio diversity (H3c). We did not find support for H3a, which may indicate that although a decline in an app’s own customer ratings will prompt an app update, a declining relative rating is not a large enough threat to prompt a competitive action. Studies have found that negative WOM decreases future sales (Chevalier & Mayzlin, 2006; Sun, 2012), but results have been inconsistent (Babić Rosario et al., 2016). In fact, WOM volume has a greater influence on sales than WOM valence (Babić Rosario et al., 2016), leading researchers to suggest that even negative buzz may be beneficial (Han et al., 2020). Therefore, one explanation for our findings may be that for app firms, any WOM is preferable to no WOM—even if the buzz is negative.

We find that app update competitive actions are taken when the buzz surrounding an app increases, relative to its competitors, supporting H3b. Increased buzz may signal awareness of and affection for an app (Chevalier & Mayzlin, 2006; Han et al., 2020; Li & Hitt, 2008; Luo & Zhang, 2013). Moreover, an app experiencing increasing buzz when its rivals’ buzz is decreasing can attract more interest in the app (Luo & Zhang, 2013). Our results indicate that app firms capitalize on the opportunity to further boost their own app’s buzz by releasing an app update when their rivals are experiencing weakening rating volumes compared to their own. We also found that app firms that compete in fewer subcategories than their competitors are more likely to update their apps. One interpretation of this result is that some app firms have purposefully decided to compete by developing deeper expertise in fewer types of games in order to target particular market segments, whereas their competitors resources may be stretched because of their need to manage greater product diversity. An alternative explanation is that app firms that compete in fewer subcategories may feel increased competitive pressure to defend their less diverse portfolio, whereas app firms with greater portfolio diversity may feel less pressure because a failure in one subcategory might be counteracted by a success in another. In other words, app firms that concentrate on fewer game app subcategories may be defenders that take competitive actions to aggressively protect their product (Slater & Narver, 1993). Our findings are summarized in Table 4.

Multiple robustness checks are provided in the Appendix, including using alternative measures for Rank to account for the days on which an app may not be listed on any of the ranking lists, using review sentiment and the review volume as alternative measures for ratings and rating volume, using an alternative competitor definition, incorporating instrumental variables for Rank to account for potential endogeneity, and using lagged dependent variables to account for possible delayed decision-making regarding app update releases by app firms. Our major findings hold with these robustness checks.

The results in Table 5 show that there are differences in the observable competitive indicators that prompt a major versus minor app update release. Notably, the results reveal that app firms respond to decreasing rank with a major update. This indicates that app firms take serious action (i.e., release a major update) when their apps’ positions on the ranking lists are threatened. The response to flagging customer ratings is to release both major and minor updates, which suggests that app firms quickly respond with everything in their arsenal to reduce negative WOM. Similarly, both major and minor updates are used to contend with the appearance of new competitors. These results indicate that app firms respond to serious competitive threats with major updates.

Minor updates are also used to thwart serious competitive threats (i.e., when customer ratings are declining or when faced with new competitors), but to a lesser extent because they are not employed when apps’ ranks are threatened. However, minor updates are used as a response to app updates released by the app’s rivals. This result could indicate that app firms feel the need to respond to competitors’ updates but that the act of responding (i.e., being seen to respond) may be more important than the content of the response. App firms that are competing across fewer game subcategories than their rivals are also more likely to release minor updates. One interpretation of the extensiveness results may be that app firms differentiate between situations in which an action simply requires a response for the sake of appearance and those where serious modifications are necessary to defend the app’s competitive position.

## Discussion

## Implications for Theory and Research

App stores feature unique competitive environments. The barriers to entry are low—i.e., extensive resources are not required to create apps and app stores are easily and equally accessible to any app firm (Boudreau, 2012; Lee & Raghu, 2014). These characteristics enable app firms to quickly respond to competitive opportunities and threats by releasing app updates (Lee & Raghu, 2014). However, the same characteristics also enable an intensely competitive environment characterized by rapid innovation and many firms vying for temporary advantages (e.g., more downloads and users). Our study draws from competitive dynamics research to examine competition in the hypercompetitive game app market. Specifically, we focus on one specific competitive action—the app update—that app firms use to vie for competitive position. We show how observable indicators that signal the app’s competitive position (i.e., past performance), the app’s competitors’ actions, and the app’s relative competitive position influence app update competitive actions. We use an archival dataset of app store data that provides market conditions prior to each app update competitive action to study our model.

We make several contributions to the literature on competitive dynamics. Prior studies using a competitive dynamics lens have focused on the packaged software industry (e.g., Turner et al., 2010; Young et al., 1996). Although it is considered a hypercompetitive market, it differs in important ways from the app markets in ecosystems. A key difference is that the platform provider curates store data in a central and easily accessible location (Grover & Kohli, 2012), which increases the visibility of data that can be used to determine competitive position and to inform competitive action decisions. We found that app firms use app update competitive actions to vie for temporary advantage and that they respond not only to indicators of their own competitive positions but also to their competitors’ actions and their relative competitive positions. Our results suggest that ecosystem data transparency is leveraged to inform decisions about competitive actions. Specifically, curated data on past performance, relative past performance, and competitors’ actions is used to inform app firms’ strategic app update decisions in hopes of improving the future performance of their apps. Our study thus adds insights to the body of competitive dynamics research by illustrating how data drives competition in a hypercompetitive market.

Few studies have considered software updating to be a competitive move (Turner et al., 2010; Zahra & Bogner, 2000), but as release cycles have become shorter and app stores have made the delivery of app updates easier (Adams & McIntosh, 2016), it has become increasingly possible to use app updating as a competitive action to improve or sustain temporary competitive advantage. Studies have shown that innovationrelated competitive actions increase software firm survival (Li et al., 2010). However, few studies in the competitive dynamics literature have studied the software industry context (Turner et al., 2010; Young et al., 1996) and even fewer have studied the drivers of the update competitive action (Turner et al., 2010). Competitive dynamics research has “broadly attempted to explain both the causes and consequences of action and reaction with particular emphasis on performance consequences” (Smith et al., 2006, p. 315). We focus on the drivers of the app update competitive action rather than the performance consequences, which have been more widely studied. We thus contribute to the competitive dynamics literature by showing what observable competitive indicators drive app update competitive actions; that is, we look at a robust set of antecedents of a novel competitive action.

Moreover, we provide insight into competition in a novel context, an app store, which differs in key ways from the packaged software contexts that have been the focus of prior competition studies (Turner et al., 2010; Young et al., 1996). We show that app firms are responsive to observable indicators of competitive position and capitalize on the data visibility provided by the platform to help inform their strategic decisions to best position their apps in an intensely competitive environment. Moreover, we show that there is variation in the type of app update that is used as a competitive action to counter different competitive indicators. We show that app firms respond with major updates to serious threats to the app’s competitive position. Our results suggest that minor updates are used in situations where the act of responding to rivals’ competitive actions may be more important than the content of the response. We also show that older firms are responsive to more observable competitive indicators than younger firms which suggests that mature app firms may be defending profitable market positions. We thus examine app update competitive actions at a granular level to tease out differences in how app firms deploy them to improve their app’s competitive position and ward off competitors.

Chen et al. (2010) showed that in markets focused on temporary advantage, strong past performance motivates competitive moves, which differs from findings in the competitive dynamics literature in mature industries that have shown that poor performance motivates moves (Smith et al., 2006). Notably, a study in the software industry also found that good past performance increased competitive action (Young et al., 1996). One explanation provided by Chen et al. (2010, p. 1542) for the conflicting findings is that “while high performers avoid competitive confrontation in the established market (where entrenched rivals have somewhat sustainable advantages and clear visibility of rivalrous moves), the ambiguity of new markets provides a shield to engage in moves.” Our results demonstrate that app firms take competitive actions when their past performance is strong (e.g., their rank or buzz increases). These findings also show that in a market focused on temporary advantage, strong past performance can stimulate competitive action. However, we provide a broader picture and show that app makers are also responsive to threats such as declining star ratings, new competitors encroaching on their rankings, and competitors’ updates. Our findings provide an extensive view of competition in a novel hypercompetitive market. Although our results largely conform to the theory of competitive dynamics, we provide nuanced results that paint a more holistic picture of competition on app stores than is often possible in studies of traditional industries. Our study thus enriches the understanding of how firms in hypercompetitive environments vie for temporary advantage in the face of intense competition and rapid innovation.

## Implications for Society and Practice

Research has indicated that software firms may adjust internally driven software updating schedules in response to external conditions, particularly in highly dynamic environments (Al-Subaihin et al., 2021; Cao & Ramesh, 2007; Nayebi et al., 2016; Turner et al., 2010). Our results lend credence to this notion, and we suggest that app firms should monitor the competitive environment to inform their app updating decisions. Obtaining a high position on ranking lists is critical to the future success of an app (Carare, 2012; Garg & Telang, 2012), and using app updates to capitalize on strong performance (e.g., good rankings) is an offensive tactic that our results suggest app firms should use. Our results also show that app firms capitalize on buzz by releasing app updates to boost user interest. Additionally, we show that app firms are responsive to competitive threats such as negative WOM, the emergence of new competitors, and competitors’ app updates. Using an app update as a response to competitive opportunities or threats leverages an ordinary process to improve or sustain temporary competitive advantage in a hypercompetitive market in which small advantages matter.

We find that younger app firms respond when the competitive positions of their apps are threatened, but older app firms are more likely to respond to competitors’ actions or changes in relative competitive position. Researchers have suggested that managers of less competitive firms are less aware of the competitive actions of their rivals (Chen et al., 2010). Our findings suggest that app firms with more experience are more responsive to what is happening in the competitive environment. It may behoove younger app firms to invest in processes that collect and analyze market data on the competition to improve and better defend their apps’ competitive positions. For example, Apptopia offers an analytics product that tracks competitors’ moves. Investing in such products or developing such competencies internally may help app firms better compete in hypercompetitive app markets.

## Limitations and Directions for Future Research

We examined how observable competitive indicators shape app update competitive actions on one popular app store. By focusing on apps of a similar nature offered by a single store, we were able to isolate the granular effects of the competitive indicators. However, this choice does limit the generalizability of our results. Research has shown that hedonic and utilitarian apps experience similar survival patterns (Lee & Raghu, 2014) but the type of app moderates the relationship between the ratings and downloads of paid versions of apps when a free version is offered (Liu et al., 2014). Similarly, it is possible that there are unique competitive dynamics in different categories of apps offered by the same app store. That is, there may be very granular, context-specific market (i.e., category) effects that future research could tease out.

Distinctive differences have been found between the two major app stores (Comino et al., 2016, 2019; Ghose & Han, 2014; Roma et al., 2016), which suggests that care should be taken in generalizing results across them. The app stores are run by different platform leaders that have different preferences and policies for managing them, which may affect app firms’ decision-making processes. For example, platform leaders can change store policy or the design of the store, which could impact the ability of app firms to respond quickly or could change the amount of store data that is visible or easily accessible.

Our study examines real-world behavior using archival data across a large sample of apps. Our approach allowed us to see how observable competitive indicators prompt app updating competitive actions and to observe which type of app update app firms employ for specific competitive opportunities or threats. Competitive dynamics studies are often quantitative and use archival data (Chen & Miller, 2012). However, this approach limits the nuance one can expect to find because the process is examined from the outside. Future studies could use mixed method approaches that could potentially capture app firm managers’ perceptions to complement empirical analyses of archival data, which could add robustness to explanations of competitive dynamics in the app market. Our study provides insight into which observable competitive indicators prompt app firms to use app updates as competitive actions. Although we provide initial insight into competition in the context of an app store, there is much more to be learned. Competitive dynamics research has much to offer and should be leveraged to better understand competition in hightechnology, hypercompetitive environments (e.g., Chen & Miller, 2012; D’Aveni et al., 2010).

## Conclusion

We examine how observable competitive indicators shape app update competitive actions. We provide evidence that app firms in the hypercompetitive game app market take app update competitive actions in response to competitive opportunities and threats. Specifically, we found that app firms are more likely to update their apps when their rank is increasing, customer ratings are decreasing, or rating volume is increasing. These findings demonstrate that app firms use app updates to both capitalize on opportunities (e.g., buzz) and to counteract threats (e.g., negative WOM) to their apps’ competitive positions. We also found evidence that app firms respond to competitors’ actions (e.g., competitors’ updates or new competitors) by releasing app updates. Moreover, app firms take app update competitive actions when their app’s rating volume increases, relative to competitors, and when they are competing across fewer game subcategories than their competitors. We provide insight into which observable competitive indicators prompt major versus minor update releases and show how app firms with different years of experience employ app update competitive actions. Our findings provide insight into the dynamics of competition in the app markets and enrich our understanding of how app firms vie for temporary advantage on app stores in the face of intense competition and rapid innovation.

## Extensiveness of the Competitors’ Updates

In our main model, we analyzed the influence of app updates released by an app’s competitors on its decision to release an app update. We found that as more of an app’s competitors released app updates, the likelihood that an app would release an app update increased. To examine this result more robustly, we separated the app update releases of an app’s competitors into two categories: major update and minor update. Specifically, we derive two variables, CompetitorMajorUpdate and CompetitorMinorUpdate, to measure the extensiveness of competitors app update releases. Software developers assign a numeric code to their software releases that indicates the extensiveness of the update. An app’s version number follows a dotted decimal format (e.g., 1.2.15), where a change in the first value of the version number indicates a major update.<sup>12</sup> Therefore, we defined an update as major when there is a change in the first section of a version number (e.g., 1.x.x to 2.x.x), and we consider all other changes to be minor updates (Comino et al., 2019). To construct the two update variables, we first determined if an update was major or not and set a dummy variable MajorUpdate if the first value in its version number changed with the app update release. We then computed CompetitorMajorUpdate<sub>ijt</sub> as the average number of major update releases among app i’s competitors at time t, and CompetitorMinorUpdate<sub>ijt</sub> to be the average number of minor update releases among app i’s competitors at time t. ## Review Sentiment and Review Volume

Another concern in interpreting our main results is that the ratings of the apps are generally positive, i.e., the mean of VersionRating is 4.171 out of 5 which is commonly observed in many online contexts (e.g., Chevalier & Mayzlin, 2006). To check the robustness of our results, we developed alternative measures to examine customer feedback. In our main model, the star rating and the star rating volume are used. We use these two variables in our main model because, if a customer leaves feedback, they must assign a star rating, whereas leaving written feedback is optional. However, the content of the written feedback and the amount of written feedback are also possible forms of customer feedback. Therefore, as a robustness check, we replaced the VersionRating and VersionRatingVolume variables in the model with review sentiment and review volume (i.e., VersionReviewSentiment and VersionReviewVolume). To construct these two alternative variables, we collected all the written reviews for each app during the data collection period. The VersionReviewSentiment score measures the cumulative sentiment of the reviews received for the current version of the app. We used the SentiStrength algorithm (Thelwall et al., 2012) to estimate the strength of positive and negative sentiment of each review, and then generate a score between -4 and +4 with -4 being the most negative and +4 the most positive. VersionReviewSentiment<sub>ijt</sub> is the sum of the scores for all the reviews of the current version of app i up to time t. VersionReviewVolume<sub>ijt</sub> is the natural log of the number of reviews of app i at time t. We reestimated all the models using these two variables and our main findings are consistent using these alternative measures (see results in Table C2).

## Competitor Definition

## Instrumental Variables

One potential concern about our main analysis is that an app’s Rank could be endogenous to the update decision. Although app firms cannot determine the rank of an app, it could be potentially correlated with unobserved app and/or app firm characteristics that together drive an app firm’s update decision. Our main model controlled for app and app firm heterogeneity and unobserved time-invariant effects at different levels. However, such endogeneity may still affect the estimated results. To handle the potential endogeneity issues, we constructed a set of instrumental variables (IVs) for Rank. We utilized the lagged terms of an app’s rank as a set of IVs for the current period rank. The intuition is that the lagged period rank should be correlated with the current period rank but should not be correlated with the current period’s unobserved shocks, which may affect the update release decision (Wang et al., 2018). Specifically, the more distant the lagged rank is, the more likely it is to be uncorrelated with the current period’s unobserved shock of an app such as promotional activities. However, it is less likely to be correlated with the current period’s rank at the same time. Since our model is a binary choice model, we adopted a control function-based estimation approach to incorporate IVs (Papke & Wooldridge, 2008). We tested three consecutive periods as IVs and the results are consistent with the majority of our findings. We report the results with these consecutive periods’ ranks as IVs in Table C4.

## Lagged Dependent Variable

One may argue that the app update competitive action may not be made daily and there could be a time lag between the observable market variables and an app firm’s app update. To test this potential delay in responding to the observed store data, we constructed a set of lagged dependent variables on the app update. Specifically, we developed a set of day-lagged dependent variables on Update up to a week after the observable market data became available. That is, we separately test the impact of our key variables on app update releases made two days, three days, four days, and so on, up to a week from the current time period. We report the results in Table C5. Our main findings are consistent with the majority of these lagged update models. Interestingly, many of the effects of the observable market variables disappear beyond a seven-day time interval,<sup>14</sup> especially the observable market variables of an app’s competitiors’ actions, which suggests that app update release decisions were made in a timely manner. App firms must respond quickly to market movements to be able to compete in this fast-paced market.

## Influence of Updates on Future Performance

Although our study focuses on the drivers of app update competitive actions, competitive dynamics describes a process in which the (1) observed market variables of past performance indicate threat or opportunity at time t-1, (2) which stimulates a competitive action (e.g., update) at time t, (3) which, if successful, results in improved future performance at time t+1. An update at time t can increase future performance (e.g., downloads or survival in the top charts) at time t+1 (Comino et al., 2019; Lee & Raghu, 2014). To show that this holds true in our data, we follow Comino et al. (2019) and demonstrated that an update at time t will improve future performance at time t+1. Specifically, we show that updates increase the number of active users in the second column of Table D1, which illustrates that updates can drive user engagement. We also show that updates increase app revenue in the third column of Table D1, which illustrates that updates can increase the financial performance of the app. We included our other variables (e.g., rating, rating volume) as controls for similarity to prior studies of app performance (Lee & Raghu, 2014; Yi et al., 2019). All the independent variables were lagged so that the results indicate that the more updates in the previous week (i.e., at time t) an app has, the better performance it will achieve in the following week (i.e., at time t+1).
