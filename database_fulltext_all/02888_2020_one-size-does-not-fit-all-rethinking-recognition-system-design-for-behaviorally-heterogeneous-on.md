## Abstract:

Online knowledge-sharing communities typically acknowledge their top contributors by implementing recognition systems. Extant recognition systems suffer from several limitations propose a framework based on socio-technical design principles for designing a multi-criterionand multi-segment-based recognition system that exploits multiple user characteristics for differential recognition according to community goals. We apply this framework on data gathered from Yelp.com and show how it can be used to recognize top members of different identified behavioral segments (amateurs, adepts, and enthusiasts) based on their performance on various relevant factors.

Keywords: Online communities, rewards and recognition, reputation systems, design framework, behavioral segmentation, Yelp.

## 1 INTRODUCTION

Internet-enabled knowledge-sharing is evolving from the conventional ways of hosting anonymous user contribution toward a more community-based sharing, where users maintain their social profiles and interact with other community members [1]. Different types of online communities have emerged, such as customer review communities (Amazon, Yelp, etc.), social Q&A forums (Quora, Yahoo Answers, etc.), etc. To survive and stay relevant, the natural inclination of these communities is to encourage higher contribution by their members (usually in terms of volume and quality) and to foster inter-member interaction and reciprocation [2,3]. To that end, community managers typically use the help of either one or both of the two, broad extrinsic motivating mechanisms – a recognition system such as ranks, badges, titles, etc., and a reward system such as offering discounts, gift vouchers, etc. [4,5]. In this study, we focus on the domain of recognition system, which is a critical aspect in the management of online knowledgesharing communities.

Recognition systems have gained prominence in recent years with many popular communities implementing them in various ways. For example, the online travel review site TripAdvisor uses a hierarchical recognition system of providing various levels of badges (starting from “Reviewer” to “Top Contributor”), whereas Yelp selectively rewards yearly “Elite” status to users based on different aspects of community contribution. In addition to being sources of external motivation for the contributors, recognition systems also act as signals of contributor expertise and credibility for other users, and in turn help in building trust on the platform [6]. Existing literature reports contradictory findings regarding the efficacy of online recognition systems as extrinsic motivators, with some studies showing a positive influence of such systems on user contribution [7–9] and community commitment [10], and some others reporting detrimental effects of the same [11,12]. Users contribute to online communities with an expectation of receiving recognition and appreciation for their efforts [13]. Research has shown that users’ motivation to participate increases when their contribution is duly valued [2,7]. Hence, it could be argued that poor design and ineffective management of recognition systems, resulting in an unfair selection of recognized members, may dissuade users to participate further. Thus, it is critical to tackle the unintended negative consequences and promote community contribution and trust on the platform by focusing on an effective design and implementation of recognition systems. The design of online community recognition systems largely refers to the mechanism of their operationalization in different communities. The most important aspect of this is the user selection criteria i.e., how certain members are selected as the recipients of community recognition. Community behavior of members may consist of several aspects, only some of positivity, involvement, experience, reputation, competence, sociability, etc., are some of the relevant reviewer characteristics in the context of online reviews [14]. An effective recognition system should ideally focus on fostering frequent and high-quality content contribution from the community members, and also on increasing the level of community commitment and camaraderie among the members [6]. However, in practice, online communities seldom design their recognition systems by considering the different aspects of contribution behavior. Some platforms encourage only a higher volume of contribution, while some focus only on the quality of the contribution, thus compromising on the overall value that can be derived by an effective recognition system. Recognition systems that are designed using only one parameter can be referred to as single-criterion recognition systems. For example, Mozilla Support Forum, Microsoft TechNet Forum, Discogs, etc. rank their contributors based on their quantity of contributions. TripAdvisor, on the other hand, has separate badges for high-volume contributors and high-quality contributors, which means that they value both quantity and quality, albeit rather disparately and can be considered as an example of using multiple single-criterion recognition systems. Other critical success factors of online platforms such as social interaction among the users [15], individuals’ community commitment, [16,17], etc. are typically not taken into consideration when developing a recognition system. Thus, by using single-criterion recognition systems, a community discourages diversity of behavioral patterns and biases its members toward acting out only a particular kind of behavior. This may lead to demotivation and eventually voluntary exit of those community members who are valuable contributors in some other aspects but fall behind in one that leads to recognition in the community. Hence, if online review communities can implement a multi-criterion recognition system for not only simultaneously promoting frequent and useful content but also facilitating social exchange and community commitment, it will be beneficial for community members. A good example of this is Yelp, which uses multiple parameters simultaneously for recognizing some members as Elite. However, it is nontransparent and is perceived to be subjectively implemented by community managers, without a systematic mechanism.

It may be noted that both single-criterion and multi-criterion recognition systems suffer from one common flaw – they use a one-size-fits-all approach by treating the entire community as a homogeneous population. Users in virtual communities differ in their behavioral patterns, e.g., some focus on frequent content generation, some concentrate on the quality of content, some engage more socially and appreciate others, some try to balance two or more aspects, etc. Generally, it can be expected that users will not fully rate high on one criterion and low on others, but rather exhibit certain combinations of these behaviors. Recognition systems should ideally be able to differentiate among various dominant user behaviors and customize the rewards and recognitions accordingly. The multiple single-criterion system addresses this issue to a certain extent by providing different recognitions for different behaviors. However, it fails to appreciate the interplay and combinations among various behavioral parameters. For instance, it would recognize a member who rates high on quantity but low on all other factors, over those who are medium-high on all aspects including quantity. Another issue is that community members may be operating at different points of the learning curve. For instance, a new member of a community might not be able to match the level of contribution of a veteran. An approach to address these issues would be to segment the entire community into meaningful categories based on multiple relevant parameters and then distribute the recognitions according to the relative importance of various behavioral segments. A simple example could be to segment the community based on one parameter such as experience (say amateurs, pros, and veterans) and using a weighted average of multiple contribution factors to rank and recognize top contributors in each segment. A more intricate system may use multiple parameters for segmentation based on the requirements of the community. Such a multi-criterion segment-based recognition system (MSR system) will provide community managers an improved and comprehensive way to various combinations of community behavior based on community goals. While it might have been difficult to implement such a complex system a few years ago, the advent of contemporary data analytics and machine learning tools and techniques has greatly simplified the process. Time is now ripe to evolve from a simplistic recognition system to a more intricate, generic, and flexible MSR system. Extending the arguments presented, it follows that virtual communities can benefit from a systematic way of designing an MSR system, which can use state-of-the-art machine learning techniques to semiautomate and enrich the process. However, existing literature does not suggest any objective approach for designing and managing such recognition systems, which propels us to take up the following research objectives: Research objective I: To propose a general guiding framework for design and management of a multi-criterion segment-based system for online communities. Research objective II: To demonstrate the applicability of the proposed framework by designing a recognition system for a real-life online review community. We address the need for developing a decision support framework for community managers, which provides them the flexibility to include various user parameters and behavioral segments and to choose from multiple high-level recognition-distribution strategies in alignment with the needs of the community. Accordingly, we propose a five-step MSR system framework, based on

socio-technical design principles and data analytics applications, which can be adopted by community managers. We demonstrate the applicability of our framework on Yelp.com, a popular review website having 77 million average monthly unique desktop visitors (in Q4 2017)<sup>1</sup>. Essentially, our design proposes the following high-level steps for a community manager:

(i) identify relevant behavioral variables for community members

(ii) extract a few independent core factors

(iii) use machine learning classifiers to gauge the influence of each core factor on the recognition status, and assign factor weights

(iv) use clustering techniques to segment the community and assign segment weights

(v) use factor weights to rank members in each segment and then use segment weights to select a specific number of top-ranked members from each segment for recognition Our proposed approach can be used to design a recognition system from scratch or upgrade existing recognition systems in online communities. It should be noted that the main purpose of our proposed approach is to provide community managers the flexibility and control to systematically distribute recognition across different behavioral segments, that can help in motivating those users who exhibit better behavioral outcomes, in alignment with the overall objectives of the community. Through the example of Yelp, we demonstrate the implementation of the proposed MSR system and empirically show that it is more effective than Yelp’s existing recognition system in recognizing reviewers who display better behavioral outcomes. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first attempt to provide a systematic guiding framework to design and manage an MSR system, which enriches the body of literature pertaining to online communities. This is also the first attempt to use contributor segments based on behavioral clusters for community management. Although we demonstrate our framework in the context of an online reviewer community, Information Systems researchers can extend the generic framework with minor modifications to study other virtual platforms such as social networking sites, Q&A forums, etc. For practitioners, especially online community managers, this paper is a useful reference to assess and improve their existing recognition system or develop a recognition system from scratch.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: Section 2 explores the background literature pertaining to the area. Section 3 provides the theoretical background for this research. Section 4 presents and explicates the five-step framework for designing an MSR system. Section 5 demonstrates the application of the guidelines in the context of Yelp. Section 6 discusses the academic and managerial implications, limitations of the study, and future research directions, and section 7 concludes the paper.

## 2 PRIOR WORK

## 2.1 Influence of recognition systems on community behavior

Extant literature on online community recognition systems has primarily focused on evaluating the efficacy of online recognition systems on community behavior [5,8,9,11,18]. Studies have found that in most cases such systems are useful for driving sustained content contribution, higher quality content, and increased user engagement and com ment [5,8,9,18]. However, some studies have also reported unintended negative consequences of recognition systems where badges, ranks, etc. undermine individuals’ intrinsic motivation and threaten their sense of autonomy, leading to a decreased level of community participation [11,12,19]. Prior studies have shown that the design of recognition systems can influence online community members’ contribution behavior and have also cautioned against poor design and management of recognition systems, which may lead to unforeseen negative consequences [6]. Past research has shown that recognition system designs that fail to consider individuals’ innate values and psychological needs may lead to diminished user engagement [19]. As an overarching guideline, past researchers have advocated system designers and community managers to consider various user behaviors and motivational factors that are most appropriate to the community while developing and managing community recognition systems [6,20]. However, there is a paucity of studies, which provide any systematic framework for supporting the community managers to recognize and encourage different aspects of user behavior in accordance with the objectives of their community.

## 2.2 Design of recognition systems

Extant literature has looked into the design of recognition mechanisms primarily as elements of gamification systems. Badges, levels, leaderboards, etc. are common gamification features that are used as recognition mechanisms to incentivize user engagement in an online environment. Thus, literature on design of badges, general design principles of gamified information systems, and frameworks for developing gamified software serves as the most closely related body of knowledge to the design of online recognition systems [21–28]. Studies on the design of social status and badges in online communities have considered how to award badges to maximize user contribution. Some have used analytical approaches to design badges, to motivate users into generating the desired level of contribution quantity [28] and quality [27] in online knowledge-sharing forums. Another study has designed an optimal badge mechanism by judiciously mixing two schemes: awarding the same badge to many users, and awarding unique badges to most users, to increase total contributions on a website [26]. However, these designs do not look into the criteria of selecting a subset of users for recognition. Another approach used by researchers is to provide some high-level guidelines for designing gamified recognition systems. Some have based their guidelines on theoretical design principles with the objective of maximizing user engagement [23]. Others have based it on their study of mechanisms for giving rewards and recognitions in existing systems and their impact on quantity and quality of contribution outcomes [21]. Even focusgroup interviews and surveys have been used to identify the best design features [25]. However, these studies do not actually demonstrate how to implement their suggestions, especially in the context of online communities. Yet another theme in the existing literature is related to developing software for recognition systems. One of the papers has listed the steps of development for building gamified software [22], whereas another one has offered a flexible framework for gamification design in crowdsourcing communities [24]. However, these studies have dealt with the broader scope of gamification and encompass learning platforms, online communities, crowdsourcing platforms, etc. The design of recognition systems for online communities has not gained exclusive attention in the existing body of literature.

Another body of literature includes patented works on online recognition systems. The filed patents have proposed designs for a system to score online community members based on their activities, sort members based on their scores, and display them on an online interface built for community managers [29,30]. However, they have not proposed any detailed methods for calculating the scores, except mentioning that it would be based on users’ plurality of activities. This indicated a multi-criterion system that will consider the entire population and not segmented behavior. Also, they do not propose a way for community managers to alter the scoring mechanism, which can make it easier for some users to game any theoretical support from established literature. Hence, there is a need to suggest a flexible and controllable mechanism to score and rank community members while keeping the behavioral diversity of a community in mind and justify it with theoretical arguments and design principles.

Extant literature has always emphasized the need for duly acknowledging the different strengths of online community members. Research on online communities shows that participants differ in their community behavior and the overall success of an online community depends on how effectively different types of desirable community behavior are being encouraged. While some members can be new to the platform with less experience in content sharing and socializing, some members can be experienced and regular participants, and most of the members display different combinations of various behavioral traits [31,32]. It is imperative for community managers to customize their recognition systems, which will accurately reflect community members’ strengths and weaknesses [33,34]. However, traditional recognition system designs use an undifferentiated approach while recognizing members; the users who are deemed worthwhile for recognition are generally selected from the entire population of community members. While this approach is simpler to implement, it maximum value from users’ community behavior. To the best of our knowledge, no prior academic work has employed a systematic segmentation of the user base to identify and exploit behavioral clusters in the design of recognition systems.

## 3 THEORETICAL BACKGROUND

## 3.1 Socio-technical principles of system design

The theoretical foundation of our paper is based on the socio-technical principles of system design. The underlying philosophy of socio-technical system design is that system design should capture the interaction of human factors (social or organizational) and technical factors because both kinds of factors influence the usage and functionality of any information system. The sociotechnical approach of system design leads to outcomes in alignment with organizational goals, better control over the system, and adaptability of the system. Recognition systems in online communities are also socio-technical systems, which emerge because of the interaction of technical factors, such as the online platform, feedback mechanism, algorithms, etc., with social factors such as human actors (community managers, community members), their psychology, and behavior. Hence, we develop the framework for the design of the recognition system based on the principles of the socio-technical system design proposed by Clegg, an extension to Chern’s seminal work [35,36]. We categorize Clegg’s system design principles into three broad design principles: design allows flexibility, design allows managerial control over the system, and design allows behavioral diversity.

Design allows flexibility: This broad principle encompasses three principles of Clegg’s socio technical design. The first one “design is a systemic process” suggests that all aspects (social and technical) of a socio-technical system are interdependent. The other two principles, which say “design is an extended social process” and “design is socially shaped” acknowledge that system design continues beyond the implementation phase and is shaped by its stakeholders throughout its lifetime making it a dynamic entity. The interdependence of social and technical aspects and the dynamic nature of a socio-technical system imply that any change in the social aspect calls for changes in technical aspects as well. Hence, system design should allow flexibility to accommodate amendments of any aspect emerging from the evolving nature of the system. Recognition systems, which use a fixed criterion (single criterion or fixed combination of multiple criteria) for recognizing users, may not be suitable when community goals continue to change. For example, for an online community in its growth phase, the quantity of contribution seems to be the most important factor for the community. However, as the community matures it may need to remove redundant content and encourage only useful contribution, shifting the community goal from higher quantity to better quality. Recognition systems should be flexible enough to change their selection criteria according to the evolving goals of the community. Design allows managerial control over the system: This principle is based on the socio-technical premises “design involves choices” and “design choices are contingent”. System design should not be restrictive, should allow users to use their discretion and follow any path in response to a specific situation. This implies greater user control (managerial control in our context) over the system. Existing recognition systems, which are completely automated, do not provide any scope for managerial discretion while recognizing community members. A fully automated recognition system based on objective criteria may be easily gamed by members. Allowing managers to control some aspect of the system keeps it adaptive across circumstances and immune to attempts of gaming the system.

Design allows behavioral diversity: The core of socio-technical design is built on human values and the underlying psychology of the stakeholders. System design should take into account the varying mindset and behavior of people interacting with the system and cater to their needs accordingly. This principle subsumes two socio-technical design principles: “human values and mindsets are central to design” and “design should reflect the needs of the business, its users and managers”. Hence, recognition systems should acknowledge various types of desirable community behavior and encourage behavioral diversity. However, existing recognition systems that recognize users based on a single criterion do not encourage behavioral diversity. Even the recognition systems that use multiple criteria treat the entire user base as behaviorally homogeneous, thereby treating experienced and novice members as equivalent. This biases the system against novices and leads to demotivation of several new and diligent members. Creating meaningful behavioral segments and segment-wise recognition of members can help encourage all types of behavioral traits, without biasing the recognition system against any particular group.

## 3.2 Segmentation

Segmentation has been a key concept in management literature, especially in marketing for categorizing heterogeneous consumers into different homogeneous subgroups, based on some shared characteristics. These subgroups respond differently to different product and service offerings and improve the efficiency of marketing [37,38]. The segmentation and profiling of users of information systems are expected to determine a few meaningful segments and help in revealing the dominant characteristics, behaviors, strengths, weaknesses, etc. of each category, which can lead to better strategies in managing the large user population.

The principle and approach of customer segmentation has been adopted in the information systems domain for various user bases, such as users of mobile internet, social media, telecom services, etc. [39–41]. Past literature suggests that end-user segmentation adds significant value in creating user-centric system design and promoting better management of system usage [42]. Behavioral profiling i.e., categorizing consumers or users based on their observed behavior is one of the popular approaches of segmentation and at times considered to be superior to other segmentation methods, such as demographic and psychographic profiling [43]. Especially, in the case of information systems adoption and usage, a number of studies have used behavioral segmentation. Some evidences of prior studies carrying out behavioral profiling of system users include clustering website users based on their browsing behavior [44] and online search patterns [45], profiling of mobile Internet adopters based on their usage and attitude [39], etc. The majority of the profiling work has been done with the objective of better customer relationship management and target marketing, e.g., recognizing passive users and incentivizing them based on their segment.

In this paper, we carry out the behavioral segmentation of online community members as a crucial step of recognition system design, so that community managers can identify key user segments, and devise their recognition strategies to maximize the value derived from such a system.

## 4 A FIVE-STEP DESIGN FRAMEWORK

The proposed five-step design framework provides generalized guidelines for community managers to design an MSR system based on community goals. The framework enables community managers to make use of the accessible user data pertaining to their community behavior to systematically select deserving members for recognizing them. Figure 1 shows the basic steps of the framework.

The framework adheres to the principles of socio-technical system design as discussed earlier. On the basis of the principle of flexibility, the MSR system allows iterative reshaping of the

recognition mechanism based on the changing needs of the community. This overcomes the inflexibility posed by existing recognition systems, which use a fixed criterion for recognizing users. Also, behavioral segments are allowed to be formed and changed because of the evolving community behavior. Based on the principle of managerial control, the MSR system allows community managers to select user variables, factor weights, number of segments, and segment weights, all of which are key parameters that can be used to shape the entire recognition system. Being semi-automated, the MSR system provides better managerial control than recognition systems that are fully automated. Finally, the principle of behavioral diversity is adhered to by allowing the MSR system to use multiple user behavior-related variables as inputs. Also, diverse combinations of behavior are accounted for by incorporating the feature of allowing behavioral segmentation of the community. This successfully addresses the biases (e.g., against amateur community members) introduced by existing recognition systems that treat the user base as homogeneous. In the following subsections, we elaborate on the individual steps of the proposed framework:

## 4.1 Step 1: Community goal identification and variable selection

As the first step, from the available metadata on community members, community managers select the attributes, which they consider critical for the sustainability of the platform. The selection of the factors is determined by the goal of the community, availability of data, and logical and theoretical backing.

According to past research, the number of posts or messages shared by members is an important determinant of an online community’s success [46–48]. Also, the quality of posting is a vital aspect of content contribution in virtual communities [46–48] and often a small percentage of users are responsible for generating high-quality content. Thus, recognition systems should be designed to encourage users to share content that is not only high in volume but is also of high quality.

Studies have shown that social interaction, especially reciprocity, which includes answering queries posted on the forum, carrying a discussion thread forward, complimenting fellow members, etc., increases users’ knowledge sharing intention in online platforms [18,20,49]. Social interaction helps in building trust among the members which leads them to share reliable knowledge [50]. Many websites facilitate social networking among users by allowing them to befriend or follow fellow community members, initiating and contributing to discussion threads and granting them some provision to thank and compliment fellow members. Hence, recognition systems may incorporate some metrics related to social networking behavior. Also, online communities sustain themselves by maintaining their user base and attracting new members [51]. Individuals’ commitment and loyalty toward the community can be measured in terms of the number of years of their participation in the platform. Users indicating allegiance to an online community for a longer period of time should be recognized to encourage community commitment and loyalty. Important user characteristics such as contribution quantity, quality, social behavior, and community commitment should be kept in mind while designing online recognition systems. Community managers may consider additional factors, which they feel are important for the community. Traditionally, community managers recognize behavior that is easily measurable such as quantity of contribution and ignore other important factors, such as quality, reciprocity, commitment, etc. because of the lack of suitable metrics. This leads to suboptimal outcomes of recognition systems. Communities are recommended to employ suitable proxy measures for all of the important user characteristics and collect data on the same.

## 4.2 Step 2: Identification of core factors

After selecting all critical user characteristics, community managers should extract and identify core factors from all selected variables. The primary aim of this step is to reduce a large number of variables to a manageable number of behavioral dimensions, on which the users are distinct. The reduction of dimensions is also important for eliminating statistical dependence between factors, and better interpretability of multivariate data without significant loss of information

[52]. Several dimensionality reduction techniques can be used, including linear techniques such as principal component analysis (PCA) and singular value decomposition, and nonlinear techniques such as Kernel PCA [53].

PCA is one of the most popular dimension reduction techniques. It uses an orthogonal transformation to convert possibly correlated variables into a set of linearly uncorrelated variables that are referred to as principal components. Each principal component is calculated as a linear combination of the variables and ordered in such a way that the first component accounts for the largest possible variance, followed by the second, and so on. So one can select the first few components if they explain most of the variance and drop the rest, thus reducing dimensions to a great extent. PCA should be used when one wants to reduce the number of variables but is not able to identify specific variables to remove, wants to ensure all variables are independent of one another, and is comfortable in making the variables less interpretable as compared to the original variables [53]. There exists a trade-off for the community managers to think about. On one hand, reducing a large number of variables into a few factors makes it easier for community managers to operationalize the system. On the other hand, dimension reduction may convert directly measured variables into abstract dimensions that can be difficult to interpret and control.

## 4.3 Step 3: Assignment of factor weights

Following the identification of the core user dimensions, community managers should assign weights to each of the factors, which reflect their relative importance in the community. For example, for an online Q&A forum, reciprocity in the form of answering questions may be more important than building a network of friends. This step allows community managers to have discretionary control over a differential preference of factors.

Community managers who consider multiple criteria while recognizing contributors, intuitively allocate disparate weights to different criteria while making a decision, leading to inconsistencies in outcomes and limiting the reliability of the system. This calls for a more objective and standardized approach of decision making, which can be achieved by explicitly identifying the

factor weights from community managers’ subjective judgments and systematically incorporating them into the design of the recognition system.

One such approach of explicitly identifying the factor weights can start with the community managers randomly selecting a subset of the user population and using their subjective judgments to recognize some of them, inherently allocating various factor weights. Next, classification models can be used to predict the recognition status of those members using important user dimensions as predictor variables. The results from the classification models will provide two important insights. First, to what extent the factors identified from user data match the implicit criteria that are taken into account by community managers subconsciously while recognizing users. Second, what is the relative importance that is implicitly given to each parameter?

The overall accuracy of the classification models will be a reliable measure to verify if the identified factors are sufficient to determine one’s recognition status. A very low value of accuracy will mean some influential factors on which the subjective judgment is based on are not identified. Hence, further probing and capturing of more factors will be needed. A high predictive accuracy will suggest a fairly exhaustive set of factors, which will be considered sufficient to proceed with the design. Also, the relative importance of the predictor variables in the classification model objectively indicates the factor weights. Community managers may now calibrate these weights if they feel some critical factor is given significantly lower importance and vice-versa. The factor weights identified in this step will be used when distributing the recognition status.

## 4.4 Step 4: Segmentation and assignment of segment weights

In the fourth step, we propose to categorize the user population into meaningful behavioral importance of each of these segments. Community users differ from each other in various aspects, such as their contribution pattern, sociability, involvement, etc., and assume specific social roles in the community [54]. Hence, instead of identifying a few influential users from the entire user pool, we propose that considering users from each segment will duly acknowledge the different kinds of social roles in the community. This step follows the principle of allowing

behavioral diversity in system design. Standard cluster analysis techniques such as k-means clustering or hierarchical clustering mechanisms can be used to operationalize this step. Different strategies can be used to assign segment weights to the behavioral segments. For instance, some community managers may choose to ignore a weak segment completely, whereas others may consider assigning equal or differential weights to each segment. Our recommendation strategy is to include all segments and assign segment weights based on their relative importance. This is crucial as overlooking any segment may lead to the undesirable case of demotivation among its deserving members and eventual decline in their intention to participate and contribute. Even some of the top members from the segments lagging in most of the factors can be recognized to keep their spirits up and encourage them to actively participate in the future.

Our proposed method of calculating segment weights uses all segments and their constituent factors. In case of n factors and N segments, for each factor $f ( \mathrm { f r o m } 1 \mathrm { t o } n )$ , an intersegment relative performance value (r) is assigned to each segment, such that $r f { = } 1$ for the segment with the lowest mean value of the factor f, and $\scriptstyle { \gamma f = N }$ for the segment with the highest value. For each segment, we calculate the segment weight as the summation of the product of weights of factors $( w f , f { = } I \ \mathrm { t o } \ n )$ and respective intersegment relative performance $( r f , f { = } I \tan n )$ of all factors in the segment in comparison to all segments.

$$
\text { Segment   Weight } = w 1 * r 1 + w 2 * r 2 + w 3 * r 3 + \dots + w n * r n \tag {Equation1}
$$

This calculation of segment weight implies that the segments, which perform relatively better in terms of the highly important factors, will get higher weights.

This helps to identify the high performers from each segment. Finally, community managers will select a specific number (based on the segment weights) of high-ranked members from each of the segments, and provide them community recognition.

The five-stage framework proposed above can provide objective guidelines to develop an MSR system from scratch or to evaluate and improve an existing recognition system. The framework aims to provide the flexibility to duly recognize members with diverse attributes from multiple deserving contributors. ## 5 APPLICATION OF THE FRAMEWORK

Yelp hosts millions of reviews for local businesses in several metro cities across multiple countries written by thousands of reviewers. At the same time, it maintains an online community of reviewers who not only share reviews but also constitute an active social network. It provides community recognition to selected reviewers every year by conferring them with the Elite title based on their contribution and community behavior. Users can nominate themselves or others for receiving the Elite badge, out of which the “Elite Squad Council” and community managers select the deserving ones<sup>2</sup>. Though Yelp does not share its specific criteria of recognizing reviewers, the community managers use their judgment on multiple factors for taking their decisions. This makes Yelp a suitable candidate to demonstrate the effectiveness of our MSR

framework. An illustration of a typical reviewer profile in Yelp.com with various reviewer characteristics is shown in Appendix 1.

A dataset, which was made publicly available by Yelp.com for a research competition was used for our analysis<sup>3</sup>. It consisted of 2.2 million reviews of more than 77,000 local businesses, such as restaurants, bars, salons, home services, auto services, etc., in various cities of the USA, the UK, Canada, and Germany. Data on various attributes of more than 525,000 reviewers, who were part of Yelp, were also published. For our analysis, we considered the reviewer dataset. We carried out preliminary data cleaning, where we removed the outliers and records with missing values. Furthermore, we retained only those reviewer records, which had at least one follower, one friend, and one year of experience in the platform resulting in 55,690 valid records. It was a cross-sectional dataset that contained information about the reviewers aggregated over 12 years (2004 – 2015). We used these filtered data to gain insights into Yelp’s existing recognition system and to demonstrate how to design and implement an MSR system for their community.

## 5.1 Applying Step 1: Variable selection

Our first task was to select the important variables pertaining to reviewer characteristics, which were instrumental in determining their recognition status. The factors from the available fields in the dataset were identified with support from the extant literature.

Previous studies have identified the number of reviews as an important factor to predict review helpfulness [55,56]. This is an important reviewer characteristic. The total number of reviews written depicts a reviewer’s involvement with the platform, and varies among reviewers based on their own motivation. Hence, we choose the aggregate number of reviews written by a reviewer as a factor of the profiling exercise. We also consider the total votes received by a reviewer for the contributed reviews as an important factor because it signals the overall impact of the contribution. Reviewer experience on the platform is another important trait to be taken into account. Individuals may develop a psychological bond with a community after spending

substantial time on it. Hence, the number of years on an online community might influence a reviewer’s commitment to the community. Also, with many years of experience on a platform, reviewers tend to become more skilled and informed [14]. The average number of helpful votes a reviewer received per review is a good indicator of her competence or effectiveness in writing reviews and is also considered to be an important trait [57]. The number of friends and followers, which are measures of sociability and popularity, are also important attributes of a reviewer. ## 5.3 Applying Step 3: Assignment of factor weights

In this step, we verify the importance of the core reviewer factors and identify their weights. As Yelp already had a recognition system in place, it was possible for us to use the existing recognition status of reviewers to ascertain relative weights of the previously identified factors by fitting classification models and predicting reviewers’ Elite status using various factors as predictor variables. The dataset used for our analyses took into account all reviews contributed over the years by both Elite and non-Elite reviewers. The four factors identified in the previous steps (reviewer involvement, contribution quality, sociability, and experience) were based on the reviewer data aggregated over all reviews contributed over the years. The four factors are used as possible predictors of Elite and non-Elite reviewers.

We checked the predictive capability of these factors using a number of classification models. A high value of overall accuracy in predicting Elite status using these four factors would prove them to be significant determinants of the community status. Also, the importance of the predictor would provide insights about the relative value of the factors in the current recognition system. Using IBM SPSS Modeler, we built six predictive models using the Bayesian network, neural network, logistic regression, C5 decision tree, CART, and CHAID decision tree. The training and testing data were split in the ratio of 70:30. With a misclassification cost of 1.0 for both false positive and false negative prediction, we obtained a very high (> 90%) value of overall accuracy. Also, with adjusted misclassification cost to increase sensitivity or true positive rate of the predictive models, we used three decision tree models (C5, CART, and CHAID). The overall prediction accuracy rate was around 85%, which still highlighted the high predictive power of the factors. Also, the true positive (TP) rate or sensitivity of the models was improved with C5 and CHAID showing a TP rate of 73%. This implied that the models were being able to correctly classify 73% of actual Elites as Elites. Table 4 shows the output measures of the predictiv models, from which we can infer that our selected core factors can predict the outcome of Yelp’s existing recognition system with high accuracy.

Furthermore, the graph showing the importance of predictors in Figure 3 reveals that involvement is the most important predictor (62%) and experience (2%) is the least important. We considered the relative importance of the factors for the allocation of weights to factors in the MSR system. From Figure 3, we obtained the weights as: w1=0.62, w2=0.24, w3=0.12, and w4=0.02 for the four factors: involvement, sociability, contribution quality, and experience, respectively.

## 5.4 Applying Step 4: Segmentation and assignment of segment weights

In the fourth step of our design, we used clustering techniques to find out meaningful behavioral segments from the reviewer population. To select the appropriate number of clusters we compared two different clustering methods. First, we used the k-means cluster analysis using SPSS Modeler on the dataset by varying the cluster number from 2 to 7 to identify the most appropriate number of clusters. The number of clusters was determined using the silhouette method, which was a popular technique for selecting the optimum number of clusters based on the comparison of cohesion and separation of the data points in a cluster [59]. After comparing the different number of clusters in terms of the cluster quality and the ratio between the number of members in the largest and the smallest cluster, we found the most appropriate number of

clusters to be three. For k=3, the average silhouette width (ASW) was 0.432. As cluster quality is a subjective measure, the admissible value of ASW depended on the clustering context. But ASW > 0.25 was an acceptable value of cluster quality for naturally occurring observations. Hence, k=3 was considered to be an appropriate number of clusters [60].

Once we identified the appropriate number of clusters, we used a two-step cluster analysis method with the cluster number specified as 3 for our segmentation exercise. Two-step clustering is suitable for large datasets with natural groupings of observations, which would not be apparent otherwise [61]. This clustering algorithm proceeded in two steps: In the first step, the original dataset was divided into preclusters, which were then used in the second step as inputs to hierarchical clustering. The ASW for the two-step clustering output was 0.4. The exact distribution of 55690 records among the three clusters on executing the algorithm was found to be cluster 1 (24709), cluster 2 (13757), and cluster 3 (17224). The ratio between the size of the largest and the smallest cluster is 1.80, which was also considered to be acceptable. Furthermore, we analyzed the factors, which helped in differentiating the segments using the cluster profiles generated by the tool. Table 5 shows the descriptive statistics of the centroids of the three identified clusters used in the MSR system.

After comparing and analyzing the mean values of each of the key factors used for clustering, the three segments could be classified as follows:

Cluster 1 comprised reviewers who were low on all factors: involvement, sociability, experience, and contribution quality. This implied that they not only demonstrated a lesser overall contribution to the community but were also lowest in terms of quality of reviews and had the smallest social network compared to the other segments. They also had lesser experience in the reviewer community as compared to others. Most of the reviewer population (44.4%) belonged to this segment. Given their lack of experience and contribution quality, this segment of reviewers could be referred to as the amateurs.

Cluster 2 consisted of reviewers displaying the highest level of involvement and sociability (number of friends). Also, they were experienced (although a bit less than the third cluster). But despite being extremely involved with the community, they were perceived to produce a moderate quality of reviews based on votes received per review. A total of 24.7% of reviewers belonged to this segment. Given that their overall distinguishing feature was enthusiastic community involvement, this segment could be named as the enthusiasts. Cluster 3 consisted of 30.9% of the total number of observations, and included the reviewers who were the most experienced as well as the most effective ones. They got the highest number of average helpful votes per review. However, they showed a significantly lower level of involvement and number of friends compared to the enthusiasts. Expertise in writing good quality reviews being their forte, this segment of reviewers could be named as the adepts. From the reviewer segments discovered, it was evident that the reviewers showing the highest involvement and sociability constituted a separate segment than the ones producing the highest quality of reviews. As we have seen in the previous section, the existing system highly recognized involvement and sociability. This undervalued contribution quality unintentionally and led to favoring one segment (enthusiasts) over the other (adepts). Based on Yelp data from the year 2004 to 2015, it was found that 5448 reviewers were conferred the Elite title for at least once during their active years on the platform. Out of which, 1041 (19.1%) Elite titles were given to the amateurs, 3570 (65.5%) Elite reviewers were from the enthusiasts and only 837 (15.4%) Elites belonged to the adepts segment. The distribution of the title clearly depicted an unintentional bias against the adepts, which might demotivate the high-quality contributors. The negative externalities became more severe in this case as the adepts constituted a large percentage of the total reviewer population. For a reviewer community, it is important to acknowledge the quality of contribution besides reviewers’ contribution quantity and social networking skills. Also, the top reviewers among the amateurs who were relatively new to the platform would

require some recognition in the initial years of their participation to be motivated to contribute further.

For the MSR system, we calculated the segment weights based on the summation of the product of factor weights and the intersegment relative performance of factors. Earlier, we found that the enthusiasts were leading in terms of involvement and sociability, the adepts were leading in terms of contribution quality and experience, and the amateurs were lagging in all four factors. For the factor involvement, the enthusiasts were assigned the highest value of intersegment relative performance contribution quality, the adepts were assigned the highest score (r=3), followed by the enthusiasts (r=2), and then by the amateurs (r=1). Similarly, the relative performance of segments for the other 2 factors was determined. Then, a segment weight for each segment was calculated by multiplying the relative performance values with factor weights. The calculation of weights for all three segments is shown in Table 6.

On the basis of the calculations, segment weights of 1, 2.86 and 2.14 were assigned to the amateurs, the enthusiasts, and the adepts, respectively. The Yelp dataset showed that 5448 reviewers were recognized as Elites, at least once during their active years on the platform. We determined how the MSR system would assign 5448 titles using the segment weights. According to our reassignment in the ratio of 1:2.86:2.14, recognition should be bestowed to 908 top reviewers from the amateurs, 2597 from the enthusiasts, and 1943 from the adepts. The MSR system would recommend the percentage distribution among these three segments to change from 19.1%, 65.5%, and 15.4% (as given by Yelp) to 16.7%, 47.7%, and 35.7%, respectively. It could be observed that adepts would now get a higher share of recognition, as deserved by them for their better quality of reviews and their loyalty to the community.

## 6 DISCUSSION – IMPLICATIONS AND LIMITATIONS

This study proposes a generalized five-stage guiding framework to design and manage MSR systems for online communities and demonstrates how it can be implemented by community managers using real data from Yelp. While our system empirically showed significantly better results in terms of selecting candidates for recognition in Yelp, it is not our intention to claim that our proposed approach is superior to the existing systems at Yelp. This is because of the fact that any assessment (either quantitative using a baseline, or qualitative) has to be completely context-dependent based on specific community needs. For instance, if a community wants to focus only on increasing the volume of content even at the cost of quality, then it would be better for the community to opt for a single-criterion recognition system with recognition for top contributors ranked by volume. Our goal is to propose a recognition system, which addresses some of the issues with the existing systems (like nonconsideration of behavioral segments and diversity, inflexibility, lack of managerial control, etc.), but it comes with its own limitations as well in terms of complexity of implementation, difficulty to fully automate, etc. Hence, we cannot state that our proposed approach is superior to the existing system in every way, and can only proclaim that it is an alternate approach for the community managers who feel limited by the existing approaches to meet the exact needs of their community. However, our approach has one clear advantage over the existing approach in terms of being a generic “superset” of all approaches. It can be easily customized to behave like others (whereas vice versa is not possible) based on setting certain parameters like number of segments, factors, and recognition categories to 1. Table 8 presents and compares the parameters, functionality, descriptive analogy (for better understanding), advantages, and limitations for various approaches that can be used to bestow recognition in online communities.

This study offers several theoretical and practical implications. First, though existing literature stresses the importance of a well-designed recognition system for the success of online platforms, to the best of our knowledge, ours is the first study to provide systematic guidelines to design and assess a multi-criterion recognition system. While previous works stop at identifying the critical success factors for online communities, this study actually incorporates the factors in an effective recognition system design, thereby enriching the body of knowledge on online communities. Second, we employ machine learning techniques at various stages of our system design to generate insights about user behavior. This contributes to IS literature by exhibiting novel avenues of exploiting data mining techniques and contributing to the nascent domain of behavioral data mining.

Third, the segmentation step itself generates valuable academic insights. This is an attempt to profile online reviewers and identify their key social roles. We broadly distinguish online reviewers as the amateurs (new and less committed in the community), enthusiasts (highly involved and social reviewers), and adepts (experienced reviewers with high contribution quality). While it is probable that the exact segmentation may not hold true for other online communities, a general overarching 3-segment pattern can be expected where some users will focus on quantity, some on quality, and some on neither. Hence, the segmentation exercise, which is carried out as a part of demonstrating our proposed framework, can be adopted for other online platforms as a standalone approach to profile members and generate interesting insights about

their behavioral patterns. Future research can look deeper into this proposition and can form theoretical models to explain such differentiated behavior in social contexts.

Fourth, this study directly benefits managers and system designers of online communities. Community managers may apply our proposed framework to develop a multi-criterion recognition system from scratch or revamp their existing recognition system. While in practice most of the existing recognition systems are based either on a single objective criterion or on subjective judgment of community managers, our model can serve as a reference for designing a sophisticated recognition system encompassing numerous factors and segments. The findings of our research are also important for gamification systems that use automated algorithms to recognize a player’s superior performance. Our research highlights the need to identify various segments of players in gamification systems and distribute recognition fairly for superior performance within each segment.

Our study has certain limitations, which can be addressed either by further research in this area or by implementing some best practices in online communities. Firstly, we do not check the implementation of the proposed framework on a real online community and only show its operationalization using Yelp data. Hence, we cannot be fully aware of the real challenges community managers may face in implementing the system, given that the design is high on complexity. Secondly, our paper does not actually evaluate how the implementation of the proposed recognition system influences community outcomes in the long run. It could only be tested by implementing it on a real online community and comparing community members’ post-implementation behavioral outcomes with those during the pre-implementation phase. Thirdly, our work is limited to segmentwise ranking of top users in a community and recognizing them. It does not provide guidelines on other elements of recognition systems such as display of recognition (badges, leaderboards, etc.), frequency of conferring recognition (annually, monthly, daily, etc.), recognition hierarchies (gold, silver, bronze), etc. Finally, our demonstration is restricted to a single online community, Yelp. Hence, we encourage further application of the proposed framework in the case of other online communities. Based on the limitations discussed above, future research may study the real

implementation challenges, post-implementation community outcomes, various other recognition elements, and extension of this research to other online communities.

## 7 CONCLUSION

Recognition systems play a pivotal role in ensuring the success of online communities by retaining contributors and encouraging them to contribute more and useful content and signaling contributors’ expertise and credibility. While existing studies agree on the importance of a welldesigned recognition system for the sustenance of online platforms, this paper is the first one to technical design principles. We use the real-life case of Yelp, an online review platform employing a multi-criterion recognition system to empirically evaluate the applicability of our five-stage design framework. Our proposed framework espouses in building multiple user dimensions and population segments within the recognition system to value various aspects of users’ community behavior. This leads to the acknowledgment of diverse user characteristics and retaining and motivating users playing different social roles that are deemed desirable by the community. The study makes several academic contributions to the literature on online communities and provides specific guidelines to community managers for the effective design of multi-criterion recognition systems.
