# Moving from Access to Use of the Information Infrastructure: A Multilevel Sociotechnical Framework

Pradeep Racherla

College of Business, West Texas A&M University, Canyon, Texas 79016, pradeepracherla@gmail.com

Munir Mandviwalla

Fox School of Business, Temple University, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania 19122, mandviwa@temple.edu

niversal access (UA) to the Internet and the associated information infrastructure has become an important economic and societal goal. However, UA initiatives tend to focus on issues such as physical access and geographical ubiquity, and they measure adoption through penetration rates. In this paper, we apply an interpretive case study approach to analyze the Philadelphia wireless initiative to provide insights into the nature of UA and extend this concept to also consider universal use (UU). UU is important because simply providing access does not guarantee use. UU is presented as a conceptual goal that starts with the challenge of physical access, but which necessarily also leads to considerations of use. The results show that the human and technological elements underlying individual access and use are deeply embedded within various institutional elements and collectives that enable but also constrain meaningful use. We integrate our findings into a multilevel framework that shows how access and use are influenced by both micro and macro factors. This framework provides new insights into the study of the information infrastructure, digital divide, and public policy.

Key words: universal access; universal use; digital divide; information infrastructure; broadband policy, telecommunications policy; sociotechnical systems; multilevel; interpretive case study

## 1. Introduction

We live in a digital era. Being connected to the information infrastructure (II), represented by the Internet and other associated technologies, is now critical for businesses, culture, education, and communities. “Universal access” (UA) has become an important national goal and a rallying cause for policy makers. For instance, in 2009, the Obama administration declared that “access” is imperative for the socioeconomic development of the country and mobilized more than \$7.2 billion in the U.S. stimulus package to extend broadband services to underserved poor and rural areas (Hesseldahl 2009). In 2010, Finland became the first country to legislate that broadband access is a legal right (BBC 2010).

Yet, a range of obstacles remains. Social and economic inequalities have spilled over into (or even been amplified by) the digital world and people who lack access are being relegated into an even more disadvantageous position (DiMaggio et al. 2004). When social and economic problems do not form a barrier, there are geographic or political imperatives that determine which regions or individuals get access. Overall, the diffusion rate of the Internet has slowed down considerably. Adoption rates have stalled well short of full penetration; few new users have come online and some existing users have gone offline.<sup>1</sup> Further, there is wide variation in broadband penetration rates in different states and communities (NTIA 2011). Many have access in the form of physical connectivity but they do not choose to use it, and socioeconomic disparity does not fully explain unequal access and use (Pew 2012, NTIA 2011). However, most policy initiatives still focus on accessibility such as physical access, geographical ubiquity, subsidized services, and measure adoption through penetration rates. Further, the existing understanding of UA is relatively weak and atheoretical (Van Dijk 2006). The emphasis is still on single factor relations. The research also remains highly differentiated among technological first versus social first, institutional focused versus individual focused, and design oriented versus use oriented perspectives.

Today, with large portions of the II already built and available, many of the technological and physical access challenges have been largely addressed and Internet service prices are now the same or lower than comparable services such as cable TV. This requires a rethinking of traditional approaches. We believe that researchers and policy makers should also consider use. Issues that govern why and how individuals and collectives want to use the II will provide insights into their willingness and desire to access the II. Sustained use of the II is after all the underlying goal of UA initiatives. In this study, we view universal access and use as a vision and societal goal that represents a continuum that starts with the challenge of physical access, but which necessarily must also lead to considerations of use. To support this position, we use the term universal use (UU), i.e., what it takes for every individual “to get it, start using it, and continue to use it.” The remainder of this paper develops the meaning of UU and identifies a set of antecedents that contribute to a formal definition. Individual use is “0 0 0 an individual user’s employment of one more features of a system to perform a task.” (Burton-Jones and Straub 2006, p. 231). Access is defined as an individual user’s access to one or more features of the system to perform a task.

The Internet and associated IIs will likely serve as a digital backbone of our society for many years to come and the success of current and future generations will be interlinked with successful use. Creating new knowledge about the Internet and about increasing access and use is thus a worthwhile research endeavor. The research goals of this study are (1) to investigate the antecedents to access and use of a specific type of II; and (2) to develop an integrated, measurable, and theory-based framework about universal use. More generally and practically our interest is in analyzing what it takes to accelerate (or decelerate) widespread use of Internet technologies—to understand the factors that lead to sustained collective use. To achieve the study goals, we integrate multiple perspectives to systematically interpret the Philadelphia Wireless Initiative,<sup>2</sup> which represents an interesting and illustrative case of a UU project. This study makes several contributions:

(1) Tilson et al. (2010) call for IS research to go beyond organizational boundaries, and they address infrastructure issues. DiMaggio et. al (2004) assert that the II is more than just technology and is continuously reshaped through the interacting effects of individual, social, political and technological forces. This study addresses the above challenges by (a) focusing on the multitude of forces involved in achieving UU instead of a subset (e.g., social only, technological only), (b) expanding the view of access to also include use, (c) drawing on the literature to identify existing as well as new factors, (d) delineating the level (micro or macro) and relationship among each of the factors, and (e) proposing an integrative framework for achieving and studying UU. The framework provides the basis for new research on UU constructs, measurement, and implementation in IS as well as the adjacent areas in sociology and management.

(2) This paper responds to scholars’ call for empirically driven analysis that explicates mutual influences of individual-level attributes and behaviors and collective level IS usage patterns and outcomes (Burton-Jones and Gallivan 2007, Jasperson et al. 2005, Nan 2011). We focus on the antecedents of access and use at both the individual and collective level. The result is an empirically based theoretical addition to the growing IS multilevel and collective use literature.

(3) We focus on “horizontal support” and “universal service” IIs that are different from vertical application or business sector infrastructures (Hanseth and Lyytinen 2010). Support IIs support generic services defined in terms of “ 0 0 0 protocols or interfaces necessary in delivering most, if not all, application services” (Tilson et al. 2010, p. 7). The support infrastructures provide the base level capabilities that support the design and delivery of applications (e.g., websites, email) for users. This study is one of the first to empirically investigate a specific instantiation of a large-scale support II that includes (a) varied use, (b) multiple stakeholders and consequences, and (c) diverse institutional and socio-cultural contexts. The multilevel approach provides the granularity and specificity to empirically induce concepts to produce a comprehensive testable variance theory. The variance approach complements the II literature that has focused on conceptual process and design oriented theories and frameworks (e.g., Hanseth and Lyytinen 2010, Edwards et al. 2007).

The study is organized as follows: We first place UA and UU within the literature on public policy, digital divide, adoption and diffusion, and information infrastructure. Based on this discussion, the analytic lens for the study, research design, and data collection methods are presented. We then analyze the Philadelphia wireless initiative, through which we develop a framework of universal use of the II. Drawing on the framework and the results of the study, we discuss research and policy implications.

## 2. Literature Review

Information systems, sociology, and management all study the information infrastructure from different perspectives and provide important antecedents to access and use based on different levels of abstraction including individuals, organizations, communities, and nations (Selwyn 2004).

## 2.1. Public Policy and Digital Divide

In public policy, UA is viewed in the context of traditional information services such as telegraph, telecommunications, and television, and has usually been conceptualized as accessibility (geographical ubiquity and physical access) and affordability (subsidized services), and measured through penetration rates (Crandall and Waverman 2000). This is reflected in the definition of UA as envisioned in the National Telecommunication Act (1996, p. 120): “0 0 0 making available, as far as possible, to all people within USA, access to an affordable, usable and standardized information and communications network capable of enabling users to receive voice, data, graphics and video services. It applies, in principle, to universal access of evolving applications on the Internet.” Although it is tempting to view the II as a utility service, it represents a complex mix of technologies controlled by different stakeholders. There is so much one can do with this infrastructure today that a simple notion of access becomes meaningless. Do users write emails, participate in chats, play games, or access business or health information? Do they access this information through their computers or mobile phones? And how does this usage fit into the dayto-day practices of individuals versus organizations? Such heterogeneity in requirements, preferences, and operating environments makes it challenging to conceptualize and measure universal access. Yet, the utility perspective still dominates current thinking. This paper builds upon the access/utility perspective of the public policy literature to include consideration of use.

The digital divide literature has also extensively focused on access, including the nature of inequality in access (Van Dijk 2006, Warschauer 2003). This research has mostly considered either binary measures (access or no access) or spatial measures (number of places in which the media is used). Studies in this stream have used socioeconomic status as a variable to explain differential access (DiMaggio et al. 2004). The basic assumption is that socioeconomic status brings about a synergy of social and economic forces that derive from assets internal to individuals and the resources contained in their surrounding environment (Bornstein and Bradley 2003). However, Van Dijk and Hacker (2003) characterize inequalities in access as a multifaceted phenomenon. In this view, access is a function of having the knowhow, a mix of professional knowledge and economic and social resources to use technologies that enhance both professional practices and social life (Kling 2007). Further, attributes of technology such as performance and reliability, interoperability, and availability have rarely been investigated as antecedents to access and use (DiMaggio et al. 2004). This paper builds upon the access and social inequality aspects of the digital divide literature to include consideration of use, infrastructure that includes technologies with varying attributes that directly impact access and use, and a focus on both the individual and collective.

## 2.2. Diffusion of Innovation and Technology Adoption

Access and use can also be examined in the context of macro large technical systems (LTS) (Hughes 1987). Innovation systems evolve because of the dynamic interplay between the technical elements of the infrastructure and system builders (Edquist 2005, Malerba 2002). Innovation development is treated as a coevolutionary process wherein system builders travel between different domains such as economics, politics, and technology and weave a seamless web of a functioning whole. However, LTS research typically considers diffusion as a function of time and the environment and minimizes the role of individual users and condenses the diversity of the user base into the “user environment.” In this paper, we apply the macro perspective of the innovation systems literature as a lens to conduct the analysis, and add to it the micro factors and variance theory building that are the strengths of IS research.

Theories of innovation diffusion (Rogers 2003) and technology adoption (Davis 1989) explain usage based on the characteristics of the adopters, social interactions, and the attributes of the innovation itself such as trialablity, relative advantage, compatibility and complexity (Cooper and Zmud 1990). Similarly, user appropriation theories (Orlikowski 1992) focus on how users integrate a technology into their unique practices and organizational routines. The technology acceptance stream opens up the black box of adoption and usage at the individual level, i.e., adoption is no passive act but requires adaptation and innovation in the user context. Further, technology adoption research emphasizes that providing technology access does not guarantee sustained usage (Agarwal and Prasad 1997, Bhattacherjee 2001, Davis 1989). Rather, it is important to focus on the variation in user context, skill sets, and the motivation to use. However, this literature tends to ignore the influence of the macro environment such as the influence of institutional structures and social environment and has been ambiguous about the mutual influences of technology and agency. Further, user acceptance studies focus on single PCs as opposed to infrastructural systems. Finally, the growth of complex systems has resulted in the integration of work and life in both local and global contexts. Use is thus a second-order effect that applies only after individuals have reasonable access to the II (Dewan and Riggins 2005). In this paper, we apply the individual cognition and behavioral use factors identified by diffusion and adoption research and complement this literature by adding in multilevel analysis and macro institutional and social factors.

## 2.3. Information Infrastructure (II)

UA has also been studied from the II perspective. Hanseth and Lyytinen (2004, p. 213) define the II as “0 0 0 shared, evolving, and heterogeneous installed base of IT capabilities among a set of user communities based on open and/or standardized interfaces.” Structurally, one important difference of the II with respect to traditional utilities is the heterogeneous collection of different technologies, platforms, components, and IT capabilities to support varied applications covering large geographical distances. This plurality of technology is further complicated by the content, and the information resources, services, and applications that individuals access via this infrastructure (Hanseth and Bra 2000). Socially, IIs are organized in such a way that they are both outcomes and conditions of renegotiation and redevelopment (Kallinikos 2004). There are no clear boundaries between those that use the IIs and those that design the IIs. Designers and users keep discovering additional capabilities or transform the existing ones to support new uses. This leads to ever evolving social and technical diversity that must be considered when designing and studying IIs (Edwards et al. 2007, Ribes and Finholt 2009).

At the same time, IIs do not grow de novo (Hanseth and Lyytinen 2010, Star and Bowker 2006) but wrestle with the inertia of the installed base, and are constrained by the strengths and limitations of that base. In this paper, we draw upon the II literature to define the focal technology, sociotechnical perspective, heterogeneity of technologies and users, and the importance of institutions in creating and delivering the infrastructure. However, the II research has focused more on process, the features of IIs, and identification design factors. The contribution of this paper is in delivering an in-depth, empirically driven, and testable variance framework that reconciles the design, access, and use perspectives.

Overall, in this paper we draw upon the UU specific elements from diverse streams of literature to propose a testable variance framework based on interpretive analysis of a unique and comprehensive case. The “large number of moving parts” places unique demands to, on the one hand, stay true to the meaning and intent of the original research, and on the other, to successfully integrate the streams to make a genuine contribution. The following highlights our strategy to achieve a meaningful integration:

1. The public policy and digital divide literature provides the underlying “societal” impetus for this study, and the basis to include access as a key theoretical construct. Our contribution is to go beyond physical access to include considerations of “use” and conceptualize access as part of the larger and more relevant goal of UU.

2. The diffusion and technology-adoption literature provide individual-level attributes such as skills, attitudes, and norms that drive technology use. The literature does not consider how macro-level factors affect the extent and quality of access. Our contribution is to apply multilevel analysis and the macro perspective of the innovation systems literature to show the interrelated nature of use in the context of UU. The innovation systems literature provides the basis for including factors such as institutional forces and technology attributes that shape the system level dynamics and in turn influence individual use.

3. UU can be considered at different levels of analysis including individual, groups, organizations, or geographic regions and nations. In this study, we label individual attributes, cognition, and behaviors as micro, and issues related to collectives such as nonprofit associations, the Philadelphia government, the wireless and computing industry, are labeled macro. UU is thus a macro construct that is likely influenced by macro factors such as the Philadelphia government and also by micro factors such as individual expertise.

4. The focus is on the II—Internet access and use technologies such as smartphones, computers, adapters, chipsets, software drivers, browsers, email programs, chat tools, and others. Dewan and Riggins (2005) also see the Internet as a set of related technologies, rather than one specific tool.

## 3. Research Approach

## 3.1. Research Method

The epistemological stance of this paper is interpretive. The research is conducted through a case study of the Philadelphia Wireless Initiative in which UU is a societal goal, and understanding this goal requires understanding human thought and action in context. UU is analyzed as a social construction and our data sources are the shared meanings, language, documents, and reality of its users (Klein and Myers 1999), and we apply grounded theory (Miles and

Huberman 1994) to induce key concepts from this reality.<sup>3</sup> Philadelphia Wireless is one of the biggest such projects ever attempted—125 square miles and more than 1.5 million potential users—the issues related to such a large scope project are likely to be relevant at a regional and national level. Given its size, scope, complexity, and diversity, the Philadelphia project is an illustrative microcosm of the issues related to achieving UU and it allows us to interpret the evolution of the meaning of access to and use of the II as it is negotiated by the relevant stakeholders. The large number of stakeholders involved in the Philadelphia case provides us with a rich context to apply multilevel analysis (Goldspink and Kay 2004, Coleman 1986).

Following the guidelines of Benbasat et al. (1987), our single case study approach is appropriate because it represents a critical and unique case for investigating the phenomenon of access and use of an II. We are able to study the case in its natural setting because of our access (see case background next). The case is also contemporary because the U.S. federal government as well as numerous cities and municipalities continue to focus on the need of their citizens to access the Internet. Philadelphia is also a “revelatory case” (Yin 1989) since it was one of the first large metro cities to embark on such an ambitious project. The case is complex and broad in scope and offers the opportunity to compare and contrast contradictory and paradoxical evidence without requiring any control or manipulation of the subjects or events; and as discussed previously, the topic can benefit from new theory, including the deep insights that are typically generated by interpretive methods (Klein and Myers 1999).

## 3.3. Data Collection

We employ multiple data collection methods (see Table 1) to capture the context and issues surrounding the phenomenon (see Appendix I for a detailed breakdown). This also allows us to crosscheck the interpretations against multiple sources. Further, because the data reflects the perspectives of different collectives (see Table 2), it provides a rich resource for considering macro-level issues. ## 3.4. Data Analysis

We follow the grounded theory approach to data analysis (Miles and Huberman 1994). In this, we use the efficiency approach (Carroll and Swatman 2000) in the sense that although we use the conceptual framework to organize the identified concepts into a cohesive model, the concepts themselves are induced from the data without a priori research hypotheses or tightly constructed research questions. The interpretive data analysis provides the means to induce and select the most important and relevant concepts. In the data analysis, we examine the utterances of focus group participants, internal archival documents, and public documents of the different stakeholders. The analysis involves three interpretive coding methods: open, axial, and selective coding (Strauss and Corbin 1990). During the open coding we examine, compare, and categorize the data by coding each paragraph or groups of paragraphs with specific codes. The preliminary examination of the data helped us to fracture and generate codes from the textual data. The open coding process yielded approximately 74 open codes (see Appendix I). These codes were used to further categorize the data and identify potential categories and subcategories. As the open coding proceeded, certain plausible concepts emerged, which were coded in relation to each other using the axial coding procedure. During the axial coding process, data was sought depending on its ability to suggest relationships between a key concept and its subconcepts, or its ability to support or falsify plausible relationships between concepts. At this stage, we first identify the regime of the Philadelphia Internet access and use system (see below). Subsequently, we conduct the selective coding process to link the different concepts to the core concepts that lead to UU.

For instance, we identify codes related to the technical issues of the wireless network based on the utterances of the stakeholders as well as the news stories and archival documents. Some of them include “Devices/users,” “Integrating services with cell phone providers,” “Privacy/Security issues,” and “Create uniform standards.” As we reread these codes, it was apparent that they belonged to the technological aspects. However, these codes also linked other concepts. The code “integrating services with cell phone providers” linked “interoperability,” operating at the micro level with “quality of services” at the macro level. We interpret this linkage to mean that the wireless network, even though a presumably radical technology innovation, is inherently interlinked with the existing institutional arrangement. This linkage might not be technical but is viewed thus by the actors who will eventually use the II. Therefore, the success of either entity is predicated on a mutually beneficial coexistence. Through the iterative coding process, we induced the key relationships among the concepts. Finally, we classify these concepts based on their level of effect into macro and micro (please see Appendix II in the online appendix for a detailed assessment of the interpretive analysis as per the seven principles proposed by Klein and Myers 1999).

## 4. Results

## 4.1. Evolution of the Philadelphia Wireless Initiative

The context of the study was the development of the Philadelphia Wireless initiative starting in 2004 (Jain et al. 2007). The technological focus very quickly shifted to a municipal wireless broadband network (MWN). MWNs have been defined as wireless Internet access networks that are created with active local government leadership and involvement (Mandviwalla et al. 2008). Typically, the goal of these projects is to provide ubiquitous connectivity to enable access and use of the II and to foster innovation and growth.

The city intended to address the apparent digital divide among the city population and champion the cause of digital inclusion for the economically and socially underprivileged. According to the Mayor’s Office of Communication (2004),

Philadelphia hasn’t had a first in technology since the Univac. We must prepare our businesses and citizens to face the challenges of the future. Just like roads and transportation were keys to our past, a digital infrastructure based on wireless technology is the key to our future. We are thrilled to expand the city’s leadership position in using the wireless technology to meet our people’s needs and enhance the services, visitor experience and the business environment.

Further, the city government found that Internet awareness and use among the city residents was much lower than the national averages. Therefore, numerous social and civic service agencies were involved in encouraging the city government to undertake the initiative. As the head of a nonprofit organization and an avid supporter of Philadelphia project indicated,

Cities see this as a way to spur economic growth on one hand to put tools in the hands of the underprivileged and give them a leg up, and on the other to provide incentives to small businesses to locate in these cities and to expand their operations. The vision is to have high-speed broadband affordable and accessible to all. Economic development in our underserved neighborhoods will be achieved by bringing the connectivity up from 58% to 80%. To be a first-class city of the 21st century, we have to have advanced telecommunications. We want the Telecoms to continue to do fiber, but we can’t wait till 2015 in Pennsylvania to do that. (Weiss 2005)

In response to this perceived need, the mayor established the Philadelphia wireless committee to explore the feasibility of the MWN initiative. The impetus to this initiative also came from firms such as Intel Corporation, IBM, and CISCO who helped launch the Intel<sup>®</sup> Digital Communities Program (Intel 2005). These firms had been aggressively courting various communities around the world to adopt wireless 802.11 technologies. From the perspective of key stakeholders, the objectives of the initiative can be summed up as follows: (a) promote universal access and use of the Internet for all individuals and institutions, (b) create an entrepreneurial and digitally literate population in the city, and (c) enhance inclusiveness and cohesion of socially and economically underprivileged segments of the city through technology provision.

However, existing Internet and telecommunication service providers such as Verizon and Comcast strongly opposed the initiative. These firms contended that the city had no right to channel public funds into projects that might not necessarily pay off. In addition, as the CEO of Verizon said,

[W]e find it unfair that municipalities that regulate us, set our taxes, set our franchise fees 0 0 0 also now want to compete with us, under a different set of rules. (InternetNews.com 2004)

These firms also argued that universal access to broadband is not a simple concept but, rather, requires a unique marriage between ease and speed of the wireless and ubiquitous coverage of the cellular and wired broadband systems—capabilities that they have built over years of investment. Hence, they are in a better position to ensure universal access rather than the city government. As one official commented,

The city is a steward of its resources. I am not going to tell them how to use them. But in Philadelphia, I think the market did a better job of addressing the issue rather than municipal backed systems. (Washington Post 2005)

This claim, however, was countered by a city official:

if you ask the local telecom companies, they would say 90% of the city is covered. But if it is at a fee that most people in the city cannot afford, and if they don’t have the computers to access the network, then having the network will not help overcome the digital divide. (Washington Post 2005)

The legislature also became a stakeholder. The Pennsylvania House of Representatives introduced a telecommunications regulation bill that prohibits any municipal government or a related entity from offering broadband services for a fee if there is already an existing private enterprise in that region. However, the bill included a grandfather clause that allowed those cities that already had proposed such initiatives to go ahead and finish deployment. At the same time, the bill eliminated the feasibility of some of the original business plans including a taxpayer funded network that would provide free service to the residents of the city. Eventually, the city adopted a hybrid model termed the “cooperative wholesale model.” As per this plan, the city would establish a nonprofit organization called “Wireless Philadelphia.” This nonprofit, in turn, will outsource the design, development, and maintenance of the network to a private company. Access to the network will be made available to Internet service providers (ISPs), telecommunication companies, and other institutions, which will eventually provide access to customers.

The final contract for building the network was awarded to EarthLink Inc., a member of the Intelled consortium that also consisted of two equipment providers, Tropos and Motorola. The company agreed to invest the \$10 million required to build the network. EarthLink would charge about \$20 to regular users and \$10 for economically disadvantaged citizens. As a part of the digital inclusion program, EarthLink agreed to provide 3,000 free accounts to the city, and also channel 5% of the revenue to provide equipment to low-income households. However, the broader aspects of the digital inclusion program would be taken care of by grants and partnerships with local nonprofits, banks, and faith-based organizations. In return, the city government with its numerous departments would become a subscriber to the network.

The initiative, after a euphoric start, faced numerous stumbling blocks and unforeseen problems that eventually slowed down the pace of development including the laying of the network itself. Further, among the city areas where the network remained active, the subscriptions were much lower than what was estimated. After several attempts to jumpstart the initiative, EarthLink finally announced in 2008 that “the operations of the municipal Wi-Fi assets were no longer consistent with the company’s strategic direction” and withdrew. The city government tried other business models to sustain the network including a public–private partnership to provide free wireless to all residents and visitors and generate revenues via donations and advertising. Eventually, the city had to scale back the majority of its goals regarding the network. Currently, the initiative has come to a halt and the city officials are negotiating its future.

## 4.2. The Philadelphia Internet

## Access and Use System

To understand the dynamics of the actors, we apply the “systems of innovation” lens (e.g., Geels and Schot 2007, Markard and Truffer 2008, Chaminade and Edquist 2005). Innovation system components are the actors, networks, and institutions contributing to the overall function of developing, diffusing, and utilizing new products and processes (Bergek 2002, Carlsson and Stankiewicz 1991). The innovation system concept is relevant because the Philadelphia goal was to diffuse a technological innovation—Wireless Internet and services. Further, this lens is sociotechnical in orientation and accommodates both micro and macro forces. The approach is particularly useful for identifying the collective underpinnings of innovations in contrast with other approaches that may emphasize individuals (only) or specific units of the economy (Chaminade and Edquist 2005).

According to Lamb et al. (2000) sociotechnical perspectives that are (only) based on organizational settings, may not be sufficient to study the globalized nature of modern IT. The systems of innovation lens thus complements the rich but local and organizational centric sociotechnical perspectives in IS research (e.g., Bostrom and Heinen 1977, Newman and Sabherwal 1996, Orlikowski and Gash 1994). From the systems of innovation literature, the “multilevel framework” (Geels 2004) is applied to analyze the interrelated structure of forces that influence the Philadelphia case.

Geels’s multilevel framework conceptualizes innovation systems as a cluster of elements including technology, environment, regulations, user practices, cultural manifestations, and the supporting supply networks. It builds on the literature of technological transitions that conceives innovation systems as interactive processes of change called niches and regimes embedded in a broad landscape (Geels 2002, 2004; Smith et al. 2005). The framework includes the following:

• A regime constitutes the key structures that guide and orient the activities of the relevant groups–in this case, the stakeholders of the Philadelphia Wireless Initiative, their problem agendas, institutional representations, technologies, technical norms, products, use patterns, standards, and local regulations.

• The landscape refers to relatively static environmental factors that influence innovation or transitions but are typically not directly influenced by forces in the regime. The landscape in our case includes environmental factors such as innovations in chipsets, the health and direction of the computer and telecommunications industry, national and international standards, and regulations that influence the relevant industries.

• A niche is a more coherent and a micro version of a regime. Innovations typically start as niches and may break through and shift the regime by bringing in changes in technology as well as user practices, markets, and supporting infrastructure. The relevant niches in the Philadelphia case include relatively nascent market and technological niches such as WiMax, Wi-Fi, CDMA, LTE, and others.

Geels’s framework provides the structure to think about subgroupings such as specific established markets as regimes (e.g., Internet access) and new innovations as niches (e.g., WiMax). We made an analytical decision to describe the problem space as a regime termed the “Philadelphia Internet access and use” system. According to the literature, systems can be technological, national, regional, or sectoral (see Markard and Truffer 2008). The research goal of this paper has to do with what it takes to get individuals to fully use a set of technologies, and thus fits the “technological” system orientation. A national or regional (only) delineation fits the Philadelphia context on the surface, but a geographic approach means focusing only on the actors and institutions that fit the selected spatial boundary (Moulaert and Sekia 2003); in the Philadelphia case, many of the institutions are national or international entities (e.g., Earthlink, Intel). Similarly, sectoral or industry delineation may initially fit the “Internet access” context, but the fundamental premise of how you access the Internet was also in question during the case (and still is). For example, Internet Service Providers, cable companies, Fiber optic firms, wireless carriers, WiMax association, chipset manufacturers, or municipal services organizations are all relevant and could be legitimately studied in groupings of regimes (e.g., the relatively stable cable company sector) or new niches (e.g., WiMax). Given our analytical focus on use, a technological centric approach is more appropriate because users ultimately care more about getting access and use than about who provides the access (e.g., cable versus DSL) and from where (e.g., local versus national provider). Further, because technological systems by definition cross both geographic and sectoral boundaries (Hekkert et al. 2007), the delineation fits the extant reality of the actors involved in the Philadelphia project.

The regime concept is also relevant for the analysis because the interest is in not just what it takes, for example, for municipal wireless networks to succeed, which could be studied as a niche, or the overall health and direction of Internet access, which could be studied as a landscape. The interest is on the factors involved in accelerating (or decelerating) widespread use of Internet access technologies that happened to converge around the wireless project. So the term “Philadelphia” in “Philadelphia Internet access and use” signifies a label for the project rather than a regional delineation.

It is important to distinguish between the structure and functions of the system. Functions reflect what a system does and how it works in comparison to how it is structured (Bergek et al. 2008, Markard and Truffer 2008a). Functions are defined as “the factors that influence the development, diffusion, and use of innovations” (Edquist 2005, p. 182). Functions and subfunctions are thus outcomes of structural configurations of the actors and institutions in the system. The relevant groups in the regime operate in relative autonomy, and at the same time participate in networks of mutual interdependencies. The elements and their linkages are actively created and sustained by the actors that have vested interests in those systems. Each set of actors has its own perceptions, values, problems, and strategies; thereby making the evolution of the regime a process that generates both macro as well as micro level forces. The autonomy of the actors is relative, i.e., the material conditions of the regime create, enable, and constrain the context for action at the micro level. Similarly, the rules and institutional representations are limited to the material possibilities of the technology at hand. At the same time, powerful regimes do not reside just in the heads of the actors at the micro level but are eventually embedded in the artifacts and practices and become macro forces.

The regime concept from the systems of innovation literature provides insights into the structure of the overall system, but the functional perspective from the same literature is also needed because it provides the basis for thinking about the actions of the system—achieving UU (Markard and Truffer 2008a). The identified structure provides the backdrop for inducing the key functions that lead to universal use. The overall system function is generation, diffusion, and use of innovation (Edquist 2005) i.e., generating and diffusing Internet access and use to achieve UU. Various subfunctions contribute toward the overall function including generation and diffusion of knowledge, creation of markets, and resource mobilization. For instance, mobilizing resources such as sourcing high-quality low-cost Internet access chipsets is a specific macro subfunction of the system but directly affects access to the II at the individual level. The delineation and analysis of structures and functions is also an important requirement in multilevel analysis (Burton-Jones and Gallivan 2007, Morgeson and Hofmann 1999). In the next section we delineate the structure of the Philadelphia Internet access and use system followed by its functions.

## 4.3. Macro-Level Structure

The structure of the Philadelphia Internet access and use system is composed of social and organizational groups that operate at the macro level. We put these groups into three broad categories: technological suppliers, sociocultural groups, and institutions (see Figure 1). Although the identified structure is an end result of the iterative data analysis, we present it upfront to frame the discussion. Figure 1 also depicts the specific relationships that establish, negotiate, communicate, and transfer the rules, resources, and standards in the regime. For example, competing technology developers are continuously influencing most of the other groups on the best way to achieve UU (e.g., wired versus wireless, DSL versus cable, Wi-Fi versus WiMax, and so on). We were able to categorize five different types of technological supplier groups, five types of institutional groups, and three types of sociocultural groups. These groups do not always fit neatly into a single category; they often play varied roles by moving between the categories:

• Technological suppliers. Includes institutions that provide technology artifacts needed to build the network as well as devices to access the network. It also includes a broader set of firms who develop enabling technologies as well as those who promote competing technology platforms. They influence the evolution of II through changes in the material characteristics of technology artifacts.

• Sociocultural groups. Entities who provide direct support to users including, in the case of disadvantaged users, subsidized equipment and training. These groups include various nonprofit organizations that often provide the cultural context in which the users are embedded. They also include political actors who provided the impetus for the debate on UU, although some of the political actors eventually saw the initiative as counterproductive.

• Institutions. Includes organizations that create and sustain the context in which the network services will be consumed in everyday life. On a day-today basis, the economics of using technology artifacts is crucial. Furthermore, it is crucial to have the necessary financial resources to promote access to the infrastructure. The groups in this category influence affordability, funding mechanisms, and the material resources required to build and sustain the initiative. This category also includes groups that can produce telecommunications services by exploiting the network.

Identifying the groups and their relationships is important because they are not homogenous and have varied influences on UU. The groups have different rules and cognitions on UU as a goal and how to achieve UU. ## 4.4. Macro-Level Subfunctions

Understanding the structural characteristics of the Philadelphia Internet access and use system provides important initial clues on the “what and who” influencing UU; the functional view is needed to focus on the “how.” It allows us to identify the most important subfunctions and constructs that will lead to UU.

4.4.1. Infrastructure Performance. The infrastructure performance subfunction includes quality of the network and services, standards, and relative cost/benefit because they are influenced by the relationships and negotiations among technological suppliers (e.g., device manufacturers) and institutions (e.g., telecommunication firms, and city administration) (see Figure 1). These factors influence the extent to which individuals can access the network on a sustained basis. Creating a network that is fast, reliable, and high quality relative to cost is complicated and requires sustained investment and negotiation of trade-offs. As one technology expert commented:

0 0 0 building the network is easy, maintaining it is tough. Cities have to also fund the operations of the network. That’s the piece most people overlook in such initiatives. If an access point goes out, who will go out at 2 <sup>a.m.</sup> to climb a pole and fix it? How much extensive support can you provide to the users? And at the same time maintain the costs? (McCullagh and Broache 2005)

Similarly, selections of the IEEE 802.11b standard also ran into challenges. This standard was considered advantageous because it operates in the unlicensed spectrum and provides a relative cost advantage as opposed to other wireless standards; reasons that very well suited the initial objectives of the city. According to the CIO of Philadelphia, Diana Neff,

0 0 0 the city decided on the 802.11b wireless standard since it was cheap and relatively easy to install, especially if you are working with a municipality, and have access to public infrastructure such as street lamps, power etc. It fits into our overall goal of digital inclusion by allowing easy access and lower costs. (Strassman 2005)

However, one business owner argued the opposite:

0 0 0 this standard is just a workable solution for today, and will seem less and less attractive with other wireless technologies currently being offered. For example, the EV-DO standard being rolled out by Verizon is much more powerful, faster, and works over larger distances. What is the guarantee that people won’t shift to it? (Hitech/Early startup companies focus group)

Experts also pointed out challenges regarding the reliability and fidelity of Wi-Fi. Since wireless networks run on unregulated spectrum, devices such as microwaves, phones, home networks, and Bluetooth all use the same frequency and can cause interference leading to higher error rates and router overload. According to a technology expert,

0 0 0 the 2.4 MHz spectrum is already very crowded. When you have a large deployment such as Philadelphia also using that spectrum there’s a lot of potential for overcrowding and interference. (McCullagh and Broache 2005)

The infrastructure performance subfunction relates to the infrastructure literature because it can provide guidance on the factors that impact design; the focus here however, is on how the output of that design process—the performance of the II—influences access and use.

4.4.2. Resource Mobilization. One of the initial efforts by the city officials was to look for a business model to provide affordable broadband. Resource mobilization and engagement with the technological suppliers, institutions, and sociocultural groups became important because such projects were not a core competency of the officials involved and created significant funding and management risk and challenges. Complicating factors include dealing with arbitrary and inflexible budgetary, regulatory, social, ideological, and political constraints such as “the project must be completed at zero cost to the taxpayers.” Yet, our analysis shows that the diverse groups think that UU can only be achieved by public-private partnerships and the involvement of multiple parties. The initial business model was to fund the initiative through tax payers’ money and provide free service to the residents and charge a nominal fee to the businesses. One city official commented,

Further, the type of resource needed may change and mobilization of one resource (e.g., creating a way in which others can sell services through the network) may lead to mobilization of additional resources. It is unlikely that one group will ever have the technology or the capital and political will to fully sustain the development of UU, and the resources of multiple technological suppliers, institutions, and sociocultural groups will be needed (see Figure 1). The resource mobilization subfunction is consistent with the literature on innovation systems and has been regularly identified as an important subfunction (Markard and Truffer 2008). An engineering consultant working with the project remarked,

I do feel that muni-wireless for ubiquitous Internet connectivity is a common good. Municipalities are about the only entity that can have the right “business model” to do it (i.e., it can’t be done by regular shareholder-based businesses). But almost all of the RFI/RFPs from the city are off by 1/2 or more on the cost with today’s technology. There is also the issue of managing 10–30 thousand mesh nodes for an urban area that has not yet been addressed, but I do think that will be doable at some point in the not too distant future. (Fleishman 2005)

Instantiation of the resource mobilization subfunction would involve the availability of data and information (e.g., an Internet accessible directory for entrepreneurs as a group to connect and engage with each other); subsidies and grants (e.g., monthly use subsidies for poor urban users); content that targets the needs of specific groups (e.g., electronic job boards for battered women shelters); training for specific groups (e.g., senior citizens); recognition of collective use (e.g., Internet use awards based on social, professional, and geographic dimensions); events that celebrate, promote, and educate the community (e.g., Internet technology expos); and finally the provision of sustained institutional services that encourage use by other institutions (e.g., open data government initiatives that lead to corporations offering add-on services).

4.4.3. Affordability. The affordability subfunction refers to the cost of the access devices, actual access and use, and complementary services. Complementary actors (see Figure 1) such as device manufacturers drive network externalities and contribute to low prices of access devices. The role of complementary actors becomes critical. A city official commented

0 0 0 we have to build partnerships with hardware manufacturers. Otherwise the price of the equipment becomes unaffordable. (Hu and Reardon 2005)

Similarly, according to a member of a local chamber of commerce,

0 0 0 the city should invite sponsorships that help cut costs. You are investing in future of Philadelphia. So let the businesses invest in, and sustain this initiative and drive low costs.

The debate on affordability reveals the inherent tension between the sociocultural groups more concerned with providing low-cost access and the interests of major institutional groups such as telcoms that consider the long-term dynamics of technologies and related business models. As the member of a public policy think tank remarked,

Although municipal WiFi efforts generally have good intentions, lack of a profit motive dulls the technology used and sets up a scenario where equipment quickly becomes obsolete. (Walsh 2005)

4.4.4. Community Involvement. Many residents felt a need for intervention from existing providers. Surprisingly, this is in contrast to the widely held community view that telecommunication firms were competitors and barriers to the initiative rather than assets. As one participant suggested,

0 0 0 use the private companies who do this. For example bundle Microsoft and Philadelphia wireless togetherschools will benefit. Involve Verizon and Comcast. They have experts and the infrastructure to help. (Focus group with small business group)

Given that personal communication devices such as smartphones and home-centric broadband access is offered by major institutions, it is consistent that locally visible incumbent firms such as Comcast and Verizon were considered important. These institutions provide information and services that are the primary goods exchanged over the II. They also drive down the costs of devices by generating economies of scale. To help communities sustain use, the involvement of local institutions in providing locally relevant expertise, tools, and services will need to be activated. Further, sociocultural groups such as community organizations are also important for promotion and outreach, as one resident opined,

How are they going to pay for it? If the city promotes it, that will cost the city money. Churches can drive it. We don’t need a sweet marketing contract for \$10 million when the preachers can do it for free. (Focus group with African-American business owners)

## One student commented,

Community centers will help the neighborhoods, organizations to help donate computers. This could help lessen the digital divide. This effort would create more of a demand /pressure to have groups to address the digital divide. Offer the needed training. (Focus group with local students)

These comments are consistent with the literature on development, where it has long been established that local communities and civic groups play a powerful role in actualizing the use of resources for development. (Kretzmann and McKnight 1993)

An interesting point that surfaced in the analysis was the issue of social control. Residents seem to demand civic ownership of the project since they consider it a social initiative. As the representative of a Wi-Fi consulting firm suggested:

0 0 0 the city needs to have some skin in the game. Someone needs to take ownership of the project to make sure it happens and is done right. There needs to be accountability. And this means that the city either needs to put up some money upfront or actually use the network to deliver some kind of mission critical application or service.

## A city resident concurred,

0 0 0 the city has to initiate the program, not businesses. They will take too much advantage of grants and empowerment zone; Comcast reaped heavy benefits at the city’s expense. Citizens should have 51% at all times. Make sure that citizens have a significant amount of input. (Focus group with Educators)

The member of a nonprofit organization, that studies municipal wireless networks across the United States, echoed this sentiment,

Municipal networks in places like St. Cloud, FL and Chaska, MN work because the local community has substantial control over the network. In Philadelphia, this control was ceded to private interests and we’re looking at the repercussions of this decision. (Brietbart et al. 2008)

This suggests that large “public works” types of projects automatically take on some civic ownership regardless of the source of funding. The issue of social control, and earlier debate on affordability among various groups, demonstrates the value of considering multiple and diverse groups—it allows us to understand that in achieving UU multiple actors in the regime will need to shape and be influenced by the system.

Instantiation of community involvement will likely mean that locally visible providers (e.g., Verizon), community groups (e.g., churches and community centers), and local institutions (e.g., city government) will enable individual use by providing ongoing support (e.g., training, help desk), legitimization (e.g., branding), and social control (e.g., public ownership over “civic works”).

4.5.1. System Performance. Performance refers to the speed, reliability, quality, and relative cost/benefit of Internet access and use for the individual. A majority of the actors agreed that if performance is poor as compared to already established communication channels such as mobile phones, it creates an expectation paradox that could be detrimental to sustained use in the long run. A city councilman remarked

Obviously, wi-fi is not a static technology (it’s brand new!)—how trustworthy would a municipality be in upgrading its infrastructure and expanding its range of services over time? Most cities can’t even put together a decent website 0 0 0 now they want to be ISPs? “Competition” (such as it is) has served cities adequately if not optimally in cable TV, long distance services, dial-up access and wireless telephony. Why start nationalizing (municipalizing?) a nascent and rapidlyevolving industry right out of the nest? (Philadelphia Inquirer 2004)

To overcome the performance and interference dilemma, some experts suggested that the city instrument a set of common standards for developing these networks. A tourism industry representative remarked,

0 0 0 the network is going to affect not only the residents but also travelers visiting the city. This requires common standards and capabilities. (Focus group with tourism professionals)

The above discussion indicates that despite the novelty of the new infrastructure, users tend to compare the new technology with existing standards and communication infrastructures. It also shows that performance is an enduring issue; improvements in technology will likely always present users with “better” alternatives and they will always compare the alternatives to what they may already have. This is an important point; an objectively measured “fast” connection speed may still be seen as “slow” if there are competing but inaccessible alternatives that are seen to be fast.

Cost is a relative concept; any relative decrease in price and overall cost will influence individual usage.<sup>5</sup> Note also that in some cases such as cell phones, it is possible today to receive free devices, but still those devices are not completely costless; some may require additional components such as memory cards, accessories such as headsets, and more importantly expensive contracts. To the individual, the overall cost of a cell phone is still quite high. Therefore, cost remains an important determinant of usage and is intertwined with quality, speed, and reliability and integrated into system performance. A doctor remarked,

The highest Phila Wireless can charge should still be less the DSL. May be \$10 or maybe \$15. Most of my bill now is paying for the modem. Since that is gone, it should half of DSL. I prefer \$10. (Focus group with medical professionals)

System performance (and interoperability and availability) are “system quality” attributes (DeLone and McLean 2003). Performance at the individual level is the realized system quality—“what is my system performance?”—and is different from infrastructure performance at the macro level—“how is the performance in Philadelphia?” System performance can be instantiated using a combination of perceptual (e.g., survey) and objective measures (e.g., log files, sales figures).

4.5.2. Interoperability. Related to the issue of performance is interoperability. Switching between different forms of communication channels coupled with myriad types of devices used for each network limits the value one network can provide to the users. One participant in a focus group commented

0 0 0 if the expectation is ubiquitous connectivity, then various entities in the communication infrastructure must be integrated. Integrate with cell phone providers—otherwise citizens might find it hard to use/not enough access devices. (Hitech/Early startup companies focus group)

It is evident from the above discussion that the users do not see the new infrastructure as a standalone system. Rather, to sustain individual use, it has to fit in and provide interoperability.

4.5.3. Availability. Availability refers to where the network will be accessed and how it will be accessed. There was considerable debate about indoor versus outdoor access. The value of an outdoor only network was perceived as limited. However, even with outdoor access, there were proponents who envisioned fantastic opportunities. As a city council member commented,

0 0 0 think of all the business transactions that can take place on something as simple as a park bench. Being outdoors is not as bad as you think. (Wirelessphiladelphia.org 2005)

In response, the president of the U.S. Internet Industry Association commented:

0 0 0 It’s too risky to build the new public networks when the market is so difficult to predict. Wi-Fi is a great technology in the confines of a limited space. We’re somehow dreaming that we can extend it in a wide mesh to cover 138 square miles, in the case of Philadelphia. What happens when the city has invested millions of dollars of taxpayer money to build a network and then all of those private Wi-Fi networks that everyone put in their homes and businesses just trash the network? (Weiss 2005)

Another aspect of availability is “access devices.” Although it seems trivial in the larger context of an exciting infrastructure, the data suggests that access devices were a major concern for many actors. The main issue was that access devices were costly and quickly become obsolete. For instance, one city resident observed,

0 0 0 the cost of ownership of a personal computer is much higher than other major electronic media such as a television or mobile phone. (African-American chamber of commerce)

The rate of technological change makes it difficult for the users to readily adopt one form of technology to sustain usage of the II. This discussion suggests that to the individual, availability is important and includes both spatial (geographical) and technical (access devices) aspects.

4.5.4. Individual Capacity. Lack of use also exists because of limited abilities of particular groups (e.g., retired residents) to benefit from the II. These groups exist at every strata of the society. Although there are macro factors that determine the need for or access to technology, it is individual capacity that determines whether individuals are actually able to put the II into some meaningful use. Individual capacity is a cognitive attribute that refers to the experience, interest, anxiety, and attractiveness of the new technology and is influenced by the physical properties of the technology, personal factors (e.g., literacy, innovativeness and social intelligence), and socialsituational context (e.g., nature of work, location, culture and technical means). Consistent with previous research in digital divide (Cronin and Davenport 1993, Van Dijk 1998), and user appropriation of technology (Orlikowski 1992), our data suggests that individual capacity is an enabler as well as a barrier to use. The above further reinforces the need to move from access to use, in which access cannot be seen as an end by itself. It is only an enabler of further activities that can only be partially specified beforehand. Consider the following statement from a member of African-American chamber of commerce,

what are people accessing the network for? We need to understand what people need and then promote the network based on use.

As one city resident noted,

Technology should be intuitive, similar to familiar systems. People have to feel comfortable with their own surroundings to use technology. Enable people to enable themselves. Don’t force usage on everyone. (Focus group of Civic and Community groups)

Further, the positive or negative outcomes from the use of an artifact arise if it enables appropriable activities. To be appropriable, usage has to connect to some well-established personal knowledge of those involved in the activity. It has to make sense in the larger social setting, and has to be personally meaningful (Papert 1980).

4.5.5. Relevance. To be relevant, use of the II should be embedded in the existing social and cultural contexts of the individual. For example, some questioned the justification for the initiative. One resident remarked,

Immediate barrier is trying to justify the expense. Why wasn’t the 10 million (the budget for the initiative) spent on schools, or flu shots? Wireless is still a luxury and there are many unmet basic needs. In this state of affairs, I am not sure how many will embrace this initiative. (Focus group of Hispanic Chamber of Commerce)

The use should also be relevant to the day-to-day practices of users. A doctor commented,

0 0 0 most of our work is definitely not time sensitive. For our profession, the benefits of instant communication are not important. Also, why would I transmit confidential medical information over a public network when I have secure digital lines for those purposes? (Focus group with Healthcare professionals)

The needs also vary in terms of information, communication, and even entertainment. For example, a city resident commented,

0 0 0 for me, it is entertainment and the excitement of meeting new friends using the new technology, and broaden my knowledge through it.

The relevance factor is also important because it addresses the heterogeneity in individual requirements, preferences, and diverse operating environments discussed earlier.

4.5.6. Interconnectedness. An interesting issue that emerged is the concept of interconnectedness.<sup>6</sup> Use is also influenced when individuals have limited possibilities to communicate and share information. In other words, an important consideration for individual use is not how many other generic people use the II but rather how many of one’s friends, family members, and business contacts are currently using the II. When looking at technology as a resource, use becomes more than a question of ownership of the artifact. Rather, it has to be defined in terms of the ability of technology to maximize the utility for pursuit of individual goals. The II is essentially a medium through which an individual achieves certain social and instrumental goals. To this end, one needs social, economic, and cultural capital networks. Therefore, interconnectedness goes beyond concepts such as subjective norm (Davis 1989) or personal network exposure (Valente 1995). Interconnectedness follows from UU, and in turn influences individual use. A small business owner illustrates this requirement:

0 0 0 to afford and acquire access, one needs money, but to use to it appropriately and meaningfully, one must have access to social and business networks.

Another city resident echoed similar sentiments:

0 0 0 what will I do accessing the wireless if I do not have people to contact on the internet? (Focus group with African-American chamber of commerce)

## 5. Framework for Investigating UU

In this section, we develop a framework (see Figure 2) that combines the macro structure, macro functions, and micro individual factors identified in the previous sections. Collectives are shown in rounded rectangles and individual constructs are shown in rectangles. Macro subfunctions are shown in capitals and individual level constructs are shown in normal case. For parsimony, the framework depicts only (a) the structures, subfunctions, and constructs that were derived via the analysis, and are interesting and relevant in terms of achieving the goal of UU; and (b) omits arrows that would show the specific enactment and assignment of individual and collective constructs.

5.1. Philadelphia Internet Access and Use System The system as outlined earlier represents the milieu of actors, rules, standards, resource bases, and activities associated with the Philadelphia wireless initiative. The structural elements of the system affect IA, IU, and UU through the specific subfunctions outlined next.

## 5.2. Universal Use

We define UU, the collective construct, as a collective employment of one more features of the II to perform tasks. This definition parallels “0 0 0 an individual user’s employment of one more features of a system to perform a task.” (Burton-Jones and Straub 2006, p. 231). Collective in our case represents the Philadelphia region and the user groups (e.g., chamber of commerce, doctors, urban, business) that make up the region. UU is a configural construct<sup>7</sup> because collective Internet use will vary based on frequency, purpose (e.g., information search versus transactional), type of user or group (e.g., expert, nonexpert, builder, urban, suburban, business, nonprofit and so on), and tool selection (e.g., Web browser, email software), i.e., across all the dimensions suggested by Burton-Jones and Gallivan (2007, p. 668). We further define UU, the vision, as a heterogeneous and sustained human-technology interaction influenced by a range of technologies, individuals, and their social context. At the same time, the human and technological elements are deeply embedded in institutional dependencies that are essential and enabling, yet sometimes constrain use of the information infrastructure. The discussion section provides suggestions on potential measures for UU.

UU is directly influenced by the macro subfunction of resource mobilization. This subfunction includes the provision of resources that encourage or discourage use by collectives. We also find that UU has a mutual influence on IU via interconnectedness. Interconnectedness is the ability to connect to social and business networks that will increase as UU increases.

## 5.3. Individual Use

IU is directly influenced by the individual concepts of system performance, interoperability, and availability. Two cognitive attributes also affect IU—individual capacity and relevance. Individual capacity is whether individual knowledge is sufficient for Internet use, and relevance is whether the use fits their day-to-day practices. The community involvement subfunction crosses the model and directly influences individual use. It is the involvement of community groups and local institutions in enabling individual use by providing support, legitimization, and social control. These relationships are important because our results show that such initiatives must also consider directly enabling and sustaining use and not just focus on enabling access.

## 5.4. Individual Access

Three subfunctions, infrastructure performance, affordability, and resource mobilization directly influence individual access. Infrastructure performance is about creating speed, reliability, quality, and cost/benefit of specific Internet access services, whereas affordability has to do with the business models, economies of scale, network externalities, and complementarities among device manufacturers and service providers that can lead to affordable individual Internet access.

## 6. Discussion and Implications for Research

This study integrates multiple streams of literature with a unique data set from a large-size II project to extend the traditional view of access to include heterogeneity in use, and multilevel micro and macro factors instantiated through empirical analysis of structure and functions. We next discuss the key implications of the research.

## 6.1. Integrating Previous Research

This paper takes a first step in integrating the technical, social, institutional, and use-oriented perspectives relevant to UU that have previously been studied separately. The technology adoption literature asserts that access does not guarantee sustained use and it is important to focus on variation in context, skill sets, and motivation to use (Agarwal and Prasad 1997, Bhattacharjee 2001). Similarly, digital divide research has focused on the multifaceted nature of inequality in access and use (Van Dijk 2006, Warschauer 2003, DiMaggio et al. 2004). Our study extends the above literature stream by connecting macro level institutional and environmental factors with the individuals’ ability and motivation to access and use technology. These factors provide new avenues of future research for traditional IS adoption and use research.

This study also builds on prior work related to the contradictory concerns of stakeholders (Pollock and Williams 2009, Star and Ruhleder 1996, Star and Bowker 2006). Concepts such as individual capacity, relevance, and community involvement address the needs and issues of different stakeholders. For instance, individual capacity reflects the idea of interpretive flexibility (Orlikowski 1992) and user appropriation of the II and provides mid-level concepts to investigate Tilson et al. (2010) idea of “generativity.” In addition, the interoperability concept complements the notion of installed base effects (feed-back loops) discussed in the II literature (Hanseth and Lyytinen 2010, Shapiro and Varian 1999) in which a large installed base is seen as an important quality of an attractive II. This sets up a demand for backward compatibility that imposes constraints on what can be designed at any time, thereby making the development of the II path dependent. It will be interesting for future research to evaluate the proposed framework (or parts of it) in the context of other types of IIs. For example, the issues represented by factors such as interconnectedness, resource mobilization, and community involvement are not unique to the public sphere and may also be important in the business and organizational context.

This study integrates the concept of digital infrastructures with the social infrastructure and provides candidate mid-level constructs to empirically investigate “digital infrastructures.” The interaction effects among the multilevel factors can be used to investigate the generative, recursive, and flexibility attributes identified by Tilson et al. (2010). For example, the paradox of control and change can be investigated by examining the contrast between the individual perceived need for interoperability and performance with the tactics of service providers who typically view performance and interoperability as a trade-off in which performance can be achieved by limiting flexibility and third-party integration.

## 6.2. Multilevel Nature of UU

The analysis illustrates that UU is interwoven with the institutional elements that determine the trajectory of the technology development and use, as well as the elements of action at the individual level of society. In other words, while the demand for or diffusion of the II is driven by the need for information as well as economic and social interactions at the individual level, it is also determined or diminished by functional transformations at the macro level. This inherent macro–micro dualism presents interesting challenges for policy making (see next section) and future research. For instance, one of the major challenges in the Philadelphia initiative is sustainability. For this to happen, it is not sufficient that the individual members of the population adopt and use the system but there has to be a larger demand and involvement from government, local businesses, and education. However, these actors have existing infrastructures that are either better in terms of performance, or not complementary to the proposed technology.

The contribution of this paper is in providing a bridge between the analytical orientation of the innovation systems literature (i.e., in identifying key functions and structures) and the outcomes and variance approach typically taken in IS research. Future research is needed to fully explicate and delineate the multiple systems of innovation that likely underlay UU. In this paper, we focus primarily on diffusion, i.e., use of the II. Future research should distinguish between radical versus incremental innovation systems, the production versus innovate functions of the II, mature versus emerging regimes and niches, and the needs of different categories of users such as information seekers, contributor, or builders.

## 6.3. Measurement of UU

To measure UU, first, it is important to analytically delineate the collective level of analysis. In the Philadelphia case, the collective is the complete region, and can be further delineated geographically through zip codes or municipalities. It could also be segmented via demographic (e.g., ethnicity, age, social and economic class), professional (e.g., doctors, teachers), institutional (e.g., nonprofit, for-profit), and type of area (urban, suburban) groupings. These collectives may provide a richer view of UU than simple zipcode-based analysis. Second, the form of use needs to be identified. Burton-Jones and Straub (2006) identify 14 different use measures including extent, frequency, nature, duration, and variety of use. Based on our analysis, we propose UU measures that focus on actual usage (e.g., number of users, frequency and extent of use), performance (e.g., speed and downtime), affordability (e.g., monthly actual cost), convenience (e.g., number of devices and locations used to access), task (e.g., number of people contacted, online friends, transactions completed, tools used), and interest (e.g., perception of control, ownership). These measures at the collective level (e.g., averages by zip code and aggregated for the overall region) will provide a richer and more accurate analysis of UU than traditional access measures such as penetration or coverage. It should be noted that even though the sources for data will likely all be different, all of the above measures are technically and practically feasible. An important question for future research is which of the above measures promotes use that will lead to high quality, differential use, or other socially desirable outcomes such as quality of life and economic development.

## 6.4. Social Influences, Network Externalities, and UU

It is well accepted that social influences and network externalities drive technology adoption and usage (Agarwal et al. 2009, Goolsbee and Klenow 2002, Lenhart et al. 2003, Kraut et al. 1998). However, social influence studies typically use geographical proximity (e.g., neighborhoods) or organizational membership as proxy measures and overlook the distinction between “general” externalities where the perceived benefit is influenced by the number of users versus “specific” externalities where the benefit follows from the identities of those who use (DiMaggio and Garip 2011). Today, an individual’s social and economic access and use are no longer limited to local neighborhoods or other forms of proximity but extend to increasingly virtual and varied communities (for instance, individual users’ membership in online support groups or networking websites such as Facebook and LinkedIn). Therefore, externalities are no longer generic in nature but tied to the identities of individual users. Future research needs to identify new measures of interconnectedness to take into account the identities and varied communities afforded by the digital world.

## 6.5. The Transitory Nature of UU

Shifting relationships among groups in the system, as well as evolution of the underlying technologies (e.g., wireless technologies) and the regimes (e.g., changing business models) guarantee that the meaning of UU will evolve. In the end, UU is a social constructed reality that will shift. New technologies with better usability and functions, as well as better economic models emerge; these new developments can fundamentally alter the dynamics among the stakeholders, resulting in a changed meaning of UU. Further, the system is highly segmented and the form of access goes far beyond the traditional model of desktop computers; instead we will see an increasing array of different devices and modalities of use (e.g., smartphone’s, kiosks, location-based travel tools, wearable devices). Our framework takes into account the interplay among the actors in the sociotechnical system and could be used to investigate the importance and role of actors and to “track” the shifting meaning of UU. Overall, to complement and improve the granularity of the variance-based theoretical work presented in this paper, a process theory is needed that can address the evolution of UU.

## 6.6. The Importance of Institutions

The macro-level analysis brings forth the important role of institutions such as local governments, community, civic groups, as well as service providers in enabling UU. The involvement of civic bodies in technology initiatives has been subject to extensive scrutiny and criticism because of either economic or political reasons. However, as Catinat and Vedel (2000, p. 184), in the context of “European eDemocracy” project, suggest, “The market place is always not the best mechanism to ensure the basic values associated with the notion of digital democracy (such as freedom of communication and equal access to information infrastructure).” From the use perspective, the II requires access to devices and skills to obtain information and services. To facilitate such use requires computer literacy, education and training, assistance from friends and colleagues, and from civic bodies and local community groups. Our analysis shows that the civic institutions’ influence on individual level use operates beyond the traditionally understood aspects such as trust in the government (Meader et al. 2002) and support programs (Kvasny 2002). For instance, there was a desire expressed in our focus groups that cities and other public institutions should take ownership of the project. It seems that civic institutional use legitimatizes the II to sections of the user base. The technology becomes as much a social commodity as it is an artifact. The proposed framework provides a basis to further investigate this “paradox of control” (Tilson et al. 2010, p. 754) and the contradictory role of private and public institutions rather than depicting them (only) as sources of inertia.

## 7. Implications for Policy

## 7.1. A Reworked Definition and Measurement of UA

One outcome of this paper is that we need new measures for governmental and societal efforts to achieve universal access and use. Even if we achieve 100% broadband access, the probability that it will automatically result in desirable social and economic outcomes is low. We suggest that policy makers apply the framework presented in this paper to generate new policy and new measures for success. Governmental action in the form of mandates or legislation may be required to collect and report data on the achievement of UU.

## 7.2. “Telecom Policy” as a Standalone Initiative is a Limiting Concept

UU is so enmeshed with social, technical, and local factors that policy makers need to rethink the concept of a standalone “telecommunications policy.” Policies that unilaterally encourage providers to expand coverage and reduce rates, or other traditional techniques commonly associated with access, will likely not yield the desired results. Policies that leverage existing communities, social structures, and local actors will likely prove more successful.

7.3. Public/Private Partnerships are a Necessity Beyond the well-documented benefits of public/ private partnerships, the scale of UU necessitates such partnerships. Consider Wireless Philadelphia, the nonprofit charged with helping disadvantaged individuals. It took the dedicated staff of this organization more than two years to reach a few hundred “clients” and their eventual reach was less than 2,000. They had to solve a daunting set of related technical, social, educational, and economic challenges before they could achieve access and then sustain use. Such an effort/cost ratio is likely not feasible. Standalone organizations such as Wireless Philadelphia as well as standalone policy initiatives are crippled upon conception in being able to (a) scale and (b) have an impact at the desired level. A more effective strategy is the following:

• Rather than tax, or require subsidies from providers, a policy that requires the formation of effective partnerships with grassroots organizations might prove more effective.

• Existing social service and education initiatives already have “hooks” in local communities. These hooks can be leveraged by adding a broadband element to the grants and regulations governing these initiatives.

## 7.4. Empowering Local Organizations is the Most Likely Path to Success

In line with the above, a “one size fits all” solution to UU seems unlikely to succeed. Contextual and local elements and even the level of local civic control seem to play a role in the minds of users and could influence success. Therefore, although a centralized telecommunications policy no longer seems necessary, it may still be necessary to economically empower local communities and municipalities to leverage the power of the Internet. This could be done through a grants program targeted toward local sociocultural groups that is funded through centralized and existing tax structures.

## 8. Conclusion

There is increasing interest to address the social, economic, and political aspirations of regions and nations regarding Internet access and use. In this paper, we provide insights into the most relevant technological, institutional, and sociocultural elements that will influence the achievement of UU, and develop a multilevel framework that expands the perspective of universal access to universal use. The paper elaborates on UU as a vision, defines UU as a measurable construct, identifies the antecedents of UU, and ends with a discussion on how the meaning of UU is socially constructed and will thus evolve. It investigates the development and consequences of a government-led initiative to enable II access and use for all segments of the population. This is one of the few studies that focus on access and use of an infrastructural system at the societal as opposed to an organizational level, and one of the first to empirically analyze a large-size II project with appropriate granularity and specificity, including diverse uses and consequences that emerge from a systematic analysis of institutional and sociocultural contexts. The main objective of identifying key antecedents to UU is achieved by delineating and incorporating into the framework an expanded set of micro and macro factors relating to universal access and use. The approach in this paper to interpret and induce concepts from data, map those concepts where relevant to existing literature, and propose a theoretical framework that operates at the societal level is viable, demonstrates rigor, and leads to new theoretical insights. The key to the success of initiatives such as “Philadelphia wireless” is to expand the meaning of access to include the nuances associated with use. The results provide insights to policy makers to devise effective interventions and measures to better manage UU-related initiatives.
