# INFECTIOUS CONFIDENCE: UNRAVELING THE EFFECTS OF CONFIDENCE CONTAGION ON OVERFUNDING IN EQUITY CROWDFUNDING<sup>1</sup>

Yijing Li and Fei Liu Department of Management and Marketing, Faculty of Business, The Hong Kong Polytechnic University, HONG KONG {yijing1.li@polyu.edu.hk} {fei-albert.liu@polyu.edu.hk}

Eric T. K. Lim The School of Information Systems & Technology Management, UNSW Business School, Sydney, AUSTRALIA {e.t.lim@unsw.edu.au}

Chee-Wee Tan Department of Management and Marketing, Faculty of Business, The Hong Kong Polytechnic University, HONG KONG {chee-wee.tan@polyu.edu.hk}

Yong Liu Department of Information and Service Management, Aalto University School of Business, Espoo, FINLAND {yong.liu@aalto.fi}

Equity crowdfunding campaign overfunding occurs when a campaign generates funds in excess of the funding goal and has hence been touted as the mark of an extraordinarily successful campaign. However, a novel theoretical lens is needed to comprehend funders’ enthusiasm in their support of such campaigns. Building on the extant literature on contagion effect, we constructed a research model that posits confidence contagion invoked by confidence cues embedded in campaign materials as a key driver of overfunding. Our hypotheses were validated in two complementary empirical studies. In Study 1, we manipulated confidence cues in a controlled experiment to verify the activation of confidence contagion at the individual level. Analytical results indicate that individual funders detect self-confidence traits from confidence cues embedded in campaign materials and assimilate the fundraiser’s confidence via a spontaneous social appraisal mechanism. In turn, confidence contagion drives funders to congregate and invest in campaigns. In Study 2, we analyzed a secondary dataset collected from a leading equity crowdfunding platform to discern how confidence contagion drives overfunding at the collective level. By modeling funding activities as a Hawkes process, we derived three key metrics that govern the emergence and magnitude of funding surges. We demonstrated that these metrics of funding surges mediate the impact of confidence cues on the level of overfunding for equity crowdfunding campaigns. The findings from this study could inform future research seeking to untangle the interdependencies between individual and collective mechanisms underlying crowd phenomena, provide strategic guidance to fundraisers interested in promoting the overfunding of their campaigns, and help crowdfunding platforms predict the potential extent of overfunding and advise fundraisers accordingly.

Keywords: Equity crowdfunding, overfunding, confidence contagion, funding surges, Hawkes process

## Introduction

Equity crowdfunding is a “form of financing in which entrepreneurs make an open call to sell a predetermined amount of equity or bond-like shares in a company online, aiming to attract a large group of investors” (Ahlers et al., 2015, p. 955). Crowdfunding has revolutionized capital markets by tapping into the enormous financial resources of the crowd (Shafi, 2021); thus, it is not surprising that equity crowdfunding has become an attractive channel of fundraising for startups, with the total amount raised in 2020 surpassing US\$5 billion (Yasar, 2021).

Equity crowdfunding platforms are a digital medium for capital exchange between capital seekers (i.e., fundraisers) and independent retail investors (i.e., funders) (Cummings et al., 2020). Fundraisers pitch business ventures through campaigns directed at prospective funders (Mochkabadi & Volkmann, 2020). Equity crowdfunding disperses funding requests among a large collective of funders rather than a few professional investors (Ahlers et al., 2015). This distributed funding process can give rise to overfunding, where funders continue to invest in a campaign that has already reached its intended funding goal (Nguyen et al., 2019).

Equity crowdfunding platforms often characterize overfunding as signaling the extraordinary success of a campaign. On Crowdcube, the leading equity crowdfunding platform in the U.K., overfunded campaigns are embellished with an “overfunding” ribbon and are featured at the top of ranking lists. Seedrs, a Crowdcube rival, featured a campaign that was overfunded by 724% in their “2021 Year in Review.”<sup>2</sup> Both platforms promote overfunding as a much rarer feat than reaching the intended funding goal. Indeed, Crowdrating (2016) showed that although over half of the 155 campaigns in their sample successfully reached their intended funding goals, only five were overfunded by more than 100%.

Due to its extraordinary nature, overfunding often comes as a surprise to the fundraisers themselves because their funding goals are based on insiders’ knowledge about the business venture. On many platforms, when a campaign is overfunded, the fundraiser must decide whether to relinquish extra equity in exchange for the excess funds (Wrisk, 2017). Overfunding may also associated with a greater risk of negative publicity due to expectations of higher returns and creates extra responsibilities regarding the proper handling of the excess funds (Lehot & Ravski, 2021). To benefit from overfunding and avoid its potential pitfalls, fundraisers need to expect the unexpected and assess whether the extra funds, publicity, and funders can help advance their strategic goals (Wrisk, 2017). When overfunding does align with the fundraiser’s strategic goals, research has shown that overfunded ventures yield higher annual returns (Signori & Vismara, 2016) and benefit from a more diverse funder community (Wrisk, 2017).

Since the funding goal represents the fundraiser’s valuation of their business venture, whether a campaign can reach this goal depends on the resolution of information asymmetry between fundraisers and funders (Shafi, 2021): In other words, a campaign can reach its funding goal if funders, as a collective, perceive sufficient quality signals to justify the requested funding amount (Mochkabadi & Volkmann, 2020). Overfunding represents an extraordinary level of success compared to merely meeting the intended funding goal (Quinn et al., 2021). A novel theoretical lens is hence warranted to understand why the funders of certain campaigns are willing to invest far beyond campaign funding goals. By revealing the mechanisms that drive overfunding, this study provides insights that fundraisers can use to strategize their campaigns. While fundraisers seeking to benefit from excess funds can leverage the insights from this study to encourage overfunding, this study benefits all fundraisers by revealing how to anticipate the likelihood of campaign overfunding. The findings of this study can help fundraisers preemptively deliberate on whether to permit overfunding in advance rather than being caught by surprise and can guide equity crowdfunding platforms in helping fundraisers predict the likelihood of campaign overfunding based on their campaign setup.

In contrast to professional investors, the majority of retail funders are not equipped with the necessary skill set to assess the quality of business ventures on equity crowdfunding platforms (Kleinert et al., 2020). Like retail traders in stock markets, retail funders must rely largely of the feeling of confidence projected from campaigns (Jansen & Nahuis, 2003). The contagiousness of the human psyche has been well documented in past studies on the contagion effect (Barsade et al., 2018), through which affect states or behavioral attitudes of an individual or organization transfer to other individuals via direct or indirect contact (Schoenewolf, 1990). Accordingly, this study advances a confidence contagion mechanism to explain how funders base their investment decisions on the spontaneous appraisal of the fundraiser’s confidence. This study then identifies four confidence cues embedded in campaign information that can project the fundraiser’s selfconfidence traits, namely ambition (Hermans et al., 2015), independence (Babalola, 2009), assertiveness (Ames & Flynn, 2007), and optimism (Dai et al., 2017).

Funders reacting to confidence contagion by investing in a campaign at an accelerated pace (Payne et al., 2009; Zafar et al., 2021) may drive surges in funding activities. Such funding surges reflect a shared mood of optimism and enthusiasm, similar to how a shared optimistic mood among traders has been found to drive surges in stock market trading activities (Jansen & Nahuis, 2003). We thus anticipate that overfunding outcomes are predicated on the number of funders who are responsive to the confidence contagion effect. To examine funders’ responsiveness to confidence contagion at a collective level, this study quantifies surges in funding activities using three key metrics. Whereas funding reactivity captures the speed at which funders congregate toward a campaign (Großmann et al., 2020), funding virality (Aral, 2011) and funding persistency (Dodds & Watts, 2004) represent the scale and momentum at which new funders continue to gather and invest.

We examined surges in funding activities induced by confidence contagion as the key driver of overfunding on equity crowdfunding platforms. To do so, we scrutinized the effects of campaign information at both individual and collective levels. At the individual level, we conducted an experiment to answer the first research question: Do information cues presented in an equity crowdfunding campaign invoke confidence contagion? At the collective level, we performed econometric analysis on a secondary dataset extracted from Crowdcube to answer the second research question: How does the activation of confidence contagion affect the extent to which an equity crowdfunding campaign is overfunded?

Findings from this study aid in understanding the driving mechanism of overfunding. Through an experiment manipulating information cues in campaign materials, this study shows how cues related to the fundraiser’s selfconfidence invoke confidence contagion via a spontaneous social appraisal mechanism. Additionally, our secondary data analysis reveals how confidence contagion at the collective level boosts overfunding by driving funding surges. To estimate the metrics of funding surges, we modeled the funding process as Hawkes processes (Rizoiu et al., 2017). Our results indicate that these metrics of funding surges indeed determine the extent to which an equity crowdfunding campaign is overfunded.

## Theoretical Foundation

## Equity Crowdfunding and Overfunding

The ever-intensifying competition in financing markets drives entrepreneurs to consider equity crowdfunding as a viable alternative to fundraising (Shafi, 2021). The extended reach of digital platforms incentivizes fundraisers to leverage crowdfunding campaigns in order to generate publicity, solicit feedback, and build communities for value co-creation (Kleinert et al., 2020). To some extent, equity crowdfunding democratizes entrepreneurial financing for both fundraisers and funders. While novice fundraisers are provided with a convenient means of financing their business ventures, retail investors can take advantage of the opportunity to join fundraisers in pursuing a potentially profitable business venture (Cummings et al., 2020).

Each crowdfunding campaign is required to specify its intended funding goal at launch. Campaigns that raise enough funds to reach their funding goals before the end of a fixed funding period are considered successful (Mochkabadi & Volkmann, 2020). Campaigns are considered overfunded if their fundraising exceeds the intended funding goal. In such cases, fundraisers can typically claim the excess funds by offering additional business venture equity in exchange (Martínez-Gómez et al., 2020).

Overfunding is a distinct feature in equity crowdfunding that indicates extraordinary success. A funding outcome is considered extraordinary if it exceeds the outcome indicated by the preset funding goal (Quinn et al., 2021), with higher levels of success reflecting the extent of excess funding. Research suggests that when people encounter extraordinarily successful campaigns, they engage in an aspirational rather than rational thought process (Quinn et al., 2021). Overfunding may come as a surprise even to fundraisers themselves, despite their intimate knowledge of the business venture (Wrisk, 2017). However, overfunding often presents a difficult choice about whether to relinquish additional shares, and perhaps even voting rights, in exchange for the excess funds and associated publicity (Martínez-Gómez et al., 2020). The exceptionality of overfunding suggests that the mere resolution of information asymmetry is not sufficient for overfunding to occur. Rather, we suspect that a previously undiscovered mechanism is at work that drives funders as a collective to invest extra funds in a campaign beyond the justified funding goal.

## Determinants of Crowdfunding Campaign Success

Whether a crowdfunding campaign will reach its intended funding goal depends on the resolution of the information asymmetry between fundraisers and funders (Shafi, 2021). Funders intentionally assess campaign quality based on multiple quality signals when deciding whether to pledge their support (Mochkabadi & Volkmann, 2020). Prior research has identified a myriad of quality signals that determine whether a crowdfunding campaign will successfully reach its intended funding goal, including campaign attributes, the fundraiser’s characteristics, and peer influences (for a more in-depth review, see Mochkabadi & Volkmann, 2020).

## Campaign Attributes

One stream of research has confirmed that funders use the campaign’s funding goal (Hellmann et al., 2019), exit plan (Agrawal et al., 2015), percentage of equity retention (Löher et al., 2018), and provision of financial information (Lukkarinen et al., 2016) to make investment decisions. As such, these attributes have been examined as quality signals that drive crowdfunding success. Ralcheva and Roosenboom (2020) argued that funders infer campaign quality based on the percentage of equity retained by a fundraiser because it demonstrates the fundraiser’s commitment to their venture. Although past studies have confirmed the linkage between campaign attributes and crowdfunding success, few studies have empirically examined funders’ actual appraisal of these attributes.

## Fundraisers’ Characteristics

Based on signaling theory, prior studies have also shown that funders perceive fundraisers with more financial (Buttice et al., 2017) or entrepreneurial experience (Hellmann et al., 2019) and those with prestigious educational backgrounds or close ties to reputable venture capitalists (Kleinert et al., 2020) to be more trustworthy. Funders would thus be expected to be more willing to invest in campaigns launched by such fundraisers. Furthermore, the fundraiser’s commitment to keeping funders in the loop during the funding process—by, for example, posting updates (Block et al., 2018) and social media activities (Lukkarinen et al., 2016)—can also encourage funders to invest.

## Peer Influences

Apart from signals disseminated by fundraisers, prospective funders’ decisions can also be affected by their peer funders due to the transparency of fundraising progression on equity crowdfunding platforms. Through fostering observational learning, equity crowdfunding platforms are often fertile breeding grounds for herding behavior (Astebro et al., 2018; Vismara, 2016; Walther & Bade, 2020). For example, past studies have reported that the early funding progress of a campaign is critical to its eventual success (Astebro et al., 2018; Vismara, 2016). This is because early-stage funders are often seen as possessing insider knowledge, causing subsequent funders to follow suit due to information cascades (Walther & Bade, 2020). Research has shown that this type of herding behavior is rational because rather than blindly jumping onto the bandwagon, funders take into account prior funders’ expertise (Hornuf & Schwienbacher, 2018; Kim & Viswanathan, 2019), investment amounts (Nguyen et al., 2019; Walther & Bade, 2020), location (Walther & Bade, 2020), and relationship with the fundraiser (Kim et al., 2020). Funders may even intentionally delay their investment decisions to gain more information about campaign quality from peer funders’ actions (Hornuf & Schwienbacher, 2018; Nguyen et al., 2019). Nonetheless, the effect of information cascades subsides as a campaign reaches its funding goal, leading funders to make investment decisions based on their own assessments (Astebro et al., 2018).

While the linkage between the three categories of information cues and the eventual funding success is well-established, past studies have argued for funders’ effortful inference of these information cues. However, the effortful inferential mechanism alone does not account for extraordinary outcomes such as overfunding. Rather, there is likely a previously undiscovered mechanism other than the conveyance of quality signals that breaches the information asymmetry responsible for extraordinary outcomes such as overfunding. This study proposes confidence contagion as a novel mechanism through which funders spontaneously assimilate the fundraiser’s confidence when assessing a business venture.

## Confidence Contagion

Compared to other forms of crowdfunding, funders in equity crowdfunding act more like retail traders in conventional financing markets who make investment decisions largely based on the feeling of confidence (Shafi, 2021). In equity crowdfunding, confidence refers to a positive feeling about the anticipated return from investing in the business venture (Payne et al., 2009; Zafar et al., 2021). It has been shown that unusual surges in trading activities can be observed in the stock market when a positive mood is diffused among investors (Jansen & Nahuis, 2003). Likewise, we argue that overfunding in equity crowdfunding signifies a collective mood of optimism and enthusiasm for the success of the business venture, which reflects the reassuring and energizing nature of confidence. When evaluating a campaign, funders may spontaneously “catch” the fundraiser’s confidence in their business venture. This contagion of confidence is expected to galvanize a collective of infected funders to pledge a level of support that exceeds the fundraiser’s own expectations.

Contagion refers to a “process in which a person or group influences the emotions or behavior of another person or group through the conscious or unconscious induction of emotion states and behavioral attitudes” (Schoenewolf, 1990, p. 50). Past studies have demonstrated the explanatory power of contagion effect by showing how people’s emotions (Goldenberg & Gross, 2020), goal conformity (Ahmadi & Mirsepassi, 2010), passion (Hubner et al., 2020), perceived competence (Kramer & Block, 2014), and product acceptance (Aral, 2011) converge toward a target through exposure to the target’s emotions, verbal communications, or behaviors.

Prior research has identified three mechanisms by which contagion takes effect. Mimicry-based contagion is an automatic interpersonal copy of affect or behaviors (Parkinson, 2020). It emerges when individuals subconsciously mimic a target’s facial, verbal, or postural expressions and subsequently experience the target’s affect via a physiological feedback process (Barsade et al., 2018). Category activation is another subconscious mechanism of contagion in which a particular affect is directly activated following exposure to cues that prime a certain emotion category (Goldenberg & Gross, 2020). Category activation can occur without physical contact with the target since it does not involve behavioral mimicry (Parkinson & Simons, 2009). Finally, social appraisal refers to a process through which individuals register the target’s appraisal of an object or event and adjust their own appraisals in line with those of the target (Hubner et al., 2020). Both mimicry and category activation take effect via an automatic affect transfer that requires no explicit interpretation or deliberation of the target’s feelings (Parkinson & Simons, 2009). Social appraisal is a more conscious contagion mechanism in which individuals take the target’s feelings into account when evaluating the affective significance of a subject or circumstance (Parkinson, 2020).

Contagion effects have been discovered in diverse contexts. For instance, Parkinson (2020) documented that individuals who are engaged in a shared task often arrive at similar emotional states because they simultaneously attend to the same information cues that promote a specific emotional orientation. Likewise, Hubner et al. (2020) noticed that entrepreneurs can boost employees’ morale, commitment, and performance by projecting their own passion for entrepreneurial activities to employees. A similar contagion effect has also been observed in reward-based crowdfunding, in which funders’ enthusiasm for a product can be amplified by the passion fundraisers demonstrate when introducing the product (Li et al., 2017).

We expect that the fundraiser’s confidence in their business venture (hereafter fundraiser’s self-confidence) can be transmitted to prospective funders through campaign materials. Prior research has pointed out that confidence can be contagious such that a leader can boost team members confidence by projecting their self-confidence in the success of their shared task through verbal and behavioral cues (Fransen et al., 2015; Kirkpatick & Locke, 1991). Accordingly, this study strives to unravel possible confidence contagion in equity crowdfunding where funders who perceive a fundraiser to be confident in the campaigned business venture spontaneously feel confident about investing in the campaign.

Past research has shown that funders indeed feel more assured about the future success of a crowdfunded business venture launched by fundraisers who demonstrate an authoritative status (Kao et al., 2022). It is hence reasonable to postulate a confidence contagion through which prospective funders defer to the fundraiser’s self-confidence when making judgments about the likely profitability of the fundraiser’s business ventures. In particular, we surmise that confidence contagion is driven by a social appraisal process in which funders use the campaign materials to assess the fundraiser’s conviction in the success of a business venture. Unlike more effortful inferential processes such as quality assessment, the appraisal process is spontaneous rather than deliberate (Kardes et al., 2004). In lieu of making an intentional effort to infer the quality of the campaigned business venture and the fundraiser’s trustworthiness from campaign materials, they spontaneously assimilate the fundraiser’s confidence without further nudging (Kardes et al., 2004).

At a collective level, we propose that confidence contagion makes funders more eager to become a part of the campaigned business venture, resulting in a clustering of their funding activities. Confidence contagion is thus expected to induce unusual surges in funding activities. A handful of studies on equity crowdfunding have attempted to attribute funding surges to information cascades driven by peer influence (Vismara, 2018; Walther & Bade, 2020). Our study extends this stream of research by investigating how confidence contagion stimulated by campaign materials serves as a key driver of surges in funding activities.

## Fundraiser Self-Confidence Traits

Self-confidence is critical to entrepreneurial success because confidence in one’s own ability for success, attainment, and accomplishment enhances motivation and performance (De Jorge Moreno et al., 2007). It also plays a pivotal role in entrepreneurial financing by reassuring investors of the potential value of the business venture (De Jorge Moreno et al., 2007). Prior research has demonstrated the commonly shared inclination for individuals to follow those who exude self-confidence through their demeanor and discourse (Kirkpatick & Locke, 1991). Remarkably, it has been shown that investors base their valuation of business ventures more on the impression given by the leading entrepreneurs than on the actual financial performance (Dai et al., 2017). For this reason, entrepreneurs are advised to engage in impression management to project a self-assured image to arouse investors’ confidence (Kirkpatick & Locke, 1991). We expect the effect of projecting self-confidence to be much more pronounced in digital financing markets due to the lack of face-to-face interaction between fundraisers and funders. On equity crowdfunding platforms, funders can become more sensitive to information cues available in campaign materials that project the fundraiser’s self-confidence.

Research has identified four personality traits of confident entrepreneurs, namely ambition, independence, assertiveness, and optimism (De Jorge Moreno et al., 2007; Kirkpatick & Locke, 1991; Zou et al., 2016). Ambitious entrepreneurs give an impression of self-confidence because they aspire to lofty goals that often appeal to investors (Judge & Kammeyer-Mueller, 2012). Past studies have shown that ambition not only leads to personal attainment and occupational prestige (Judge & Kammeyer-Mueller, 2012) but also contributes to the success of entrepreneurial endeavors (Hermans et al., 2015).

Self-confident entrepreneurs are also reluctant to surrender control of their business ventures to investors because of their conviction that their business ventures may fare better if they are at the helm (Babalola, 2009). Koestner and Losier (1996) termed such desire for autonomy independence, cementing it as a defining trait of self-confidence. More independent entrepreneurs are usually more possessive with regard to the percentage of their shareholding. It is essential for an entrepreneur to be the majority shareholder if they want independence when making strategic decisions.

Confident entrepreneurs act with an assertive demeanor and display a tendency to candidly communicate their values, beliefs, goals, and views (Ames & Flynn, 2007). Past studies have underscored the importance of assertiveness in the effective communication of leadership visions (Ames & Flynn, 2007; Kirkpatick & Locke, 1991) for reducing doubt and fostering confidence in recipients (Kirkpatick & Locke, 1991).

Lastly, optimism is another prominent trait of confident entrepreneurs (De Jorge Moreno et al., 2007; Zou et al., 2016) and represents a positive outlook on the attainability of desired outcomes (Dai et al., 2017). Entrepreneurs who have an optimistic attitude about the future of their business ventures have been found to be more persistent in goal pursuit (Adomako et al., 2016) and more likely to secure loans with lower interest rates (Dai et al., 2017).

## Hypotheses Formulation

We constructed a research model to illuminate how confidence contagion at the individual level affects overfunding at the collective level (see Figure 1). Whereas confidence contagion emerges at the individual level when funders pick up confidence cues embedded in an equity crowdfunding campaign, it manifests at the collective level as funding surges when funders who share the fundraiser’s confidence become eager to pledge support.

## Activation of Confidence Contagion

The fundraiser’s self-confidence is projected to funders predominantly through information cues in campaign materials (Löher et al., 2018). First, ambitious fundraisers who strive for daunting objectives tend to set larger funding goals (Hermans et al., 2015; Kirkpatick & Locke, 1991). Consequently, the magnitude of the funding goal of an equity crowdfunding campaign is the most accessible cue for funders to get a sense of the fundraiser’s ambition (Mollick, 2014). Funders will deem a fundraiser to be more ambitious if they aim for a higher funding goal in their equity crowdfunding campaign. We therefore hypothesize that:

H1a: The funding goal of an equity crowdfunding campaign positively influences funders’ perception of the fundraiser’s ambition.

Fundraisers who are confident in the success of their business ventures likely value their independence. Confident fundraisers would thus be expected to avoid relinquishing too many of their equity shares for fundraising purposes in order to maintain their position as the majority shareholder (Shafi, 2021). The equity shares offered by a campaign in exchange for funds are thus reflective of the fundraiser’s equity retention. High equity retention is indicative of the fundraiser’s independence since the fewer equity shares the fundraisers are willing to part with initially, the more committed and the more financially self-sufficient they will appear to be (Ahlers et al., 2015; Troise, 2019). Therefore, funders will likely perceive the fundraiser to be more independent if the campaign offers fewer equity shares in return for their investment. We therefore hypothesize that:

H1b: The equity retention of an equity crowdfunding campaign positively influences funders’ perception of the fundraiser’s independence.

Confident fundraisers likely feel comfortable presenting their business ventures in an assertive manner (Ames & Flynn, 2007). Assuming they have a clear vision about the future of their business venture, they will likely prefer to communicate their vision in a forthright and unambiguous manner (Kirkpatick & Locke, 1991). This assertiveness can be perceived by funders through the tone fundraisers choose in presenting their campaign materials. Funders will likely perceive fundraisers to be assertive if they pick up assertive tones from an equity crowdfunding campaign. We therefore hypothesize that:

H1c: The tone assertiveness of an equity crowdfunding campaign positively influences funders’ perception of the fundraiser’s assertiveness.

Confident fundraisers are likely to expect a successful outcome of their business venture. The most optimistic outlook for any business venture is the initial public offering (IPO), which speaks to the general public’s recognition of its value (Nguyen et al., 2019; Vismara, 2018). Fundraisers can claim an IPO as their exit plan to display their optimism about the prospect of their business venture. Nonetheless, fulfilling the criteria for an IPO is challenging—especially for nascent business ventures that need to kickstart the financing process through equity crowdfunding (Schwartz, 2013). Funders will likely view fundraisers who explicitly express an IOP intention in their exit plan as optimistic. We therefore hypothesize that:

H1d: The IPO intention of an equity crowdfunding campaign positively influences funders’ perception of the fundraiser’s optimism.

Contagion describes the phenomenon in which an individual or group mimics the emotions, attitudes, or behaviors of a target. The contagion effect has been found to be a prominent cause of the transmission of entrepreneurial passion (Hubner et al., 2020) and product adoption (Aral, 2011) among individuals. Likewise, it has been shown that funders are more attracted to campaigns that project the fundraiser’s confidence (Fransen et al., 2015; Löher et al., 2018). Consistent with the social appraisal mechanism, we posit that prospective funders infer a fundraiser’s confidence in their own business ventures through information cues that project the fundraiser’s self-confidence traits (Parkinson & Simons, 2009). As a consequence, how confident prospective funders feel about investing in the campaigned business venture will be affected by the perceived self-confidence traits of the fundraiser. We therefore hypothesize that:

H2: Confidence contagion occurs such that funders’ perception of the fundraiser’s (a) ambition, (b) independence, (c) assertiveness, and (d) optimism positively influences their investment confidence.

## Impact of Confidence Contagion

Past studies suggest that funders who are confident in pledging their support are less likely to delay their decisionmaking because they do not need to observe the funding progression of the campaign in order to be convinced of its desirability (Kim & Viswanathan, 2019; Nguyen et al., 2019). Driven by confidence contagion, funders would be expected to display little hesitation in pledging their support for campaigns that project the fundraiser’s self-confidence. Funders’ shared feelings about a fundraiser’s self-confidence would thus manifest as funding surges rather than in a steady accumulation of funding (Nguyen et al., 2019). Funding surges describe unusual spikes in funding activities, the patterns of which can be characterized by three distinct metrics: reactivity, virality, and persistency.

We define funding reactivity as the immediacy of funders reaction to confidence contagion. If funders are susceptible to the influence of information cues that project the fundraiser’s self-confidence, i.e., confidence cues, it would take less time for funding surges to emerge. Past studies have shown that a more potent source of contagion can reduce recipients’ resistance to its influence (Crane & Sornette, 2008). Aspara (2013) argued that investors who resonate affectively with a source of influence tend to give it less scrutiny. Consequently, investors who resonate affectively with a company are likely to experience tunnel vision and feel optimistic about its success. Similarly, funders would be expected to resonate more strongly with fundraisers whose campaign materials project greater self-confidence.

A few studies have revealed that contagion effects occur sooner if the recipients are more susceptible to their influence (Suzuki et al., 2016). Setting a high funding goal, retaining a large proportion of equity shares, adopting an assertive tone, and aiming for an IPO are confidence cues that convey a strong sense of the fundraiser’s self-confidence in their equity crowdfunding campaign. If they resonate with the confidence projected through confidence cues, funders will presumably be more likely to pledge their support. We therefore hypothesize that:

H3: The (a) funding goal, (b) equity retention, (c) tone assertiveness, and (d) IPO intention of an equity crowdfunding campaign positively influence funding reactivity.

We further posit the virality of the contagion effect as a key determinant of its impact. We define funding virality as the capacity for a campaign to reach a larger number of funders. The size of the affected group has been repeatedly employed to assess the virality of cognitive, emotional, and behavioral contagion (Mishra et al., 2016). Indeed, past studies have attributed content sharing on social media (Dobele et al., 2007), real estate purchases (DeFusco et al., 2013), and viral product diffusion (Dodds & Watts, 2004; Suzuki et al., 2016) to the contagion effect.

More potent confidence cues tend to elicit reactions from more funders. Each funder determines whether to commit to supporting a business venture when their confidence in it exceeds a certain threshold (Crane & Sornette, 2008). Aral (2011) showed that consumers use information cues to help them alleviate feelings of ambiguity and uncertainty. For this reason, as the strength of confidence cues increases, we predict that more funders would feel the urge to join the campaigned business venture. This rationale is consistent with previous work on crowdfunding in that campaigns that project the fundraiser’s self-confidence tend to attract more funders (Löher et al., 2018). Taken together, it is to be expected that confidence cues, including setting a high funding goal, retaining a large proportion of equity shares, adopting an assertive tone, and aiming for an IPO, can transmit confidence to more funders. We therefore hypothesize that:

H4: The (a) funding goal, (b) equity retention, (c) tone assertiveness, and (d) IPO intention of an equity crowdfunding campaign positively influence funding virality.

A number of studies have also touched on the persistency of intermittent surges (Aral, 2011; Dodds & Watts, 2004). The temporal decay of funding surges, which indicates its persistency over time, is subjected to the strength of their driver (Aral, 2011). Past studies have shown that the influence of contagion effects decays faster when individuals are presented with conflicting information or when they harbor doubts (Sun, 2013). Nonetheless, recipients’ self-confidence can shield them from the interference of conflicting information and doubt, which accumulates over time (Zhang & Liu, 2012). Contagion effects that boost recipients confidence are expected to persist for longer time periods.

In the context of equity crowdfunding, as time since the launch of a campaign elapses, prospective funders have more opportunities to be exposed to competing campaigns. Campaign materials that project stronger confidence are more likely to induce prospective funders’ tunnel vision (Aspara, 2013). Stronger confidence cues can continuously attract prospective funders and entice them to invest. In this sense, the impact of stronger confidence cues, including setting a high funding goal, retaining a large proportion of equity shares, adopting an assertive tone, and aiming for an IPO can persist longer. We therefore hypothesize that:

H5: The (a) Funding goal, (b) equity retention, (c) tone assertiveness, and (d) IPO intention of an equity crowdfunding campaign positively influence funding persistency.

## Funding Surges and Overfunding

While there is little research attempting to examine the relationship between funding surges and overfunding, inflated desirability generated by overcrowding has been reported by scholars. Past studies have shown that investors are more likely to acquire stocks that are highly sought after in the stock market, as reflected in unusual spikes in trading activities (Jansen & Nahuis, 2003). DeFusco et al. (2013) observed surges in the sales of real estate projects that generated ample buzz early on. Similarly, products that generated a great deal of publicity at launch may experience a substantial boost in sales (Drehmann et al., 2007).

## Experimental Procedure

We recruited participants from Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk). To minimize potential confounds caused by language barriers, only MTurk workers from Englishspeaking countries were recruited. During the screening phase, participants were required to complete a qualification test. Only those who demonstrated a basic understanding of equity crowdfunding were invited to participate in the experiment. We adopted the same qualification questions<sup>3</sup> utilized by Crowdcube. Qualified participants were then directed to a pretask survey to report their demographic background, crowdfunding experience, and financial knowledge before being randomly assigned to one of the 32 experimental conditions.

The main experiment comprised two investment tasks. Following an experimental design that has been widely adopted in financial behavior studies (Ehm et al., 2018; Gneezy et al., 2003), we provided each participant with a US\$1,000 virtual starting fund for each investment task. Each task asked participants to allocate any amount of the starting fund to purchase equity shares from a real campaign that had been successfully funded on Crowdcube. In addition to compensation of US\$5, participants were informed that they had a 10% chance of receiving a bonus based on their task performance. The bonus was 1% of the current valuation of the equity shares the participant purchased since the campaign was originally funded on Crowdcube plus the unspent starting fund.<sup>4</sup>

We chose funded campaigns for both tasks to control for the intrinsic quality of the campaigned business ventures. Past studies suggest that funders evaluate crowdfunding campaigns in the technological sector differently from those in nontechnological sectors (Kleinert et al., 2020; Nguyen et al., 2019). To control for the potential impact of each sector, we selected one technologically driven campaign in renewable energy (henceforth Campaign A) and a less technologically driven campaign that focuses on local plant delivery and caring service<sup>5</sup> (henceforth Campaign B). We choose the same US\$10 share price for both campaigns.

For both campaigns, we kept the same content format, which displayed the funding goal, equity offering, campaign description, and exit strategy. We excluded information such as the funding progress and the funders who had already invested to avoid introducing potential peer influences. Funding duration that constantly changes throughout the funding process was also excluded since it can potentially affect funders’ decision-making. We manipulated our focal treatments while keeping all other information identical to the original campaigns on Crowdcube (henceforth original campaign) to ensure information equivalency across treatment groups. To prevent participants from gaining any informational advantage, we pseudonymized all identifiable entities, such as the company, person, and geographic location. After viewing each campaign, participants were required to answer a survey to indicate their perceptions of the self-confidence traits of the fundraiser, their own investment confidence, and other control variables before making their investment decisions. We randomized the order of the two investment tasks for each participant to eliminate ordering effects.

Figure 2 depicts our experimental procedure. Several rounds of pretests were conducted to ensure the realism of the selected campaigns and to refine the experimental procedure and survey instruments. We then carried out a pilot study with 40 participants to rule out any potential logistical issues in our experiment. Most participants took 20 to 30 minutes to complete both investment tasks in the pilot study. We thus decided on a US\$5 base compensation for participants who completed the experiment.

## Treatment Manipulation

Funding goal (FG): Two levels of funding goals (High FG versus Low FG) were implemented for each campaign by manipulating the funding goal of the original campaign. We increased the original funding goal by 50% for the High FG condition while decreasing the original funding goal by 50% for the Low FG condition.

Equity retention (ER): Equity retention was manipulated by changing the percentage of the equity offering of the original campaign. We reduced the original percentage of equity offering by 50% for the high equity retention condition (High ER) and increased the original percentage of equity offering by 50% for the low equity retention condition (Low ER).

Tone assertiveness (TA): We employed the IBM Watson Tone Analyzer <sup>6</sup> to assess the fundraiser’s use of assertive tones in presenting their business venture. The analyzer can identify the segments of a document that are presented in a tentative tone, the opposite of an assertive tone. We utilized the analyzer to assess the description of each campaign and counted the percentage of sentences with a tentative tone. We then calculated tone assertiveness by reversing the percentage of tentative sentences throughout a campaign description. Our results show that over 93% and 91% of the content in Campaigns A and B, respectively, were presented in an assertive (i.e., nontentative) tone. Thus, we preserved the original campaign descriptions of both campaigns for the high tone assertiveness condition (High TA). To manipulate campaign descriptions for the low tone assertiveness condition (Low TA), we adjusted the tone adopted in presenting each campaign to a more tentative tone while preserving all factual information. After the adjustment, the tone assertiveness of Campaigns A and B was reduced to 53.1% and 52.8%, respectively. To ensure that our manipulation of tone assertiveness did not undermine information equivalency across the treatment groups, we conducted a validation test with 20 MTurk workers. Each worker was asked to assess whether the information provided in the campaign descriptions in the High TA and the Low TA condition were identical. All workers reported that the two versions of campaign descriptions presented equivalent information. This result confirms that our manipulation of tone assertiveness did not alter the information of the two campaign descriptions.

IPO intention: We manipulated IPO intention by modifying the exit strategy in which fundraisers specified options of equity liquidation. For campaigns in the IPO condition (IPO), the exit strategy explicitly indicated an intention for IPO. For campaigns in the non-IPO condition (Non-IPO), IPO was not mentioned explicitly as a potential option in the exit strategy.

Time pressure: In the condition with time pressure (TP), we set a time limit for viewing each campaign such that participants would feel pressured to finish reading the campaign material while still having sufficient time to do so. We conducted a pretest among 20 postgraduate students to estimate a reasonable time for reading through the campaign material. We set the time limit used to apply time pressure to the longest read-through time found in the pretest. Participants in the TP condition were given 90-second and 85-second time limits, respectively, to read Campaigns A and B. In the condition without time pressure (Non-TP), we set a generous 20-minute time limit so that participants would not feel pressured to read quickly. Doing this allowed us to ensure an information equivalency between the TP and Non-TP conditions (Kocher et al., 2013). ## Variables and Measurements

Both survey instruments and participants’ behavioral traces were leveraged to measure our focal and control variables. We adapted established multi-item measures for all perceptual variables (see Appendix A). All items were measured using a seven-point Likert scale.

Perceived fundraiser self-confidence: We assessed participants’ perceptions of the fundraiser’s ambition (FAMB) by adapting Elchardus and Smits’s (2008) measurement items of career ambition. Carter et al.’s (2003) items that gauge entrepreneurs’ need for independence were adapted to evaluate participants’ perceptions of the fundraiser’s independence (FIND). Similarly, we measured participants’ perceptions of the fundraiser’s assertiveness (FASS) and optimism (FOPT) with items adapted from Ames and Flynn (2007) and Adomako et al. (2016), respectively. We assessed participants’ perceptions of the fundraiser’s overall self-confidence (FCNF) using Dimov’s (2010) measure of entrepreneurial self-confidence.

Investment confidence: We used participants’ investment confidence to assess their confidence in the positive prospect of the campaigned business venture (Venture\_Confidence) and the excess return they would expect to gain from investing in the campaign (Return\_Confidence). Venture\_Confidence was assessed with three items adapted from Yang et al. (2012). Return\_Confidence was evaluated with five items adapted from Hoffmann and Post (2016) that captured how confident participants are about gaining excess returns from investing in a campaign.

Investment decision: We relied on a dummy variable to indicate whether participants would decide to invest in the campaign that was presented to them (Invest\_Decision). Furthermore, we measured the number of shares a participant wished to invest (#Shares\_Invested) as an indicator of their commitment to the investment.

Control variables: Campaign materials can potentially be leveraged by funders to gauge the quality and trustworthiness of the campaigned business venture (Mochkabadi & Volkmann, 2020). We controlled for the potential influence of participants’ perceived quality (Perceived\_Quality) (Tan et al., 2013) and trustworthiness (Trust) (Adjei & Clark, 2010) of the campaigned business venture on both their perceived fundraiser selfconfidence and their investment decisions. We further included five demographic variables in our analysis, including participants’ Age, Gender, Income, educational background (Edu), and work status (Work). We also included six extra control variables pertaining to participants’ crowdfunding experience: (1) the number of equity crowdfunding platforms they have visited (#Platform\_Visited), (2) the number of ventures they have invested in (#Venture\_Invested), (3) the number of industries they are interested in investing in (#Industry\_Interested), (4) their selfassessed knowledge of the campaigned business venture (Venture\_Knowledge), and (5) their self-evaluated financial literacy (Fin\_Literacy) (Bellofatto et al., 2018).

## Data Analysis

## Sample Description

total of 1,005 MTurk workers participated in the experiment; 34 were disqualified since they failed to pass the qualification test. A trap question was inserted into each post-task survey as an attention check mechanism to catch participants who did not pay attention when answering the questionnaire. We excluded 42 participants who failed to answer the trap questions correctly. The remaining 929 samples were included in the data analysis.Our samples were evenly distributed in both gender and age. Among the 929 participants, 55% were male and 45% were female (Gender). In terms of age, 15% of the participants were under 30, 41% were between 31 and 40, 23% were between 41 and 50, 13% were between 51 and 60, and 9% were above 60 (Age). Over half of the participants reported a yearly disposable income of between US\$25,000 and US\$75,000 (Income). A majority of the participants were paid employees (88%). Analytical results revealed no significant difference in the distribution of demographical variables across all treatment groups, indicating successful randomization of samples in our experiment.

## Effects of Confidence Cues

## Effects of Time Pressure

In the absence of time pressure, participants’ perceptions of campaign quality and fundraiser trustworthiness varied significantly between the Low FG condition and the High FG condition and between the Low TA condition and the High TA condition. A high funding goal significantly diminished participants’ perception of campaign quality and fundraiser trustworthiness when they did not experience time pressure. Conversely, participants in the High TA and Non-TP conditions reported significantly higher Perceived\_ Quality and Trust than those in the Low TA and Non-TP conditions. Noteworthily, when time pressure was applied, the differences in participants perception of campaign quality and fundraiser trustworthiness became nonsignificant across all treatment conditions. These results further substantiate that imposing time pressure when participants read the campaign materials indeed suppressed their effortful inferences regarding campaign quality and fundraiser trustworthiness based on the fundraiser’s funding goal and tone assertiveness.

## Discussion for Study 1

Study 1 offers empirical evidence for the role of confidence contagion in driving funders’ decisions to invest in an equity crowdfunding campaign. Our findings unveil how funders can sense each fundraiser’s self-confidence trait from the corresponding confidence cue. We conclude that funders perceive fundraisers to be more ambitious if they have a higher funding goal and perceive them to be more independent if they offer fewer equity shares. Further, funders perceive fundraisers to be more assertive if they sense a more assertive tone in their campaign materials and perceive fundraisers who target an IPO in their exit strategy to be more optimistic. The impacts of confidence cues on funders’ perceptions of these four selfconfidence traits persist even when the cognitive evaluation of the campaign material is suppressed by an imposed time pressure. Although funders may interpret the funding goal, equity retention, and IPO intention as quality signals of the campaigned business venture (Mochkabadi & Volkmann, 2020), we found that the aforementioned three information cues, along with tone assertiveness in campaign materials, can function as confidence cues that convey the self-confidence traits of fundraisers.

Our findings demonstrate that funders can indeed be infected by the fundraiser’s confidence in a campaigned business venture via confidence contagion. Funders’ perceptions of the selfconfidence traits of fundraisers can be translated into their own confidence in the eventual success of the business venture. Funders affected by confidence contagion would also expect to gain more excess returns from investing in the campaign. We conclude that funders’ confidence in both the venture’s success and the investment returns drives their investment decisions.

Taken together, the Study 1 findings suggest a social appraisal account of confidence contagion, which suggests that funders align themselves with the fundraiser’s confidence when determining the desirability of investing in a campaigned business venture (Parkinson & Simons, 2009).

To further validate the social appraisal mechanism of confidence contagion, we applied time pressure in Study 1 to suppress a parallel inference process that could have affected funders’ investment decisions. As pointed out by past studies (Kleinert et al., 2020), with sufficient time to deliberate, funders may rely on quality signals when deciding which campaign to invest in. They may also pay attention to trustworthiness signals to steer clear of opportunistic fundraisers (Mochkabadi & Volkmann, 2020). After imposing time pressure to disrupt these effortful inference processes, the effect of confidence contagion on funders’ investment decisions remained consistent while the effect of quality and trustworthiness inference subsided.

## Study 2: Impact of Confidence Contagion

## Data Collection

In Study 2, we leveraged a dataset collected from Crowdcube to validate our hypotheses about how these four confidence cues generate funding surges (H3 to H5) and, in turn, lead to campaign overfunding (H6). We deployed an automated scraper to track the daily funding activities of all 272 campaigns launched on Crowdcube between Jan 2013 and May 2015. Since its initial launch in 2011, Crowdcube has grown into the largest equity crowdfunding platform worldwide, facilitating 25% of all early-stage investment deals in the U.K. (Shafi, 2021). Our initial sample size was comparable to past studies that have investigated equity crowdfunding using Crowdcube data from similar observation periods (e.g., Shafi, 2021; Vismara, 2018).

Business ventures that raise capital on Crowdcube are subject to a prescreening process in which the platform exercises due diligence by examining the business’s value proposition and financial status. Upon successfully completing the prescreening process, fundraisers can set up their campaign by providing their funding goal, percentage of equity offering, exit strategy, and other supporting documents using the template offered by the platform. Crowdcube operates in an “all-or-nothing” manner such that the platform only releases funders’ investment to fundraisers if the campaign reaches its predetermined funding goal. All campaigns launched on Crowdcube during our observation period remained active for a fixed duration of 60 days. On Crowdcube, if a campaign reaches the predetermined funding goal before the end of the funding period, the fundraisers must decide whether to overfund the campaign or terminate the funding process early and collect the funds. In exchange for accepting funds beyond their original funding goal, fundraisers are required to offer additional equity shares.

We observed 191 campaigns that reached their funding goals. The number of successful campaigns in our dataset closely matched the number reported in publicly available statistics.<sup>7</sup> Among the 191 campaigns, 64 were terminated before the end of the default 60-day funding period because the fundraisers of these campaigns decided not to overfund. The remaining 132 campaigns accepted the excess funds and were overfunded by 52.9% on average. For each campaign, we collected its daily funding activities and all other available attributes, including campaign description, exit strategy, market sector, target funder (i.e., U.K. or worldwide), predetermined funding goal, and equity reserved by fundraisers. We employed all 54,869 funding activities of the 191 successful campaigns to test the impact of confidence cues in generating funding surges (H3- H5). To examine the relationship between funding surges and overfunding (H6), we excluded the 64 campaigns that terminated early since they declined to overfund the campaign.

## Modeling Funding Surges as Hawkes Intensity Process (HIP)

According to the extant financial literature (Bacry et al., 2015; Hawkes, 2018), intermittent surges in a sequence of events (e.g., funders’ investment) can be effectively modeled as a Hawkes process (Hawkes, 1971). The Hawkes process is a self-excited point process that has demonstrated its capacity to fit bursty intermittent dynamics in many social, biological, and economic systems (Bacry et al., 2015; Hawkes, 2018). For example, the Hawkes process and its variations have been applied to model popularity trends of social media content (Yoo et al. 2019), epidemic trends (Schoenberg et al., 2019), and fluctuations in financial markets (Bacry et al., 2015). In this study, we adopted a generalized Hawkes process model, namely the Hawkes intensity process (HIP) (Rizoiu et al., 2017), to model the funding surges of each campaign. HIP is particularly well-suited for this study because it models each investment event as a joint outcome of endogenous reactions to previous events and exogenous influences (i.e., campaign materials). Like the standard Hawkes process, the estimated parameters of HIP can be directly translated into metrics of intermittent surges (Yoo et al. 2019).

According to HIP (Rizoiu et al., 2017), the investment intensity of a given campaign at time ?? is governed by an intensity function ??[??] that combines exogenous stimulus ?? (e.g., campaign materials) and a convolution of its own investment history with a power-law memory kernel $( \tau + c ) ^ { - ( 1 + \theta ) }$ . While HIP can model the variation of an exogenous stimulus ?? along the time dimension, we set ?? to be constant because campaign materials remained unchanged throughout the funding process. The funding intensity function ??[??] is given as follows:

$$
\xi [ t ] = \pmb {\mu} s + C \sum_ {\tau = 1} ^ {t} \xi [ t - \tau ] (\tau + \pmb {c}) ^ {- (1 + \theta)},\tag{1}
$$

where $\mu$ quantifies the influence of $s$ , and $c$ is a cut-of parameter that determines funders’ reactivity to an equity crowdfunding campaign.<sup>8</sup> The power-law exponent ?? dictates the temporal decay of funding intensity, while ∁ is a scaling factor for the expected number of future investments. One of the key metrics that describes virality in Hawkes processes is the branching factor $n ^ { * }$ , defined as the expected number of descendant events directly spawned by a given event (Mishra et al., 2016). Specifically, $n ^ { * }$ is the integration of the triggering kernel over time and can be computed as $\frac { \textsf { C } } { \theta c ^ { \theta } }$ (Rizoiu et al., 2017). Accordingly, we reversed the cut-off parameter ?? to measure funding reactivity. We then computed $n ^ { * }$ to gauge funding virality. Finally, we inversed temporal decay $1 / \theta$ to measure funding persistency.

For each campaign, we modeled its daily funding activities using HIP. We included funding activities made before the campaign reached its funding goal to generate proxies of the three metrics of funding surges. Excluding funding activities made during the overfunding period allowed us to derive consistent measures of funding surges for both overfunded campaigns and those that refused overfunding by terminating early. It also helps circumvent the potential reverse causality caused by the simultaneity between funding surges and the degree of overfunding. We followed Rizoiu et al.’s (2017) method to estimate all parameters of HIP by minimizing the squared error between the observed daily funding activities ??̅[??] and the predicted funding intensity ??[??] using the L-BFGS optimization algorithm.

We conducted a goodness-of-fit test to assess the performance of HIP. In line with Kong et al. (2020) and Yoo et al. (2019), the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) test was employed to examine the fitness of each of the fitted HIPs to the observed funding activities of the corresponding campaign. All null hypotheses of our KS tests were rejected at a significance level of 0.05 (KS distance ranged from 0.028 to 0.350, p-values ranged from 0.063 to 0.999), meaning that the predicted funding intensity ??[??] of the corresponding HIP matched the actual funding activities of all 191 campaigns in our dataset.

## Defining Focal Variables

We employed HIP to model the funding surges of each campaign and calculated the reversed cut-off parameter ??, the branching factor ??<sup>∗</sup>, and the inverse power-law exponent 1/?? to operationalize funding reactivity (Reactivity), funding virality (Virality), and funding persistency (Persistency), respectively. Consistent with Study 1, the fundraiser’s funding goal (FG), equity retention (ER), and IPO intention (IPO) were extracted from the campaign materials. Similarly, we employed the IBM Watson Tone Analyzer to detect tentative tones in campaign descriptions in order to operationalize the fundraiser’s tone assertiveness (TA) as the proportion of nontentative segments in each campaign description.

Potential predictors of crowdfunding success, as identified in extant literature (Csepy et al., 2020; Mochkabadi & Volkmann, 2020), were included as control variables in our analysis. These factors spanned campaign attributes, fundraiser characteristics, and temporal effects. We controlled for a wide range of campaign attributes including the (1) length of campaign description (Description\_Len), (2) provision of financial snapshots (Fin\_Snapshot), (3) inclusion of tax relief policy (Tax\_Relief), (4) target funder (Target\_Funder), (5) location of headquarters (Location), and (6) whether the campaign targeted technological sectors (Tech\_Sector). Additionally, we controlled for the informativeness of each campaign description. To do so, we performed an intent analysis <sup>9</sup> on campaign descriptions to classify the intent of each segment of a campaign description into opinion, news, marketing, query, or spam. We then operationalized the informativeness of each campaign (Informativeness) by calculating the proportion of informative content (i.e., opinion, news, and query).

Two coders were recruited to count the number of appearances by the fundraiser in the introductory video to control for funders’ exposure to the fundraiser (#Video\_Exposure). Past studies have found that veteran fundraisers who have launched multiple campaigns on the platform are more likely to succeed due to the social capital amassed by previous campaigns (Buttice et al., 2017). We therefore included a dummy variable Serial\_Pitch to indicate campaigns launched by veteran fundraisers. The year at which each campaign launched (Year) was included as a fixed effect.

We also controlled for the interplay among the metrics of funding surges in our analysis. For instance, an earlier emerging or more viral funding surge will have more momentum and will thus take longer to dissipate (i.e., Persistency) (Yang & Leskovec, 2011). We therefore included Reactivity and Virality as control variables when estimating Persistency. Similarly, Reactivity was included as a control variable when estimating Virality. We calculated the degree of overfunding (DOF) as the proportion of excess funds over the predetermined funding goal. ## Post Hoc Analysis

Mediation testing: Following Rosseel (2012), we conducted a mediation analysis to substantiate the mediating role of funding surge metrics (see Table 8). We found that all four confidence cues contributed to the degree of overfunding by stimulating funding surges. The inclusion of funding virality as a mediator significantly diminished the impact of the funding goal on overfunding. The effects of equity retention on the degree of overfunding were fully mediated by funding virality. Although tone assertiveness and IPO intention exerted both direct and indirect positive impacts on the degree of overfunding, the indirect impact was comparatively stronger.

Noteworthily, although not directly increasing the degree of overfunding, funding reactivity significantly contributed to overfunding by stimulating funding virality and persistency. Additionally, funding virality also indirectly amplified the degree of overfunding by boosting funding persistency. In conclusion, all three metrics of funding surges, including funding reactivity, virality, and persistency, inflated the degree of overfunding, both directly and indirectly.

Self-selection bias: Because fundraisers are free to choose whether to overfund the campaign after surpassing the predetermined funding goal, excluding campaigns that refused overfunding could have introduced self-selection bias in estimating the relationship between funding surges and campaign overfunding (H6). To determine whether our model estimates were confounded by this potential issue, we applied Heckman’s two-stage estimation procedure to estimate the magnitude of the potential sample selection bias. Heckman’s two-stage estimation procedure leverages the generalized residuals (i.e., inverse Mills ratios) estimated from a probit selection model (first-stage estimation) to estimate the selection bias in the model of interest (secondstage estimation) (Clougherty et al., 2016).

Heckman’s two-stage estimation procedure requires a valid exclusion restriction (i.e. an observed variable that impacts the fundraiser’s decision to overfund but not the degree of overfunding) for the identification of parameters in the second-stage model. Since we were unable to satisfy the exclusion restriction with the variables in our data, we leveraged a statistical procedure developed by Zhelonkin et al. (2016) to circumvent this issue. Zhelonkin et al. (2016) developed a robust two-stage estimator that does not require the exclusion restriction to provide a reliable estimation. First, Zhelonkin et al. (2016) enhanced the robustness of Heckman’s two-stage model by replacing the original maximum likelihood and ordinary least square estimators with two general M-estimators of the Mallows type equipped with the classical Huber functions. The robustified model was shown to be less sensitive to the influence of outliers and the violation of sample distribution assumptions (e.g., normality assumption of the error terms). Second, they relaxed the exclusion restriction by splitting the design space of predictors when computing the robustness weights in the Huber functions. Their Monte Carlo simulation showed that for observations that are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.), their estimator can produce a more reliable estimation compared to Heckman’s method, especially when the normality assumption or the exclusion restriction cannot be guaranteed.<sup>10</sup>

We applied Zhelonkin et al.’s (2016) robust two-stage estimator implemented in the R package “ssmrob” (Zhelonkin & Ronchetti, 2021) to reestimate Model 14. We assume that our observations were i.i.d. since each campaign in our dataset was created and managed independently on Crowdcube. Two tuning parameters in the Huber functions of the M-estimators, the tuning constant and leverage weights, were set to 1.345 and the hat matrix, respectively, as recommended by Zhelonkin et al. (2016) and Cantoni and Ronchetti (2001). Models 15 and 16 in Table 7 present the results, which were estimated by applying Zhelonkin et al.’s (2016) robust estimator and the original Heckman’s estimator, respectively. Similar to Model 14, the positive effects of funding virality and persistency on the degree of overfunding remained consistent in the second-stage model estimated by both methods. Neither mothed produced a significant inverse Mills ratio, hence ruling out the potential confound of self-selection bias.

## Discussion for Study 2

Study 2 shows how confidence cues can stimulate surges in funding activities. It complements Study 1 by showing how confidence contagion manifests at a collective level in funding processes. By modeling the daily funding activities of each campaign as a Hawkes process, we distilled three key metrics of funding surges: funding reactivity, virality, and persistency. We found that equity retention, tone assertiveness, and IPO intention can stimulate funding reactivity so that the initial funding surges emerge sooner in the funding process. All four confidence cues, including the funding goal, can enhance funding virality so that more funders would be likely to pledge support in a synchronized manner. Furthermore, tone assertiveness and IPO intention can help to sustain funding surges by enhancing funding persistency.

We also discovered reinforcement effects among the metrics of funding surges, meaning that funding surges that emerge at an earlier stage would have more participating funders and last longer. This finding is in line with Etter et al. (2013), who noted that early progress achieved in a crowdfunding campaign is often deterministic of its eventual success. Moreover, funding surges that galvanize more funders can better sustain their momentum over time.

Study 2 also substantiates the prominent role of funding surges in mediating the effects of confidence cues on overfunding. Our findings show that funding virality mediates the impacts induced by all four confidence cues, while funding persistency mediates those induced by tone assertiveness and IPO intention. In line with the extant literature (Martínez-Gómez et al., 2020), the funding goal imposes a negative direct effect on overfunding. This effect offsets the positive indirect relationship between the funding goal and overfunding via funding virality.

## Discussion and Conclusion

In combination, Study 1 and Study 2 jointly reveal the surges in funding activities stimulated by confidence contagion as a key predictor of the extent to which an equity crowdfunding campaign is overfunded. Study 1 answers our first research question by showing how information cues in campaign materials can act as confidence cues to engender confidence contagion. Funders can sense the fundraiser’s self-confidence traits, including ambition, independence, assertiveness, and optimism from these confidence cues and in turn become more confident about investing in the campaigned business venture. In other words, funders align their appraisal of a campaigned business venture with the fundraiser’s self-confidence, which they perceive from confidence cues. Moreover, Study 1 helps to distinguish this social appraisal mechanism of confidence contagion from parallel effortful inference mechanisms that can potentially affect funders’ investment decisions such as quality assessment and trust formation.

Study 2 answers our second research question on how the confidence contagion induced by confidence cues can drive overfunding by stimulating funding surges. We show that funding virality and persistency mediate the effects of confidence cues on the degree to which an equity crowdfunding campaign is overfunded. Additionally, our findings accentuate funding reactivity as the determining factor in intensifying funding surges. Surges that emerge earlier are likely to galvanize more funders and gain more momentum. In this sense, we confirm that an equity crowdfunding campaign that contains confidence cues can magnify surges of funding activities and inflate the excess funds it receives beyond its funding goal.

## Implications for Theory

The findings of this research contribute to the extant literature on crowdfunding on three fronts. First, we contextualize contagion theory in equity crowdfunding and uncover a distinct social appraisal mechanism that stimulates confidence contagion from campaign materials among prospective funders. We then identify four types of confidence cues from the campaign materials as drivers of confidence contagion. Funders can perceive each of the four key self-confidence traits highlighted in the entrepreneurial context (De Jorge Moreno et al., 2007; Kirkpatick & Locke, 1991; Zou et al., 2016) from corresponding confidence cues. As a result, prospective funders adjust their feelings toward a campaigned business venture in line with the fundraiser’s confidence in it. Compelled by this transferred feeling of confidence, prospective funders may find it difficult to resist the urge to become part of the business venture.

This finding extends Li et al.’s (2017) work on the contagion of passion for products/services in reward-based crowdfunding. The confidence contagion we examined is prevalent in the context of equity crowdfunding where investments are predominantly driven by confidence in the profitability of the campaigned business venture (Payne et al., 2009; Zafar et al., 2021). Findings from this study also resonate with extant literature on equity crowdfunding suggesting that most funders on equity crowdfunding platforms are casual investors who do not feel compelled to make investment decisions based on sophisticated financial analysis (Kleinert et al., 2020). Our findings show that the role of confidence contagion in steering funders’ decision-making becomes even more prominent when they have less time to engage in quality assessments of the campaigned business venture.

In validating the confidence contagion enacted by confidence cues, this study is among the first to offer empirical evidence of the role of confidence contagion in driving overfunding, an extraordinary outcome for funders and fundraisers alike. This study extends prior research on overfunding (Adamska-Mieruszewska et al., 2019; Martínez-Gómez et al., 2020) by investigating overfunding as an extraordinary outcome driven by an alteration of affect states rather than a mitigation of information asymmetry. Confidence contagion induced by confidence cues at the individual level can manifest on a collective level by arousing a collective mood of optimism and enthusiasm for the success of the campaigned business venture. Our study is among the first to take advantage of the Hawkes process to quantify this shared confidence manifested as surges in funding activities. Our empirical results demonstrate that all three metrics of funding surges, which indicate the activation of confidence contagion, play a prominent mediating role between confidence cues and the degree of overfunding.

Methodology-wise, we showcase how experiments can be combined with secondary data analysis to enhance our understanding of a collective phenomenon driven by a bottomup driving mechanism. On the one hand, the experiment we conducted in Study 1 allowed us to isolate the core mechanism we sought to investigate (i.e., confidence contagion) from the parallel mechanism at an individual level and prove its activation with greater confidence. Subsequently, by analyzing secondary data collected from a real equity crowdfunding platform in Study 2, we proved that confidence cues can indeed drive up overfunding by shaping funding surges at a collective level. Study 1 complements Study 2 because of the infeasibility of directly observing the activation of confidence contagion in our secondary data. The findings in Study 1 helped to connect the metrics of funding surges to the activation of confidence contagion via confidence cues. Study 2 elevates Study 1 because the real-world impact of confidence contagion (i.e., overfunding) requires a certain time period to take effect and a large funder base to manifest, making it extremely difficult to realistically recreate in an experimental setting.

## Implications for Practice

Our findings can help fundraisers take advantage of overfunding, the distinctive feature of equity crowdfunding, to generate publicity and build a larger community of supporters. In particular, for fundraisers seeking to overfund their campaigns, while offering fewer equity shares, adopting a more assertive tone, and laying out a concrete plan for an IPO would seem to be counterintuitive for a startup that has yet to be launched, such confidence cues can sometimes trump rationality, as demonstrated by this study. The insignificant total effect of the funding goal on overfunding implies that it is possible for equity crowdfunding campaigns at all scales to be overfunded. Hence, fundraisers need not specify an inflated funding goal to project their ambition. Rather, it is recommended that they set their funding goal at a level that aligns with the financial need of their business venture.

Since our findings testify to the mediating role of funding surges on the relationship between confidence cues and overfunding, fundraisers could explore more options to induce such funding surges. Because the timing of the initial emergence of funding surges, i.e., funding reactivity, was found to be highly impactful for the effectiveness of funding surges, fundraisers might want to plan the initial launch of their equity crowdfunding campaign more carefully to try to promote an early surge in funding activities. For instance, fundraisers might consider preannouncing their campaign launch or organizing prelaunch events across all major social media platforms to increase public awareness. Fundraisers could also offer early-bird benefits to entice prospective funders to invest as early as possible following the campaign launch.

The findings could be valuable for fundraisers seeking to strategize their campaigns in that they could use them to preemptively plan for the possibility of overfunding ahead of the campaign launch rather than passively react when initial funding goals are reached. Equity crowdfunding platforms can facilitate such preemptive planning by leveraging the confidence cues embedded in the campaign material to predict the extent to which a campaign would be overfunded. If the overfunding of their business venture would contribute little to advancing a fundraiser’s strategic objectives, it may not be worthwhile to relinquish additional equity shares in exchange for the extra cash (Wrisk, 2017). Platforms could employ the Hawkes process modeling approach conducted in this study to adjust the prediction made for the extent of overfunding in real time based on intermittent surges in funding activities. A more accurate prediction would allow fundraisers to foresee the previously unforeseeable and, in turn, make more informed decisions about whether to overfund their business ventures.

## Limitations and Future Research

This study comes with three caveats that are worth noting. First, due to privacy protections, the exact monetary value of the investment pledged by each funder was not accessible on the equity crowdfunding platform where we collected our secondary data. By concentrating on the number of funders who participated in daily funding activities, we examined the impact of confidence cues at a collective level. Nonetheless, future research could further this line of investigation by examining whether confidence contagion can impact individual choices regarding investment amounts. Second, while the ideal sample for Study 1 would be those who have actually participated in the equity crowdfunding campaigns covered by our secondary data, due to the Crowdcube privacy policy, we were unable to reach out directly to funders on the platform. Following prior research combining experiments with secondary data analysis (Feola et al., 2019), we sought to ensure the representativeness of participants in our experiment by implementing the same screening questions adopted by Crowdcube. Those who successfully passed these screening questions were also eligible to invest on Crowdcube. Third, although we made our best efforts to ensure the comprehensiveness of the confidence cues, we acknowledge the possibility of other information cues at play that could have given rise to confidence contagion. We encourage future studies to seek more information cues that could potentially lead to confidence contagion in other crowdfunding settings.

## Appendix B

## Measurement Validity for Study 1

We assessed the convergent and discriminant validities of our measurement model. To examine individual item reliability, we checked all item loadings and confirmed that all loadings (see Appendix A) were above the recommended tolerance of 0.70 on their intended constructs and below 0.40 on the other factors (Hair et al., 2011). We further tested the internal consistency of the constructs by examining their average variance extracted (AVE) values and Cronbach’s alpha values. The results, as presented in Appendix A, show that all AVEs were all higher than the recommended threshold of 0.5 (Hair et al., 2011). In addition, the high Cronbach’s alpha values, ranging from 0.86 to 0.97, confirm the reliability of all scales. These demonstrate the convergent validity of our measurement model. We verified the discriminant validity by comparing the square root of the AVE to the interconstruct correlations. Discriminant validity was supported since the square root of the AVE are all greater than the interconstruct correlations (Hair et al., 2011) and the cross-loadings of the items on other constructs are low.

To rule out the possible contamination of common method bias in our experimental results, we, first, applied the Harman one-factor extraction test by performing principal component analysis for all measurement items. Results showed that the first factor accounted for 40.6% of the total covariance, lower than the threshold of 50% (Podsakoff, 2003). Second, we confirmed that there were no significant multicollinearity problems regarding our predictors. All of the VIFs were lower than 3, with the highest VIF being 2.42 (Hair et al., 2011).
