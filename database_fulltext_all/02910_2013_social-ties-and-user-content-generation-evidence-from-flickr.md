# Social Ties and User Content Generation: Evidence from Flickr

Xiaohua Zeng, Liyuan Wei

College of Business, City University of Hong Kong, Kowloon Tong, Kowloon, Hong Kong S.A.R. {xiaohua.zeng@cityu.edu.hk, liyuan.wei@cityu.edu.hk}

he content created by the users of social networking sites has reached such high levels of quality and variety Tthat it is comparable to that produced by professional agencies. Therefore, understanding what types of content users generate and the underlying motivational factors is vital to the success of the sites. The extant research on content generation has primarily focused on the amount of content and on how to encourage participation in content creation, and less attention has been paid to the content itself and how social relations affect the types of content that users upload. This study aims to empirically document the relationship between social ties and the similarities between the types of content that people create online. We collected a large data set from the photo-hosting website Flickr detailing the users’ social relations over time in conjunction with their photo-uploading behavior. We found that around the time of the formation of a social tie, members of dyads began to upload more similar photos than they did before that time. After a social tie was formed, this similarity evolved in different ways in different subgroups of dyads. Whereas the similarity between photos uploaded by dyads experiencing notably different popularity levels on the site continued to grow, the dyads of users with similar levels of popularity gradually began to upload less similar photos. In cultural production, individuals appear to present themselves as unique; this feature is more salient when the social contacts are similar in popularity status. Photo-shooting behaviors have been found to exhibit the same patterns. Furthermore, we show that the most divergent uploading behavior is observed when a high-popularity user initiates a tie with a user with lower popularity. We use social psychological motivations to explain these results.

Key words: user-generated content; social networks; computer-mediated communication and collaboration; within-subjects design; photography; distinctiveness; tags; Flickr

## 1. Introduction

One of the most important developments in the areas of media and information systems over the past decade has been the explosive growth of usergenerated content (UGC) with the help of social networking sites such as Flickr and YouTube. These online platforms integrate content generation and content sharing through social networks. The original content created by the users of these sites has developed such remarkable quality and variety that it is said to rival content produced by professional agencies. For instance, over 120,000 Flickr photos have been selected into the Flickr Collection on Getty Images, and can be licensed for use in presentations, brochures, or newsletters.<sup>1</sup> This provides the Flickr users with an opportunity to monetize their interesting and marketable content. The users of such sites do not merely share their photos with a community: they also become suppliers of content exhibiting their idiosyncratic interests, tastes, and skill sets.

Through the social functions available at sites such as Flickr, users can access and learn from others. UGC has become an integral part of the culture business and a means by which users may present themselves. Given the increasing economic, cultural, and sociological significance of UGC, there is no doubt that it deserves more attention from the purveyors of social media platforms, their users, and academic researchers alike.

Content management is critical for individual and organizational users who have begun to realize the potential of these content-sharing communities for marketing purposes. The millions of photostream owners on Flickr or video channels on YouTube must compete strategically for attention, which is currently a central issue in social media marketing (Chan 2011). Users are driven to be well connected and to maintain distinct identities. Social media marketers need to carefully determine whom they should connect with socially, what types of content they should upload, and the relationship between these two decisions. Building on the social psychological theories of distinctiveness and self-identity, this research aims to examine the relationship between social connectedness and the content that social media users generate and publicly display.

The quantity, variety, and quality of the content that users contribute are vital to social media for a number of reasons. First, the appeal and existence of social media sites depend on users’ contributions. Many online communities have reported problems with undercontribution, e.g., fewer than 20% of the users contribute to the website, and the remaining users share nothing (Hughes et al. 2005). Second, with the proliferation of social media sites, the competition is growing (Gnyawali et al. 2010). The variety and the quality of UGC help social media sites differentiate themselves from and gain the advantage over their competitors (Zhang and Sarvary 2011). Third, UGC lends social media sites a strong competitive status against professional or corporate media content providers. The Google-owned YouTube, for instance, is said to pose a real threat to cable television companies (Agarwal 2011). Finally, original and diverse content can attract a wider user base, increasing the site’s value to advertisers. For social media sites to motivate and manage contributions of content, they must understand the interaction between the social ties they help foster among their users and the content being generated.

This study aims to investigate how users’ contentcreation behaviors evolve as they form social ties. We collected data from the photo-hosting website Flickr on a large number of users’ social ties over time and photo-uploading behaviors, such as the types of uploaded photos as indicated by their tags. We compared the cosine similarities between photos that pairs of users uploaded during three time periods: before, around the time of, and some time after a social tie is formed between them. We found that people tend to upload more similar photos around the time of the formation of a social tie than they did long before the tie was formed. This is consistent with existing studies that suggest that the formation of social ties is driven by similar interests or shared activities. However, the high similarity between the types of photos the dyad’s members upload is not maintained, and gradually their uploaded photos become somewhat less similar. The difference between the popularity levels of the users’ content moderates this relationship. The dyads in which the two users are similarly popular diverge much more than those with greatly different popularity levels. We will use social psychological motivations to explain these results.

This research contributes to the literature in the following ways. First, we characterize and document the content being generated on social media. Studies abound on user contributions to online sharing communities, a majority of which discuss the undercontribution problem and suggest factors to motivate user participation (Butler 2001, Koh et al. 2007). Surprisingly little research has addressed the types of UGC. In the present study, we look beyond the questions of whether and how much the users contribute to online sharing communities and examine content types. In the Flickr context, we are able to examine cultural tastes and interests through the users’ uploaded photos.

Second, this study examines the relationship between social ties in an online social network and the content generated by its users. In particular, we rely on social psychological motivations to explain why online community users contribute certain types of content and how such behaviors are associated with the social ties they form with other users. Past research has generally shown that social ties encourage conformity and the diffusion of products or ideas. Our results show that the relationship between social ties and content creation is not uniform across subgroups of users; thus, we provide a more nuanced answer.

Third, we contribute to the literature on social networks by empirically testing our hypotheses regarding cultural processes. These social media data can provide a natural setting for identifying patterns in cultural tastes and their evolution in social media. Finally, using large-scale social media data, the findings of this study will complement the existing literature in cultural sociology that relies largely on surveys for data collection (e.g., Abrams 2009, Mark 2003). The panel data used in this study allow us to control for unobserved sources of user heterogeneity that may be correlated with the explanatory variables.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. We first discuss the institutional context of Flickr and its distinct features relative to various other social networking sites. Next, we review the related literature and develop the hypotheses. In §4, we describe the data and the data collection process. In §5, we discuss the similarity measure and perform the empirical analyses to test the hypotheses. We conclude the paper and briefly discuss the limitations of the study in §6.

## 2. The Research Context: Flickr.com

Flickr.com is currently one of the most popular photohosting websites, providing a wide range of online services and functioning as a content-sharing community. Created in 2004 and acquired by Yahoo! in 2005, Flickr now has 51 million registered members and nearly 80 million unique visitors worldwide.<sup>2</sup> Flickr users are primarily photography hobbyists, many of whom are serious amateurs or even professional photographers. Flickr allows its users to participate in this community in a number of ways, with some forms of participation being more social than others. In this section, we discuss the two major aspects of Flickr’s functionality; namely, photo management (i.e., the functions that are useful even to users who are unwilling to socialize) and social interactions (including building social ties, joining groups, and marking photos owned by other users as personal favorites). These two aspects correspond to the two main forms of participation on Flickr: contributing media content and socializing within the social network.

## 2.1. Photo Uploads and Tags

Flickr’s primary functionality is as a system for uploading and organizing photos. In addition to allowing users to upload photos directly, Flickr is integrated with many photo management applications, so the process of photo uploading is highly streamlined. For example, users of iPhoto, the photo management software for the Macintosh system, can directly upload photos from their iPhoto collections to Flickr. Furthermore, photo owners can define access rights to their images within the application and even choose a licensing protocol for their photographic works (Cheliotis 2009). The collection of all the photos uploaded by a user constitutes her “photostream.”

Flickr allows photo owners and permitted viewers to design and attach tags to uploaded photos. Tags are user-defined keywords or category labels that provide summary description about a photo to network search agents. They can be classified into four categories: time (e.g., “2007”); geographical information (e.g., “California”); technical information (e.g., “Canon”); and narratives or characterizations of people or occasions (e.g., “carnival,” “tree,” and “snow”). Clicking on a tag allows a user to retrieve all the photos labeled with that tag. By providing information on the photos, tags facilitate their organization and allow them to be retrieved by all users, including the photographs’ owners and all permitted viewers (Ames and Naaman 2007, van Zwol et al. 2008). Tags differentiate the photos semantically; they also help shape a semantic network among the photos and, more importantly, among their owners, which may or may not coincide with the social network observable through the dyadic ties (Capocci et al. 2010, Nov and Ye 2010, Schifanella et al. 2010). Thus, we consider tags to be appropriate indicators of photo types and use them to measure similarity among photos. Because tags are essentially categorizations that photo owners attach to their work, it is reasonable to regard them as a means of self-categorization. The public nature of photo sharing implies that the tags can represent the uploader’s social identity, as defined by the individual’s interests, tastes, and capabilities.

## 2.2. Favorite Marking

The number of times a photo is marked by viewers as a “favorite” (or “fave”) is one of the most important indicators of its general appeal. When viewing a photo, a user can mark it as a favorite, a process that is similar to bookmarking. All the photos she has marked are displayed in a collection called “Favorites.” The total number of times each photo is marked as a favorite is displayed on the Web page dedicated to that photo. Thus, favorite marking serves a social function by communicating a viewer’s appreciation for the photo and offers positive feedback directly to the uploader. A user whose photos are marked as a favorite more times is considered a more popular photo contributor. We use the average number of favorite markings per photo uploaded by a user as an indicator of her status.

## 2.3. Contacts and Groups

Similar to all other social networking sites, Flickr includes strong functionality to allow users to interact socially. For instance, users can subscribe to others’ uploaded content by adding other users as “contacts” and engage in conversations via comments and replies. A user can unilaterally decide to add another user as a contact, and the relationship does not have to be reciprocated. In this sense, adding contacts is similar to subscribing to channels on YouTube, where one party receives updated content from the subscribed channels but not the other way around. The type of social connection between two Flickr users can be any of the following: no social tie, one-way tie, or mutual tie. This practice is unlike “friending” on Facebook, in which two users have either no relationship or a mutually agreed-upon “friendship.” To add a contact is to form a directional social tie with another user in the community. Such an act suggests that the subscriber believes the photo owner is worth watching. A contact can be someone from the subscriber’s offline social network, such as a friend or family member, or someone unknown to the subscriber but considered interesting purely because of their uploaded photos. The effect of tie directions on users’ uploading behavior is analyzed and discussed in this study. Flickr users also have the option of joining photo groups. Being a member of a group allows the user to contribute to the group photo pool and obtain access to photos contributed by other members. The number of groups shared by user dyads is controlled for in our analyses.

We chose Flickr as our research context for several reasons. First, Flickr is a content-centric social medium and is well known for its large collection of user-created digital images. Hence, it provides a suitable context for the study of user content generation. Second, to understand a user’s interests and tastes, it is better to analyze a single media format without fearing the loss of user preference information that could be disclosed in other media formats. Flickr specialized in only one media format, i.e., digital images, at the time when the data used in this study were collected.<sup>3</sup> In this sense, it is regarded as a content-sharing community that is very similar to YouTube (Kaplan and Haenlein 2010). Unlike other social media, such as Facebook, where networking is the main purpose and users can share games, news links, and videos as well as photos, Flickr users express themselves primarily through photographyrelated activities. The availability of a single media format removes the possible externality imposed by the sharing of other media formats. For example, if both music and photos can be uploaded, then a user’s decision to upload a photo of a concert she just attended may be affected by whether she has also shared a song by the concert artist, confounding the analysis. Finally, the majority of photos on Flickr are identified by tags. This substantially facilitates the analysis of the similarity and dissimilarity among photos and between Flickr users.

## 3. Theoretical Background and Hypotheses

The literature on content generation has focused primarily on users’ levels of participation. Some researchers have attempted to identify the factors that affect user participation in the online environment (e.g., Chen et al. 2010, Trusov et al. 2010), especially the effect of motivational factors on users’ willingness to participate. These factors include user characteristics, such as community commitment (Bateman et al. 2011); characteristics of the existing network, such as group size (Zhang and Zhu 2011); and information system design features, such as the provision of quality feedback (Moon and Sproull 2008). Other researchers have examined how users allocate the time they spend on mobile devices between content generation and content consumption (Ghose and Han 2011). However, little is known about the motivational factors behind what content is being generated. This research aims to examine the content itself and how it is associated with the presence of social ties.

One general finding about social ties states that they are correlated with a convergence in behaviors or preferences. Two mechanisms associated with this convergence, homophily and contagion, are both supported by rich theory and empirical findings. Homophily refers to similar actors’ tendency to relate to each other (McPherson et al. 2001). Theoretically, homophily could be due to opportunity, shared true intrinsic characteristics, or affinity. In the Flickr context, two users could become social contacts because they have participated in the same event, they are interested in certain types of photographs, or they are located near each other. If so, we would be able to observe an increase in the similarities between the photos they uploaded leading up to the time at which they established a social tie. This leads to the first hypothesis.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1</sup> <sup>(H1).</sup> Around the time of forming a tie, the users in the dyad upload photos that are more similar than they were long before the formation of the dyadic tie.

Contagion describes the process whereby individuals become more alike because of their social relations. Evidence of contagion is abundant in the diffusion of products, services, and ideas. The research in this area usually adopts one of two approaches. The first relies on statistical methods and sociological perspectives on network topologies and dynamics (reviewed by Granovetter 2005 and Snijders 2011). The research adopting this methodology aims to study the relationship between social network structures and the diffusion of a variety of behaviors, such as the adoption of information systems (Yuan et al. 2009), physicians’ prescription choices (Nair et al. 2010), the spread of marketing campaign messages (Bampo et al. 2008), retweeting (Macskassy and Michelson 2011), and the diffusion of user-produced video content on YouTube (Susarla et al. 2012). Further, Aral and Walker (2011a) have suggested that firms can explicitly design features in their products, such as automated notifications and personalized invites, that facilitate the viral spread of product awareness. The other approach employs social psychological theories to reveal the driving factors of contagion and transmission (Cosley et al. 2005, Ling et al. 2005). For example, content associated with positive emotions and physiological arousal can drive the viral transmission of New York Times articles online (Berger 2011, Berger and Milkman 2012). Sheldon et al. (2011) have examined the need for relatedness as an underlying psychological driver in Facebook use, finding that people use Facebook to cope with their feelings of disconnection. Because the above literature concerns the diffusion but not the creation of content, it is natural to ask whether contagion is also a relevant factor in user content creation.

However, theories of social identity suggest that the force driving user content generation is the desire to establish a distinct identity. In particular, the uniqueness theories predict divergence, rather than convergence, in content generation. Social networking sites such as Flickr provide a technologically enhanced environment in which users can easily showcase their cultural tastes and interests. Previous research has established that consumers participate in online communities for the purpose of self-expression (Schau and Gilly 2003). Qualitative research also confirms that Flickr users seek to showcase their images, daily lives, life events, and friends as a way of self-presentation (Miller and Edwards 2007). Another perspective, self-performance, suggests that in social network sites, users seek to express their own viewpoints, aesthetic tastes, interests, and creativity and to manage the impressions made on the viewers through their personal profiles (Liu 2007) and/or shared photos (Van House 2007). Dellarocas et al. (2010) have found that consumers prefer to contribute reviews for obscure movies and big hits over movies with intermediate popularity. Feedback on the uniqueness of users’ contributions to a movie-rating website stimulates participation (Ludford et al. 2004). All this confirms that the need for uniqueness is a strong factor driving individuals to differentiate themselves. Two processes have been identified to explain the drive to differentiate: individuals do so either to help construct and maintain self-identity and self-worth (the internal process) or to signal their identity to others (the external process).

Focusing on the internal process, the uniqueness theory (Snyder and Fromlin 1980) posits that being overly similar to others induces negative emotions in individuals. The optimal distinctiveness theory states that people strive to optimize their self-evaluations by identifying with groups or social categories that are neither too inclusive nor too differentiating. The optimal level of distinctiveness is a dynamic equilibrium (Brewer 1991, see Leonardelli et al. 2010 for a review). Studies in the field of consumer psychology provide supporting evidence for the distinctiveness theory. For example, consumers satisfy their need for optimal distinctiveness by acquiring, using, and disposing of products (Lynn and Harris 1997, Tian et al. 2001), shopping in special venues (Lynn and Harris 2006), or even providing negative word of mouth to prevent others from owning what they themselves have bought (Cheema and Kaikati 2010). According to cultural sociology, self-esteem is maintained by participating in cultural activities and displaying distinct cultural tastes. For example, Abrams (2009) has found that young people attempt to achieve an optimal level of distinctiveness through their musical preferences. It is conceivable that Flickr users who post their own photographic work are concerned with their selfworth and are, therefore, attempting to be similar to their social contacts while simultaneously differentiating themselves.

As an external process, identity signaling has been proposed to explain why individuals differentiate themselves from others. For example, Zhang and Zubcsek (2010) have proposed a signaling theory, showing that users invest in online content generation that serves as an observable signal and can be used to infer their true types (e.g., intrinsic enjoyment, prosocial attitude, or high skill levels). Empirically, Berger and Heath (2007, 2008) have found that people tend to differentiate from groups with which they do not wish to be identified with by choosing divergent cultural tastes and publicly observable consumer goods. This identity-signaling theory is consistent with the context of this study, in which user-generated content serves as a signal that can be observed publicly to communicate the true identity of the photo owner. Hence, we expect to find that divergent content is generated between socially connected users.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2</sup> <sup>(H2).</sup> After a social tie is formed, the types of photos that the users in the dyad upload gradually become less similar.

Blau (1974) has suggested that actors in social networks can be different in multiple dimensions and has distinguished two types of actor attributes as the parameters of social structure: normative (horizontal attributes, such as sex and occupation) and graduated (vertical attributes of status rank order). Following this framework, in the Flickr context, we examined two such parameters to describe the differentiation between users. The similarity between uploaded photos is regarded as the horizontal dimension; the popularity, defined by the average number of favorites that a user’s photostream receives, provides the status rank order, or the vertical dimension. We hypothesized that the relative popularity status that the two users enjoy on Flickr may moderate the relationship between the users’ social ties and uploaded content. In particular, when two users of similar popularity establish a social tie, the competition between them is heightened, and they are tempted to diverge in the horizontal dimension by uploading photos of less similar themes or types. When a photographer who is much less or much more popular is added, the users have less motivation to differentiate; therefore, the users’ uploaded photos may continue to appear highly similar. Put differently, if an individual observes that her popularity is very close to that of the other person in the dyadic tie, her uploaded photographs come to diverge more strongly from those of the other user.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>3</sup> <sup>(H3).</sup> The popularity difference between the users in the dyad moderates the relationship between the social ties and photo similarity such that the similarity between photos declines more after tie formation in dyads exhibiting smaller differences in popularity.

We further speculated that when the users in the dyad have different levels of popularity, each individual user’s uploading behavior will depend on her relative status. Having highly popular photographers as contacts provides an opportunity to follow and appreciate their work, and accordingly, other users will sometimes gradually adopt similar tastes or skills. This predicts that lower-status users will tend to upload photos that are more similar to those of their higher-status contacts. In contrast, the extant literature suggests that an individual with a higher status is more likely to differentiate from her social contacts of lower status. Diverging cultural aesthetics driven by the motivation to differentiate from lowerstatus others can lead to the emergence of high culture (DiMaggio 1982, Mark 2003). This predicts that after forming a social connection with a lower-status user, a higher-status Flickr user will maintain the dissimilarity between their photo types. Social ties initiated by a lower-status user toward a higher-status user may be associated with less divergence in their uploaded photos. In other words, the moderating effect of H3 may depend on whether it is the lowerstatus user who initiated the social relation or the other way around.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>4</sup> <sup>(H4).</sup> Social ties directed from a lowerpopularity photo uploader to a higher-popularity photo uploader are associated with a higher similarity between the photos uploaded by the members of the dyad, compared to social ties initiated in the reverse direction.

## 4. Data

A substantial part of the data were kindly provided by Cha et al. (2009). The data include daily snapshots of a subnetwork of Flickr over a period of 41 days, from February 3 to March 15, 2007. Individual users were sampled in a snowball fashion. On day one, the crawler started from a single known user as a seed, retrieved everyone in the user’s list of contacts, and added them to the search list. The crawler went on to search the contact lists of these users and added those users to the search list, and so on. The crawling stopped when it obtained all the users who could be reached from the seed user. On day two and on all subsequent days, the crawler reexamined the contact lists of all the users in the previous sample; if they had added any new contacts, the crawler added those contacts to the sample and obtained all the users who were reachable from the new nodes. A one-way social tie exists from user i to j if i adds j as a contact; when j reciprocates by adding i as a contact, the tie becomes mutual. The snowball sampling captures all the outgoing ties from the users in the sample but not necessarily all the incoming ones. This limitation is typical in studies that crawl directed graphs online (Cha et al. 2009).

At the beginning of the data period, our sample contained 1.8 million users (called the “initial users”). On average, a user had 16 ties, but the distribution was uneven across people: 16% of users had no contacts, 47% of users had only one contact, and fewer than 5% of users had many contacts (> 50). At the end of the period, our sample size increased by 11.6%, and the number of ties increased by 14%, with a daily increase of 85,246 ties. Table 1 summarizes the sample’s statistics.

Four sets of information were collected: (a) network ties, which are the lists of contacts of every user in our sample on a given day; (b) uploading behaviors, including what photo(s) a user has uploaded in the 41-day period and the date(s) when she did so;<sup>4</sup> (c) photo tags, including all the tags attached to the photos in (b); and (d) photo faving, including the number of times each photo in (b) was marked by other users as a favorite, the users who marked it, and when.

Over the 41-day period, our data show that 933,144 photos were uploaded and marked as favorite by at least one user in the crawled network. These photos were contributed by 131,577 users (or 7% of the sample), and the remaining users uploaded no photos during the data collection period. The photos varied greatly in their popularity. The majority of these photos (90%) were marked as favorites fewer than five times, and a small percentage of photos (0.1%) were very popular and was marked more than 100 times.

User dyads were selected for inclusion in our study sample when they satisfied the following criteria. First, the dyadic social relations 4i1 j5 were established within the study’s 41-day period. That is, i added j as a contact during the data collection period, although j may or may not have reciprocated. In this way, we are able to observe the behaviors of the two users in the dyad both before and after they form a relation, which allows us to adopt a within-subjects design. Second, both i and j should be among the 1.8 million “initial users” in the snowball sample at the beginning of the data period so that the data would be able to identify when they established new ties during the data period. This results in 1,511,895 dyadic relations. Third, both i and j in each dyad must have uploaded at least one photo before and after they became linked. Because the majority of users uploaded no photos during our data collection period (including some accounts that are no longer active), this excludes the majority of the dyads, and only 14% of the dyads remained in the sample. Fourth, tag information is available for 71% of the photos. Photos with no tag information were excluded from our analysis. Thus, we also removed the dyads in which either member uploaded photos but none of the uploaded photos had tags. Finally, we dropped users with an extremely large number of ties (> 3,000). For users with this many contacts, adding one more social tie would, presumably, have only a minimal, marginal effect on their behavior. We also dropped users whose number of uploads during the period was much higher than that of the majority of users (> 200). The eventual sample contained 116,237 dyads.

The unidirectional nature of Flickr contacts adds some complexity to defining the dyads. Among the 116,237 dyads, 49% were one-way only during the data collection period, and 51% were mutual. To be consistent, we used i to denote the user initiating the connection. In our sample, in most cases, j reciprocated the tie very soon after the initial contact: 58% of j added i on the same day that i added j, and 95% of j added i within three days.

The users in these dyads uploaded an average of 24 photos over the 41-day data period; that is, approximately one photo every two days. The distribution of the number of uploads is described in Figure 1. The average numbers of uploaded photos are roughly the same before and after the dyads were connected. On average, each user had 176 out-degree ties by the end of the data collection period, a much higher number than the out-degree centrality of the entire sampled network of 16, indicating that those users are active in both uploading and socializing with other users. ## 5. Analyses and Results

In this section, we describe the empirical analyses used to test the hypotheses stated in §3. We first introduce the dependent variable, i.e., the content similarities between photos uploaded by the users in a dyadic social tie, and then describe the empirical model and present our results.

## 5.1. Photo Similarity Measure

Tags are semantic annotations of photos. Hence, to measure the photo similarities between users, we characterize the photos according to the tags assigned to them. We use a time-evolving vector to represent a user’s Flickr photostream. Suppose there are M possible distinct tags. The uploaded photos by user i on day t are described by an M-dimensional vector, where the kth element, $T a g _ { i t k } ,$ indicates the extent to which user i’s photos are associated with the kth tag. Hence, the kth element is the frequency that the kth tag appears in i’s photos:

$$
P h o t o _ {i t} = [ T a g _ {i t 1}, T a g _ {i t 2}, \ldots , T a g _ {i t k}, \ldots , T a g _ {i k M} ]\tag{1}
$$

$$
P h o t o _ {j t} = [ T a g _ {j t 1}, T a g _ {j t 2}, \ldots , T a g _ {j t k}, \ldots , T a g _ {j k M} ].\tag{2}
$$

To calculate the vector similarity, we use the cosine similarity, one of the most widely used similarity metrics in the information retrieval literature (Manning et al. 2009). The cosine similarity between i’s and $j ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s }$ photos on day t is calculated as follows:

$$
S I M _ {i j t} = \frac {\sum_ {k = 1} ^ {M} (T A G _ {i t k} \cdot T A G _ {j t k})}{\sqrt {\sum_ {k = 1} ^ {M} (T A G _ {i t k}) ^ {2} \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {M} (T A G _ {j t k}) ^ {2}}}.\tag{3}
$$

Because the uploading frequency varies across users and across days, we include the n most recent photos uploaded by each user by day t when computing the photo similarity of day t. For the analyses in this paper, we set $n = \dot { 7 } . { } ^ { 5 }$ We calculate the cosine similarity by dyad and by day. Our results show that the average cosine similarity is 0.043 (std. dev. = 00077). For 45% of the dyad-day observations, the cosine similarity is zero. This implies that given the unlimited options available when producing a photo, people do not exhibit much overlap in the types of photos they uploaded. Similar statistics have been observed in other user-generated content sites (Crandall et al. 2008). The nonzero values follow a log-normal distribution, as shown in Figure 2.

## 5.3. An Empirical Model

Regression analyses are used to model the patterns of changes in photo similarity, as described above. Because the formation of social ties is a key variable in this investigation, we divided the data period into three discrete stages and denoted them using two dummy variables, Tie1 and Tie2. For the period relatively long before two users, i and $j ,$ form a social tie $\left( t < - 3 \right)$ , Tie1 and Tie2 are both set to be 0; in the seven-day period around the formation of a social tie $( - 3 \leq t \leq 3 )$ , Tie1 is 1 and Tie2 is 0; and Tie1 = Tie2 = 1 denotes the period after the tie was formed $( t > 3 )$ We set the second period at one week because people need time to establish a social relationship $( \mathrm { e . g . , }$ get to know each other and each other’s photos). It also allows us to capture the pattern around the time that a tie is launched, which appears to be distinct from other periods. Thus, we specify our model as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{r} \ln (S I M _ {i j t} + 1) = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \cdot T i e 1 _ {i j t} + \beta_ {2} \cdot T i e 2 _ {i j t} \\ + \beta_ {3} O u t D e g r e e _ {i t} + \beta_ {4} O u t D e g r e e _ {j t} \end{array}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{l} + \beta_ {5} \text {NumOfCommonFrd} _ {i j t} \\ + \beta_ {6} \text {NumOfCommonGP} _ {i j t} + v _ {i j t}, \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

where

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \text {OutDegree} _ {j t} & \\ & = j ^ {\prime} \text {s out - degree centrality on day} t; \end{array}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{c} v _ {i j t} = \text { Unoberved   factors   that   influence   the } \\ \text { similarity   between } i \text { and } j \text { on   day } t. \end{array}
$$

The dependent variable is the cosine similarity between i and j on day t. Considering the highly skewed distribution of the similarity measure, we apply the log transformation. A constant, one, is added to cope with the zero-similarity values. In this specification, a positive (negative) coefficient for Tie1 implies that the average photo similarity around the time of the formation of a social tie is higher (lower) than before tie formation. A positive (negative) coefficient for Tie2 implies that the average similarity after tie formation is higher (lower) than the similarity around the time of tie formation.

There are several challenges involved in econometrically identifying peer influence. The estimation of peer influence in our model framework is based on the photo similarity of the user dyads over time. However, this photo similarity could arise from several other sources. First, some of the user characteristics that drive individuals to adopt certain behaviors may have been omitted. For example, homophily occurs when unobserved tastes and preferences drive the formation of social ties; thus, the linked user dyads tend to be more similar in their uploaded photos than the average users (Aral et al. 2009, McPherson 2001, Shalizi and Thomas 2011). The presence of unobserved individual-level heterogeneity can lead to response patterns similar to those of social contagion (Bemmaor 1994). Second, contextual effects may drive the uploading behavior of the two users in the dyad. For example, certain photo themes may suddenly surge after days of hot weather (e.g., “beach”) or major events at a location (e.g., “beerfest”). Time-varying factors, such as companies’ marketing efforts, may be critical in other contexts (Van den Bulte and Lilien 2001), but they play a minimal role in photo uploading on Flickr. Third, socially connected individuals may co-influence each other; this simultaneity implies an upward bias in the estimation of the interactions (Manski 1993, Godes and Mayzlin 2004). Although the individuals in most of the social networks studied in the literature are mutually connected and influenced (for instance, college roommates or users engaging in word-of-mouth communications in online forums), approximately half of the user dyads on Flickr had a one-way connection, which eliminates the simultaneity problem (Sorensen 2006). Thus, we can examine the impact of simultaneity by comparing the results obtained from the full sample with the one-way-linked dyads.

Various econometric strategies have been proposed to address these challenges. The most effective method appears to be randomized experimentation, as it avoids several known sources of bias, such as homophily and external stimuli (Aral and Walker 2011b, 2012). Instrumental variable methods based on natural experiments that attempt to solve the simultaneity problem are also available (Sacardote 2001, Tucker 2008). Unfortunately, the data available on Flickr did not enable natural experiments on social influence, and we were unable to execute randomized trials. In a study of product diffusion, to separate the influence and homophily effects, Aral et al. (2009) developed a dynamic matched sample based on the users’ longitudinal behavior, demographics, and geographic location. The availability of a large number of detailed user characteristics is critical to ensure the robustness of this matching. However, because of the limited information on individual users present in our data, this approach is not appropriate.

Therefore, we chose to use the fixed-effects model, which has been applied in previous studies on social influence (e.g., Nair et al. 2010, Hartmann et al. 2008). The panel nature of the Flickr data makes it possible to include dyad-specific fixed effects to control for unobserved sources of individual heterogeneity. Specifically, we can decompose the error term $\upsilon _ { i j t }$ in Equation (4) as follows:

$$
v _ {i j t} = \mu_ {i j} + \eta_ {i j t},\tag{5}
$$

where $\mu _ { i j }$ is a fixed effect specific to the dyad $( i , j ) ,$ which controls for the time-invariant similarity of the dyad because of individual members’ unobserved (to the researcher) temporally stable tastes in photo uploading. Thus, only within-dyad variations in similarity are used to estimate the peer influence. Note that we assume that the dyad’s similarity caused by their intrinsic interests is fixed over time. This appears to be supported by the patterns that we observed in the baseline groups, as discussed in §5.2 (Figure 3(a)).

Further, we include time-fixed effects that control for any time trends that are common across all Flickr users. To account for location-specific shocks, it would be ideal to include the location information of the uploaders. However, only a small percentage of Flickr users reveal their locations online. Moreover, many report only their hometowns or cities of employment, which do not necessarily correspond to the locations at which their photos were taken (e.g., people often take photos while traveling). To address this, we include a covariate that measures the similarity between the photos by i’s other contacts and $j ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s }$ other contacts. It is conceivable that the behavior of a user’s neighbors will provide information on unobserved events in the user’s network that may influence her behavior. Thus, the similarity between $i ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ other contacts and $j ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s }$ other contacts can be used as proxies for unobserved shocks in $i ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ and $j ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s }$ locations that may affect the similarity between i and j. One implicit assumption is that j does not influence the other contacts of $i ,$ and i does not influence the other contacts of j. Note that when measuring the behaviors of the other contacts, we examined the complete contact lists of each user: we did not limit our consideration to the users in the selected dyads in the study sample. We decompose $\eta _ { i j t }$ in Equation (5) as follows:

$$
\eta_ {i j t} = \alpha_ {d} + \beta_ {7} S I M _ {- i, j t} + \varepsilon_ {i j t},\tag{6}
$$

where $\alpha _ { d }$ is a fixed effect specific to the calendar day $d$ during the 41-day period; SIM $- i , j t$ is the photo similarity between $i ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ other contacts and $j ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s }$ other contacts on day $t ;$ and $\varepsilon _ { i j t }$ is a mean zero error term. Following the preceding specifications, the model can be rewritten as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \ln (S I M _ {i j t} + 1) = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \cdot T i e 1 _ {i j t} + \beta_ {2} \cdot T i e 2 _ {i j t} \\ \qquad + \beta_ {3} O u t D e g r e e _ {i t} + \beta_ {4} O u t D e g r e e _ {j t} \\ \qquad + \beta_ {5} N u m O f C o m m o n F r d _ {i j t} \\ \qquad + \beta_ {6} N u m O f C o m m o n G P _ {i j t} \\ \qquad + \beta_ {7} S I M _ {- i, j t} + \mu_ {i j} + \alpha_ {d} + \varepsilon_ {i j t}. \end{array}\tag{7}
$$

The model also controls for a number of timevarying user- and dyad-specific characteristics that may have an impact on the response variable, including the out-degree centrality (OutDegree), the number of common friends shared by i and j (NumOfCommon-Frd), and the number of common groups of which i and j are both members (NumOfCommonGP). A high out-degree centrality indicates that the user is following many users and their photo-related activities and is likely to be more susceptible to environmental influences when deciding how to upload her photos. We do not include the in-degree centrality because the snowball sampling may not completely crawl all the ties pointing to the node. Both NumOfCommonFrd and NumOfCommonGP can capture the sources of similarity in the tastes of the two people in a dyad and reflect any photo sources or common external shocks to which i and j are exposed, both of which can affect the types of photos they upload.

For a dyad $( i , j )$ , let $F a v _ { i } \ ( F a v _ { j } )$ denote user $i ( j ) ^ { \prime } s$ popularity as measured by the average number of favorites per photo that the user’s photostream received by the time the dyad was linked. To understand how the dyad’s popularity difference, $| F a v _ { i } -$ $F a v _ { j } | ,$ moderates the relationship between the social tie and the users’ photo similarity, we document its interaction with the period dummies. Let the coefficients of the two period dummies (Tie1 and Tie2) be $\beta _ { 1 } = \beta _ { 1 0 } + \beta _ { 1 1 } * \lvert \mathrm { \Delta } \mathrm { \hat { F } } a v _ { i } - F a v _ { j } \rvert$ and $\beta _ { 2 } = \beta _ { 2 0 } + \beta _ { 2 1 } *$ $| F a v _ { i } - F a v _ { j } |$ , respectively. In this setup, $\beta _ { 1 0 }$ and $\beta _ { 2 0 }$ capture the similarity change when the two people in the dyad have the same level of popularity, and $\beta _ { 1 1 }$ and $\beta _ { 2 1 }$ capture the marginal change in similarity when the dyad’s difference in popularity increases by one unit (i.e., one favorite marking per photo), ceteris paribus.

The parameter estimates of our model are listed in Table 4. The base model (column 1) includes all the variables in Equation (7) except for time-fixed effects and location-specific shocks. The coefficient of $T i e 1 , \beta _ { 1 } ,$ is positive and significant, indicating that the launch of a tie is associated with a significant increase in the dyad’s content similarity, consistent with the prediction in Hypothesis 1. Moreover, $\beta _ { 1 } = 0 . 0 0 6$ corresponds to a $0 . { \dot { 6 } } \%$ increase in the value of $( S I M _ { i j t } + 1 )$ For example, if two users’ cosine similarity $( \hat { S } I M _ { i j t } )$ is 0.050 before establishing a tie, it increases to 0.056 around the time that the tie is formed. We are more interested in how similarity evolves a short time after the two users form a tie, as captured by the coefficient of Tie2, $\beta _ { 2 }$ . In the base model, $\beta _ { 2 }$ is negative and statistically significant, which is in the opposite direction from $\beta _ { 1 } ,$ , suggesting that the photo similarity decreases, not increases, after tie formation, although the magnitude of the decrease is small in this case.

After the model controls for time-fixed effects (column 2) and location-specific shocks (column 3), the estimates of both $\beta _ { 1 }$ and $\beta _ { 2 }$ are lower. This is to be expected, as common shocks capture the factors that may lead the two people in a dyad to upload similar photos. Failing to account for these effects may result in overestimating $\beta _ { 1 }$ and $\beta _ { 2 }$ . Note that the decline in similarity after tie formation, as captured by $\beta _ { 2 } ,$ is more noticeable (= −0.003). This implies, for example, that if a dyad’s similarity is 0.050 around the time of tie formation, the similarity decreases to 0.047 in the following few weeks. Although this change appears small mathematically, its economic importance should not be ignored. This is because our current model estimates the marginal effect of a single friend, while it keeps the impact of all the other friends fixed. Given that a user may have many hundreds of friends, the cumulative influence of one’s network can significantly affect her uploading behavior. Our results demonstrate the importance of controlling for common shocks. The number of groups shared by a dyad and the similarity between the dyad’s friends both capture substantial variations in similarity change, whereas the number of common friends shared by a dyad contributes to a smaller effect. In summary, the results in columns 1 through 3 present support for Hypotheses 1 and 2.

## 5.5. Analysis of Photo-Shooting Time

The time-series data of uploading behaviors may not reveal the latent interests of Flickr photographers. For example, the uploaded photos could have been taken long before they are uploaded. In addition, the uploaded photos may represent only a small selection of a photographer’s entire photo library. Hence, the relationship between the observed similarity of the posted photos and social ties may or may not be the same as the relationship between the user’s latent interests when taking photos and her social ties. In other words, the results so far demonstrate more about how people present their photos than about what photos they prefer to take. As people’s photo-taking behaviors reflect their latent content creation preferences, the types of photos taken (versus uploaded) at a point in time provides us with information about the user’s innate tastes at that time, which is beyond the Flickr presence she creates through her uploaded photos. As discussed in §3, the underlying psychological process behind the individual drive for uniqueness can be external (i.e., identity signaling) or internal (i.e., self-identity and self-worth). Photo-taking behavior is more likely to shed light on the internal processes, while photouploading behavior reveals the identity that individuals would like to signal to others. To test whether the same findings are true for photo-shooting behaviors, we collected information on the shooting times of all the photos uploaded by the selected dyads. This information indicates that 31% of the photos were posted on the same day they were taken, 73% were posted within the same week, and 99% were posted within five years. Overall, it appears that people have developed a preference for uploading newly taken photos; for all the users in the sample, the average time interval between taking and uploading a photo is 73 days at the beginning of the data period and 54 days at the end of the period.

## 5.6. Direction of the Social Tie

Now that we have determined that dyads with large differences in photo popularity tend to be associated with larger increases in similarity than dyads with smaller differences, the next question concerns the direction of the pattern: do the more popular user and the less popular user move toward each other, does only one of them move toward the other, or does one move away from the other? We are unable to disentangle these possibilities for dyads with mutual relations, as both users may change their photo types at the same time. Therefore, we select dyads with oneway ties to answer this research question. In these dyads, one user essentially subscribes to the other user’s work and is exposed to that user’s influence but not the other way around. This allows us to attribute any changes in photo similarity to the user acting as the subscriber, not the one being subscribed to. In Table 5, the dummy variable, $I ( F a v _ { j } > F a v _ { i } )$ , is 1 if the tie is initiated by a less popular user and 0 if initiated by a more popular one. This dummy interacts with the period dummies and the dyad’s popularity difference, resulting in a three-way interaction model (column 7).

The results show that the degree of differentiation depends on the relative status of user i. The three-way interaction, $T i e 2 \times | F a v _ { i } - F a v _ { i } | \times I ( F a v _ { i } > F a v _ { i } )$ , is positive and significant, indicating that a dyad with different popularity levels produces even more similar content if a less popular user has initiated the social tie with a more popular user. Therefore, Hypothesis 4 is supported. We further confirmed this by analyzing the dyads in each direction. When i adds a more popular user as a contact, the further apart they are in terms of photo popularity, the more similar the photos i uploads are to the new contact’s photos after the tie is formed (column 8). However, if i adds a less popular user as a contact, her photo similarity to this contact declines after tie formation, regardless of how different their popularities are (column 9).

## 6. Discussion and Conclusions

In this research, we attempted to explore the social relations of Flickr users and their cultural outputs in the form of user-generated photography. It is our belief that online social media provides a unique opportunity to understand the production and consumption of cultural products (Lewis et al. 2008). As one of the most popular social networking sites for photography aficionados, Flickr provides a fitting context for our purpose. Building on the past literature in social psychology and cultural sociology, we observe a more nuanced relationship between the users’ online social relations and their cultural outputs. The results show that photo similarity evolves over time, along with the stages of social tie formation. This relationship is moderated by the differences in user popularity within the dyad. In particular, dyads of users whose photos enjoy nearly the same popularity gradually diverge in the types of photos they upload after forming a social tie, while users in dyads with greatly different popularity levels upload more similar photos.

We further explored the moderating effects of the direction of the social tie by considering only the dyads with one-way ties. The results suggest that in user dyads with different popularity levels, the less popular user uploads more similar photos to those of her peer as her difference in popularity from that peer increases, but this effect is insignificant for the more popular user in the dyad. Learning is a possible explanation for this observed convergence in the uploaded photos.

UGC is the lifeblood of social media. Social networking sites focus on the creation and consumption of online content: users “create” content by posting text messages, photos, and videos in their spaces, and they “consume” content by viewing others’ spaces and bookmarking their favorite content. Understanding the determinants of the creation and consumption of user-generated content is vital to the success of these websites, especially those whose value centers on cultural outputs, such as Flickr. Our research provides insight into the creation aspect of usergenerated content. Going beyond the previous literature, which focuses primarily on user participation rates, we document how the social relations between users are associated with the types of content they produce online.

Our findings can be generalized to other usergenerated content sites, such as YouTube and Twitter. These sites are also content-centric communities (Kaplan and Haenlein 2010) that allow users to interact with one another. Many functions in such online communities facilitate user socialization. As occurs in Flickr, users can become aware of others who upload similar content by searching for relevant tags, browsing others’ contact lists, commenting on each other’s tweets, or simply browsing the featured videos promoted by the website. When designing content-sharing platforms, the websites can devise and experiment with various ways to influence the formation of social relations between users and, in turn, the content those users create. However, as this study demonstrates, whether a greater number of social relations is associated with more diversified content depends on other user characteristics, such as status or popularity. Social networking sites striving for content diversity should, therefore, be careful in adopting the strategies that promote socialization.

The findings highlight individuals’ desire for uniqueness when using online communities. The tools adopted by online communities that enable their users to personalize their content are likely to add value for those users. For example, Instagram, another popular photo-sharing service, allows users to apply various filters to digital pictures to immediately give their photos a different visual style, making it easier to differentiate them from others’ photographs. Similarly, many UGC sites provide users with tools to customize their profile pages and arrange the way their content is displayed. Because these tools satisfy users’ need for distinctiveness, they engage the users and encourage them to contribute content, especially those who lack the advanced skills necessary to make truly unique content.

This study includes a few limitations. First, the measure of content similarity does not account for the fact that some tags represent closer semantic categories than others (Cattuto et al. 2008). For example, “girl” is more related to “woman” than to “winter,” but they are all considered as equally different categories in the current analysis. A more precise measure may be obtained by exploiting the semantic network on Flickr or on a lexical database. Further, we used only photo tags; we did not capture the other visual characteristics of digital photos (e.g., color, contrast, sharpness) that help differentiate the photos in important ways.

Second, identifying peer influence is empirically challenging. Although the concern of the homophily effect is reduced by the panel nature of the data and the model’s control for overall time trends, it is still possible that we have not accounted sufficiently for factors that may contribute to the correlation between photo-uploading behaviors. One potentially important factor is spatially correlated shocks; we lack accurate location data on the users and can only use the actions of their neighbors as a proxy. Because the key finding of this study is the differentiation of behaviors in the presence of social ties, not contagion, the common external shocks that lead people to behave similarly are not much of a threat. Rather, their presence may dampen the relationship observed in this study; the actual magnitude of the coefficient estimates may be even greater. However, unobserved external shocks, such as exposure to different landscapes or the experience of different emotional states, may motivate users to upload photos on dissimilar themes, posing a problem for our results. In this regard, we must exercise caution when interpreting the results.

Finally, the natural drift argument has not been completely eliminated as a possible alternative explanation. As shown in Figure 3(a), for the sample of user dyads studied here, both the similarity peak and the social link could be attributed to unobserved sporadic events, such as joining the same safari trip or photographing the same meteor shower. The decline in photo similarity after the formation of a social tie may represent a regression to the mean. This possibility is not excluded by comparisons with randomly selected dyads without ties because these dyads are already at the mean level. Using metadata written into photos by smartphones and digital cameras that record the time and location at which each photo is taken, researchers may identify these event-related similarity peaks and better explain the patterns described here.
