# Bidding strategies in online single-unit auctions: Their impact and satisfaction

## A B S T R A C T

Online bidders use different strategies in their biddings. However, we are unsure of the bidders strategies and their effectiveness in achieving auction goals. Our study was an attempt to identify bidding strategies empirically in online single-unit auctions and evaluate their outcome in terms of cost saving, perceived bidder enjoyment, and bidder satisfaction. A research model was developed and used to examine the relationships. Both objective and subjective data of online single-unit auctions were collected from Taobao.com to validate our postulated hypotheses. We found three main bidding strategies: early, snipe, and agent-supported ratchet bidding based on their behavior patterns. These resulted in different levels of winning outcome and cost saving, but had no significant effect on perceived enjoyment. The hypotheses of the relationship between auction outcomes and bidder satisfaction of their bidding strategies were confirmed.

## 1. Introduction

The online auction provides several advantages such as flexible pricing, convenient access, time saving, and diversified product offerings [25] over traditional sales channels. The single-unit auction is the most popular one online. Most famous auction sites like eBay and Yahoo! Auctions have adopted it [18]. Though such auctions have different bidding mechanisms, English mechanism is the most prevalent. A single-unit English auction begins with the lowest acceptable price and continues until no bidder further increases the bid. With this mechanism, one unit is auctioned at a time and the winner is always the one with the highest bid. This is easy to understand for buyers because it follows a natural way of thinking and provides a convenient approach to C2C business online.

The bidding experience in online single-unit English auctions is different from that in other popular mechanisms such as fixedprice, multi-unit Yankee, and Dutch Internet auctions. Due to their differences, bidders are expected to adopt different bidding strategies for success. In fact, recent studies on Yankee auctions have confirmed that bidders are not homogeneous in terms of their bidding behavior, and that different strategies generate different auction outcomes in terms of winning probability and consumer surplus extraction [3]. These findings are of importance to the understanding of how bidding should be executed in multi-unit Yankee auctions. However, research investigations on single-unit auction’s bidder types and bidding strategies are still lacking. Thus we decided to try to fill the gaps in order to produce a better understanding of bidder behaviors in this mainstream single-unit auction mechanism. As a subsequent step, we also felt that the identified strategies should be evaluated to determine whether they provided user satisfaction.

The objectives of our research were thus twofold: identify the bidding strategies in online single-unit auctions and evaluate the satisfaction of their users. Accordingly, we followed the guidelines of yield-shift theory (YST) of satisfaction to design a model of strategy satisfaction and adopted the principles of transaction cost and self-determination theories to derive the goals of winning outcome, cost saving, and perceived enjoyment.

We proposed three research questions to answer in our investigation:

1. Given the differences between multi- and single-unit auctions, do bidders adopt different bidding strategies when they bid in single-unit auctions? If so, what bidding strategies do they adopt?

2. Bidders will choose strategies that maximize their benefits. Is there any bidding strategy that is more effective in achieving auction goals, including winning outcome, cost saving, and bidding enjoyment?

3. Effective bidding strategy promotes satisfaction. However, being effective is only necessary but not sufficient for producing satisfaction. Is there a bidding strategy with which bidders are likely to be more satisfied?

## 2. Research background

A bidding strategy represents a series of interrelated types of bidding behaviors that a bidder adopts with a certain purpose or tactic in mind across different phases of an online auction. Bapna et al. [4] using data extracted from multi-unit online Yankee auctions contributed to our knowledge of bidding strategies and auction performance. A significant finding from their studies was the identification of five classes of bidders: evaluators (early and middle), who often place just one bid at either the early or middle stage of an auction, opportunists, who are late bidders at an auction, sip-and-dippers, who are a variant of opportunists (they usually place only two bids – the first early in the auction to establish their time priority and the second toward the end to revise their early bid), participators (characterized by an early entrance and a late exit, usually following other bidders using minimal bid increments), and agent bidders, who use the automatic bidding agents provided by auctioneers, normally bid at the minimal level required to outbid the current highest bidder until the bid exceeds the reserved price they previously set. Bapna et al. correlated bidder behavior to winning likelihood and consumer surplus extraction, and found that these correlations varied significantly across different bidding behaviors.

Easley and Tenorio [12] also empirically identified the existence of jump bidding; this is bidding in excess of the minimal necessary increment. With this strategy, bidders not only submit fewer bids, but also tend to intimidate their rivals. Jump bidding is more likely to be adopted in the early stage of an auction to deter other bidders from continuing to bid. It is also likely to be applied when auction competition becomes intense. Although multiple jump bids can be seen in an auction, the first jump is usually the greatest [26].

Another strategy is the strategic exit, which is the bidder’s decision to use the fixed-price Buy-It-Now feature to terminate the auction. The use of this strategy seems surprising, but risk-averse buyers may prefer to use this way to avoid losing an item that they perceive as a must-have. Angst et al. [2] tried to predict bidder’s decision to use strategic exit and confirmed that bidder’s traits of impulse-buying tendency, trait competiveness, and hedonic need fulfillment were determinants of this behavior. Such bidders generally had to pay a higher price than bidders who waited until the end of the auction.

Snipe bidders, bid late or close to the end of an auction, leaving other bidders with little time to respond; this significantly improves the chance of a snipe bidder to win [7]. Gonul and Leszczyc [14] investigated the use and outcome of snipe bidding strategy in eBay. In their study, they developed a multivariate logit choice model to estimate the probability of sniping during an auction. Their findings suggested that auction winners are more likely to be snipe bidders, particularly when the auction’s ending price is low. Also, they found that experienced bidders were more likely to engage in snipe bidding.

In addition to the investigation of e-auction behavior and strategy, Bapna et al. [5] attempted to quantify consumer surplus levels in eBay auctions and found that consumer surplus varied significantly across different currencies and item categories, with US currency auctions and antique and collectible items generating a higher level of consumer surplus. They also found consumer surplus to be negatively influenced by seller experience, auction duration, and competition, but positively influenced by bidder experience, bidder aggressiveness, and item price.

Efforts have been made to investigate Internet auctions, technology and service adoption [19], reputation and trust [22], winner’s curse [1], auction mechanisms [17], online auction fraud [11], and online auction addiction [28]. Our model was based on the findings of several studies such as [5,8] but modified to consider single-unit auctions. We felt that the conclusions drawn from other types of auctions might not apply to online single-unit English auctions because of the differences in the auction mechanisms [27]. In addition, bidders in different auction mechanisms have different profiles and bidding experiences [29].

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

The objective of our study was to identify bidding strategies and evaluate bidder satisfaction with them, and to examine the effect of the strategies on bidder performance in online single-unit auctions. Yield shift theory of satisfaction (YST) asserts that satisfaction is a function of yield shifts in an individual’s active goal set, which are processed subconsciously. If a person believes that the yield in his/her active goal set has increased, then a positive affective arousal occurs. However, a decrease in perceived yield in the goal set results in a negative affective arousal. YST, with transaction cost and self-determination theories were used as guidelines in constructing our model.

In the context of online auctions, the goals of bidding are quite similar, but the strategy to win varies. When bidding strategy changes, the yield may be affected, shifting the magnitude of satisfaction either positively or negatively. Hence, satisfaction of bidding strategy rests on the outcome of goals achieved by each bidding strategy. The better the outcome, the more satisfied the bidders with their strategies.

The goals for using respective bidding strategies can be classified into two major categories: financial and psychological. In deriving financial goals, the principles of transaction costs theory were applied. Transaction and product costs are the major ones involved in producing and distributing a product. In the context of our research, bidders wish to minimize the costs they pay for the product and hope to reduce any cost associated with its acquisition. Hence, the use of cost saving as a financial goal that predicts the satisfaction derived from using a strategy.

Two overarching intrinsic and extrinsic types of motivation are proposed in self-determination theory: extrinsic (which can induce an individual to participate in pursuit of a reward) and intrinsic (which provokes a person to participate for his or/her own enjoyment). Hence, winning outcome, extrinsic, and perceived enjoyment, intrinsic motivations were deemed appropriate psychological goals for evaluating the satisfaction of using a bidding strategy.

## 3.1. Bidding strategies

Our literature review showed that bidders exhibited heterogeneous behavior and used different strategies in Yankee auctions. Although they support multi-unit auctions and have rules that are different from those of single-unit auctions, we believe that the research findings in Yankee auctions can be extended to singleunit auctions. We hypothesized:

H1. Bidders in online single-unit auctions use heterogeneous bidding strategies.

## 3.2. Winning outcome

Self-determination theory argued that external reward provided an important extrinsically controlled motivation for an action or employment of a specific strategy. In the context of online bidding, this reward has been empirically confirmed to be a key component of bidding [24]. Of all bidding strategies, agent bidding has the least flexibility. It also involves the lowest level of commitment, time and resources, and this could adversely affect winning outcome. Hence, different bidding strategies have different characteristics. We therefore hypothesized:

H2. Different bidding strategies yield different winning outcomes in online single-unit auctions.

## 3.3. Cost saving

Transaction cost theory has been used to explore strategic outcomes, including cost savings [20]. Cost saving in online singleunit auctions is the difference between willingness-to-pay (WTP) and actual price paid. If a bidder wins an auction with a winning price lower than his/her WTP, then the bidder has achieved a cost saving. Prior literature (such as [9]) confirmed that cost saving is one of the major motivators for bidders to go online. Varied bidding strategies were then developed to win the auction with minimal cost. Using ratchet bidding, participants raise the price by small (or minimum) amounts, sometimes termed nibbles. In this way, they may avoid winner’s curse, and lower the final price. Hou found late bidding had a negative effect on the final price of auctions. Hence:

H3. Different bidding strategies result in different levels of cost saving in online single-unit auctions.

## 3.4. Perceived enjoyment

Self-determination theory posits that curiosity and fun are motivations for individuals to learn and behave. They learn better and are more creative when they are intrinsically motivated. Many bidders perceived auctions to be a game, providing enjoyment [16]. Sniping other bidders near the end of an auction saves time and resources and is also exciting. Ratchet bidding can also be enjoyable as bidders closely respond to the bids of others, turning an auction into an exciting game. Although the agent bidding process is automatic because of the use of software, bidders may still enjoy the process, which is similar to window shopping. Hence, we proposed:

H4. Different bidding strategies result in different levels of perceived enjoyment in online single-unit auctions.

## 3.5. Satisfaction with bidding strategy

YST asserts that satisfaction is determined by the yield shift in the active goal set prescribed by an individual while performing a behavior. In online single-unit auctions, bidders adopt various strategies, which could result in different yield shift in the winning outcome, cost saving, and enjoyment. Bidders also have different participation patterns. Some prefer frequent participation while others prefer to bid only once. We hypothesized:

H5a. Different bidding strategies result in different levels of bidder satisfaction.

In the IS field, the relationship between systems and users, and their performance and satisfaction, has been extensively studied [10,30]. Most results have shown a positive relation between them. Several theoretical models have been proposed to explain this relationship. One is the expectation confirmation theory, which suggests that individuals make predictions about future performance, and compare this with actual performance. If actual performance is better than expected, then individuals will be satisfied. A key factor that might influence bidding strategy selection and satisfaction is the estimation of the probability of winning, which may be influenced by a variety of factors, including bidding intensity, bidder number, auctioned item, bidding duration, and bidding mechanism. In general, winning has a positive and significant impact on consumer satisfaction. Hence, we proposed:

H5b. Winning outcome has a positive impact on bidding strategy satisfaction.

Cost saving is another indicator of satisfaction. Marketing research has consistently found a positive correlation between consumer surplus and satisfaction [13]. In online bidding, a gain in cost saving reinforces its bidding strategy. Due to the heterogeneity of bidders’ experience, bidding strategies can result in different cost savings and thus different levels of satisfaction. Hence, we proposed:

H5c. Cost saving has a positive impact on bidding strategy satisfaction.

Bidding behavior is often dictated by the enjoyment or fun associated with the act, which depends on novelty, fun, or surprise. Each bidding strategy involves unique competitive tactics, risk taking, compromise, and resource investment. It is thus natural to expect that the greater the perceived enjoyment, the greater the level of satisfaction with the bidding strategy. Therefore, we hypothesized that:

H5d. Perceived enjoyment has a positive impact on bidding strategy satisfaction.

Our model, with its hypotheses, is shown in Fig. 1. We validated it empirically using a four-stage process.

1. We analyzed the objective data collected from our auction site and developed a taxonomy of bidding strategies to validate hypothesis H1.

2. We compare the different bidding strategies and their outcomes on their goals, including the winning outcome, cost saving, and perceived enjoyment. These were then used to validate hypotheses H2, H3, and H4.

3. The different strategies were compared to determine whether they generated different levels of satisfaction, as hypothesized in H5a.

4. The bidding strategy satisfaction was regressed on winning outcome, cost saving, and perceived enjoyment for relationship analysis. The statistical results were then used to validate hypotheses H5b, H5c, and H5d.

In our study, winning outcome measures whether a bidder wins an auction or not. Cost saving measures the difference between the winning price and the price a bidder would have been willing to pay. Perceived enjoyment assesses the hedonic effect achieved through auction participation. In contrast, bidding strategy satisfaction is the degree of fulfillment from using a specific strategy in the bidding process.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Instrument development and pre-testing

In our study we collected both subjective and objective data. The former were collected using a survey questionnaire (mainly for perceived enjoyment and bidding strategy satisfaction), and the latter were collected from an auction website. A three-stage validation process was performed to ensure the validity and reliability of our instrument. First, whenever possible, previously validated questions and generally accepted instrument construction guidelines were employed. Second, the survey was pre-tested by four business professors and six graduate students with expertise in survey research, IS, and auctions. The feedback from this phase resulted in some restructuring and refinement of the survey that improved the content validity. Third, a pilot test of the questionnaire was administered to thirty-five online bidders randomly selected from Taobao.com, a major auction website in China. These bidders were initially contacted and invited to participate in the pilot-test through WangWang – an online instant messenger of Taobao.com. If the invitee agreed to our invitation, he or she was emailed our questionnaire, along with a cover letter explaining our purpose. Subsequently, their responses were collected and analyzed for reliability using Cronbach’s alpha tests. The results indicated that all alpha values were above 0.80, suggesting adequate reliability of the questionnaire. The pilot test also resulted in a few wording and sentence changes, improving the validity of the survey’s content.

## 4.2. Data collection

Data were collected from Taobao.com, which was launched in 2003 by Alibaba, one of the largest B2B companies in China. It is the most popular online auction website in China (its name can be translated as treasure rush). It was originally developed as a C2C platform to facilitate transactions between individuals. To enhance the auction website’s branding and competitiveness, Taobao was provided as a free-service C2C platform, charging no fees to its customers. This policy, along with other strategic services including WangWang (an online communication system), Alipay (a payment system), and a feedback system (seller reputation system), allowed Taobao to surpass its major competitor, EachNet, in market share in 2005. In 2008, Taobao’s business was extended to include a B2C platform for direct sales to eligible consumer product makers and retailers.

In our study, the data were collected from online bidders randomly chosen from recently completed single-unit auctions in

Taobao.com as shown in Fig. 2. The company validated bidders in 1300 recently completed auctions of Taobao.com were randomly selected and they were contacted through the online instant messenger, WangWang. The bidders were sent a brief introduction to the study, including the research objective and requirements, and an invitation to participate in our survey. They were also informed that they would receive ¥10 if they successfully completed the online survey.

A total of 443 bidders responded positively to our invitation. They were asked to complete an online questionnaire hosted on the website of a professional online survey company. The use of this service allowed us to deal with the problems of access, authentication, and multiple responses associated with online data collection. After two rounds of reminders, 425 bidders had completed the questionnaire. User IDs were then used to match bidders with their auction information recorded by Taobao.com. This bidding data was used to evaluate the research model and validate or reject our hypotheses.

## 4.3. Measures

The sequence number of entry (SNOE), sequence number of exit (SNOX), number of bids (NOB), and number of agent bids (NOAB) were selected as proxies in cluster analysis to identify different bidding strategies. The values of these four proxies were standardized (divided by the total number of bids in each auction) to eliminate any adverse effect on the identification of bidding strategies.

The winning outcome and cost saving were mainly measured by the objective data. Winning outcome indicated whether the respondents won the auction after using a bidding strategy set. Cost saving was calculated using a normalized equation

## WTP - winning price winning price

to reduce the effect caused by different levels of prices and different expectations of bidders. To allow this calculation, all bidders were asked to provide the maximum price they had decided to pay (WTP) for the auctioned product.

We also collected bidder feelings about their perceived enjoyment and satisfaction with bidding strategy using a sevenpoint Likert scale. The measure of perceived enjoyment was reflective and adapted from research in hedonic shopping, including adventure and joy perception.<sup>1</sup> We kept all the six measurements, but changed their context from ‘‘shopping’’ to ‘‘bidding online’’. The measure of bidding strategy satisfaction, which was also reflective, was adapted from that of Mckinney et al.<sup>2</sup> in which there were six question items (four originally were the evaluation of website satisfaction – satisfied, pleased, contented or delighted). We only used satisfied and contented, plus two other items recommend it to my friends and use it again, rephrased for our research context.

## 5.2. Reliability and validity of the subjective measures

We used Cronbach’s alpha to ensure the reliability of the instrument measuring the subjective variables (perceived enjoyment and bidding strategy satisfaction). The alpha values of perceived enjoyment and bidding strategy satisfaction were 0.865 and 0.867, respectively, which are significantly above the recommended 0.70 threshold level. We also applied confirmative factor analysis to test the validity of the measures. It was chosen due to its superiority in establishing the convergent and discriminative validity of the constructs of our model. The overall model fit was assessed using five goodness-of-fit indices: the chisquare, root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), nonnormalized fit index (NNFI), comparative fit index (CFI), and standardized root mean residual (SRMR). The results of these indices met the recommended criteria, thereby suggesting an adequate fit of the measurement model. In addition to this validation of our data, we collected qualitative interviewed data from 21 bidders through WangWang. In these interviews, bidders were asked to explain their motivation, strategy, satisfaction, process, and fulfillment of online biddings. The qualitative data was then compared with results derived from our quantitative data. This allowed us to validate and cross-check our results [6]. Overall, the qualitative findings provided additional insights to the understanding of bidding behavior.

## 5.3. Results

## 5.3.1. Cluster analysis of bidders

We adopted a two-step cluster analysis method to identify the bidding strategies adopted in online single-unit auctions with the proxies SNOE, SNOX, NOB, and NOAB. In the first step, hierarchical cluster analysis was performed to generate a hierarchical dendrogram and an agglomeration schedule table. The results are shown in Table 2, showing that the percentage of the change in agglomeration coefficients from two to three clusters (83.5%) demonstrates the greatest increase and that from three to four (28.5%) a dramatic decrease, which suggests a three-cluster solution. This finding also confirms H1: that is, bidders are heterogeneous and adopt different bidding strategies in online single-unit auctions.

Once the optimal cluster number was determined, we conducted K-mean cluster analysis to generate the details of the three-cluster solution. The results, presented in Table 3, show that Cluster 1, named the early bidding strategy, has the lowest values of SNOE, SNOX, NOB, and NOAB, indicating that bidders using this strategy enter and exit auctions relatively early, bid infrequently, and seldom use software agents. Cluster 2, named the snipe bidding strategy, has very high SNOE and SNOX, but very low NOB and NOAB values, suggesting that the bidders using this strategy enter and exit auctions very late, place very few bids in the auction process, and use agent software very infrequently. Cluster 3 bidders enter auctions relatively early and stay very late. They place the greatest number of bids and use agent software much more frequently than do the bidders of the other two clusters. Their combined use of ratchet and agent bidding suggests that these bidders are using agent-supported ratchet (ASR) bidding strategy.

## 5.3.2. Effect of bidding strategies

ANOVA was performed to compare the means of winning outcome, cost saving, and perceived enjoyment across the three bidding strategies. The results are presented in Table 4; they show that winning outcome and cost saving are significantly different (p < 0.01), which supports our hypotheses that different bidding strategies are associated with different winning outcome (H2) and cost saving (H3). The use of different bidding strategies, however, was not found to have caused a significant difference in perceived enjoyment, indicating thus H4 was not supported.

We also perform Tukey tests to examine the different impacts of bidding strategies on winning outcome and cost saving. The results demonstrate that snipe bidding and agent-supported ratchet bidding strategies resulted in a greater number of winning outcome, whereas the early bidding strategy resulted in the lowest number. Early bidding was also found to generate significantly lower cost saving to bidders than the other two strategies.

## 5.3.3. Satisfaction with bidding strategies

ANOVA was performed to compare the level of bidder satisfaction across the three strategies identified. Our findings showed a significant difference in level of satisfaction across the strategies, thereby supporting hypothesis H5a. As satisfaction level is different across strategies, so a follow-up Tukey test was performed to evaluate how strategies differ from each other. The findings, which are shown in Table 4, revealed that bidders are more satisfied with the snipe bidding strategy and less with an early bidding strategy.

The final test to be performed is the regression analysis between our dependent and independent variables. Prior to the performance of regression, the standard assumptions of multiple regression were checked for compliance. These include the checking of variance, outliers, and normality by the tests of residual values, rankit plots of the residuals, Cook’s distance, and Shapiro–Wilk. No irregularity was identified, which justified the use of our data set for regression analysis.

We then regressed bidding strategy satisfaction against winning outcome, cost saving, and perceived enjoyment for possible relationship. The results, which are presented in the ‘‘overall’’ column of Table 5, show that winning outcome, cost saving, and perceived enjoyment are all positively and significantly related to bidding strategy satisfaction, thus supporting hypotheses H5b, H5c, and H5d.

We also investigated the differential effect of respective bidding strategies on winning outcome, cost saving, and perceived enjoyment by running additional regressions. The results indicated that almost all outcome variables had significant effects on satisfaction across all bidding strategies, except for early bidding. In this exceptional case, cost saving was found to have an insignificant effect on bidder satisfaction for early bidding strategy. This might be explained by the fact that early bidders usually aim to evaluate (as indicated in Bapna et al.’s studies) or explore the auctions. They would not respond to other bidders’ private information as revealed in their bidding prices, and cumulated throughout the bidding process. As a consequence, they have very limited chance of winning the auction. These reasons probably explain the insignificant relationship between cost saving and satisfaction of early bidding strategy.

Finally, we performed a post hoc mediation analysis of the determinants (winning outcome and cost saving) on the strategy– satisfaction relationship. Our results confirmed that winning outcome and cost saving had significant mediation effect on satisfaction for early bidding, but not for snipe and ASR bidding.

## 6. Discussions

We hoped to confirm the heterogeneity of bidding strategies in online single-unit auctions, and to determine the most satisfactory one by investigating auction outcomes. The practical significance of our study was that it did identify three single-unit bidding strategies. When compared to Bapna et al.’s findings, our study could not identify participators and sip-and-dippers in single-unit auctions. There are two possible reasons. First, the use of automatic bidding agents can save bidders’ time and resources in the auction, thereby reducing their participation frequency in the auction. Second, single-unit auctions do not consider time priority, thus saving bidders’ trouble in placing an early bid. When the need for early bid disappears, the sip-and-dip strategy becomes irrelevant.

The theoretical significance of our study was that it applied YST of satisfaction to assess bidding strategy satisfaction. Also transaction cost theory and self-determinant theory were validated in the context of online auctions. All these theories were pertinent to our research objectives and relevant to our hypotheses

They also confirmed that self-motivations and cost consciousness are critical determinants of satisfaction, which align with the principles of self-determination and transaction cost theories. In addition, some of our findings merit further investigation and discussion.

First, we found snipe bidding to be most popular, followed by agent-supported ratchet. These findings are comparable to conclusions drawn by Gonul and Leszczyc. However, snipe bidding helps reduce bidding wars with ratchet bidders by not allowing them sufficient time to respond to being outbid. With the benefits of snipe bidding outweighing its risks, it is not surprising to find that this strategy is more widely adopted than others in online single-unit auctions.

Second, we discovered that the combined use of bidding strategies was common. For example, some bidders sniped at the end of the auctions but submitted their bids using ratchet bidding. This hybrid bidding strategy is a result of technological advancement: the use of bidding agents has become increasingly common.

Third, we confirmed that winning outcome, cost saving, and perceived enjoyment were all determinants of bidding strategy satisfaction. However, cost saving seems to have the weakest effect on satisfaction, suggesting that self-determination theory was better in explaining bidding strategy satisfaction than transaction cost theory.

Fourth, we found that early bidders were the least satisfied among bidders with their strategy. The consequence of their early entrance causes them to miss other bidders’ private information. Ignorance of the true value of an item as perceived by other bidders can only lead to the problem of auction loss or paying a price higher than the true value.

## 7. Conclusions

The identification of the wide application of bidding agents in ratchet bidding suggests that auctioneers need to upgrade the functions of their agents to adequately fulfill the dynamic nature of bidding. Auctioneers should evaluate the possibility of extending the capability of their bidding agents to include intelligent systems that automatically collect relevant auction data and make recommendations to improve the decision making of auction participants. The Auction Advisor system designed by Gregg and Walczak [15] is a relevant prototype.

Our findings suggest that snipe bidding generates high winning outcome and cost saving, thus resulting in bidders’ most satisfaction with this bidding strategy. However, auctioneers may find this bidding strategy controversial as it may increase network traffic during the close of an auction. Such consequences are likely to generate bidder complaints and dissatisfaction. In dealing with such an issue, auctioneers may consider redesigning their auction mechanism. For example, eBay has a fixed end-time (hard-close auction), but it allows the placement of a maximumbid in advance, which automatically will compete with other bids while not increasing much network traffic, even near the end of an auction. Amazon, on the other hand, allows an auction’s scheduled end-time to be extended automatically until ten minutes have passed without a bid.

Although bidding strategy was found to have a significant impact on winning outcome and cost saving, the nature and distractions of online auctions may have modifying effects on all of these variables. For example, Nowlis et al. [23] suggested that a delay in consumption is pleasant only if the outcome is positive. In the context of an online single-unit auction, there can be only one winner. The losers, who are the majority, may feel frustrated and uncomfortable. These negative feelings may eventually override the enjoyment and excitement they originally achieved in the bidding process.

## 8. Limitations

First, our data were collected from one online auction website, which may have caused sampling bias. Data from one website prevents the investigation of bidding strategies that involve multiple websites (such as cross-bidding [21]). Second, our sample size was small compared to that of other auction studies using objective data. Third, our sample may not even represent the bidder population of Taobao because of our sampling procedure. Given the requirement of memory recall in our data, our sample could only be recruited from those who had recently participated in auctions. Fourth, our respondents are all Chinese, suggesting that caution must be exercised when generalizing our findings to other economic, social, and cultural contexts.
