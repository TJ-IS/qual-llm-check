## INTRODUCTION

Managers’ recognition of opportunities, along with problems and crises, has long been studied in the management literature (e.g., Kiesler and Sproull, 1982; Mintzberg, Raisinghani, and Theoret, 1976), most recently serving as a focus of entrepreneurship research (e.g., Baron, 2006; Gavetti, 2012). While these same phenomena have held the interest of scholars studying information technology (IT) adoption (e.g., Venkatesh, Morris, Davis, and Davis, 2003), the vast majority of IT adoption research implicitly assumes that potential adopters are cognizant of promising new IT. Here, being cognizant involves both the act of noticing and the act of sensemaking, i.e., processes by which people give meaning to ephemeral (in noticing) and deliberate (in sensemaking) cognitive experiences. Importantly, Starbuck and Milliken argued that "… noticing may be at least as important as sensemaking ... If events are noticed, people make sense of them; and if events are not noticed, they are not available for sensemaking" (1988, p.60).

Taking as a given the (unstated) assumption that a new IT has already been noticed as a viable candidate for adoption, the extant IT adoption literature has focused on sensemaking processes (e.g., Davis, 1989; M ore and Benbasat, 1991; Thompson, Higgins, and Howell, 1991; Venkatesh, et al., 2003) that adopters go through in evaluating a candidate IT for adoption. For example, Venkatesh et al (2003), after integrating eight prominent user acceptance models, proposed and tested a unified model of IT acceptance in which adoption decisions are affected by adopters’ assessments of performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, and facilitating conditions. The extant IT adoption literature also has plenty of research efforts advancing the post-adoption side (e.g., Bhattacherjee 2001; Jasperson, Carter and Zmud, 2005). As will be detailed later, despite the existence of prior related research, what the extant IT adoption literature is unable to adequately explain is the phenomenon of adopters initially noticing an emergent, possibly relevant new IT.

This paper seeks to address the above-mentioned research gap by providing a theoretical conceptualization of managerial noticing of a new organizational IT referenced in alerting messages. Personal IT (a focus of much of the IT adoption literature) is excluded given this paper’s attention to IT that are applied to organizational opportunities and challenges. An organizational IT (OIT) is an information system or service, enabled through computer/ communication software/hardware (Cooper and Zmud, 1990, p.123), intended to be used by an organizational aggregate (Fichman, 1992), e.g., a set of business units, functional units, administrative units, departments, etc. Most typically, new OIT are noticed, envisioned and/or advocated by managers<sup>1</sup> and technology strategists (Peppard, 2007; Sambamurthy and Zmud, 1994, 2014) serving, formally and informally, as an organization’s eyes and ears (Swanson and Ramiller, 1997). To simplify the arguments, such individuals are referred to as managers. Those managers receive, via searching or scanning, a myriad of information about OIT (e.g., vendors bragging about an OIT, analysts criticizing an OIT, competitors adopting an OIT). Such information about OIT that managers receive is referred to as alerting message.

Why would understanding managerial noticing of a new organizational IT be useful? Gavetti (2012) has defined behavioral lures in opportunity recognition as (p. 270): “… impediments to firms’ abil ompete for opportunities.” After discussing the root causes of such behavioral failures, Gavetti identifies three capabilities for preventing behavioral failures (p. 276): “… rationality (the capability to identify opportunities), plasticity (the capability to act on opportunities), and shaping ability (the capability to legitimize opportunities by constructing the opportunity space).” In the specific context of OIT adoption, behavioral failure occurs if

managers fail to notice a new OIT, fail to grasp the organizational capabilities needed to implement a new OIT, or fail to fashion a well-reasoned OIT adoption rationale (Sambamurthy and Zmud, 2014). This paper’s concern herein is with the first of these three causes of behavioral failure, i.e., failing to notice a promising (i.e., possibly relevant) new OIT.

Developing a theoretical conceptualization of managerial noticing of a new OIT may also shed lights on other related research. One well-established field of research that may benefit from such a conceptualization is regarding the influences of IT attributes on adoption outcomes. New IT are anything but homogeneous, with systematic differences in IT attributes affecting individuals’ assessments of benefits and costs. Scholars interested in IT adoption have made considerable progress in identifying and understanding the variety of IT attributes influencing IT adoption outcomes (e.g., Al-Natour and Benbasat, 2009; Venkatesh, et al., 2003). However, prior research examining the influences of IT attributes focused on the sensemaking and postadoption phases and has not examined these influences during the noticing phase. To complement the existing research on the influences of IT attributes, this paper uses the process-based conceptualization of managerial noticing as a theoretical grounding and develops research propositions to understand how alerting message contents describing OIT attributes influence noticing outcomes. Such hybrid approach combining process and variance approaches has been recognized in the IS literature (e.g., de Guinea and Webster, 2017).

Understanding how managers initially notice a new OIT (as well as the influences of IT attributes) is practically valuable for at least two reasons. First, it is not uncommon for new, potentially value-adding OIT to either fail to be noticed for adoption or suffer from delayed consideration (Overby, Bharadwaj, and Sambamurthy, 2006). Second, the abilities of a new OIT’s proponents/detractors to influence managers’ noticing processes via the crafting and transmitting of alerting messages are likely to prove critical in preventing faulty managerial noticing. In order to influence managers’ noticing processes, proponents/detractors must compose and transmit attention-grabbing messages with parts of these messages describing attributes of the focal new OIT (e.g., Hill and Levenhagen, 1995; Sambamurthy and Zmud, 2014). A robust theory of how and why managers notice new OIT can provide practical guidance to inform proponents/detractors of how best to craft these alerting messages.

This paper is expected to contribute to the literature in several ways. First, this paper contributes to the IT adoption literature by providing finely delineated descriptions of two noticing processes: awareness and arousal. Such theoretical conceptualization provides a robust framework for ensuing research aimed at better understanding why organizations exhibit considerable variations in their uptakes of new OIT. Second, this paper also contributes to the IT adoption literature by applying presented conceptualization to develop research propositions linking influential OIT attributes to these two noticing processes, thus complementing and extending existing research on the role served by IT attributes in the adoption processes. Third, this paper contributes to the literature on technology frame and organizing vision by providing a firmer cognitive foundation for why technology frame and organizing vision are also useful for the noticing of technology. Finally, the detailed description of the two cognitive processes under noticing may also help deepen or clarify existing understandings in the managerial cognition and opportunity recognition literature.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. First, prior work related to managerial noticing of new OIT is discussed. Then, the theoretical foundation is introduced. This foundation incudes: the primary theoretical lens (information processing theory, IPT) and and the set of OIT attributes expected to be most influential regarding managers’ noticing. Next, a process-based conceptualization of managerial noticing of new OIT is presented. Then, using the developed conceptualization as a theoretical grounding, this paper develops research propositions regarding how alerting message contents describing OIT attributes influence noticing outcomes. This paper concludes by explaining contributions, suggesting directions for future research, and offering message-crafting guidelines to proponents/detractors of new OIT.

## PRIOR RESEARCH RELATED TO MANAGERIAL NOTICING OF NEW OIT

Prior IT adoption research (Huff and Munro, 1985; Nambisan et al., 1999), though quite limited and not focused specifically on OIT, has recognized the importance of managers being cognizant of new IT. Below, related prior research as well as its inadequacy is discussed.

Huff and Munro (1985), having interviewed managers involved in IT assessment and adoption, built on Rogers’ (1962) stage model of innovation adoption to describe IT assessment and adoption. Specifically, they suggested that IT assessment and adoption is driven by a variety of forces (e.g., organizational issues, technological opportunities) and is comprised of four primary processes: awareness (i.e., initial recognition), interest (i.e., seeking additional information), evaluation (i.e., contrasting benefits and costs), and trial (i.e., small-scale testing). Huff and Munro provided illustrative examples, rather than comprehensive descriptions, of these processes. While Huff and Munro described organizational roles (e.g., users, gatekeepers.) and information sources (e.g., other organizations, vendors) associated with the processes, no discussion is provided on how these roles and information sources play out across the four processes nor on the influences of IT attributes.

Nambisan et al. (1999) viewed the initiation phases of IT adoption as a knowledgecreation process. Specifically, information-gathering and information-processing mechanisms are applied to acquire three types of knowledge about an IT (Type 1), about the application of an IT in the general industrial context (Type 2), and about the application of an IT in a spe ic organization’s context (Type 3)—and to convert one type of knowledge to another. These information-gathering and information-processing mechanisms are then associated with three antecedents of technology users’ propensity to innovate in IT: technology cognizance, ability to explore, and intention to explore. Further, specific mechanism (e.g., subscription to IT journals, vendors’ customer support units, etc.) are identified that are likely to facilitate knowledge acquisition and conversion. A comprehensive description of this knowledge-creation process and the influences of IT attributes, however, are not provided.

Another related work is Griffith’s (1999) features-based theory of sensemaking triggers. According to Griffith, a technology’s features (i.e., concrete versus abstract, core versus tangential, etc.) may trigger managers’ sensemaking by grabbing their attention. Specifically, when a new technology is sensed as something novel or otherwise discrepant from the expected, the technology is noticed. Griffith, however, did not provide detailed discussion about this noticing phase, nor about the influences of IT attributes during noticing.

Technology frame refers to individual (or possibly group or community)-level understanding regarding “the nature and role of the technology itself…conditions, applications, and consequences of that technology in particular contexts” (Orlikowski and Gash, 1994, p. 178). Similar to the concept of technology frame but often with a focus on IT in inter-organizational settings, organizing vision refers to community level understanding regarding an IT (e.g., its purpose) and the applications of IT in organizations (Swanson and Ramiller, 1997) such as legitimation (e.g., how may the IT address issues important to the organization) and mobilization (e.g., attracting resources required for adoption). The current literature on technology frame and organizing vision emphasizes discourses about the focal technology (e.g., Currie, 2004; Davidson, 2006)—contributed from developers, vendors, customers, analysts, and etc.— and how technology discourses influence technology sensemaking, and consequently, the development, adoption, use, and change/adaptation of technology (e.g., Currie, 2004; Davidson, 2002, 2006; Mishra and Agarwal, 2010; Orlikowski, 1992; Orlikowski and Gash, 1994; Swanson and Ramiller, 1997; Yang and Hsu, 2011). The current literature, however, paid inadequate attention to the influences of technology discourses on noticing and to the role of IT attributes described in the discourses (rather than attributes/characteristics of the discourses, e.g., interpretability, plausibility, importance, and discontinuity, Ramiller and Swason, 2003). In summary, while prior research has signaled the importance of noticing and of the influences of IT attributes, our understanding remains, at best, quite limited. This paper

addresses these gaps by, first of all, developing a theoretical conceptualization of the cognitive processing engaged in by managers in the noticing of a new OIT prior to the managers making sense (i.e., engaging in adoption evaluation) of the OIT; after that, this paper applies the developed conceptualization to understand the influences of IT attributes during noticing.

The principal theoretical lens, IPT, is first introduced–although this paper focuses on the noticing phase, the sensemaking phase will be briefly discussed, for comparison purpose, when later conceptualizing managerial noticing of new OIT; concepts important for illustrating the differences between noticing and sensemaking will also be introduced here. Those concepts are identified via a review of related literatures, as summarized in Appendix A. After that, locations of cognitive information processing are discussed to explain how noticing occurs in general. Then this paper identifies the set of OIT attributes serving as the focus of research propositions.

## Applying IPT to Managerial Noticing

IPT (e.g., Miller, 1956, 1986), which focuses on the role of information processing in reducing the uncertainty and equivocality associated with a focal object, has been used to model managerial and organizational cognition in general (see Walsh, 1995 for a review) and, in particular, opportunity (or threat) recognition (e.g., Baron, 2004, 2006; Kirzner, 1979; Mitchell et al., 2002; Shane and Venkataraman, 2000; Shaver and Scott, 1991; Vaghely and Julien, 2010) and managerial environmental perception (e.g., Hambrick and Mason, 1984; Haukedal, 1994; Kiesler and Sproull, 1982; Milliken, 1990; Starbuck and Milliken, 1988; Sutcliffe, 1994). And, its applicability to research on innovation adoption is well recognized, with Rogers arguing a half century ago that the “... innovation-decision process is an information-seeking and informationprocessing activity…” (1962, p.20).

Researchers have long recognized that distinct noticing and sensemaking phases exist in information processing (e.g., Griffith, 1999; Louis and Sutton, 1991; McMullen and Shepherd, 2006; Starbuck and Milliken, 1988). For example, researchers argued that managers need to first notice changes in their environment and then correctly interpret those changes before responding to them (e.g., Kiesler and Sproull,1982; Milliken, 1990; Pfeffer and Salancik, 1978; Weick, 1987); noticing focuses on data collection while sensemaking focuses on giving meaning to data (Daft and Weick, 1984). In innovation adoption, Rogers’ model (1962; 2003) argued that prior to evaluating an innovation in forming a decision to adopt/reject (or the decision process), individuals need to first recognize the existence of the innovation (or the knowledge process) and then become interested in it (or the persuasion process). Essentially, the noticing phase identifies an object worthy of further examination with respect to some decision context, and the sensemaking phase reaches a conclusion regarding the object within this decision context.

The literature on managerial cognition and opportunity/threat recognition suggests that the noticing phase involves two processes, i.e., awareness and arousal (e.g., Cowan, 1986; Starbuck and Milliken, 1988). With the awareness process, individuals initially recognize the existence of cues about a focal object. A review of social psychology, opportunity recognition, and innovation adoption literatures identified one cue attribute that increases the likelihood of individuals becoming aware of cues (e.g., Lang, 2000; Nilakanta and Scamell, 1990; Rogers, 1962; Schneider and Shiffrin, 1977; Shane, 2000)—that of vividness, or the extent to which the cue is “…emotionally interesting, concrete and image-provoking, and proximate in a sensory, temporal or spatial way…” (Nisbett and Ross, 1980, p.45). Scholars studying cue vividness distinguish between the vividness of a cue’s content and the vivid presentation of a cue (see Taylor and Thompson, 1982 for a review). Considering this paper’s focus on message content, cue vividness refers to the vividness of a cue’s content, or “the extent that (it) evoke(s) cognitive elaboration of cue-relevant information in memory" (Kisielius and Sternthal, 1984, p.55). With the arousal process, individuals decide, after rudimentary analysis, whether the focal object is relevant and worthy of further examination (e.g., Kiesler and Sproull, 1982), or object relevance.

Four elements are of central importance with IPT: the issue (or issues) examined in information processing; the focus of information processing; the tactic employed in information processing; and the nature of the conclusion(s) reached. The birth of laptop computer at Toshiba, though originally used by Rogers (2003) to talk about internal product innovation, illustrates the relevance of these four elements for managerial noticing. In the early 1980s, Toshiba experienced a huge loss in the desktop computer market and was about to exit the computer business. Lead engineer Tetsuya Mizoguchi, however, believed in the strategic importance of computing to the company’s future and, thus, of the necessity to preserve and develop the company’s core competencies related to computing. Attempting to envision a computer that was compatible with but better than the market-leading IBM personal computer (the issue examined), Mizoguchi looked for indicators of flaws with the desktop computer (information processing focus). Two such flaws were the desktop computer’s foot-print and immobility. Mizoguchi came up with the idea of laptop computer when he pieced together (information processing tactic) the overly crowded office space in Japan (where several employees share a small desk), the tendency for Japanese employees to bring work back to their small homes, and the lack of desktop computers at home. Mizoguchi envisioned and commenced championing the notion of a laptop computer (the conclusion reached), which proved to be a market success.

Researchers applying the IPT lens focus their attention on sensed-information triggering an actor’s noticing and sensemaking cognitions. Generally, this information is conceptualized as the contents of sensed messages, with message contents serving one of two functions (e.g., Kruglanski and Thompson, 1999): a cue function or an argument function. A cue<sup>2</sup> refers to a signal embedded within a message that stimulates an actor’s cognitive activity, with these signals conveying information about the focal object or about related objects (i.e., events, people, etc.) (Gibson, 1960). The cue function attracts an actor to a message, creating an opportunity for the actor to allocate greater cognitive effort to message contents (e.g.,

Harrington, Lane, Donohew and Zimmerman, 2006; Palmgreen et al., 1991; Stephenson and Palmgreen, 2001). For example, the mentioning of customer loyalty is a cue that is likely to attract a manager of a company suffering from a low customer retention rate. The argument function applies a message’s contents to actor cognitions aimed at reaching conclusions about the object within some decision context (Harrington, et al., 2006). Continuing with the example of customer loyalty, after making sense of message arguments (i.e., how specific factors impact customer loyalty), the manager may reach certain conclusions regarding what to do (or to avoid) to boost customer retention rate.

Common information processing tactics include, among many others, pattern matching, learning-by-doing and learning-about. The opportunity recognition literature suggests that the information processing tactic likely to apply to managerial noticing is patter matching, the most common tactic used by actors in connecting the dots among sensed cues as well as between sensed cues and held-cognitions or memories (e.g., Baron, 2006; Baron and Ensley, 2006; Gaglio and Katz, 2001; Kruglanski and Orehek, 2007; Sloman, 1996). In the earlier example of Toshiba, Mizoguchi recognized the oppo necting the dots among the overly crowded office space, the tendency for employees to bring work home, the small homes that employees typically have, and the lack of personal computers at home.

As to the sensemaking phase, learning-by-doing and learning-about likewise contrast two broadly construed information processing tactics (e.g., Baum, Li and Usher, 2000; Haunschild and Miner, 1997 Huber, 1991; Srinivasan, Haunschild and Grewal, 2007). With learning-by-doing, actors themselves process information about the focal object in order to fashion and evolve arguments about the object. With learning-about (also referred to as vicarious learning, e.g., Terlaak and Gong, 2008), actors process information regarding informants and about these informants’ statements regarding a focal object in order to fashion and evolve arguments about the object. Using Rogers (1962)’s example of Iowa farmers deciding whether or not to adopt new hybrid corn seed to replace open-pollinated corn seed, a farmer applying learning-by-doing would plant hybrid corn seed on a small trial plot along with their normal planting of open-pollinated corn seed and then contrast outcomes; a farmer applying learning-about would listen to what various informants (e.g., neighboring farmers, Iowa Agricultural Extension Service staff, etc.) had learned, somehow weight these informants’ opinions, and then infer from the informants’ statements a likely outcome to be realized from switching to hybrid corn seed. Generally, learning-by-doing tends to be undertaken when actors believe that such a tactic is necessary and that the capability to do so exists (e.g., Chen, 1996; Chen, Su and Tsai 2007; Sussman and Siegal, 2003). Situations that render learning-by-doing unnecessary include the presence of trusted informants and of opportunities to learn without the actor having to directly engage with the focal object (Mintzberg, et al., 1976; Terlaak and Gong, 2008; Wang and Ramiller, 2009).

Table 2 describes three locations of cognitive information processing (sensory register, short-term memory, and long-term memory) and their relationships with one another (e.g., Cowan, 1988; Craik and Lockhart, 1972). Generally, environmental cues are first processed by sensory registers. Even hough sensory registers have potentially unlimited capacity, some cues may still be lost fro y registers (e.g., the smell of flowers to someone with anosmia). Cues sensed by sensory registers enter short-term memory, which decay overtime unless refreshed, i.e., receiving continued attention (Cowan, 2008; Craik and Lockhart, 1972), or rehearsed, i.e., repeated (Atkinson and Shiffrin, 1968). Information in short-term memory may be transferred to long-term memory via rehearsal/repetition or coding, i.e., associating the to-beremembered information with already-held information (Atkinson and Shiffrin, 1971). Information in long-term memory is maintained via rehearsal/repetition or organization, i.e., establishing or specifying consistent or stable relations (e.g., groups, categories, concepts) among information (Mandler, 1967); information in long-term memory can also be moved to short-term memory if activated by associations. For example, when a strawberry lover senses the word “strawberry” from received messages, related information held in long-term memory (e.g., a strawberry’s smell and taste) may be activated and moved to short-term memory.

A subset of short-term memory is “focus of attention,” also termed “working memory”<sup>3</sup>. Focus of attention holds a limited number (thought to be a range around four to seven) “chunks” of information used in active cognitive processes (Cowan, 1995, 1999, 2008; Mynatt, Doherty and Dragan,1993; Miller, 1956). Even though all short-term memory may affect individuals’ behavioral responses, it is mainly the focus of attention subset that affects individuals’ conscious and preconscious behavioral responses (e.g., Miller, 1956). For example, it is the focus of attention that retains partial results while solving an arithmetic problem without pen and paper or that holds the assumptions being combined to form a lengthy rhetorical argument.

Finally, a cue (cue c) that has entered short-term memory may, through rehearsal/ repetition or coding, be moved to long-term memory via path 3 (t=0)<sup>4</sup> (e.g., Atkinson and Shiflrin, 1968; Cowan, 1988). Later when individuals sense the same cue (cue c) or a related cue (cue c’), long-term memory content (cue c) will be activated by the sensed cue (cue c or cue c’) and subsequently enter the focus of attention via path 2 (t=1). The sequential combination of path 3 (t=0) and then path 2 (t=1) indicates that repeated exposures to or intentional coding of sensed cues help build up long-term memory, which then facilitates the matching of sensed cues (or related cues) in subsequent encounters. This sequential combination may also explain the finding in the managerial noticing literature that familiarity with a domain increases indi iduals sensitivity to subtle changes occurring within that domain (Norman, 1971; Schro Streufert, 1967). As argued by Starbuck and Milliken (1988, p. 42), “an exe moves into a new industry would initially overtook some phenomena that seem unimportant, but would gradually learn to notice those phenomena as experience clarifies their significance.”

With the awareness process, the issue examined is the detection of cues related to a new OIT and/or desired (utilitarian, social and/or hedonic) outcomes, i.e., OIT-related cues. To address this issue, the focus of information processing is on cues embedded within alerting messages received by managers. Specifically, managers actively search or passively scan their information environment for cues, some of which are likely to be about a new OIT (e.g., a new OIT mentioned during a conversation), about problems currently being faced (e.g., comments voiced during a meeting about operational efficienc , and about potential opportunities (e.g., industry murmurs that an influential customer is ab initiate a major redesign of its most profitable product line) (Haukedal, 1994; Kiesler and Sproull, 1982; Shane, 2000). OIT-related cues embedded within alerting messages may have counterparts in managers’ focus of attention and/or long-term memory. For example, an organization’s struggles with operational efficiency may leave memory traces in a manager’s focus of attention (for current concerns) or long-term memory (for lingering concerns). The information processing tactic used is pattern matching between OIT-related cues embedded within alerting messages and those in managers’ focus of attention and/or long-term memory.

As discussed earlier, individuals are more likely to become aware of cues that are vivid (i.e., evoking cognitive elaboration of related information in memory). In the context of managerial noticing of a new OIT, a manager becomes aware of OIT-related cues embedded within alerting messages when these cues match with cues maintained in either the manager’s focus of attention (via path 1b in Figure 1) or long-term memory (via path 2 in Figure 1) (Sloman, 1996; Smith and DeCoster, 1999), hence appearing vivid. That is, the vividness of OIT-related cues determines the occurrence of awareness.

Cue awareness is a necessary but not sufficient condition for arousal, where the issue examined is whether the new OIT is potentially valuable for the manager’s organization. While managers are receiving alerting messages, OIT-related cues build up at managers’ focus of attention (Cowan, 1988); these accumulated cues become the focus of information processing for the arousal process. The information processing tactic used at the arousal process is pattern matching within and across these accumulated cues in the focus of attention (Baron, 2006). Arousal occurs when cues about a new OIT are seen as being associated with cues about desired (utilitarian, social and/or symbolic) outcomes (Sproull and Kiesler, 1986; Starbuck and Milliken, 1988), thereby possessing value relevance (Dehning, Richardson and Zmud, 2003). That is, the occurrence of arousal depends on the identification of value-relevant associations among OIT-related cues that have accumulated in the focus of attention. Support for such a view is provided by Mintzberg et al. (1976, p 253): “A decision maker may be reluctant to act on a problem for which he sees no apparent solution; similarly he may hesitate to use a new idea that does not deal with a difficulty. But when an opportunity is matched with a problem, a manager is more likely to initiate decision making action.”

A manager, when aroused, detects that a new OIT may hold promise for his/her organization. The noticing phase ends and information processing then switches to a conscious, effortful sensemaking phase (Louis and Sutton, 1991)–when discussing how individuals switch between automatic and conscious cognitive processing modes, Louis and Sutton linked their ideas to Starbuck and Milliken’s distinction between noticing and sensemaking, arguing that “noticing encompasses ‘sensing and switching’…while sensemaking is equivalent to functioning in a conscious mode…” (1991, p.58). That is, in the context of OIT adoption, the noticing phase identifies a new OIT worthy of further examination, but cognitive processing needs to switch to the conscious sensemaking mode to make decisions about the OIT.

During the sensemaking phase–the focus of extant adoption literature (e.g., Venkatesh et al., 2003)–information processing focuses on the argument (rather than cue) function of received alerting messages. Essentially, managers try to assess whether the noticed OIT is preferred to a precursor or to alternatives, i.e., relative advantage beliefs. For example, consider two new OIT seen to possess comparable outcome potential but vary regarding deployment complexity. Adopters are expected to prefer the OIT perceived as being easier to deploy as it would more likely result in desired outcomes being realized, would more likely have lower implementation costs, or both. The tactics of learning-about, learning-by-doing, or a combination of the two could be utilized to evaluate the noticed OIT and form relative advantage beliefs (e.g., Bhattacherjee and Sanford, 2006; Terlaak and King, 2007). For exam ers could charge a group with the task of carrying out an assessment of the OIT for ones’ company and/or could infer from the decisions of informants (e.g., competitors). The sensemaking phase concludes with managers making an adoption decision (adopt, reject) about the noticed OIT.

To summarize, in the context of OIT adoption, the noticing phase finds a manager processing socially encoded cues embedded within alerting messages that reference a new OIT or valued outcomes. Cues that a manager becomes aware of are attended to such that certain of these cues may result in the manager becoming aroused by the opportunity-potential of the new OIT. The occurrence of awareness depends on the vividness of OIT-related cues, and the occurrence of arousal depends on the likelihood of identifying value-relevant associations.

## Influences of OIT Attributes during the Awareness Process

Result demonstrability increases the vividness of OIT-related cues in the same manner as does concreteness (Table 6), a defining characteristic (Kisielius and Sternthal, 1984; Taylor and Thompson, 1982) that enhances vividness via increasing the availability and “imageability” of related memory. Recall that the influence of cue vividness on awareness occurs through a cue matching information held in an individual’s focus of attention or long-term memory. Greater OIT result demonstrability increases the clarity and concreteness of outcomes from adopting a new OIT (Rogers, 1962; Zaltman, Duncan, and Holbek, 1973). As a result, both OIT cues and desired-outcome cues embedded within alerting messages describing greater result demonstrability are more likely to match related cues in the focus of attention and long-term memory (via path 1b or path 2 in Figure 1), appearing more vivid.

Proposition 1a

Alerting messages describing greater OIT result demonstrability increase the vividness of OIT-related cues.

Fashionness may increase the vividness of OIT-related cues via two mechanisms. First, fashionness increases the vividness of desired-outcome cues by increasing message content desirability (Table 6). Information about desired outcomes is likely to receive continued attention and hence be maintained at short-term memory (including the focus of attention). Alerting

messages about a fashionable OIT often contain (many) expressions of potential instrumental, social and/or symbolic values. This increases the likelihood that desired-outcome cues within alerting messages be matched with retained information in the focus of attention (via path 1b in Figure 1), hence appearing more vivid to managers (Wang and Ramiller, 2009). Support for such an argument can be found in the management fashion literature (e.g., Abrahamson, 1991; Abrahamson and Fairchild, 1999), which suggests that messages about a fashionable innovation appear emotionally charged, appealing and eye-catching to potential adopters.

Second, fashionness may increase the frequency of exposure to OIT-related cues (Table 6), and consequently the vividness of those cues in subsequent messages. Alerting messages about more-fashionable OIT are likely to appear more often than alerting messages about less-fashionable OIT (Rogers, 1962; Wang, 2010; Wang and Ramiller, 2009). Cues (about OIT or desired-outcomes) that appear with heightened frequencies are likely to be maintained at the focus of attention or be moved to long-term memory (via path 3 (t=0) in Figure 1) such that, over time, a new cue about this fashionable OIT or about desired-outcomes is more likely to match with information held in the focus of attention (via path 1b in Figure 1) or long-term memory (via path 2 (t=1) in Figure 1) (e.g., Feustel, Shiffrin, and Salasoo, 1983; Maclnnis, Moorman, and Jaworski, 1991; Starbuck and Milliken, 1988).

In summary, OIT-related cues embedded within alerting messages about a fashionable OIT may appear more vivid because those cues are more likely to match (via path 1b in Figure 1) with information in the focus of attention (where information is maintained due to continued attention or repetition), or because those cues are more likely to be moved to long-term memory first (via path 3 (t=0) in Figure 1) and later be matched with (or activated by) subsequently sensed cues (via path 2 (t=1) in Figure 1). Proposition 1b

Interpretive flexibility may increase the vividness of OIT-related cues paralleling the impact of complexity in Table 6. Complexity refers to the number of distinguishable elements in the cue, the dissimilarity between elements, and the degree to which combinations of cue elements are responded to as separate (versus as a unit) (Berlyne,1960). Complex cues are more likely to stimulate elaborated pattern matching with cues held in the focus of attention or long-term memory (via path 1b or path 2 in Figure 1) (Fichman, 2004), hence appearing more vivid (e.g., Morrison and Dainoff, 1972; Schleuder, 1990). Alerting messages referencing an interpretively flexible OIT contain cues that possess the potential to be related to a variety of OIT deployment configurations and, hence, to a variety of related outcomes. As a result, the probability is increased that OIT-related cues (embedded within alerting messages) will match with information in the focus of attention or long-term memory (via path 1b or path 2 in Figure 1) (Fichman, 2004), thus appearing more vivid.

Alerting messages describing greater OIT interpretive flexibility increase the vividness of OIT-related cues.

Radicalness may increase cue vividness in the same way that novelty does (Table 6). Novel cues, when first sensed by managers, are unlikely to enter the focus of attention due to a lack of associated memories with which these es could be matched. Novel cues, however, may leave a trace in the focus of attenti long-term memory (e.g., Tulving and Kroll, 1995; Van Kesteren, Ruiter, Fernández, and Henson, 2012). For example, neuro science research suggests that novel may stimulate dopamine release, consequently facilitating the formation of memory traces (Lisman, Grace, and Duzel, 2011; Lisman and Grace, 2005). Those novel cues, sequently sensed by a manager, may match with memory traces in the focus of at ntion (via path 1b) or long-term memory (via path 3 (t=0) then path 2 (t=1)). Hence, while cues about a radical OIT may not appear vivid initially, they are more likely to appear so in subsequent alerting messages.

Proposition 1d

Alerting messages describing greater OIT radicalness increase the vividness of OIT-related cues.

## Influences of OIT Attributes during the Arousal Process

Recall that the information processing focus during the arousal process is on OIT-related cues accumulated in the focus of attention and that a manager’s likelihood of becoming aroused by the OIT-related cues that have accumulated in the focus of attention is determined by the detection of value-relevant associations.

The literature on factors affecting IT value relevance has examined macro-economic factors such as a country’s technological infrastructure and law (e.g., Kim, Xiang, and Lee, 2009; Swierczek and Shrestha, 2003; Zhu, Kraemer, and Dedrick, 2004), industry factors such as industry type (e.g., Chatterjee, Pacini, and Sambamurthy, 2002; Dehning et al 2003; Dos Santos, Peffers, and Mauer, 1993; Im, Dow, and Grover, 2001), firm factors such as IT capability (e.g., Muhanna and Stoel, 2010; Santhanam and Hartono, 2003), and innovation factors such as innovation type (e.g., infrastructure versus application, Chatterjee et al. 2002; benefits to show (e.g., Dehning et al, 2003, 2004; Im et al, 2001; Otim, Dow, Grover, and Wong, 2012; Nagm and Kautz, 2008), and type of IT investment (e.g., transformative versus informational versus automate, explorative versus exploitative, Beccalli, 2007; Dehning et al, 2003; Otim et al 2012). Those influential factors involve highly contextualized issues that require in-depth analysis and are likely to affect value relevance assessment during sensemaking but not the identification of value-relevant associations during noticing, the focus of this paper.

OIT attributes (described within alerting messages) may affect the likelihood of managers identifying value-relevant associations among OIT-related cues that have accumulated in the focus of attention. Result demonstrability may increase the likelihood of identifying value-relevant associations. The tangibility of OIT adoption results increases the discernibility of the covariation between OIT adoption and desired outcomes (Venkatesh and

Davis, 2000). As a result, managers are more likely to identify value-relevant associations among OIT cues and desired outcome cues.

Proposition 2a Alerting messages describing greater OIT result demonstrability increase the likelihood of identifying value-relevant associations.

Fashionness and interpretive flexibility may increase the likelihood of identifying valuerelevant associations in a similar way. Research suggests that a high frequency of exposure may help with identifying associations/connections among cues (e.g., Craik and Tulving, 1975). Alerting messages about a fashionable OIT, as discussed earlier, are likely to appear more often (e.g., Wang, 2010) and contain a greater number of OIT-related cues (about the OIT, current adopters, adoption outcomes, etc.) than are alerting messages about a less fashionable OIT. Accordingly, a greater number of OIT-related cues may accumulate in t attention. The increased number of OIT-related cues accumulated in the ntion is likely to help managers identify associations between OIT cues an desired outcome cues.

Similarly, interpretive flexibility may increase the likelihood of identifying value-relevant associations. As discussed earlier, cues embedded within alerting messages describing greater interpretive flexibility possess a potential to relate to a variety of deployment configurations and a variety of outcomes. Hence, these cues are more likely to match with information in the focus of attention or long-term memory and are more likely to enter and remain in the focus of attention. As a result, there are more OIT-related cues accumulating in the focus of attention. The greater number of accumulated cues, consequently, increases the likelihood of identifying value-relevant associations.

Proposition 2b

Alerting messages describing greater OIT fashionness increase the likelihood of identifying value-relevant associations.

Proposition 2c

Alerting messages describing greater OIT interpretive flexibility increase the likelihood of identifying value-relevant associations.

Radicalness may reduce the likelihood of identifying value-relevant associations. Research on causal ambiguity (i.e., individuals’ lack of clear understanding of causa

relationship, e.g., the causal relationship between organizational competency and organizational performance, King, 2007; Powell, Lovallo, and Caringal, 2006; Reed and Defillippi, 1990) suggests that a highly radical OIT, which contains a great degree of new knowledge, is less likely to fit with managers’ existing mental models during pattern matching. As a result, managers are less likely to identify associations between OIT cues and desired outcome cues.

Proposition 2d Alerting messages describing greater OIT radicalness decrease the likelihood of identifying value-relevant associations.

## DISCUSSION

This paper’s research objectives were to develop a theoretical conceptualization of how managers initially notice a new OIT referenced in alerting messages and, using the developed conceptualization as a theoretical grounding, to understand when and how alerting message contents describing OIT attributes influence managers’ noticing. In achieving these objectives, this paper contributes to the literature in several ways. Figure 3 depicts how this paper contributes to the nomological network of existing ideas.

First, the comprehensive conceptualization of two cognitive processes (i.e., awareness and arousal) comprising noticing contributes to the IS adoption literature by providing a robust framework for ensuing research aimed at better understanding why organizations exhibit considerable variations in their uptakes of new OIT. In this conceptualization, the focus is on cues embedded within alerting messages that managers receive. Cues contained within efficacious alerting messages attract managers to those messages, enabling a new OIT (referenced in alerting messages) to be filtered into managers’ noticing. In the absence of wellcrafted alerting messages, ample opportunities exist for managers to overlook a potentially valuable new OIT. This paper’s treatments of how cues enter, are processed, and remain in a manager’s short-term and long-term memory during awareness and arousal may prove especially useful for scholars focused on the cognitive processes related to adoption.

Second, this paper contributes in a significant way to the rich literature on the influences of IT attributes by developing propositions regarding the influences of OIT attributes on noticing. Attributes examined in this paper have been observed to affect sensemaking processes that lead to adoption decisions (e.g., Venkatesh and Davis, 2000) and post-adoption implementation processes (e.g., Fichman, 2004) in the extant adoption literature. Here, this paper focuses on how OIT attributes described in alerting messages affect the detection of cues (about new OIT or about desired outcomes) and of value-relevant associations among cues.

While much of this paper’s theorizing regarding the influences of IT attributes suggests consistent influences of IT attributes acro noticing and sensemaking, the focus on noticing has surfaced nu nces likely to sti te further insights by others. As but one example, existing research on adoption-related semaking has a mixed view towards the radicalness of a new OIT (e.g., Damanpour, 91; Dewar and Dutton, 1986; Ettlie et al, 1984). This paper argues, however, that refer radicalness within alerting messages may cause problem for noticing. Specifically, when receiving alerting messages describing a radical OIT, managers may have trouble identifying value-relevant associations among OIT-related cues accumulated in the focus of attention and hence are less likely to become aroused. As a result, a radical OIT may not even have a chance to go through the sensemaking phase that normally precedes adoption decisions.

Another example of fresh insights regarding IT attributes is the discussion about fashionness. Although fashionness is argued to have the same favorable impact on noticing as on sensemaking, the reason for the favorable impact is different. During sensemaking, the favorable impact is due to the social and symbolic values associated with a fashionable OIT and to the information-revealing function of fashionness that helps potential adopters infer OITrelated information from others’ behaviors (e.g., Wang, 2010; Wang and Ramiller, 2009). However, during noticing, the favorable impact of fashionness is attributed to the high frequency of fashionable OITs being referred in alerting messages in addition to (or, perhaps, instead of) the social/symbolic values associated with fashionness and its information-revealing property.

Third, this paper contributes to the literature on technology frame and organizing vision. Existing literature has extensively documented the value of technology frame and organizing vision for adoption sensemaking and post-adoption phenomena. Ideas discussed in this paper provide a firmer cognitive foundation for why technology frame and organizing vision are also useful for the noticing of technology: technology discourse may affect information stored at individuals’ focus of attention or long-term memory (e.g., expectations about the technology) and the nature of alerting messages being transmitted. Consequently, technology discourse may variously affect the noticing of OITs with different attributes.

This paper’s detailed description of managerial noticing processes (i.e., information processing elements and associated information movements and locations) may also contribute to the managerial cognition and opportunity recognition literature in two major ways. First, despite recognizing the existence of distinct processes under noticing, Starbuck and Milliken (1988) did not offer a comprehensive description of noticing, an issue not adequately dealt with in subsequent research on opportunity recognition (e.g., McMullen and Shepherd, 2006; Shane and Venkataraman, 2000). A lack in specificity regarding the cognitive processes associated

with noticing may obscure our understanding of the causes for behavioral failure in opportunity recognition. Baron (2006), for example, ascribes the success of Baby Einstein Company not to the founder’s awareness of both the increasing proportion of babies born to college-educated parents and these parents’ desires to provide their children with intellectually stimulating toys and games but rather to the founder’s linking together of these two observations. Applying the conceptualization offered by this paper, the failure of most entrepreneurs to recognize such opportunity is likely to be ultimately traced to failures within the arousal process, thereby better directing researchers’ intellectual efforts.

Second, the detailed description about managerial noticing may help deepen or clarify existing understandings related to managerial cognition and opportunity recognition. Although the extant literature discussed information processing elements, the discussion is often fragmented and shallow. For example, researchers recognized the use of pattern matching to “connect the dots” during opportunity recognition (e.g., Baron and Ensley, 2006; Gaglio and Katz, 2001), but did not discuss how this dot-connecting occurs. This leads to superficial interpretation of findings. For example, while it has bee shown that individuals’ prior knowledge or experience affects opportunity recognition (e.g., Baron, 2006), the specific roles served by prior knowledge or experience are not well explained. Ideas provided herein may provide an explanation: using Figure 1, this paper can show that individuals’ prior knowledge or experience affects information stored at long-term mory, which could be activated by incoming sensed cues and consequently enter one’s focus of attention (i.e., path 2), affecting the awareness of sensed (and related) cues, cue arousal and the recognition of opportunities. Another example of superficial understandings that could be clarified using this paper is the finding in the organizational attention literature (e.g., Ocasio, 1997) that what organizational decision-makers do depends on what they focus their attention on. Using Figure 1, this paper shows that what decision-makers focus their attention on affects information stored in the focus of attention (for current concerns) or long-term memory (for lingering concerns), which then affects the awareness and arousal processes and, consequently, decision-makers’ behavioral responses.

## IMPLICATIONS FOR FUTURE RESEARCH

This paper opens a number of future research opportunities. Four such opportunities are identified below.

First, this paper encourages research that provides an enriched descriptive understanding of behavioral failures (Gavetti, 2012) regarding IT opportunity recognition. While information systems scholars have, after three decades of study, evolved robust models of adoption and post-adoption behaviors, important but unanswered questions exist, such as (but not limited to): “What behaviors and processes precede an intensive sensemaking about a new IT?” “What is the duration of noticing, and what explains variations in noticing durations?” “What is the natures of the sensed cues most likely to trigger noticing?” and “How often and why are viable OIT dismissed without sensemaking?” Without a firm theoretical base from which to examine such questions, subsequent scholarship is likely to be grounded on either anecdotal commentaries or on extant empirical findings observed within adoption and post-adoption contexts. This paper’s conceptualization of the two cognitive processes comprising noticing provides an encompassing theoretical framework from which to conduct needed empirical research on phenomena related to noticing.

As to empirical research, the second opportunity is to assess the conceptualization and propositions developed in this paper. A potential assessment method is the think-aloud approach, which has been used to understand opportunity recognition (e.g., Grégoire, Barr, and Shepherd, 2010), managerial reasoning (e.g., Isenberg 1986), and decision-making (e.g., Hall and Hofer 1993), etc., in the management literature. The management literature also provides insights into other empirical testing methods that could be used (e.g., see Kaplan, 2011 for a reflection on research methods used in the strategic management literature). When empirically testing this paper’s ideas, researchers may also consider other opportunities mentioned below.

##

Third, the developed theoretical conceptualization of managerial noticing of a new OIT could be applied to understand the role of other influential factors during noticing. In particular, this paper recognizes the value to be derived from incorporating both individual and organizational characteristics into the conceptualization and propositions offered herein. Individual characteristics, for example, have received extensive attention in the innovation and IT adoption literature (e.g., Al-Natour and Benbasat, 2009; Nambisan, et al., 1999; Rogers, 1962) and in the opportunity recognition literature (e.g., Baron, 2004; Grégoire and Shepherd, 2012). The existing literature suggests that individual characteristics such as personal innovativeness (Agarwal and Prasad, 1998), mindfulness (Butler and Gray, 2006; Sun, Fang and Zou, 2016), regulatory focus (e.g., Higgins, 1998; Baron, 2004), and absorptive capacity (Grégoire and Shepherd, 2012) are highly likely to affect managers’ noticing, both directly and in interaction with OIT attributes. Similar lines of reasoning could be made regarding organizational characteristics. Future research examining the influences of individual and organizational characteristics across the two noticing processes is especially encouraged.

Finally, the focus on cognitive information processing calls into question what is meant by an adoption decision. The current literature seems to hold an action-oriented, dichotomous view of adoption decisions (Bhattacherjee, 2001; Taylor and Todd, 1995; Venkatesh, et al., 2003): Yes, adopt and No, do not adopt. The importance of individuals’ focus of attention and long-term memory in noticing suggests that it might be beneficial to complement this actionoriented perspective with a cognitive perspective. In other words, an individual might cognitively adopt a new OIT but not commit resources to deploying it. Such a view is consistent with Fichman’s (2004) observation that adoption decision-making may involve a two-step process: first, a potential adopter decides whether to adopt an OIT for immediate application; then, if this first decision is negative, the adopter decides whether the OIT is likely to be beneficial at some future time. If this second decision is affirmative, the OIT is consciously archived (ideationally in the focus of attention or long-term memory and, possibly, materially) for future consideration,

thereby affecting subsequent episodes of noticing associated with the archived OIT. Future research examining this and related conceptualizations of adoption decision is encouraged.

## PRACTICAL IMPLICATION

Research propositions suggest a number of guidelines for OIT proponents and detractors to follow in crafting alerting messages. Prior research has identified a broad spectrum of technology proponents/detractors likely to engage in such messaging. Some proponents or detractors may be external to an organization such as vendors (e.g., Thong, Yap, and Raman, 1994), suppliers (e.g., Teo, Wei, and Benbasat, 2003), securities analysts (e.g., Benner, 2010) and external consultants (e.g., Ko, Kirsch, and King, 2005); some may be internal to an organization such as technology champions (e.g., Wixom and Watson, 2001), internal consultants (e.g., Cooper, 2000), and managers (e.g., Karahanna, Straub, and Chervany, 1999).

Proponents and detractors regularly transmit alerting messages, with these messages sometimes targeting at a general audience and sometimes a specific individual or small group of like-individuals (Rogers, 1962). For ex ple, an onsultant striving to interest clients in a new OIT may target a general audience through a story placed in an industry periodical and may target a specific client through a sales call; and, an internal IT consultant striving to introduce this same OIT to ization may target a general audience through a presentation given at a technology-update workshop offered to all managers and may target a specific manager when working directly with the manager to improve the operational performance of the manager’s unit.

In Table 7, a tentative set of guidelines is derived from propositions for use by proponents and detractors in crafting alerting messages regarding a new OIT. These guidelines are expected to vary in their applications, depending on the nature of the audience (i.e., a general audience versus a specific audience) being targeted (McMullen and Shepherd, 2006). Take as an example the guideline derived from Proposition 1a, which says that, to facilitate

awareness, proponents should provide clear and concrete messaging about the OIT and outcomes (opportunities to be addressed and/or problems to be resolved) from deploying the OIT. When applying this guideline to alerting messages targeting a general audience, proponents should clearly and concretely state all possible outcomes from deploying the OIT; in contrast, when targeting a specific audience, proponents should focus on specific outcomes of known interest to the audience (e.g., existing problems faced by the audience).

## CONCLUSION

This paper develops a theoretical conceptualization of how managers initially notice a new OIT referenced in alerting messages prior to the manager initiating sensemaking of the OIT. Elaborated descriptions of two cognitive processes that compromise noticing are provided and research propositions based on this conceptualization specifying when and how OIT attributes influence noticing are offered. These ideas provide significant contributions to information systems research on adoption and on technology frame and organizing vision as well as to research on managerial cognition and opportunity recognition.

## Appendix A: Literature Review Approach

A senior MIS scholar whose research expertise is on IT management was first consulted to provide a list of leading journals likely to publish articles related to this research (Webster and Watson, 2002). The list includes AIS senior scholars' basket of eight journals and several leading journals in management (i.e., organization science, academy of management review, academy of management journal, administrative science quarterly, management science, and strategic management journal). This list of journals was used as a starting point of literature review and, as will be explained below, the actual review went beyond journals on this initial list.

Next, the author used several different methods to identify research to be included in the review–included research (e.g., journal article, book, book chapter) needs to be related to cognitive processing issues (of IT, innovation or other related such as opportunity). First of all, the author engaged in “practical screen” (Okoli and Schabram, 2010) for every single article published since 2000 for each of the journals on the initial list. Specifically, the author looked at the title and abstract of each article to decide whether or not it is worth reading further; if in doubt, the article was included to “err on the side of inclusiveness” (Okoli and Schabram, 2010, p. 22). Next, the author conducted backward search (Webster and Watson, 2002), i.e., reviewing references of the previously identified articles. This helped to identify relevant research published prior to 2000, especially those classic works. After that, the author conducted forward search (Webster and Watson, 2002) on Google Scholar to identify additional research that cited the previously reviewed works. Multiple levels of backward and forward searches were conducted. For example, if article A was included after the initial “practical screen,” the author read the references of article A (i.e., backward search) as well as research that cited article A (i.e., forward search); this helped to identify, for example, articles B and C, and for both articles, the author read their references as well as research citing them. The multiple levels of backward and forward searches continued until the author could not find any new concepts (Webster and Watson, 2002), or, as will be explained below, achieving theoretical saturation as discussed in inductive qualitative research (Glaser and Strauss, 1967).

When reviewing identified research, the author applied a modified version of constant comparative analysis (Charmaz, 2006; Glaser and Strauss, 1967)—reviewing the existing literature is akin to analyzing qualitative data; qualitative analysis methods (e.g., constant comparative analysis) haven been applied and shown to be a convincing way of conducting literature review in IS (e.g., Bandara, Furtmueller, Gorbacheva, Miskon and Beekhuyzen, 2015; Onwuegbuzie, 2008; Onwuegbuzie, Leech and Collins, 2012). Analysis proceeded in the following steps: First of all, the author conducted open coding for all reviewed research to identify relevant concepts. Next, the author engaged in a process of sorting all open codes into similar categories. This process, sometimes labeled, “focused coding,” involves an ongoing and iterative comparison of codes to codes, codes to categories (i.e., a set of codes that are similar), and categories to categories in order to find the best-fitting category. In this case, the categories are concepts relevant to managerial noticing of new OIT. Finally, in a process similar to axial coding (i.e., analyzing data as a coherent whole in relation to emerged categories, e.g., Charmaz, 2006), the interrelationships among identified categories (i.e., key concepts) were determined. As recommended by the literature (Eisenhardt, 1989; Tracy, 2010; Wolfswinkel et al., 2012), the searching for and coding of research happened iteratively and continued until no new category (i.e., key concept) was identifiable from the existing literature and existing categories remained stable, achieving theoretical saturation (Glaser and Strauss, 1967).

An example might be helpful to illustrate the coding process. When reviewing the literature, the author noticed that many works talked about how individuals process information. Several different terms were used (e.g., vicarious learning as in Terlaak and Gong, 2008; learning-about or learning without material engagement as in Wang and Ramiller, 2009), and, in some cases, the researcher(s) did not use any explicit term and instead just described what

happened (e.g., Iowa farmers listening to the opinion of neighboring farmers as in Rogers, 1962). Hence, vicarious learning, learning-about, and listening to others all became the initial open codes. In focused coding, the three open codes were all labeled as learning-about as they described the same way of processing information; and this way of information processing is different from the other methods discussed in the literature (i.e., learning-by-doing as in Wang and Ramiller, 2009, and pattern matching as in Baron, 2006). Since learning-about, learning-bydoing and pattern matching all described different ways to process information, a higher category information processing tactic was generated. In axial coding, the author identified interrelationships among all higher categories (i.e. concepts), e.g., information processing tactic is the method individuals employ to address the examined issue(s) (another higher concept) before arriving at any potential conclusions (another higher category concept).

In the end, the author discussed his/her synthesis of the literature with the senior scholar who provided the initial journal list as well as several other colleagues. The purpose of the discussions is to solicit feedbacks especially potential “disagreements,” e.g., could they think of any concept that is not included? This is similar to negative case analysis, a validation strategy frequently used in qualitative research (e.g., Creswell, 2007).
