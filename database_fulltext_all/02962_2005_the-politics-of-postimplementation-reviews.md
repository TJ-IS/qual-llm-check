# The politics of post-implementation reviews

David Gwillim,\* Ken Dovey<sup>†</sup> & Bernhard Wieder<sup>‡</sup>

University of Technology Sydney, PO Box 123, Broadway Sydney 2007, Australia, email: \*david.gwillim@optusnet.com.au, <sup>†</sup>kend@it.uts.edu.au, and <sup>‡</sup>bernhard.wieder@uts.edu.au

Abstract. The post-implementation review (PiR) literature emphasizes the benefits of ex post evaluations of information technology (IT) projects. However, empirical studies of actual practice show that few organizations undertake any substantive form of ex post evaluation. This article presents the results of case study research in the Australian offices of two global corporations. These results support the findings reported in the literature and contribute new insights into the political factors that discourage the undertaking of PIRs in organizations. Furthermore, the results reveal a significant gap between theory and practice with respect to IT investment and IT governance practices, with no evidence found of such practices influencing ex post evaluation behaviour in these two organizations.

Keywords: ex post evaluation, IT governance, organizational leadership, postimplementation review, strategic IT investments

## INTRODUCTION

This article reports on research conducted in the Australian offices of two global corporations with respect to the factors which influence post-implementation reviews (PIRs) of information technology (IT) projects in these organizations. In particular, it focuses on the factors affecting the evaluation decision. The findings confirm claims made in the PIR literature that few companies engage in ex post evaluation of IT projects, and provide new insights into the inhibitors of ex post evaluation practices. Using an interpretative research methodology, the study illuminates the political factors that inhibit PIRs in organizations and highlights the failure of IT governance practices to address these factors effectively. In addition to the problems that are well covered in the literature, such as those relating to time, measurement and cost, this study found that the socio-political conseguences of evaluation were far more pertinent to the evaluation decision. By providing evidence of significant disincentives for managers to undertake ex post evaluation, regardless of the success of a project, this article concludes that evaluation occurs within a socio-political organizational context where power relations and vested individual interests influence the evaluation decisions made by individuals. The article also explores the influence of IT governance on ex post evaluation practices and the role of organizational structure in relation to the absence of ex post evaluation and, in particular, the problems of conducting PIRs in functional hierarchical organizational structures where functional and individual interests override the collective interest of the organization.

## THE IMPORTANCE OF PIRS

Current global IT spending exceeds one trillion dollars per annum (Trade Partners UK, 2003), accounting for more than half of most large firms’ capital expenditure (Ballantine et al., 1999), and yet there are consistent doubts that IT investments are economically sound at either the macro level – the so-called ‘IT productivity paradox’ (Willcocks & Lester, 1999a) – or the organizational level (Remenyi & Sherwood-Smith, 1999). This partly results from the difficulty of measuring the benefits of IT investments (Weill & Ross, 2004).

Smithson & Hirschheim (1998, p. 160) define information system evaluation as ‘the assessment or appraisal of the value, worth or usefulness of an information system’. Generally, information systems evaluations are divided into two categories: ex ante evaluation and ex post evaluation (Remenyi & Sherwood-Smith, 1999). Ex ante evaluation refers to evaluations undertaken at the inception of the project up to the point of implementation. A general feature of ex ante evaluation is that it is characterized by estimates, often financial, used to select and justify the investment (Farbey et al., 1995). Ex post evaluation refers to evaluations conducted after a system has been implemented or after an implementation has been abandoned. A feature of ex post evaluations is the objective of measuring the actual impact of the project or investment on business performance (Farbey et al., 1995; Smithson & Hirschheim, 1998; Lin & Pervan, 2001; Seddon et al., 2002). In this respect, Willcocks & Lester (1999b, p. 84) argue that ex post evaluation ‘is one of the most neglected, yet one of the most important areas’ of IT investment evaluation.

The IT evaluation literature identifies considerable benefits for organizations undertaking ex post evaluation of IT projects. These include improvements that come from organizationa learning (Kumar, 1990); feedback on the value derived from IT investments (Norris, 1996); improved confidence in the IT department (Hillam et a/., 2000): and the facilitation of business benefits (Seddon et al., 2002; Nicolaou, 2004). However, empirical studies consistently show low uptake of ex post evaluation in practice. suggesting either that these benefits are not beind valued or that significant inhibitors to these practices are active in organizations (Serafeimidis, 2001: Seddon et a/., 2002). In this respect. Norris (1996) identifies four reasons for the failure of organizations to conduct ex post evaluations: the perception that evaluation is unimportant or unnecessary; the difficulty of appropriate measurement in evaluation; the cost of evaluation; and political or cultural constraints. On this point, Remenyi et al. (1997, preface) argue that

the infreguency of post implementation reviews or periodic benchmarking of established information systems is one of those glaring examples of technology managers’ failure to ‘practice what they preach’. We should expect investments in information technologies to be just as carefully scrutinized as other aspects of a firm’s operations no matter how difficult the measurement of benefits may be.

Evaluating the success of IT projects has been problematic since the advent of IT systems (DeLone & McLean, 1992; Bannister & Remenyi, 1999). However, a number of factors are putting increasing pressure on business leaders to ensure the evaluation of IT investments. These include globalization, deregulation and privatization; profitability and productivity concerns; expected return on IT investments; and quality standards such as the ISO 9000 series (Smithson & Hirschheim, 1998; Remenyi & Sherwood-Smith, 1999); disruptive business strategies, based on IT, in an era of discontinuous change (Evans & Wurster, 2000); and recent auditing and governance-related legislation (Sarbanes-Oxley Act, USA; CLERP 9, Australia).

The emergence of IT governance as a business discipline also has potential to influence evaluation practices. Weill & Ross (2004. p. 3) argue that ‘effective IT governance is the single most important predictor of the value that an organization generates from IT’ and that ‘much of the challenge of effective IT governance results from the difficulty of assessing the value of IT’ (Weill & Ross, 2004, p. 103). The IT Governance Institute (2003, p. 10) describes IT governance as follows:

IT governance is the responsibility of the board of directors and executive management. It is an integral part of enterprise governance and consists of the leadership and organizational structures and processes that ensure that the organization’s information technology sustains and extends its strategies and objectives.

Information technology governance covers broad areas of IT management including IT strategy setting, risk management, value delivery, performance measurement (ex post evaluation) (Peters, 1996; IT Governance Institute, 2003) and decision rights (Weill & Ross, 2004). Ex post evaluation is one of the domains of IT governance (Van Grembergen et al., 2004) and is embedded within the best practice methodologies such as Information Technology Infrastructure Library (ITIL) (OGC, 2004), CobiT (IT Governance Institute, 2003) and portfolio management (Weill & Ross, 2004) that are used as implementation tools for IT governance. An organization that implements formal IT governance mechanisms would therefore be expected to undertake ex post IT evaluation.

A recent trend away from positivist approaches towards the greater use of interpretative methodologies that are sensitive to the socio-political factors that influence the role of IT in enabling organizational change is discernible in the research literature on evaluation (Walsham, 1997; Remenyi, 2000; Palvia et al., 2001; Serafeimidis & Smithson, 2003). This change in methodological orientation has occurred in tandem with the growing complexity of IT systems and their increasing mission-critical status in organizations (Walsham. 1997: Weill & Broadbent, 1998; Doherty & King, 2000). As the investment stakes get higher, the ‘politics of evaluation’, and claims like that of Legge (1984) that the meta-function of all evaluation is to either sustain or question the status quo, have to be addressed. Increasingly, political factors that influence the evaluation decision are being articulated in the research literature. For example, Serafeimidis (2001, p. 99) acknowledges that evaluation ‘often becomes a political instrument which influences the balance of organizational power’, a point supported by Hillam et al. (2000) who argue that such politics include the use (or non-use) of evaluation to protect or undermine functional and individual power interests. Similarly, Smithson & Hirschheim (1998)

raise the political problem concerning who determines what. how. when or whetherto evaluate and argue that these decisions can influence power interests and relations in organizations. In practice, it seems that evaluation decisions are more likely to be based on the perceived interests of those with the most power in an organization (Walsham, 1997; Serafeimidis & Smithson, 2000; Serafeimidis, 2001), and any fear among powerful individuals or functions that an evaluation is likely to result in a negative outcome for them will effectively prevent its use in organizations (Willcocks & Lester, 1996).

## RESEARCH METHODOLOGY

As our intention was to understand the phenomenon of ex post evaluation decision-making within the complexity of its organizational context, we followed the interpretative research paradigm (Burrell & Morgan, 1979) and used a dual case study research method. Primary data were gathered through interviews with senior executives in two selected companies in an attempt to access their experience of the factors that influence the evaluation decision in their companies and, thereby, to gain a deeper understanding of the motivation of individual managers with respect to the evaluation decisions taken by them. Following Hakim (1987, p. 61), our intention was to provide a ‘richly detailed portrait of a particular social phenomenon’.

The two companies were selected for the study on the basis of having sizable IT investments; having operations in Australia; not having IT as a ‘product’ (thus keeping the research focus on IT as a business enabler); being for-profit business companies; and claiming to practise ex post evaluation of IT investments. Only two of the companies canvassed met all these criteria. Company A is a retailer of consumer convenience items with 170 employees and a turnover of A\$100 million in Australia and Company B is a manufacturer of high-value consumer/commercial durables with 1500 employees and a turnover of A\$7 billion in Australia Both companies implemented SAP enterprise resource planning (ERP) software in the past 5 years and are currently undertaking major business intelligence and customer facing systems projects. Company A has 16 full-time IT employees, Company B has 72 full-time employees in the IT organization itself plus a significant number of ‘IT’ employees hired and managed directly by other business units. Both companies franchise distribution, have been operating in Australia for over 20 years and have a high level of local autonomy with respect to IT investment decisions and IT evaluation choices.

Those interviewed in each company were chosen on the basis of being senior enough to have direct input into IT evaluation decisions and IT governance and/or company strategy in general. As a result, interviews were conducted with the Chief Financial Officer (CFO) and Chief Information Officer (CIO) in Company A and a Divisional General Manager, the CIO and a Senior Business IT Executive in Company B.

The interviews were semi-structured, allowing the interviewer to use open-ended questions (see Appendix 1) to explore the experience of the interviewees with respect to the topic under research and then to probe, through subsequent questions, the issues raised by the executives interviewed. In this way, rich data were accessed with respect to the implicit and tacit dimensions of the organizational contexts in which evaluation decisions are made (Brenner. 1985). The interviews, each of approximately 1-hour duration, were tape-recorded and transcribed, with each interviewee being provided with a transcript of his/her interview to validate their recorded responses. Furthermore, an attempt was made to collect additional documentary evidence, such as minutes of meetings, evaluation reports, policy documents and procedural documents, in order to corroborate the information gathered through the interviews (Stake, 1995).

Investigator triangulation was achieved by comparing the independent analyses of the interview transcripts conducted by each of the three authors. Dominant themes upon which the three authors achieved agreement were identified and those recurring across all interviews within a specific company were taken to reflect an accurate picture of evaluation practices within the company.

## RESEARCH FINDINGS

## Evaluation practices

Despite both companies claiming in preliminary qualifying discussions that they were active in IT evaluation processes, the interviews revealed that, on the contrary, substantive ex post evaluation is not undertaken in either company. Despite the Senior Business IT Executive in Company B admitting that ‘many multimillion dollar [IT] projects [have been implemented] in this organization that have not delivered the benefit’ and the Divisional General Manager of Company B that ‘the company has spent thousands upon thousands of dollars on good ideas in the IT respect, with very little benefit’, neither company has any substantive ex post IT evaluation process at project, investment, IT portfolio or IT departmental level. Evaluation, to the extent it does exist, is focused on ex ante evaluation and in the case of Company A, on project delivery. Organizational learning, benefits realization and total cost of ownership issues appear to be ianored.

Both CIOs initially indicated that they were (independently) undertaking ex post evaluation on IT projects. When neither company was able to provide any documented evidence of ex post evaluation practices, further probing questions revealed confusion regarding the difference between ex ante and ex post evaluation and led to their subseguent admission that no ex post evaluation is performed in either company.

Furthermore, no change in ex post evaluation practices over the past 5 years was reported in either company, although managers in both cited increased interest in ex ante evaluation and project delivery processes. In Company A, the rise in interest was attributed to the increasing cost of individual IT projects, with the implementation of SAP R/3 being cited as a turning point, and also an increase in corporate governance practices by the Board of Directors following the recent publicity over major corporate failures and the resulting prosecution of company directors. As the CFO of Company A noted, ‘the risk management part of IT [governance] has really become a driver since the collapse of Enron and HIH and a few directors went to jail’.

In Company B, high IT spending and the perception that IT does not always deliver value to the business (Remenyi & Sherwood-Smith, 1999; Seddon et al., 2002) was driving change. In addition, a new CEO appointed 3 years before our interview, emphasized the utilization of technology for major business improvement (Ross & Weill, 2002) and had a desire to gain a better understanding of the IT investment. As a result, project approval processes have been tightened with an emphasis on ex ante control of expenditure on new IT projects and improving project delivery processes.

## Inhibitors to evaluation

Several inhibitors that are noted in the literature were confirmed by managers in both companies. The first of these is the assumption that ‘nothing will change as a consequence of evaluation’. As the CIO of Company A observed, ‘if you buy a SAP solution you don’t embark on that lightly. Once you have got it, you very rarely pull it out'. Managers in Company B stressed the importance of choosing the correct solution with business buy-in ‘up-front’ – during the initial business approval stage – in order to get it right. These comments reflect a view of IT projects as singular events rather than as emergent or evolutionary phenomena. Thus, both companies subscribe to the sunk cost theory (Seddon et al., 2002) and show no recognition of the value of organizational learning or of trying to improve systems after roll-out (Nicolaou, 2004).

A second inhibitor raised by these managers is the prevailing attitude among managers that there is no need for formal ex post evaluations as the results are obvious. As the CIO in Company A exclaimed, ‘I don’t think you need a formal review when the results are hitting you in the face!’ Furthermore, managers in both companies felt that formal evaluation processes would distract them from their core function. As the CIO of Company A argued, ‘sometimes you have all these measurements and governance and I’m not sure they don’t take the focus away from what you are actually here to do’.

Other inhibitors mentioned that are well represented in the literature were: too much effort to collect data (Seddon et al., 2002); the difficulty of identifying benefits (Willcocks & Lester, 1996); and the lack of resources and time to conduct the evaluation (Kumar, 1990; Seddon et al., 2002).

Those managers interviewed also referred to the difficulty and cost of obtaining base line data for use in ex post evaluations. As the Senior IT Business Executive in Company B noted, ‘To be able to actually assess it you have to capture the baseline data. If you don’t capture the baseline data what are you actually measuring against?’ Thus, comparable measures and metrics may need to be planned and developed in the early stages of project development. Without these data it was felt that effective evaluation may not be possible.

With respect to inhibitors to evaluation that are not well covered in the literature, the interview data reflect a strong disincentive for managers to evaluate ex post because of the potential loss of resources and staff if the project is shown to be successful. In justifying IT projects at the approval stage, business managers often identify cost savings or revenue increases as business benefits to be gained from the investment. As a result, managers in both companies alluded to the point that it is not in the interests of a manager to undertake evaluation as ‘negative benefits may be realized' (the resource reductions that had been promised would have to be conceded). Thus, project success comes at a cost for them. As the CIO in Company A observed, ‘If you are to say ok, we are going to implement a [IT] project and it is going to impact the X department, the benefit is you’re only going to need a resource half time. Well, are we going to get rid of half a resource? Oh no, I don’t think that is right’. A similar concern was raised by the CIO in Company B, ‘I guess the difficulty always is that in a large organization many of the benefits, if you like, are cost savings or headcount savings and people are reluctant to actually put them back on the table [through ex post evaluation] because then the expectation from the finance group is that those savings are actually baked into future budgets’. The Senior Business IT Executive in Company B related an experience in a previous company: ‘When we got it to a point where we nailed it down to actually delivering it, they [the business managers] said, “oh no, no, no, it is not like that. We are not taking that many people out” ’. Thus, the attitude prevails that if ex post evaluation is conveniently forgotten, the staff reductions or other benefits promised at the project approval stage will be forgotten as well.

Another consequence of evaluation raised in the interviews is that it may highlight failings in the system. The CIO of Company B described it as ‘a measure of embarrassment’, citing this as another reason for reluctance to evaluate ex post. This is consistent with the argument of Norris (1996) that evaluation is often about finding failures and thus creating a negative outcome for a manager. Kumar (1990, p. 210) refers to this inhibitor as the ‘feeling that evaluation will unearth problems better left undisturbed’.

Taken together, these two issues reflect the perception that, regardless of the outcome of the evaluation, a manager can expect negative consequences to arise from it.

There is, at least at the rhetorical level, a belief among managers that ex post evaluation ‘should’ be undertaken. In this respect, the CIO of Company A noted, ‘my experience says you have to go back across these things to make sure you do get the benefit that you actually specified you would get’. Similarly, the CIO of Company B said, ‘certainly it is important to measure success'. However, these beliefs are not leading to ex post evaluation practices because of the lack of a clear value proposition for such practices. As Symons & Walsham, (1988) speculate, the notion of formal ex post evaluation reflects an abstract belief in rational management more than a perception of its value as a trusted aid to decision-making. This situation is supported by references in the literature to ex post evaluation being a ‘necessary evil’ (Smithson & Hirschheim, 1998).

## The issue of IT governance

To assist in the characterization of IT governance within each organization, questions were asked that allowed each organization to be ranked against the IT Governance Maturity Model developed by the IT Governance Institute (2003, p. 36, see appendix). The model consists of six levels ranging from Level 0 'non-existent' to Level 5 optimized' IT governance. On the IT governance maturity scale both companies can be classified as being at Level 1 (the ‘initial/ad hoc’ maturity phase), the characteristics of which are described as: ‘the concept of IT governance does not exist formally and oversight is based mostly on management’s consideration of IT related issues on a case by case basis. The governance of IT depends on the initiative and experience of the IT management team, with limited input from the rest of the organization. Upper management is involved only when there are major problems or successes. The measurement of IT performance is typically limited to technical measures and only within the IT function’. This description is consistent with our findings in both companies.

Although the terminology of governance was used by managers during the interviews, they were confused about what the term actually meant and they clearly had no defined programme of IT governance. Their interpretation of IT governance was firmly focused on risk management with respect to the investment approval process. As the CIO in Company A commented, ‘If I have 20 things to do, I see IT governance as number 25’. Thus, in these companies, IT governance is not a factor in the ex post evaluation decision.

## DISCUSSION OF THE RESULTS

In the interviews, both Company A and Company B managers initially indicated that they were active in the measurement and ex post evaluation of IT performance. However, probing interview questions and the inability of managers to produce documentary evidence in support of their claims revealed that no substantive ex post evaluation is practised in either company, a fact that they admitted to in the latter part of their interviews. If this research had been conducted as a survey administered to the CIOs – a method upon which much of the research literature is based – the results may have been very different. Our experience during the interviews suggests that as survey respondents, these managers would have claimed that ex post evaluation was undertaken in their companies and without careful construction of the survey instrument these claims could have been accepted uncritically. This observation is consistent with the view of Walsham (1997) and Serafeimidis (2001) who argue that interpretative research methods appear better able to uncover the political nature of evaluation and to provide richer explanations on how political factors influence the evaluation decision.

This study also reveals that in the absence of formal IT governance procedures and formal drivers of ex post evaluation the managers interviewed would undertake evaluation only if the personal benefits of such practices outweighed any personal costs; or if they were compelled to do so by a more senior manager who perceived a benefit in undertaking evaluation. This finding is consistent with the assertion made by Seddon et al. (2002) that specific push factors must be in place to overcome the inhibitors and force managers to undertake ex post evaluation, and with the position of Kumar (1990) who stresses the responsibility of senior managers to influence the ex post evaluation decisions of their line managers. While not interviewed directly in this study, the Board of Directors in each company appears to be more concerned about controlling spending on new IT projects than on leveraging the value of IT projects through ex post evaluations. Thus, there appears to be no pressure from the Board of Directors for managers to undertake ex post evaluation, or as Norris (1996) terms it. no Board cata/yst for such action. This is consistent with the finding of Sohal & Ng (1998) that Boards do not consider the realization of expected benefits from IT to be of significant importance. As IT governance practices are immature in both organizations they neither influenced evaluation decisions nor override manager self-interest.

Without a formal evaluation policy or direction from the Board of Directors, it seems that IT and business executives will act rationally in their own interest (Walsham, 1997) and/or in response to personal business pressures (Serafeimidis, 2001). As this study shows, managers perceive themselves to be damned whether evaluations show projects to be successful or not. Thus, as long as managers perceive personally negative consequences irrespective of the outcome of ex post evaluations, a strong disincentive to undertake them exists. The paradox, though, is that what is in the interests of the organization (the delivery of promised business benefits such as headcount reduction and organizational learning) is not perceived to be in the interests of managers. On this point, Seddon et al. (2002, p. 2) comment that ‘there may be sound organizational reasons why managers might prefer not to evaluate IT as comprehensively and thoroughly as researchers have advocated’.

In the literature organizational learning is considered a major benefit of ex post evaluation. For example Seddon et al. (2002, p. 14) argue that. 'the ultimate benefits are the improvements in organizational performance that are expected to result from organizational learning that, in turn, is expected to result from formal evaluation of various IT investment decisions’. However, organizational learning was not cited by any of the respondents in this study as a reason for undertaking ex post evaluation. The prevailing view reflected in these interviews was that once a system is implemented, that ‘is it’ – nothing can be changed or gained from evaluation. The Senior IT Business Executive in Company B related an experience in active ex post evaluation in another organization where 6 months of intensive evaluation was undertaken before abandoning the evaluation as ‘too hard’. As he claimed, ‘no one was utilizing or getting any value out of them [the metrics] – either the culture, the structure or the desire was not there to realize continuous change’.

One factor upon which the evaluation research literature is almost silent is the influence of organizational structure on evaluation practices. The dominant organizational form, the functional hierarchy, is notorious for the political culture that it breeds, a culture characterized by interfunctional competitiveness and the pre-eminence of individual interests (Handy, 1995; Schein, 1997). The failure of organizations to link this structure to the political interests that inhibit ex post evaluation may be another negative consequence of the historical dominance of positivist assumptions in human affairs. The project management literature is beginning to address the role of structure in the problems of implementing cross-functional initiatives such as a strategic proiect management office, portfolio selection methodologies and proiect teams (Bishop, 1999; van Der Merwe, 2001). This research may have relevance for ex post evaluation research.

## CONCLUSION

By exploring the dynamics of the ‘evaluation decision’ within the Australian offices of two global corporations, this study confirms several findings reported in the research literature and raises issues that are less well covered. First, in spite of the reported high value assigned to ex post evaluation by managers. this study confirms that few organizations undertake it in practice. While the ‘need’ to undertake ex post evaluation is acknowledged by all managers interviewed, both companies in this study have opted for the alternative strategy of ex ante control in practice. Similarly, to the extent that they exist, IT governance processes in both companies are focused on ex ante control over IT expenditure. A key insight gained from this research is that socio-political factors, more than technical factors, inhibited the ex post evaluation practices in the organizations under study. This finding adds support to the few, but growing number of, researchers such as Seddon et al. (2002, p. 2) who argue that there are often ‘sound organisational reasons as to why managers might prefer not to evaluate IT as comprehensively and thoroughly as researchers have advocated’. More research into this issue is needed if greater clarity on such reasons is to be gained.

Second, the study raises questions concerning the validity of some of the conclusions reported in the research literature. In particular, the emphasis within this literature upon technical factors as the key inhibitors of ex post evaluation in organizations, and claims that 50% of organizations do engage in ex post evaluation (see Seddon et al., 2001), may reflect paradigmatic research issues (such as the ontological, epistemological and methodological assumptions of the researchers) rather than factual situations. In this respect, this study suggests the need for a more extensive debate on research paradigms and their relative appropriateness to various research questions and issues.
