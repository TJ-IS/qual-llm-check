# Is theory king?: questioning the theory fetish in information systems

## Abstract

This paper suggests that there is too much emphasis on the requirement for theory use and theory building in qualitative research published in our leading journals. We discuss six concerns that we have that relate to this high status of theory in such papers. We argue for what we refer to as ‘theory light’ papers where theory plays no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant part in the paper and the contribution lies elsewhere, for example, new arguments, facts, patterns or relationships. Some examples of theory light papers (and research) are provided from other disciplines and one exemplar information systems paper is studied in depth. We see these papers as equally worthy as those which demonstrate the applicability and predictive qualities of theory use as well as the potential of theory building. We propose a list of 10 questions that authors and reviewers might ask themselves when writing or reviewing such theory light papers. The more demanding role of the reader is also discussed along with the requirement for editorial teams to adapt. We suggest that the requirement for a contribution to theory would be replaced with the requirement that any journal paper has a high potential for stimulating research that will impact on information systems theory and/or practice.

## Motivation

his paper questions the role of theory in qualitative research in information systems. At present theory has a very high status. In an MIS Quarterly editorial commentary<sup>1</sup> on what makes a paper accepted in a leading journal, Straub (2009: vi) argues that ‘Theory is King’ (original capitalization). He continues that a ‘required element’ for any excellent paper is that it ‘suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently uses or develops theory. The highly rated Journal of the AIS has held a workshop at the International Conference on Information Systems each year since 2002 where ‘theory development’ has been the consistent theme. The workshop ‘promotes scholarship that focuses on new theoretical advances in the information systems <sup>fi</sup>eld’ (JAIS, 2012). This consistent theme of the JAIS workshops would seem to concur with the view that ‘Theory is King.’ Again, Gregor (2006: 613) argues: ‘Developing theory is what we are meant to do as academic researchers and it sets us apart from practitioners and consultants.

As we shall see in the next section, we have six inter-related concerns about this pre-eminence of theory in qualitative papers in IS. Of course we are not ‘against’ theory in our research papers, rather its pre-eminence, and because of that, the potential of researchers to fall into the traps expressed in our six concerns. As co-founding editor of the Information Systems Journal, the <sup>fi</sup>rst author suggests that a paper expressing new and exciting ideas is not only at least equally deserving of such monarchic status as theory but also devilishly dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to achieve and somewhat rare in our leading journals. We ask readers to look at all the papers published in our leading journals (which we de<sup>fi</sup>ne as the eight journals in the senior scholars’ basket of leading research journals (AIS, 2014)) over a period of, say, one year. How many of these papers do you <sup>fi</sup>nd truly original, challenging and excite you? We fear that the response might be ‘not many.’ In our view papers in our leading journals should have that impact.

Why then has theory gained this pre-eminent status in IS research journals? One explanation is related to the several years’ discussion about the status of the discipline as a whole. King and Lyytinen (2006) provide excellent source material on this discussion in its most recent form sparked off by Benbasat and Zmud (2003). The title of Orlikowski and Iacono (2001) Desperately Seeking the ‘IT’ in IT Research: A Call to Theorizing the IT Artifact (discussed most recently in Akhlaghpour et al., 2013) not only suggests limiting IS research to rather a narrow <sup>fi</sup>eld but the ‘desperate search’ seems to apply to the need to theorize in order to enhance the status of the discipline. Ron Weber perhaps expresses this ‘desperation’ best in his MIS Quarterly editorial (Weber, 2003) where he compares IS with his academic training: ‘In the information systems discipline, I searched in vain for the powerful, general theories that underpinned research in disciplines like physics and economics.’

It seems that the drive for theory has been seen as the way forward to make information systems an ‘acceptable’ discipline, particularly with it suffering a ‘crisis,’ at least judged by the titles of papers such as that of Benbasat and Zmud (2003). Thankfully, many of the responses to that paper have been more positive such as Agarwal and Lucas (2005) who argue that ‘the IS community has a powerful story to tell about the transformational impact of information technology’ (p. 381). As we discuss below, this is a concern discussed in other disciplines as well as IS.

An interesting aside, and following on from Ron Weber’s comment above, is that the <sup>fi</sup>rst author of this paper was trained in the discipline of economics in the mid-1960s and remembers similar kinds of debates in that <sup>fi</sup>eld: ‘Is economics a science?’ ‘Is it a social science?’ ‘Is it a quantitative or qualitative?’ and so on. At that time it was developing into an exciting and broad discipline with many points of view and many contributions (rather like IS). It was also in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by many theories (I was trained in particular by a Marxist professor of economics). As I expressed in Avison (2012), economics has become much more one-dimensional since those exciting times and is much poorer as a result. The history of the discipline of economics is a warning to us and not a model to follow.

Finally, with regard to motivation, the <sup>fi</sup>rst-named author in this paper has himself been complicit in this emphasis on theory when editing the Information Systems Journal. I would also claim that I have made contributions to theory building myself, for example in my work with Trevor Wood-Harper (Avison and Wood-Harper, 1990) developing the Multiview framework for information systems development using action research (Avison et al., 1999). Further we do not have any personal animosities with any IS colleague, certainly not those cited in this paper. But we feel the emphasis on theory has gone too far and we wish to argue a contrary viewpoint. As an editor I was also concerned about the possibility of ‘acceptance by stealth,’ that is papers that had all the ‘necessary’ ingredients (including theory) and referees had dif<sup>fi</sup>culties rejecting them, yet the paper itself was basically dull, made minimal real contribution and would make very little, if any, impact on the community. We fear that many of these papers are still being published.

Before looking at our six concerns that relate to the high status of theory in information systems papers, it should be made clear that this is not the ‘rigor versus relevance’ debate, see for example, Applegate and King (1999) and Straub and Ang (2011) in another form. We contend that theoretical and non-theoretical papers can be rigorous (with others of both types not rigorous) and theoretical and non-theoretical papers relevant (with papers of both types not relevant). The title of Van de Ven’s (1989) paper ‘Nothing is quite so practical as a good theory,’ taken from Lewin (1945), would suggest that theoretical papers can also be relevant. Van de Ven argues (p. 36) that ‘Good theory is practical precisely because it advances knowledge in a scienti<sup>fi</sup>c discipline, guides research towards crucial questions, and enlightens the profession of management.’ Further, as Eisenhardt (1989) has shown, it is possible to build and evaluate theory from qualitative case study research. However, in this paper we will discuss how papers that do not stress theoretical contributions may be rigorous as well as relevant, and thereby make an important contribution to IS research and practice.

## Six concerns regarding the emphasis on theory in IS research In this section we discuss six inter-related concerns about this pre-eminence of theory in our papers.

1. The temptation to revert to ‘ideal types’ in our understanding process: One basis for concern is that researchers might oversimplify their data to look like an ‘ideal type’ data set. Researchers need to ‘tell a story’ and conforming to an ideal type will help the researcher’s ability to gain the readers’ understanding as they try to make sense of the data. The restrictions of word count, appealing to the general-interested reader (not just experts in that branch of the <sup>fi</sup>eld) and gaining the acceptance of referees, cannot help but tempt the author to simplify their empirical data sets. Ideal types are ones with which there is some familiarity to give the new data a familiar context for understanding. However, as Weber (1946: 88) argues, ‘an ideal type is formed by the one-sided accentuation of one or more points of view according to which concrete individual phenomena are arranged into a uni<sup>fi</sup>ed analytical construct in its purely <sup>fi</sup>ctional nature. It is a methodological utopia [that] cannot be found empirically anywhere in reality.’ It might be argued that we need to form these exaggerated depictions ‘to see’ things, patterns and relationships, but we live in a complex world and in our view we need to study this complexity to truly understand phenomena. There is a temptation to include only those parts of the data that conform to a particular theoretical framework rather than trying to see the data as a whole and understand it holistically. These ideal types may miss part of the organizational complexity that might otherwise lead to valuable insights. One solution might seem to be to write another paper discussing another slice, and this might seem good for an academic career. But ‘the whole is greater than the sum of the parts’ and the potential contribution of a holistic piece much greater. Of course it is never possible to fully understand the world, but simplifying to <sup>fi</sup>t into an ideal type might be too reductionist. Such simpli<sup>fi</sup>cation reduces the potential contribution of information systems research to the community.

2. Distortion of our understanding of the research setting: Related to the <sup>fi</sup>rst concern, and following an interpretivist stance (Walsham, 1995), it is commonly accepted that the paradigms and theories to which we are exposed not only guide analysis and interpretation processes but also our perception of the empirical <sup>fi</sup>eld as a whole (Gregor, 2006). The theoretical frameworks from which a social scientist is sympathetic have an in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the future orientations of a research project, the questions asked in the <sup>fi</sup>eld, and the stance adopted for data collection (Alvesson and Sköldberg, 2009). Empirical researchers are socially, culturally and historically exposed (Giddens, 1984), and thus choice of theory has an in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the way research is carried out, as we are all re<sup>fl</sup>exive agents (Jones and Karsten, 2008). As Walsham (1995: 76) puts it ‘there is a danger of the researcher only seeing what the theory suggests, and thus using the theory in a rigid way which sti<sup>fl</sup>es potential new issues and avenues of exploration. It is desirable in interpretive studies to preserve a considerable degree of openness to the <sup>fi</sup>eld data, and a willingness to modify initial assumptions and theories.’ Following this argument, it is conceivable to expect that some dimensions of the research <sup>fi</sup>eld might be neglected, ignored or reduced inappropriately as the theoretical and worldview in<sup>fl</sup>uences to which we are exposed condition our orientations to carry out research (Weick, 1989).

3. Assessing the fit of a theory: Researchers usually acquire a good knowledge of both research methods and theory in their doctoral studies. However, even though doctoral programs help researchers to build solid foundations for knowledge, expertise and self-awareness (Klein and Rowe, 2008), there is no ‘recipe’ to choose, guide and evaluate the <sup>fi</sup>t of a theoretical framework to empirical data collected. Truex et al. (2006) and Straub (2012: iv–v) discuss the theory de<sup>fi</sup>nition and theory creation processes, as do DiMaggio (1995), Sutton and Staw (1995), Van de Ven (1989), Weick (1989, 1995) and Whetten (1989) outside IS, but there are too few re<sup>fl</sup>ective accounts (but see Pozzebon and Pinsonneault, 2005) addressing how any potential gap between theory and data can be addressed. Again, to ‘tell a good story’ the temptation might be to choose data that <sup>fi</sup>ts. In our view this is not only ethically unsound, it is to the detriment of the discipline as it is just those data that do not <sup>fi</sup>t the theory that might signal further research and potentially provide a much greater contribution. But editors and reviewers may be left unsatis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the unresolved issues and decide not to publish.

4. The applicability of ‘borrowed’ theories: We also question the direct applicability of some theories from other discip lines to IS research. Borrowing theories from other disciplines may well be appropriate (Orlikowski and Barley, 2001) as our discipline is at the cornerstone of different <sup>fi</sup>elds (Avison and Myers, 1995). However, sometimes ‘the shoes do not <sup>fi</sup>t’ (Miller, 2007). Truex et al. (2006) suggest general reasons why this might apply. Some examples of these dif<sup>fi</sup>culties have been discussed in the literature. For example, Pozzebon and Pinsonneault (2005) discuss dif<sup>fi</sup>culties of empirical applicability in the context of structuration theory; Mitev (2009) discusses actor network theory in a similar vein; and Lacity et al. (2011) discuss the inappropriate importation of transaction cost economics into IT outsourcing research. Further, editors, reviewers and readers may be less interested in ‘yet another paper using theory X,’ but rather prefer to accept a paper referencing a theory not yet ‘imported’ into information systems even if it is not particularly appropriate. On the other hand, there is also an element of ‘fashion’ (Baskerville and Myers, 2009) in the use of theories. To give two examples, at the time of writing, there is a special issue on sociomateriality (Cecez-Kecmanovic et al., 2011) forthcoming and this view has partly superseded that of socio-technical theory (Mumford, 2006), while contingency theory (Weill and Olson, 1989) seems to have completely gone out of fashion. In addition, the use of multiple theoretical frameworks to make sense of rich empirical data from multiple layers or perspectives might be relevant in some situations, but this adds additional dif<sup>fi</sup>culties for the data interpretation process, and raises the question of framework compatibility. These problems are explored in Mingers (2001) and Venkatesh et al. (2013).

5. Opportunity cost of emphasizing theory: Again, inter-related with the others, is the lost opportunity of making other valuable contributions in our papers fully because of the necessity of providing a full account of theoretical contributions. Responding to Shirley Gregor’s comment cited earlier, we think that developing theory is not the only contribution that should set researchers apart from practitioners and consultants. In the same editorial that Detmar Straub (2009) argues that ‘Theory is King,’ he also encourages papers that move us into ‘intellectual territory that is unexploited,’ which we enthusiastically support. He refers to these as ‘exciting, blue ocean ideas.’ For us a paper that does this well, and all too rarely, is that of Schultze (2000) in her confessional account of her ethnography project which we discuss fully below. We share Straub’s (2009) disappointment that ‘if the ideas are too radical and lie too far beyond the reviewers’/evaluators’ experience, their comfort zones will be stretched beyond their limits and the sad result will be rejection of the paper’ (p. v1). However, our reaction is not to encourage researchers to resort to ‘popular themes’ nor to follow ‘a recognizable formula’ as he suggests and neither is it to avoid contradicting ‘the work of movers and shakers’ (Straub, 2009), but rather to encourage reviewers and editors to take a risk if the paper is exciting and makes an excellent contribution, even if it might be ‘dif<sup>fi</sup>cult’ for readers and does not discuss theory fully. Otherwise, the process leads to what Straub (2008) describes in an earlier editorial as ‘Type II reviewing errors’ where exciting papers are rejected (whether the paper’s innovative contribution is theoretical or otherwise). He is correct to argue that there is ‘an underlying conservatism in science and scienti<sup>fi</sup>c endeavors’ (Straub, 2009: viii), but our view is not to pander to this conservatism but <sup>fi</sup>ght it. However, such innovative papers need to convince with strong evidence (in short, be rigorous), and that may mean (but not necessarily mean – as the contribution might be theoretical) that theory takes a ‘back seat.’ At an ICIS panel (Lee et al., 2000) re<sup>fl</sup>ecting on Markus’ (1983) exciting contribution on case study research, the author herself intervened by saying that she did not think the paper would have been accepted in 2000 by a leading journal – in other words, reviewers and editors might have become even more conservative. This trend needs to be reversed if we are to avoid going from one ‘crisis’ to another.

6. The risk of triviality: Another danger in an insistence on theory is that a paper might make an excellent contribution where, for example, empirical results are convincing and interesting, but is rejected by the reviewers because the theoretical contribution (but perhaps not the word count) of that aspect included in an attempt to ‘get the paper accepted’ is, in reality, minimal. Perversely, triviality might also be the result of the requirement for theoretical rigor ‘which favours validation rather than usefulness’ (Lindblom, 1987: 512). As Weick (1989) argues: ‘a good theory is a plausible theory, and a theory is judged to be more plausible and of higher quality if it is interesting rather than obvious, irrelevant or absurd, obvious in novel ways, a source of unexpected connections, high in narrative rationality, aesthetically pleasing, or correspondent with presumed realities’ (p. 517). Papers that contain theoretical contributions of this type are important to our discipline, but those that do not, and where contributions lie elsewhere, are best left, we contend, ‘theory light’ with their main contribution that lies elsewhere developed to the full.

All these concerns are not about appropriate emphasis of theory in papers, but the potential of inappropriate emphasis or inappropriate use of theory or theoretical frameworks. For a discussion of types of theory and theories used rigorously and appropriately, Gregor (2006) is an excellent resource, but that is not our concern of this paper. The implication from our discussion is that some papers in our leading journals might emphasize contributions other than theory, what we refer to as ‘theory light.’

Theory light qualitative research papers are those papers where theory plays no significant part in the paper and the contribution lies elsewhere.

Although these papers may contain some reference to established theories or theory construction, they would not pass the ‘acid test’ that it signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly uses or develops theory. It should make other signi<sup>fi</sup>cant contributions to IS research and practice. Klein and Rowe (2008) refer to one potential alternative contribution that is based on practical experience, but there are other possible contributions that should also ‘be considered as different from but of equal value to theory’ (p. 675). These papers might highlight ‘the discovery of new arguments, facts, patterns or relationships that, in a convincing way, help us to better understand some phenomenon that is of consequence to a social or scienti<sup>fi</sup>c constituency. Such research may bear little or no connection to pre-existing or future theory, span many theories, or give rise to understanding that only eventually will form the basis of new theories’ (Miller, 2007: 6).

We look next at discussions and exemplars of papers (and research) that exhibits these qualities, that is, papers that make an excellent contribution, but one which is not theory based. Following Orlikowski and Barley (2001) who argue that management and organization studies are comparable with our research domain, we researched these areas, among others, and found some advocates.

## The view from management (and elsewhere) …

In his paper in the Academy of Management Journal, Hambrick (2007: 1346) pulls no punches when arguing that the ‘blanket insistence on theory, or the requirement of an articulation of theory in everything we write, actually retards our ability to achieve our end: understanding.’ He sees the following phrase repeatedly in rejection letters to himself and colleagues: ‘The reviewers all agree that your paper addresses an important topic and is well argued; moreover, they <sup>fi</sup>nd your empirical results convincing and interesting. At the same time, however, the reviewers believe the paper falls short in making a theoretical contribution. Therefore, I’m sorry … . He describes this as ‘Our <sup>fi</sup>eld’s theory fetish.’ We see a similar fetish in information systems, hence our use of the term in this paper’s subtitle. Like editors of leading journals in IS, ‘Where’s the major theoretical contribution’ seems to be the pivotal test above all others, a test, he argues, that is applied in the domain of management with ‘religious fervour.

Hambrick (2007) argues that such a stance prevents the reporting of facts ‘no matter how important or competently generated’ that lack theoretical explanation, even if once published, they might stimulate other related researches, the search for an explanation, and eventually a major contribution, perhaps eventually one that is theoretical (but which could not have come out of the original study alone – and yet would not have happened without that earlier study).

As an example of a paper making an important contribution which is not theoretical, Hambrick cites Thompson et al. (2006) in the Journal of Marketing who look at brand imaging at Starbucks where they ‘document and dissect a fascinating, important phenomenon,’ without any reference to theory or theory building. He also cites Faccio et al. (2006) in the Journal of Finance which discusses government bailouts of companies ‘without any theoretical trappings, relying instead on the prima facie importance of the topic and the strength of their empirical work to hook the reader.’ Hambrick (2007) suggests that the emphasis on theory has cost the management discipline in terms of important facts waiting on theories, and contorted and ponderous prose.

Helfat (2007) argues, in a similar vein, that facts are the most important, suggesting perhaps that ‘data can speak for itself.’ ‘Imagine grounding the study of real organizations only in theory; we ought to be laughed out of the academy [of management] for that. Yet our journals appear to insist on theory testing. In a <sup>fi</sup>eld that seeks to understand the real world, it makes little sense to always put theory before facts. We must understand at least the broad outlines of ‘what a phenomenon consists of before we try to explain ‘why’ it occurs. That is, we need research directed toward uncovering empirical regularities, otherwise known as ‘stylized facts.’ Only then are we in a position to build theory that in turn can serve as the basis for more re<sup>fi</sup>ned tests and extensions’ (p. 185).

In his paper in Strategic Organization on atheoretic research (which we might refer to as ‘theory free’), Miller (2007) draws on science for support to <sup>fi</sup>ght the ‘paradigm prison.’ He refers to Alexander Fleming’s mysterious spore (which turned out to be penicillin) that killed his bacterial cultures. Rather than continuing his original research and research domain, he devoted his time on this spore. Miller argues (p. 177) that ‘the current institutional setting within which administrative studies develop has evolved to de-legitimize Fleming’s type of research’ that led to such an outstanding research contribution. ‘Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, it degrades and discourages research that is nonparadigmatic or atheoretical.’ Miller asks editors to be more tolerant of pre-theoretic research, even research that is ‘brutally empirical’. He also contends that management journals will not publish empirical research that does not develop, extend or test theory. ‘This is unfortunate, as had the natural sciences adopted such an attitude, we’d still be dying of “minor” infections.

From the <sup>fi</sup>eld of education, Thomas and James (2006) discuss Einstein, who sees the essence of science as seeking ‘in whatever manner is suitable, a simpli<sup>fi</sup>ed and lucid image of the world … There is no logical path, but only intuition’ (pp. 772–773). They cite Feyerabend (1993) who argues that the ‘only principle that does not inhibit progress is anything goes’ (p. 14). They also cite Becker (1996: 70) who argues that there are no ‘recipes’ for doing social science research, researchers need to have imagination, ‘smell’ a research problem and <sup>fi</sup>nd a good way to address it. ‘Theory, if Einstein is right, does not give birth to discovery’ (Thomas and James, 2006: 773).

We do not argue for atheoretical or theory free research. To us it suggests an anti-theoretical stance which we do not share. We also totally reject the view that anything goes. We are arguing here for theory light research papers whose outstanding contribution lies in areas other than theory. We are of course also not arguing against the acceptance of papers that make an excellent theoretical contribution.

The paper of Thomas and James (2006) discusses grounded theory (Corbin and Strauss, 1990; Glaser and Strauss, 2009), which may start in theory free or theory light mode. Indeed there has been much discussion in the grounded theory literature about whether we can start from a position which assumes no pre-conceived theoretical concepts (see, e.g., Seidel and Urquhart, 2013; Urquhart and Fernández, 2013) but in making sense of the data, grounded theorists are expected to create theory that emerges from the sense-making process (rather than pre-conceived theoretical hypotheses or theoretical perceptions). However, we are discussing in our paper something different, that is, whether theory needs to be an important and integral part in papers published in the leading journals and whereas the research journey for grounded theorists will lead to theory (which is likely to be integral to the paper), we are discussing research that may lead to other important contributions, though they may also lead to theoretical contributions later.

Miller (2007) also provides examples of pivotal research that is theory light. He refers to Goffman’s ethnographic studies of asylums and presentation of self in everyday life, for example, Goffman, (1968, 2009), which were based on ‘little a priori theory’ (p. 182) – what we would call ‘theory light.’ Although not proof of rigor or excellence, at the time of writing (March 2014) Goffman’s Google Scholar citation count is 144,482!

Miller refers also to some of the very varied works of leading management scholars such as Bill Starbuck, David Hickson, Andrew Pettigrew, Michael Tushman and Henry Mintzberg (along with Don Hambrick), as being pre-theoretic.

We would also add the work of Robert Stake who has been in<sup>fl</sup>uential. Stake (2000: 437–438) distinguishes between three types of case study: intrinsic, instrumental and collective. For his intrinsic type, exploring the features of a case study, its socio-cultural context, its actors, and the mechanisms that lead to the events under a study is a contribution in itself since it adds a real organizational account to the literature. He also seems to be arguing that the ‘data should speak for itself’ and the reader should do the interpretation (and/or be inspired to develop the work further himself) as this is likely to lead to different interpretations. Discussing multiple interpretations is quite appropriate for this type of research. With regard to that, Stake (1995) quotes William Blake (1982):

Both read the Bible day and night.

But thou read black where I read white.

## … and Information systems

It is of course dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to <sup>fi</sup>nd excellent examples of theory light papers from our leading journals precisely because of the impact of the ‘Theory is King’ arguments discussed above. In their paper advocating professional training for our doctoral students, Klein and Rowe (2008) provide support for some of the arguments we have been making. Although the context of their paper is Ph.D. training, as doctoral students are our future, then by implication their arguments relate to our research and the discipline as a whole. They suggest that ‘applicative knowledge’ should be viewed as ‘different from but of equal value to theory’ (p. 675). They quote Markus (1997) who argues that ‘practical research’ should not replace or overshadow research that builds or tests academic theory, but rather to complement theoretical research with ‘rigorous research that describes and evaluates what is going on in practice’ (p. 18). Of course, empirical research and papers can have a strong theoretical component and we do not wish to suggest that doctoral students should not have a thorough grasp of theory (nor our leading journals neglect theory!), but in our view it should not be exclusive.

As exemplar of an IS paper which re<sup>fl</sup>ects what we have been discussing, we refer to Schultze (2000). We look at this in some depth to bring out how a qualitative piece can make important contributions without emphasizing theoretical aspects. Hers is an ethnographic study, but our appeal for theory light papers applies to other qualitative approaches. Reading it for the <sup>fi</sup>rst time when published, we really experienced ‘the shock of the new’ (Hughes, 2000). This was the title of a television series which described the effects of contemporary architecture on people. Interestingly, authors associated with IS research have published a study of the design practices of the contemporary architect, Frank O. Gehry, in Organisation Science (Yoo et al., 2006).

Schultze’s paper was published in a special issue of MIS Quarterly on ‘Intensive research in information systems: Using qualitative, interpretive, and case methods to study information technology.’ The accepting editor was Allen Lee, who is a qualitative researcher in the main and perhaps more open than most to studies such as that of Schultze discussed here. The same can be said of the other special issue editor, Lynne Markus, whom we have already referred to. Clearly they were very in<sup>fl</sup>uential in producing a special issue of six very interesting papers, this paper plus Gopal and Prasad (2000), Klein and Myers (1999), Nelson et al. (2000). Trauth and Jessup (2000), and Walsham and Sahay (1999).

Schultze (2000) looks at three groups of knowledge workers – computer system administrators, competitive intelligence analysts, and librarians. But unconventionally she not only reports on these subjects, she recognizes herself as a knowledge worker also and therefore includes herself in the study. ‘This is accomplished through a representational genre known as confessional (Van Maanen, 1995) or vulnerable writing (Behar, 1996). Confessional writing requires the ethnographer to give a self-revealing and self-re<sup>fl</sup>exive account of the research process. The ethnographer designs such an autobiographical exposition to draw readers into the text so that the assumptions and practices of the ‘foreign culture’ serve as a mirror in which the reader's own assumptions and practices are re<sup>fl</sup>ected. The confessional ethnographer intends this mirroring to challenge readers to examine their own informing practices’ (p. 4).

This self-re<sup>fl</sup>exive and self-revealing account of the research process ‘presents the ethnographer's role as a research instrument and exposes the ethnographer rendering his/her actions, failings, motivations, and assumptions open to public scrutiny and critique. By revealing themselves in their confessional writings, ethnographers put themselves on a par with their “subjects” who typically feel exposed and criticized by ethnographic texts’ (p. 8).

The style of this self-re<sup>fl</sup>exive account is somewhat different than conventional writing as we see from the following examples: (a) ‘Negotiating access was easier than I had anticipated. Within a week of contacting Jerry Hunt’ (p. 9) rather than a more conventional ‘access was granted within a week’; (b) ‘I believe that the promise of visibility, legitimacy and prestige that the research activities of a Ph.D. student lent to [the project] made gaining access fairly easy’ (p. 9) (probably not included in a conventional account); (c) ‘a typical week started early on a Monday … (a three hour car ride) … I stayed at a motel’ (p. 10) (not included in a conventional account); (d) ‘Being a woman I established an easier rapport and a closer relationship with the women in the <sup>fi</sup>eld’ (p. 10) (not included in a conventional account unless related perhaps to gender studies); (e) ‘My decision to leave the <sup>fi</sup>eld after eight months was in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by … my desire to complete my dissertation by the following summer, and my need to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll my responsibilities as a teaching assistant’ (p. 12) rather than ‘the case study took place over an eight month period.’ The paper abounds with such material that would not normally be apparent in a conventional research paper.

What follows this section in the paper is a section on the results of her work about the informing practices of computer system administrators, competitive intelligence analysts, and librarians which she classi<sup>fi</sup>es as ‘ex-pressing,’ ‘monitoring’ and ‘translating’ and this provides an excellent contribution to the <sup>fi</sup>eld. She provides lots of evidence coming from her <sup>fi</sup>eld notes for these assertions, which also express personal re<sup>fl</sup>ections such as (f) ‘writing notes until 12.15 tonight … and I did not get much sleep last night because I wrote the dissertation statement’ (p. 16).

At this point you might be asking ‘So what?’ and/or ‘Why all that unnecessary material?’ But this material helps us to understand the research and assess the contribution. To give a few potential scenarios, (a) and (b) suggest that she was well received in the <sup>fi</sup>rm and that she was granted good access to observe and interview, thus the data is likely to be reasonably accurate and complete; (c) and (f) might suggest why her observations on Monday mornings seem less revealing or an interview carried out then oddly short and sharp; (d) might explain differences in some interviews compared with others (because of the gender of those interviewed); and (e) why a follow-up study was not undertaken that may have provided added-value to the results (but could be taken up by another researcher or indeed Schultze herself later).

As Schultze (2000: 29–31) argues, such re<sup>fl</sup>ections provide authenticity, plausibility and criticality (Golden-Biddle and Locke, 1993) to make her contributions about knowledge work and knowledge workers believable. We think she is fully justi<sup>fi</sup>ed in claiming that her study helps us understand better the practices of knowledge work; that she is fully justi<sup>fi</sup>ed in suggesting that she is making contributions to practice; and through its introduction of confessional writing in her ethnographic study, making an equally justi<sup>fi</sup>ed claim of providing methodological contributions to the <sup>fi</sup>eld of IS.

As for its theoretical content, there are mentions of theory in the work, but it certainly would not satisfy those arguing that ‘Theory is King.’ Nonetheless, we wish to quote in full a (confessional) paragraph from Schultze (2000: 26):

However, my <sup>fi</sup>rst attempts to compose coherent narratives of the three groups’ informing practices brought to light that the combination of the three categories of worker identity, information, and technology and a generic structurational framework was not theoretically sound enough to develop consistent interpretations across the three groups. I felt that I needed an established theory that would help me look at my data deductively, i.e., from a more distant, top-down and objective vantage point. Such a theory, I argued, would give my analysis more credibility because it represented an authority that lay beyond the <sup>fi</sup>eld and me. I also anticipated that it would be easier to convince my dissertation committee of the robustness of my work if I applied a ‘real’ theory rather than a ‘home-spun’ one.

Although the paper is theory light, therefore (indeed there is very little mention of theory in the paper itself), it would seem that she would have to apply a ‘real theory’ to her data in order to convince her dissertation committee. In our view this is justi<sup>fi</sup>ed as doctoral students must demonstrate knowledge and use of theory as part of their quali<sup>fi</sup>cation (but this is a different issue to a paper that makes its contribution elsewhere). In fact she used the theory of practice (Bourdieu, 1977) for this purpose. However, as far as the paper is concerned, there is no evidence of reducing the emphasis on the new and exciting research contribution to emphasize theoretical aspects of the research (our sixth concern expressed above).

## What we would expect from theory light papers

As we stated earlier, we do not agree with Feyerabend (1993), above, that anything goes. There needs to be some support to help researchers write excellent theory light papers and editors and referees to evaluate such papers. Help already exists to support us write and evaluate qualitative research. These include Klein and Myers (1999) on interpretive <sup>fi</sup>eld studies, Myers and Klein (2011) on critical research, Venkatesh et al. (2013) on mixed method research (combining qualitative and quantitative research in one study), and Wynn and Williams (2012) on critical realist case study research. We recommend that authors of theory light papers refer also to the above contributions as well as what follows, although clearly taking a different view to, for example, Klein and Myers’ (1999) stress on theory in two of their seven principles (the principle of abstraction and generalization and the principle of dialogical reasoning).

Rather than add to these principles and guidelines, we propose that authors and reviewers ask themselves a set of questions which may well also apply to more conventional qualitative papers but are likely to be essential to those that are theory light. The emphasis which each places in a paper will depend on the focus of the paper as in our view researchers and referees need to exercise judgment with theory light papers. We think that this more re<sup>fl</sup>ects the spirit of our paper. We look at the roles of the author, reviewer, reader and editor in our discussion section that follows.

1. Is it interesting? This is the <sup>fi</sup>rst of three questions inspired from Miller (2007: 183–184). He asks: Does the paper respond to a question or detects a pattern that is of major concern to other researchers and practitioners? Whetten (1989: 494), who writes of theoretical contributions, asks the equivalent question, ‘So what?,’ for example, might the contribution change practice? He also asks the question ‘Who cares?’ (p. 495), ‘what percentage of academic readers are interested in the topic?’ The eight journals in the senior scholars’ basket of leading research journals (AIS, 2014) represent journals aimed at the IS research community as a whole and therefore papers published in them should appeal to a reasonable proportion of IS scholars (and perhaps students and practitioners).

2. Is it original? Is the research truly original, that is, the originality does not describe the smallest nuance of difference (or indeed con<sup>fi</sup>rmation) with what has been published before but something more fundamental. Whetten (1989) asks the equivalent question, ‘What’s new?’ (p. 94), that is, does the paper make a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant value-added contribution to current thinking?

3. Is it rigorous? Can the <sup>fi</sup>ndings be replicated by third parties or are they so <sup>fi</sup>ne-grained as to impress most sceptics? Some of the other guidelines following, for example, authenticity and plausibility, enforce this guideline for rigor. The question of rigor in IS research more generally is discussed fully in Applegate (1999) and in the papers that follow, and most recently in Straub and Ang (2011).

4. Is it authentic? This is one of three questions asked by Golden-Biddle and Locke (1993) including plausibility and criticality that follow. Is there suf<sup>fi</sup>cient <sup>fi</sup>eld evidence collected, for example, time spent in the <sup>fi</sup>eld, comprehensive coverage, suf<sup>fi</sup>cient detail, and representativeness of the data, to convince readers of full immersion in the <sup>fi</sup>eld and that any assumptions made are reasonable? This will make up the supporting evidence that will make the paper plausible.

5. Is it plausible? When writing-up, is the text backed up by relevant references to related work (for example, other case studies) and other experiences in organizational life, so that the assertions suggest completeness and thoroughness and are both well founded and believable also by practitioners?

6. Does it show criticality? Are there challenges to readers to pause and think about what is being said, perhaps asking for responses to questions or requiring them to think differently? We have referred above to ‘data speaking for itself,’ and to some extent we mean here that readers need to be challenged to interpret data for themselves, not necessarily have the interpretation almost forced on them through the writer’s own theoretical perspective. But data does not speak for itself. Data need to be interpreted by researchers writing the paper and scholars reading the paper. This has parallels with one of Myers and Klein’s (2011) principles for critical research: ‘the principal of revealing and challenging prevailing beliefs and social practices’ (pp. 26–27) and that of Klein and Myers (1999)’s principles of ‘multiple interpretations (for example evidence of case study participants disagreeing on an issue) and ‘suspicion,’ for example being sensitive to biases and distortions in the interview data (pp. 77–78).

7. Is there access to the original data? Finlay (2002) argues that researchers need to make available the tools to criticize our accounts. Having the data set available will facilitate interested researchers to question the author’s arguments re<sup>fl</sup>ecting on their own cognitive schemes, worldview in<sup>fl</sup>uences and previous research experience. This is a sensitive area and would need company permission, agreement to retain con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality and non-proliferation of the data set. Because of these issues, it cannot be insisted upon, but it is desirable so that readers can see the whole picture before making their own interpretations. Assuming there is agreement by all parties, such provision might include <sup>fi</sup>eld notes, audio and video tapes of interviews, work being observed and so on.

8. Is the approach appropriate? Is theory light an appropriate way to research and discuss the phenomena investigated? For example, if the data discussed particularly lends itself to explanation through theory, then the explanation via theory would seem appropriate. We are not arguing exclusivity, but inclusivity! But if there is a major contribution, not based on theoretical aspects, then a theory light approach might well be appropriate.

9. Is it done well? Whetten (1989) suggests that we should ask (p. 494) ‘Is the paper well written? Does it <sup>fl</sup>ow logically? Are the central ideas easily accessed? Is it enjoyable to read? Is the paper long enough to cover the subject but short enough to be interesting? Does the paper’s appearance re<sup>fl</sup>ect high professional standards?’.

10. Is it timely? Similarly, Whetten (1989) asks the question ‘Why now?’ that is, ‘Is this topic of contemporary interest to scholars in this area? Will it likely advance current discussions, stimulate new discussions, or revitalize old discussions?’ (p. 494).

## Discussion

We hope it is obvious that a call for theory light papers is NOT a call for an easier researching and writing process, a less rigorous refereeing process, a lowering of standards in our leading journals and an easier read. On the contrary, it imposes signi<sup>fi</sup>cant challenges for the author, referees, editor and reader. We have introduced the roles of the author and referees in our previous section by suggesting 10 questions which authors and referees need to consider when they perform their role. Concentrating on contributions other than theory implies that these contributions need to have been done particularly well. It can be achieved, as our exemplar (Schultze, 2000) illustrates, but the paucity of excellent examples in IS is only partly due to the policies of our journal editors. It also evidences the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty of researching and writing in this vein. Referees need to ensure that these high standards are achieved before suggesting acceptance. But the role of the reader is also different and particularly challenging, and we look at this next.

In our discussion of six concerns regarding the emphasis on theory in IS research, we raised the possibilities that, for example, authors might be tempted to <sup>fi</sup>t the data to an ‘ideal type’ to tell the story coherently and succinctly and also seeing only what the theory suggests which might sti<sup>fl</sup>e potential new issues and avenues for the reader to think about and perhaps explore himself. Theory light papers require a more demanding role for the reader in his interpretation process.

But why be patronizing to our readers? In his text ‘The role of the reader,’ Umberto Eco (1984) explores how the individual performer has to choose how to interpret some pieces of music as there is such ambiguity in the scores. He later draws parallels to interpreting text. The so-called French ‘new novels’ (many were written 40 years ago) of people like Duras, Robbe-Grillet, and Sarrault were deliberately ambiguous and their books exciting to the reader who has to interpret what he reads. The reader is asked to play the role of detective (whether they are detective novels or otherwise) to get meanings from the books. Just as the author of an IS paper might offer two interpretations of the data for readers to consider, John Fowles offers two endings in his novel The French lieutenant’s woman. Going further back to Proust’s master work A la recherche du temps perdu, the numbers of possible interpretations seem to be in<sup>fi</sup>nite, and that is one of its huge attractions.

We are not advocating the arti<sup>fi</sup>cial creation of ambiguity in our research writings, of course, but accepting ambiguity where it exists. The process of reading and interpreting requires the active rather than passive participation of the reader. He has to work harder and contribute more himself, but the rewards for doing so are that much greater. This might also support the longevity of our research as it enables new interpretations of our work as we discover new things over time, something akin to the police using new techniques to investigate ‘cold cases.’ Eventually, researchers might convert this pre-theoretic research into a major theoretical as well as empirical contribution.

The last of the roles to discuss in this internal process is that of the journal editor and board of editors of our leading journals. In this regard, we can do no better than cite Hambrick (2007: 1350) who argues that ‘leading journals [in information systems] should broaden their scope to include papers that do not directly contribute to theory but are nonetheless of great potential consequence. These might be papers that identify compelling empirical patterns that cry out for future research and theorizing. They might be rich qualitative descriptions of important but unexplored phenomena that, once described, could stimulate the development of theory and/or other insights. Or they might be of other types altogether … the requirement for a “contribution to theory” would be replaced with the following test:

Does the paper have a high likelihood of stimulating future research that will substantially alter [information systems] theory and/or practice?

(p. 1350)

This message that such important papers are actively encouraged needs to be broadcast loud and clear to authors, reviewers and the board of editors as a whole, otherwise we will not make as much progress in the discipline as that which is desirable. We think a good way to start is to encourage our leading journals to have special issues such as the one edited by Lynne Markus and Allen Lee in 2000 discussed above. Perhaps the special issue of the European Journal of

Information Systems (Avital et al., 2012) calling for alternative genres in IS research will also encourage such papers.

Where we disagree with Hambrick (2007) is his call for a new journal devoted to this kind of research (p. 1350) because we imagine such a journal would be regarded as ‘second tier’ in information systems. We do not wish to stimulate ‘second tier’ papers. We wish to stimulate different types of ‘<sup>fi</sup>rst tier’ papers in ‘<sup>fi</sup>rst tier’ journals. In fact his call has led to a new management journal which speci<sup>fi</sup>- cally encourages such papers called Academy of Management Discoveries (AOM, living) edited by Andrew Van de Ven. However, we recommend enthusiastically a series of videos from the AMD editorial team starting with AMD (2014) which discusses some of the issues introduced in this paper.

For such papers to be accepted in our leading research journals, however, does require a change of climate. As we suggest above, the emphasis on theory has gone too far. Just like the discussion above on Lynne Markus’ doubts about whether her paper (Markus, 1983) would have been accepted in the climate of 2000, we doubt whether Ulrike Schultze’s (2000) paper would be accepted by the same journal in today’s climate of 2014. This would be regrettable.

But there are others who are also signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in this hopedfor climate change. If the <sup>fi</sup>rst author may again indulge himself as this is a debates and perspectives piece, he can only too vividly remember being laughed at by some of the audience when discussing action research at a panel at ICIS New York in 1991. I was also told around a couple of years later at two interviews for a full professorship which I was applying for (and being rejected) that action research was not an acceptable research method for information systems professors. Thus there are others to convince, in particular deans, appointments, tenure and promotion committees, that excellent papers are excellent papers, whether their contribution lies in theory or elsewhere. Acceptance in our leading journals, though perhaps not a specialist journal for theory light research, would have a major impact in convincing these colleagues.
