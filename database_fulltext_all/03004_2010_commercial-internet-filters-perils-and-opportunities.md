## a b s t r a c t

Organizations are becoming increasingly aware of Internet abuse in the workplace. Such abuse results in loss of workers' productivity, network congestion, security risks, and legal liabilities. To address this problem, organizations have started to adopt Internet usage policies, management training, and <sup>fi</sup>ltering software. Several commercial Internet <sup>fi</sup>lters are experiencing an increasing number of organizational adoptions. These products mainly rely on black lists, white lists, and keyword/pro<sup>fi</sup>le matching to <sup>fi</sup>lter out undesired web pages. In this paper, we describe three top-ranked commercial Internet <sup>fi</sup>lters – CYBERSitter, Net Nanny, and CyberPatrol – and evaluate their performance in the context of an Internet abuse problem. We then propose a text mining approach to address the problem and evaluate its performance using six different classi<sup>fi</sup>cation algorithms: naïve Bayes, multinominal naïve Bayes, support vector machine, decision tree, k-nearest neighbor, and neural network. The evaluation results point to the perils of using commercial Internet <sup>fi</sup>lters on one hand, and to the prospects of using text mining on the other. The proposed text mining approach outperforms the commercial <sup>fi</sup>lters. We discuss the possible reasons for the relatively poor performance of the <sup>fi</sup>lters and the steps that could be taken to improve their performance.

## 1. Introduction

While the use of Internet technology is becoming widespread, both at home and at work, it is also causing severe problems. The Internet addiction problem is making some people's personal lives unmanageable [59]. Organizations are getting increasingly worried about threats to the security of their corporate data and invasion of privacy. In the workplace, the Internet abuse problem results in waste of employees' time [31], loss of workers' productivity [44], network congestion, and other problems.

To address these problems, more and more companies are adopting Internet usage policies [45], management training [60], and software packages to monitor and block employees' abuse usage [51]. Internet usage policies and management training are used for Internet abuse prevention by providing general guidelines. These two approaches allow only loose controls on Internet usage. Using software packages to automatically monitor, <sup>fi</sup>lter, and block employees' Internet abuse behavior is another popular approach for Internet abuse prevention and detection. Several commercial Internet <sup>fi</sup>ltering software products are currently available in the market. Most of these software packages rely on white lists or black lists to allow or block

Internet access. Regular updates of the white lists and black lists are needed as new websites and web pages are being constantly developed and generated. For organizations, maintaining the lists is extremely expensive. Some Internet <sup>fi</sup>ltering software providers, such as CYBERSitter (http://www.cybersitter.com/), periodically provide updates of <sup>fi</sup>ltering lists by categories, such as gaming, violence, and so on. However, the lists may not have adequate coverage for newly developed websites and web pages.

In this study, we empirically evaluate the performance of three top-ranked <sup>fi</sup>lters by focusing on the problem of Internet abuse, which arises when employees use the Internet for non-work-related purposes. Such abuse includes online chatting, gaming, investing and shopping, illegal downloading, pornography and cybersex, and online crimes [19]. We also propose a text mining approach to address this organizational problem and evaluate the approach using six different text mining techniques.

The text mining approach we present focuses on the contents of web pages, rather than on creating and maintaining lists, as contentbased detection provides easier maintenance. Classi<sup>fi</sup>ers are trained to classify web pages into two categories: abuse and non-abuse. We examine whether the proposed classi<sup>fi</sup>cation models can be used to effectively detect web-based Internet abuse in the workplace, compared to three leading commercial Internet <sup>fi</sup>ltering packages. For evaluating the effectiveness of the <sup>fi</sup>lters and classi<sup>fi</sup>cation models, we conduct experiments in the domain of the workplace of software programmers. The experimental results expose the shortcomings of <sup>fi</sup>lters vis-à-vis the text mining classi<sup>fi</sup>ers, and provide pointers as to what could be done to boost or substitute the <sup>fi</sup>ltering methods currently employed by commercial <sup>fi</sup>lters.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. First, we review the related literature on Internet abuse and commercial Internet <sup>fi</sup>ltering software packages. Next, we present the proposed text mining approach and related techniques. We then describe the details of the empirical evaluation and discuss the results. Finally, we conclude the paper and outline potential future research directions.

## 2. Literature review

In this section, we <sup>fi</sup>rst present a brief review of the Internet abuse problem. We next review the state-of-the-art in commercial Internet <sup>fi</sup>lters, which are commonly used for Internet abuse prevention and detection in the workplace.

## 2.1. Internet abuse in the workplace

Due to the rapid growth in use of the Internet at work, there has been a growing interest among researchers in studying the problem of Internet abuse in the workplace. Several names have been ascribed to this problem, including Internet abuse in the workplace [17,21,29,44,45,56,60], Internet misuse in the workplace [57], problematic Internet use in the workplace [13], personal web usage in the workplace [2,30], non-work related computing [27,55], and cyberloafing [28]. In this paper, Internet abuse in the workplace refers to employees' non-work-related Internet usage.

Chou et al. [11] provided a summary of recent studies on Internet abuse in the workplace. Prior studies on Internet abuse in the workplace can be categorized into the following research areas: prevention of abuse behavior, behavior evaluation and identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of factors toward the behavior, and issues on monitoring and blocking employees' Internet usage. To prevent such behavior, Grif<sup>fi</sup>ths [21] recommended practical steps to minimize problems that may occur by preventive activities, such as Internet monitoring and bookmark checking. Young and Case [60] found Internet use policies to be the most widely used strategy in some surveyed companies. Arnesen and Weis [5] suggested guidelines for developing an effective policy. In the policies, general e-mail abuse and unauthorized usage and access are among the more common issues being addressed [45]. Mahatanankoon et al. [30] developed a measurement model evaluating personal web usage in the workplace, which can be used before hiring employees.

Several factors leading to abuse behavior have been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by prior studies, including diminished impulse control, loneliness/ depression, social comfort, distraction [13], affect, social factors, habit [56], Internet addiction [9], perceived IS accessibility [27], employees' personality [57], addiction, self-justi<sup>fi</sup>cation, job satisfaction, peer culture, and supervisor culture [17]. Panko and Beh [33] raised legal issues on monitoring employees' Internet abuse behavior. Table 1 summarizes the recent studies on Internet abuse into these three categories.

## 2.2. Commercial Internet filters

Internet <sup>fi</sup>ltering software has been widely adopted by parents, schools, libraries, and businesses to protect users' Internet access on either private or public computers. However, only a few studies have empirically examined the performance of commercial Internet <sup>fi</sup>ltering software packages. Hunter [24] evaluated four popular commercial products (CYBERsitter, CyberPatrol, Net Nanny, and Surf Watch) in the context of objectionable or non-objectionable contents on language, nudity, sex, and violence. Overinclusive blocking and underinclusive blocking error rates were reported. Overinclusive blocking refers to the incorrect blocking of allowed contents, while underinclusive blocking refers to the incorrect passing of undesirable contents. Overinclusive blocking error rate and underinclusive blocking error rate are also referred to as OK-sites over-block error rate and Bad-sites under-block error rate [37] (explained in more detail later). Hunter's evaluation showed that overinclusive blocking and underinclusive blocking error rates were 21% and 25%, respectively, based on the combined decision of four <sup>fi</sup>ltering programs. Individually, overinclusive blocking error rates of CYBERsitter, Cyber-Patrol, Net Nanny, and Surf Watch were 14.6%, 9.2%, 7.3%, and 3%, respectively, and the corresponding error rates for underinclusive blocking were 30.6%, 40.4%, 55.6%, and 83.3%, respectively. Overall, underinclusive blocking error rate was higher than overinclusive blocking error rate for all four programs individually.

Such poor performance could be attributed to the techniques used by the products. Green<sup>fi</sup>eld et al. [20] classi<sup>fi</sup>ed the techniques used by 24 evaluated <sup>fi</sup>ltering programs into source-based <sup>fi</sup>ltering and content-based <sup>fi</sup>ltering. Source-based <sup>fi</sup>ltering relies on pre-de<sup>fi</sup>ned white lists and black lists, which include URLs of allowed and undesirable websites, respectively. A major weakness of source-based <sup>fi</sup>ltering is the limited coverage of the lists due to the frequent updates of websites. Content-based <sup>fi</sup>ltering determines whether a web page is undesirable by evaluating the contents of the page. However, the content-based <sup>fi</sup>ltering techniques used in most commercial products simply compare the loaded contents with pre-de<sup>fi</sup>ned keywords, phrases, and pro<sup>fi</sup>les. This approach is problematic since blocked keywords are sometimes legitimate in certain documents. For instance, some blocked sexual keywords might appear in health information pages, which are considered legitimate for health knowledge acquisition. Most current state-of-the-art Internet <sup>fi</sup>ltering packages utilize a mix of source-based <sup>fi</sup>ltering and content-based <sup>fi</sup>ltering to provide higher <sup>fl</sup>exibility and ensure better performance. The technologies used by three top-ranked Internet packages examined in this paper are discussed in the next section.

## 2.3. CYBERSitter, Net Nanny, and CyberPatrol

CYBERSitter (http://www.cybersitter.com/) is a software package provided by Solid Oak Software. It has received the prestigious PC Magazine Editor's Choice Award <sup>fi</sup>ve times and the 2008 TopTenRE-VIEWS Silver Award on Internet <sup>fi</sup>lters (http://internet-<sup>fi</sup>lter-review. toptenreviews.com/). It relies on <sup>fi</sup>ltering lists with coverage of over 30 categories. Customized <sup>fi</sup>ltering settings can be set by checking or un-checking those <sup>fi</sup>ltering options. The <sup>fi</sup>ltering lists are maintained by the software company and can be updated automatically to a user's PC when the setting is enabled. The lists include undesired URLs, keywords, and phrases. Although users cannot change the contents in those pre-de<sup>fi</sup>ned <sup>fi</sup>ltering lists, they can con<sup>fi</sup>gure their own black lists and white lists. They are able to enter bad sites, words, and phrases into black lists, and allowable sites into white lists. CYBERSitter is also able to record websites visited and violations at the page level. Users can examine the logs to further con<sup>fi</sup>gure <sup>fi</sup>ltering options and customize their own black lists and white lists.

NetNanny (http://www.netnanny.com/) is powered by Content-Watch, Inc. It was awarded the PC Magazine Editor's Choice Award for best parental controls in 2008 and received the 4-year TopTenRE-VIEWS Gold Award for Internet <sup>fi</sup>lters. The Walt Disney Company uses it in their Disney Dream Desk PC specially designed for children of all ages. It provides 41 <sup>fi</sup>ltering categories for con<sup>fi</sup>guration. NetNanny only maintains lists of undesired URLs in categories and does not support content-based <sup>fi</sup>ltering. It does not allow users to create black lists, white lists, allowed keywords/phrases, and undesired keywords/ phrases. To track the <sup>fi</sup>ltering activities, it provides a pop-up dialog for each single page loaded, as well as the URLs for the <sup>fi</sup>ltered sites in the log.

CyberPatrol (http://www.cyberpatrol.com/) is another Internet <sup>fi</sup>ltering product from CyberPatrol, LLC. It was awarded the 2008 TopTenREVIEWS Bronze Award on Internet <sup>fi</sup>lters. It allows administrators to create user pro<sup>fi</sup>les to meet the needs of different users. Each user pro<sup>fi</sup>le includes its settings for <sup>fi</sup>ltering categories, userde<sup>fi</sup>ned allowed/denied URLs, and keyword lists. It utilizes layered <sup>fi</sup>ltering technologies to provide different levels of <sup>fi</sup>ltering strength. The technologies consist of CyberLIST, CyberPATTERNS, Web Page Analysis, and Web Link Analysis. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, CyberLIST is a database of URLs belonging to 13 categories. CyberPATTERNS is based on contextual keyword patterns. Web Page Analysis utilizes neural network technology to review the contents of those sites that are not categorized within CyberLIST. Web Link Analysis stops inappropriate images from reaching the user's screen, because sometimes even an innocent search could return undesirable content. In addition, there are <sup>fi</sup>ve levels of <sup>fi</sup>ltering strength, including “allow all”, “low”, “medium”, “high”, and “maximum.” The low-level setting for a speci<sup>fi</sup>c category enables CyberLIST technology. The medium-level setting enables CyberLIST and CyberPATTERNS. The high-level setting makes use of CyberLIST, CyberPATTERNS, and Web Page Analysis technologies. The maximum-level utilizes all four technologies. In addition, CyberPatrol periodically updates CyberLIST and CyberPAT-

TERNS. Similar to CYBERSitter, it provides page level logging for <sup>fi</sup>ltering activities.

Among the three <sup>fi</sup>ltering packages, only CyberPatrol claims to utilize advanced content-based technology, viz., Web Page Analysis. More sophisticated techniques that take into account multiple informative attributes about a page collectively to make better decisions still warrant further investigation.

## 3. A text mining approach to Internet abuse detection

Internet abuse is a major problem af<sup>fl</sup>icting organizations nowadays. To address the shortcomings of the existing techniques used in commercial Internet <sup>fi</sup>ltering software packages, we design web page classi<sup>fi</sup>ers, using text mining, for Internet abuse detection in the workplace. In this paper, we focus on web-based Internet abuse detection. Treating non-work-related web pages as abuse cases and work-related web pages as non-abuse cases, we apply text classi<sup>fi</sup>cation techniques to train prediction models (classi<sup>fi</sup>ers) for categorizing web pages. We <sup>fi</sup>rst review related text classi<sup>fi</sup>cation techniques.

## 3.1. Text classification

The objective of classi<sup>fi</sup>cation is to predict the class (a categorical dependent variable) of a problem case based on a set of attributes (independent variables) describing the case. It is a supervised learning process since pre-de<sup>fi</sup>ned categories are given. A prediction model, called a classifier, is automatically learned from a set of training cases with known class values. The classi<sup>fi</sup>er can then be applied to predict the class values of other cases. Some examples of classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problems include web query classi<sup>fi</sup>cation [7], pro<sup>fi</sup>ling Web usage in the workplace [1], generating document taxonomies [49], assessing risks of prostate cancer patients [12], forecasting <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance [52], and credit evaluation [47].

Text classi<sup>fi</sup>cation (also referred to as text categorization) utilizes machine learning techniques to classify textual documents into one of several pre-de<sup>fi</sup>ned categories [42]. Many different types of machine learning algorithms, such as naïve Bayes, multinomial naïve Bayes, support vector machine, k-nearest neighbor, decision tree, and neural network, have been successfully utilized for different text classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problems [25,42]. Examples of problems include junk e-mail <sup>fi</sup>ltering [3,36,38–41,53], webpage classi<sup>fi</sup>cation [10,26,50], web page <sup>fi</sup>ltering [8,48], anticipatory event detection [22], online deception detection [35,63], web database classi<sup>fi</sup>cation [18], and information <sup>fi</sup>ltering based on content and user's speci<sup>fi</sup>c interests [32]. Although Internet abuse detection is a good candidate for text classi<sup>fi</sup>cation, it has not been examined as such in previous research.

Before learning methods can be applied, several data preprocessing steps are necessary. Two critical preprocessing steps are indexing and dimensionality reduction [42], which are discussed below.

## 3.2. Indexing

The indexing process is used to represent textual documents with a set of attributes. Those attributes are informative and representative of the document indexed. The contents of textual documents are strings of characters, which are not suitable for machine learning algorithms [42]. Prior research in information retrieval suggests that word stems work well as representation units and the sequence of these units in a document is of little importance [25]. Also, using pairs of words has been found to result in poorer performance than using single words [4]. Thus, single words or word stems have been frequently used for indexing in previous studies [42].

Using word stems as representation units, a document can be described by a vector of term weights $< w _ { 1 } , w _ { 2 } , . . . , w _ { | T | } > ,$ where T is a set of selected terms and $w _ { i }$ is the weight of the i-th term. For example, a <sup>fi</sup>nancial document can be represented by a vector of term weights bw<sub>investment</sub>, w<sub>loan</sub>, …, w<sub>banking</sub>N, where investment, loan, and banking are possible word stems (terms) on that page. There are various indexing schemes. Different indexing schemes might be better in different contexts [15]. As our focus in the paper is not on indexing, we limit our consideration to TF (term frequency) and TF/ IDF (term frequency/inverse document frequency), which have been widely used and found to be effective for text classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problems [6,42].

## 3.3. Dimensionality reduction

Text classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problems are typically characterized by large numbers of attributes. Large attribute dimensionality incurs high computational cost and, more seriously, causes overfitting on many classi<sup>fi</sup>cation methods. Over<sup>fi</sup>tting refers to a situation where a classi<sup>fi</sup>er <sup>fi</sup>ts the training dataset well but does not perform well on cases outside the training dataset. Dimensionality reduction can help reduce computational costs and alleviate possible over<sup>fi</sup>tting [42].

In text classi<sup>fi</sup>cation, there are two main approaches for dimensionality reduction. First, the number of attributes can be reduced by removing stop-words and markup tags and stripping suf<sup>fi</sup>xes [42]. Stop-words are frequent terms providing limited meanings. Examples of stop-words are articles (“an”, “a”, and “the”), prepositions (“in” and “on”), and conjunctions (“and”, “but”, “although”, and “because”). Markup tags in HTML provide layout capability of web pages but are not related to the main contents of the pages. Words with a common stem usually have similar meanings and can be merged into a single term by a suf<sup>fi</sup>x stripping process. For example, “invent”, “invented”, “inventing”, “inventive”, “invention”, and “inventions” can be combined into the same term “invent” by removing the suf<sup>fi</sup>xes.

Another approach is attribute selection, which selects a subset of the top-ranked attributes. Information–theoretic measures, such as information gain, Chi-squares, and gain ratio, are commonly used in text classi<sup>fi</sup>cation [42,43]. These measures are designed to measure the dependency between the class and an attribute in some sense. Yang and Pedersen [58] reported that information gain and Chisquares are more effective than other functions such as mutual information, document frequency, and term strength. Debole and Sebastiani [14] reported that gain ratio and Chi-squares outperformed information gain.

## 4. Empirical evaluation

We conducted an empirical evaluation, comparing six text classi<sup>fi</sup>- cation techniques with three leading commercial Internet <sup>fi</sup>ltering products: CYBERSitter, CyberPatrol, and NetNanny. In this section, we describe the dataset we collected for our experiments and the details of our approach.

## 4.1. Data collection

Internet abuse detection is a domain-speci<sup>fi</sup>c task; different roles in the workplace have different Internet abuse pro<sup>fi</sup>les. In this paper, the domain of our investigation is the workplace of programmers in the IT industry. To identify the possible sources of web pages, we consulted with four domain experts, including three experienced programmers and a professor teaching programming languages. Based on their expertise, we treated web pages containing personal investment, business news, general news, entertainment news, and sports news – which are not related to programmers' work – as abuse cases. Non-abuse cases included web pages containing technology news and online resources related to programming (e.g., PHP, ASP. NET, Visual C++, Visual Basic, Visual C#, Visual Studio, and web service), which are crucial to programmers.

We collected 10,000 web pages during a half-month period, with a balanced sample of 5000 abuse and 5000 non-abuse cases. Abuse cases were collected from 16 popular news websites and an auction site. Non-abuse cases were collected from of<sup>fi</sup>cial websites of the above-mentioned programming languages. We checked the uniqueness of all collected <sup>fi</sup>les by applying pairwise byte-by-byte comparisons and <sup>fi</sup>lename comparisons.

We randomly separated the full dataset into a training dataset (with 7000 instances) and a test dataset (with 3000 instances). We kept the proportion of each category in each dataset the same. For example, there was the same proportion (10%) of sports web pages in the training dataset and the test dataset.

## 4.2. Text mining

Internet abuse detection can be formulated as a binary text classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problem. Web pages can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into two categories, abuse or non-abuse, depending on the target employees line of work (e.g. programmer in the IT industry in this paper).

The four domain experts helped us label the collected pages as abuse or non-abuse cases. As discussed in the literature review, TF and TF/IDF have been widely used and found to be effective in text classi<sup>fi</sup>cation. Also, the choice between TF and TF/IDF was found to have no effect on the performance of text classi<sup>fi</sup>cation [11]. Thus, we selected TF as the term weighting scheme for indexing web pages and developed a document indexing program based on the WekaIndex tool (http:// www.ainetsolutions.com/eng/soluciones/aplicaciones/ir.html).

We performed several dimensionality reduction steps, including removing stop-words and HTML tags, Porter's [34] suf<sup>fi</sup>x stripping, and attribute selection by three popular information–theoretic functions – gain ratio, information gain, and chi-squares – which measure the dependency between an attribute and the class [54]. We evaluated six classi<sup>fi</sup>cation algorithms provided by the Weka data mining package: naïve Bayes, multinominal naïve Bayes, support vector machine, C4.5 decision tree (called J4.8 in Weka), k-nearest neighbor, and backpropagation neural network (see [54] for a description of the algorithms). We used one hidden layer with two nodes and 50 training epochs for backpropagation neural network and kept the default settings for the other <sup>fi</sup>ve algorithms.

All three packages have different coverage of pre-de<sup>fi</sup>ned <sup>fi</sup>ltering categories. The experts independently determined which <sup>fi</sup>ltering options should be blocked or kept open for programmers at work. ## 4.6. Pilot study for dimensionality reduction

After some preliminary steps on dimensionality reduction – such as stop-word and HTML tag removal, and suf<sup>fi</sup>x stripping – there were still over 40,000 unique attributes (word stems) in the full dataset. We applied attribute selection with the three information–theoretic measures (gain ratio, information gain, and Chi-squares) to further reduce the number of attributes. We ranked the attributes by each measure separately and selected a number of top attributes. Some examples of the top attributes were “msdn”, “knowledge”, “download”, “health”, “movi”, “librari”, and “develop” (with the representation after applying suf<sup>fi</sup>x stripping discussed earlier).

These three information–theoretic measures provided us with three sets of ranked attributes. We looked for the most appropriate size of top-ranked attributes. For each set of ranked attributes, we evaluated several different numbers of top-ranked attributes, from 80 to 200 with increments of 20, using the six classi<sup>fi</sup>cation algorithms. In these pilot tests, we found very similar patterns for both training and test datasets, using gain ratio, information gain, and Chi-squares. In Fig. 3, we present the performance plots using only gain ratio.

Based on the pilot results (see Fig. 3), around 140 attributes (with 50 training examples per attribute) appeared to be appropriate for naïve Bayes, multinominal naïve Bayes, support vector machine, decision tree, and k-nearest neighbor algorithms, which provided stable results. Neural network was relatively unstable in comparison to these methods, but with 140 attributes, it yielded an accuracy of 90% on the training set and a little less than that on the test set. These results are consistent with the experimental results obtained by Fuhr and Buckley [16]. They suggested that 50 to 100 training examples per attribute are needed. Similarly, Yang and Pedersen [58] found that roughly 50 examples per attribute gave the best performance by using kNN with information gain.

The experimental results indicate that naïve Bayes and multinominal naïve Bayes resulted in better performance on both datasets with top attributes ranked by gain ratio. K-nearest neighbor, neural network, and support vector machine worked well when using top attributes ranked by information gain. Decision tree outperformed other learning algorithms across all three measures but peaked when information gain or Chi-squares was used.

Among the six classi<sup>fi</sup>cation algorithms, decision tree provided the highest overall accuracy on both training and test datasets, no matter which information–theoretic measure was used. When gain ratio was used, neural network yielded the lowest accuracy on both datasets, compared to other machine learning algorithms. Naïve Bayes yielded much lower accuracy on both datasets when information gain or Chisquares was used instead of gain ratio. The best performance results were achieved when information gain or Chi-squares was used for attribute ranking, and decision tree was used for classi<sup>fi</sup>cation. In these settings, decision tree correctly classi<sup>fi</sup>ed 99.59% of the pages in the training set and 98.67% of the pages in the test set.

## 5.2. Statistical analysis

To determine if the differences in performance among the different methods employed in this study are indeed statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, we conducted a series of non-parametric statistical tests. For the text mining methods, we only used the results derived from applying the information gain measure to limit the number of comparisons. In all, therefore, we compared ten methods, including two versions of CyberPatrol. In particular, we applied the Cochran Q test, which tests if three or more related samples are signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different, based on matching frequencies or proportions [46]. The matching is achieved by using the same web page across all the methods. And the frequency of interest here is that for accuracy, which is a dichotomous variable (1=accurate, 0=incorrect). We report the results on the test set (N=3000).

The results of the test (Cochran's Q=6927, df=9, p=.000) revealed that the methods are signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different in terms of accuracy. Conducting the test separately on the <sup>fi</sup>lter methods (Cochran's Q=990, df=3, p=.000) and on the text mining methods (Cochran's Q=858, df=5, p=.000) revealed similar results.

Following the multiple comparisons, we conducted a series of pairwise comparisons between methods. In particular, we applied the McNemar test to examine the differences in accuracies between any two methods. We found a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference in accuracy for each pair of <sup>fi</sup>lter methods (p=.01 for the CyberPatrol pair, and pb.001 for others), as well as for each pair of text mining methods (p=.037 for the NN-SVM pair and pb.001 for others). When the best-performing <sup>fi</sup>lter, CyberSitter, was compared separately with each text mining method, we found that text mining always outperforms the <sup>fi</sup>lter method (pb.001).

## 5.3. Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the performance of three top-ranked commercial Internet <sup>fi</sup>lters in the domain of Internet abuse detection, a major problem af<sup>fl</sup>icting organizations. We also proposed an alternative text mining approach for addressing the problem. In particular, we evaluated the performance of six text mining algorithms and found that they outperform the commercial <sup>fi</sup>lters. Given that organizations will continue to invest in such <sup>fi</sup>lters, it is important to draw lessons from the results of the study.

The <sup>fi</sup>rst lesson we learn is that commercial <sup>fi</sup>lters – at least the ones examined in this study – do not meet the performance expectations that organizations would typically have for addressing Internet abuse in the workplace. Filtering based on white lists and black lists is clearly inadequate for addressing such an important organizational problem. One way to boost the performance would be to introduce a learning component into Internet <sup>fi</sup>lters, so that they can learn from successes as well as failures, instead of employing a list of pre-de<sup>fi</sup>ned categories. This would be akin to how some email <sup>fi</sup>lters learn what to block and what not to block. For example, the Barracuda Spam Firewall allows a user to classify an email message as “spam” or “not spam” and, based on the feedback, adapts its blocking behavior in the future.

Second, we found that even <sup>fi</sup>lters like CyberPatrol, which employs advanced technologies such as neural network, fall woefully short of any realistic performance expectations. A possible reason behind such poor performance is that such <sup>fi</sup>lters do not allow the user to train the classi<sup>fi</sup>ers. One way of improving performance is to provide users with the <sup>fl</sup>exibility to train the classi<sup>fi</sup>ers, instead of treating the <sup>fi</sup>lter as a black box.

Third, the results from the text mining classi<sup>fi</sup>ers should encourage organizations to seriously explore text mining as an alternative approach. Clearly, this would need much more skill and resources than deploying a commercial <sup>fi</sup>lter. But investing in text mining may turn out to be a better option in the long run for organizations that are af<sup>fl</sup>icted by Internet usage problems.

## 6. Conclusion and future directions

In this study, we evaluated the performance of three top-ranked, award-winning Internet <sup>fi</sup>lters, as well as that of web page classi<sup>fi</sup>ers built using six text mining algorithms, on the organizational problem of Internet abuse. The web page classi<sup>fi</sup>ers convincingly outperformed the <sup>fi</sup>lters. Some overheads of using the proposed text mining approach include the need for preparing training examples, training classi<sup>fi</sup>ers, and evaluating and selecting classi<sup>fi</sup>ers. The process may need to be applied periodically as new types of web pages are constantly being developed.

The focus of design science is on the creation and evaluation of IT artifacts geared toward solving identi<sup>fi</sup>ed organizational problems [23]. IT artifacts could be constructs, models, methods (algorithms and practices), or instantiations (prototypes and implemented systems). In this paper, we proposed a method – using text mining techniques – to address the organizational problem of Internet abuse. We created and evaluated several artifacts of interest – the constructs for text mining, the text mining algorithms, the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation models, and the instantiated models.

The design process included several dimensionality reduction steps, including stop-word and HTML tag removal, suf<sup>fi</sup>x stripping, and attribute selection. Six different classi<sup>fi</sup>cation algorithms were used to build web page classi<sup>fi</sup>ers, whose effectiveness in detecting Internet abuse was evaluated. The experimental results con<sup>fi</sup>rm that text mining provides a more attractive alternative to traditional <sup>fi</sup>lter methods for detecting Internet abuse. In addition, our experimental results indicate that advanced technologies utilized by CyberPatrol are not effective in <sup>fi</sup>ltering Internet abuse. The text mining approach that we have proposed in this paper is much more effective than state-ofthe-art Internet <sup>fi</sup>lters.

This work opens up several areas for future research. It would be interesting to employ and evaluate other text mining techniques for addressing the Internet abuse problem. The generalizability of our proposed method could be tested by applying it to address other important organizational problems. The usefulness of additional features/constructs of web pages (links, multimedia contents, etc.), besides text content, could also be examined. Another interesting future direction would be to design methods for combining multiple (source-based and content-based) techniques to further improve performance. It would be also interesting to conduct <sup>fi</sup>eld studies to see how different techniques actually compete with and complement each other in real-world settings. For the Internet abuse detection problem, the prior probability distribution of the class (abuse and non-abuse) may be largely skewed while the two types of errors (over-blocking and under-blocking) may not be equally costly. Costsensitive meta learning methods, such as instance weighting and threshold adjusting, could be used to minimize the overall cost, rather than plain error rate, according to the skewed distribution and asymmetric costs [61,62].
