## Introduction

The rise of the digital native, along with the growth of ubiquitous information systems (UIS), has important implications for research in information systems. Digital natives are those who have grown up in a digital world. They have grown up in a world where the use of information and communications technology is pervasive and ubiquitous and where information and communication technologies (ICT) are used in organizational and nonorganizational contexts. It has become the norm for people to carry mobile phones, laptop computers, and personal digital assistants almost everywhere they go. It has become the norm for people to use UIS such as Facebook, YouTube, and Twitter for both personal and professional purposes.

However, most IS research until now has focused on digital immigrants and their use of information systems in organizations. Digital immigrants were not born into the digital world; rather, they have learnt to engage with it in their adult lives. Of course, it may be the case that there is a continuum between digital natives and digital immigrants and some people exhibit more digital nativity than others. Nevertheless, it is the argument of this paper that these two phenomena taken together—the rise of the digital native and the growth of UIS—potentially represents a fundamental shift in our “paradigm” for IS research. What does the rise of the digital native and UIS mean for IS research? That is the issue explored in this article.

## The Rise of the Digital Native

Prensky (2001) delineates between two types of users based on their levels of comfort within the digital world: “digital immigrants” and “digital natives.” He defines digital immigrants as those who were not born into the digital world—they are users of information systems who started using systems at some stage in their adult lives. Digital natives, however, are users who have grown up in a digital world. Palfrey and Gasser (2008) and Palfrey et al. (2009) define digital natives as a subset of the millennial generation who have grown up immersed in a networked world, with access to ubiquitous digital technologies and the ability to learn and use them in fluent and sophisticated ways. A 2004 survey by the Net Day project of 200,000 U.S. students concluded that digital natives are not just using technology differently—their lives are being moulded by technology differently. They are often described as digitally literate, highly connected, experiential, social, and in need of immediate gratification (McMahon and Pospisil 2005). By age 20, digital natives will have spent 20,000 hours online using a whole host of systems from transaction to decision support systems to collaboration support for personal and professional purposes. They typically use these information systems to explore their places and identities in the world (Valkenburg and Peter 2008). Unlike their digital immigrant counterparts, they tend to be more comfortable with extensive peer-to-peer collaboration and the resultant disclosure of personal data (Leung 2003).

Even though many digital immigrants have become proficient users of technology, their use differs significantly from their digital native counterparts. Communication via new technology is one such area; digital immigrants prefer to use e-mail for online communication whereas digital natives prefer the more synchronous forms of instant messaging. With mobile phones, digital immigrants favour speaking to people whereas digital natives prefer speed texting. Digital natives prefer to share differently as well. Blogging is increasingly gaining currency for both digital immigrants and digital natives but once again for different reasons. Digital natives use blogging to share personal experiences and treat personal blogging websites as forms of online journals. By contrast, digital immigrants tend to use blogging sites more as intellectual tools to share and discuss ideas with their peers (Prensky 2001). Whereas digital immigrants are regarded by IS scholars and practitioners as “users” of IT (hence the extensive IS research literature on user involvement and user acceptance), digital natives are creators of online content (Huffaker 2004). Digital natives are adept at uploading their own videos to YouTube, building websites, and communicating via text or Twitter. Hence, digital natives are not passive users of information systems; rather, they are creators and active participants in a new digital media culture, designing personal websites and sometimes launching their own online enterprises (Sharp 2000). More so than their digital immigrant counterparts, digital natives have intertwined the digital world and its numerous technologies as a part of their daily lives. Many of the systems that digital natives engage with are ubiquitous, as is explained in the next section.

Although most previous writers have tended to treat digital natives and digital immigrants as mutually exclusive, we suggest that the concept of nativity with respect to digital technology is perhaps better conceptualized as a continuum. Some people, depending upon their experience, are likely to be more native than others. However, to what extent can digital immigrants become digital natives and vice versa? The phrase “you can’t teach an old dog new tricks” is perhaps relevant here. Though people may place themselves at different points in the continuum, we suggest that learning a new language and becoming comfortable with a new culture is not easy. Our research questions with respect to digital nativity are discussed in more detail next.

## Ubiquitous Information Systems and Digital Natives

Srivastava (2004) asserts that there are different ways to characterize the “ubiquitous” information society. The word “ubiquitous” is derived from the Latin “ubique,” meaning “that which exists everywhere” (Sorensen et al. 2005). In the context of information technologies, ubiquitous digital connectivity can be seen in the indispensability of the Internet for digital natives (Srivastava 2004) and the rapid uptake of mobile phones, laptop computers, and personal digital assistants. Mark Weiser in the article “Computer for the 21st Century” (Weiser 1991) proposed computers/devices the size of tabs (iPhones, Blackberries, smart phones, cards), pads (iPads, laptops, Kindles, touch screens), and boards (interactive blackboards/whiteboards) participating in a ubiquitous network. Poslad (2009) extends this to include dust (embedded or untethered micro- and nanosized devices), skins (nonplanar material that can be used to cover natural and artificial objects), and clay (programmable synthetic matter). More and more, these devices and environments tend to be hybrid and smart (cars and buildings), enabling very rich and flexible interaction modalities. In this article, when we talk about ubiquitous technologies, we are including hardware such as tabs, pads, boards, dust, skins, and clay that are interconnected and interwoven into the very fabric of our lives through ubiquitous networks. When we use the word “system,” we mean it in the broadest possible sense of being made up of people, processes, information, and communication systems and technologies. UIS brings all these things together to impact all facets and phases of human living (Funabashi et al. 2008).

We suggest that the rise of the digital native along with the growth of UIS has profound implications for IS research. The digital natives of today represent the first generation to grow up surrounded by information and communications technology (Tapscott 2008). They have spent their entire lives surrounded by computers, video games, digital music players, video cams, cell phones, and so forth (Prensky 2001). Prensky (2001) claims that the digital culture and environment in which the digital natives have grown up has changed the way they think: “It is now clear that as a result of this ubiquitous environment and the sheer volume of their interaction with it, today’s students think and process information fundamentally differently from their predecessors” (p. 1). Research by social psychologists shows that people who grow up in different cultures do not just think about different things—they actually think differently (Hayles 2007, Nisbett 2004). Therefore, the UIS environment and culture in which digital natives are being raised may affect many of their thought processes. Additionally, neurological research suggests that digital natives raised in a ubiquitous computing environment may actually undergo a change in the wiring of their minds—a change in neural circuitry that is fundamentally different from previous generations (Small and Vorgan 2009). Of course, further neurological research is needed to understand the extent and importance of these changes.

Stohr and Konsynski (1992) explore some of the research challenges in information systems and decision processes using dimensions such as the IT artifacts; the individual, group, or organization that uses them; and the environment or context. Similarly, Yoo and Gothenburg (2010) propose a framework for experiential computing that includes artifacts, actors, space, and time. The space and time elements define the “where” and “when” (that is, the context) the actors engage with the artifacts. A synthesis of these led us to propose four key dimensions that appear to be relevant for understanding digital natives in the context of UIS: users (digital immigrants versus digital natives), systems (traditional information systems versus ubiquitous information systems), activity (professional versus personal), and context (office versus home). When we chart these four intertwined dimensions together, we see a clear pattern emerging (see Figure 1). Most research in IS has focused on the inner/central regions of the chart, namely, the “traditional information systems used by digital immigrants for professional purposes at the office.” However, there is a paucity of research at the mid-to-outer regions of the chart that looks at “ubiquitous information systems used by digital natives for professional and personal purposes at the office and at home.”

Traditional information systems help to improve the efficiency and effectiveness of organizations. The users of these systems in the past have been digital immigrants for whom functionality has been of paramount importance. For digital natives, however, interactivity, usability, flexibility, and connectivity are as important as functionality. Most aspects of traditional information systems and the types of users associated with these systems have been well-researched within the

IS academic discipline. However, there is only a limited amount of IS research in the area of UIS at the moment (one notable exception is the book by Sorensen et al. 2005) and even less on the design, implementation, and usage of these UIS by the new emerging audience of digital natives.

We conducted a search for words relating to digital natives and UIS in the abstract and key words section of articles in six leading information systems journals. We used various synonyms for these words such as “pervasive computing” for UIS. The journals we chose are those in the Association for Information Systems (AIS) basket of six top IS journals, i.e., Information Systems Research, MIS Quarterly, Journal of Management Information Systems, Information Systems Journal, European Journal of Information Systems, and Journal of the AIS. Using the EBSCO database, we looked at all the articles that were published in these journals over a ten-year period from January 1, 2000 to December 31, 2009. Words relating to digital natives and UIS did not appear once in any of these six journals. A further in-depth analysis of the abstracts of MIS Quarterly and Information Systems Research from January 1, 2000 until June 30, 2010 was conducted. After reading all the abstracts of these articles, we found only two research commentaries (Lyytinen and Yoo 2002 and March et al. 2000) that mention ubiquitous environments and none that mention or allude to digital natives. Though there are a number of papers on social networks, networked-enabled organizations, and telecommuting, none of them deal with UIS or digital natives in-depth.

Two other aspects that tend to have been taken for granted in most IS research projects are the activities and the contexts. Implicitly, most IS research has focused on the use of IS for professional purposes in the context of the workplace/office. This is perhaps understandable given that most IS researchers are currently based in business schools. However, the use of IS for personal purposes in nonoffice contexts such as the home has tended to be ignored. Nowadays, however, digital natives seamlessly transition between the use of traditional and ubiquitous information systems for both personal and professional purposes. Hence, the boundaries between these different uses and contexts are no longer very clear.

In a few related disciplines such as psychology, behavioral science, and marketing, there is often a segmentation of users/consumers/humans into various age groups (e.g., children, youth, adults, and the elderly). These disciplines consider the particular problems, issues, requirements, and/or needs of these particular segments of the population. However, IS researchers have tended to treat information systems users in a singular fashion without distinguishing between the various age groups. With the rise of the digital native, we suggest that there is a need for us to distinguish between the various user segments or at least between digital natives and digital immigrants.

## A Research Agenda

We suggest an IS research agenda that focuses on how digital natives are interacting with and transforming UIS, how UIS can be designed and implemented for digital natives, and what impacts and governance issues are related to the use of UIS by digital natives. Lyytinen and Yoo (2002) suggest that IS researchers are uniquely positioned to understand how to integrate diverse technological, social, and managerial issues arising from UIS environments while designing, building, and managing such environments. Yoo and Gothenburg (2010) suggest that the IS community has developed a rich repertoire of theoretical language and methodological tools that could be leveraged to study the changes taking place in the new UIS environment.

Of course, information systems is not the only discipline with a potential interest in digital natives and UIS. We envisage that researchers in other disciplines will also have an interest in these two phenomena. However, our questions are designed for an IS audience. The IS research space can be viewed as 2 × 2 matrix made up of digital immigrants and digital natives on one axis and traditional and ubiquitous information systems on the other. In the past, research has focused on the traditional IS-digital immigrant space, but there are many other relevant questions that could be asked in relation to the other three quadrants. In this article, we limit ourselves to one of the quadrants, i.e., the intersection of the research space defined by UIS and digital natives. In the following sections, we explore a tentative list of candidate questions that could be posed with respect to this quadrant, although the following questions are in no way meant to be exhaustive.

• How and why are digital natives engaging with UIS?

• How are traditional IS being transformed by digital natives and UIS?

• How do we design and implement UIS for digital natives?

• What are the positive and negative impacts of UIS on digital natives, organizations, and society?

## Research Question 1: How and Why Are Digital Natives Engaging With UIS?

Communications between digital natives, even with close friends, is often mediated by UIS. Digital natives use instant messaging, mobile phones, portals to Internet chat rooms, and multiplayer games to communicate with each other. Digital natives are said to prefer receiving information quickly, be adept at processing information rapidly, prefer multitasking and nonlinear access to information, have a low tolerance for lectures, prefer active rather than passive learning, and rely heavily on communications technologies to access information and carry out social and professional interactions (Prensky 2001). The proliferation of Web pages and message board postings as well as the popularity of instant messaging among young people all contribute to the booming use of UIS for communication among digital natives (Madden 2003).

Many reasons have been suggested as to why digital natives use UIS. These include the influence of family and friends (Subrahmanyam and Lin 2007), a socially supportive network, and the level of emotional support (Caplan and Turner 2007) as well as feeling close and connected to others on a daily basis (Hartup et al. 2001). Valaitis (2002) identifies three reasons for the use of UIS by young people— information, community, and collaboration. Valaitis says that technology empowers young people to share their views and information with the community, obtain others’ opinions, and gain access to influential people. These three elements of UIS (information, community, and collaboration) for digital natives are now discussed.

Many digital natives face the challenges of adolescence in a digital environment. This period of their lives is dominated by many questions, questions about themselves and questions about their changing relationships with the outside world. In this phase of life, there is a struggle for independence from parents and an increased reliance on peers for support (Larson and Richards 1991). However, there are some areas of a young person’s life that he does not feel comfortable sharing, even with his closest friends. For all these reasons, the Internet has become increasingly popular among digital natives as it facilitates the increased communication needs with existing friends and the creation of new social relationships as well as the need for anonymity when looking for information on sensitive topics (Borzekowski and Rickert 2001). Collaborative information systems and technologies allow learners to construct their own knowledge and their own world.

The three main underlying theoretical domains that can inform us about this particular research question are developmental psychology, cultural anthropology, and information systems. There is significant theory that can be adopted from all three disciplines, and we suggest an opportunity for the development of a new hybrid theory where theory from one domain may influence theory from another.

Developmental theory may be an important domain to draw from in exploring this research question because major psychological and social changes are faced by adolescents as they transition from being children to becoming adults (Petersen and Taylor 1980). Caspi et al. (1993) suggest that the quality and timing of these changes influence and are influenced by psychological, social, cultural, and historical contexts. The difference between digital natives and preceding generations is that these processes are now taking place within the context of the Internet.

The social development model (SDM) is a developmental theory of human behavior that is used to explain the origins and development of delinquent behavior during childhood and adolescence. The SDM hypothesizes that children adopt the beliefs and behavioral patterns of the social unit—such as family, peers, or neighbourhood—to which they are most firmly bonded. If the social unit has prosocial attitudes, then the child adopts a prosocial orientation; if the social unit is antisocial, then the child often manifests problem behavior (Catalano et al. 1996). In the context of digital natives, this theory could provide an important mechanism in understanding the underlying impact of UIS on their social development.

In cultural anthropology, the process of enculturation is a major theme. Enculturation is the process by which a person learns the requirements of a culture. Becoming enculturated means not only becoming competent in a language but it also means acquiring certain values and behaviors that are deemed to be appropriate (Carrithers 1992). If the process of becoming a digital native is seen as one of enculturation, the theory of enculturation could help us understand the process of digital nativity and how some people become more or less native than others.

Two types of IS theories are of potential relevance to our first research question. The first type of theory is related to the adoption and diffusion of innovations (e.g., Baskerville and Pries-Heje 2001, Rogers 2003). It might be interesting to study digital natives who, as a group, can be defined as early adopters of new technologies. Rogers (2003) found that there are certain characteristics of new innovations that motivate adoption such as relative advantage (the innovation is better than the status quo), compatibility (the innovation is consistent with current values), low complexity (the innovation is not difficult to understand and use), observability (the results of the innovation are noticeable to others), and trialability (the innovation can be tried out on a partial or temporary basis). These types of characteristics undoubtedly appeal to digital natives.

The second IS theory that may be of relevance is the technology appropriation model. A recent study found that young people are innately attracted to IT, but any new technologies are experimented with and evaluated as to whether they add value to the Young people’s lifestyles. If the technology resonates with their needs and provides a “fit” with their lives, then it will be appropriated (Carroll et al. 2002). Additional research is needed to explore this subject in more depth.

One type of theory that may not be so relevant for understanding digital natives and UIS is related to the user acceptance of information technology. Several theories related to the acceptance and use of IT have been developed and tested by IS researchers. For example, Venkatesh and colleagues (Venkatesh et al. 2003) compared eight previous models (such as the technology acceptance model and the theory of planned behavior) in order to formulate a unified theory of acceptance and use of technology. Although this type of theory may be useful, Venkatesh et al. (2003) note that most of the theories related to user acceptance are based on the assumption that users tend to resist new technologies and systems (p. 426). However, this assumption clearly does not apply to digital natives: Digital natives eagerly adopt new technologies and consider themselves to be technologically savvy. Nevertheless, as Agosto (2004) notes, many digital natives may not be good at evaluating the usefulness of a specific information system. Therefore, a modified version of some user acceptance-type theories may be applicable. Further research is needed to evaluate the relevance of such theories for digital natives and UIS. Further research would also enable us to engage in a more systematic exploration of the perception of technology, cognition, and the mediated experience in general as a consequence of digital natives’ having lived their entire lives surrounded by certain types of technology.

Our first research question lends itself to being explored via a variety of means. At one end of the spectrum, a survey and/or field study of digital natives in schools and universities regarding the kinds of UIS that they use would be valuable. At the other end of the spectrum, one could use netnography (a type of ethnographic research to study online communities) (Kozinets 2002) or virtual ethnography (Hine 2000) to study the use of online social networks, bulletin boards, newsgroups, and Twitter by digital natives.

## Research Question 2: How Are Traditional Information Systems Being Transformed by Digital Natives and UIS?

According to the Gartner group, as digital natives begin to enter the work force, they bring with them their ease of use of technology and preference for

UIS that enable them to collaborate more freely in enterprise environments. Though traditional organizational IT tools were more rigid and siloed, Gartner suggests that modern organizational IT technologies are being motivated by this new generation of workers to become more “free form,” informal, and participatory (Boulton 2007). Organizations are increasingly making use of Web-based technologies that provide rapid and agile collaboration, information sharing, emergence, and integration capabilities in the extended enterprise (Oracle White Paper 2008).

Along with the evolution of IT tools and technologies in the modern day organization, some industry observers such as Alsop (2008) have suggested an evolution of organizational employees that are distinctly different from previous generations. This new generation of workers (digital natives or millennials) is keen to try out new technologies and ways of doing things, that is, rather than considering the potential risk of failure with IT, these workers see opportunities. They are not limited to concepts such as “we’ve always done this way,” “not invented here,” “our company is different    this won’t work here.” The digital native employee prefers a flexible, informal, and spontaneous organizational structure rather than a “command and control” structure (Oracle White Paper 2008).

The introduction of UIS and digital natives into organizations requires a reconsideration of the strategies, processes, and roles of employees in firms. Mehling (2007) suggests three key changes that need to be considered in transforming traditional IS to incorporate elements of the UIS and their uses by digital natives. The first is the flow of ideas; in traditional organizations, ideas tend to flow in a unidirectional fashion. Management decides on a business strategy and motivates its employees to implement or work toward that strategy. Information flow is also controlled by management (Hansen and Birkinshaw 2007). In the UIS world, ideas do not need to follow any particular direction. Any individual with an excellent idea has the ability to galvanize support and create an ecosystem around that idea. Second, in traditional organizational systems, the power structure is vertical and top-down. The actions of the employees within an organization are directly influenced through management actions (Bartlett and

Ghoshal 1993). Management provides the direction as to where to take their organizations. However, with UIS, the power structure tends to be horizontal. The person with the greatest idea can influence a community of practice. These communities of practice can become an effective way for organizations to handle unstructured problems and to share knowledge outside of traditional structural boundaries (Lesser and Storck 2001). Last, employee incentives may need to change. In traditional organizations, individuals are promoted, which generates an environment of competition between employees. UIS, on the other hand, are oriented toward rewarding people who collaborate rather than compete. The best ideas come up as multiple people come together to cooperate and add value. Instant recognition tends to be what motivates digital natives in this new UIS environment

Of course, we do not believe that UIS actually causes these changes in a deterministic way. What matters it is the interplay between individuals and organizations and how they use UIS. The new UIS environment makes it possible for these changes to occur. The following theories and research methods may be relevant in answering our second research question.

Theories related to the adoption of innovations may be relevant. For example, Flanagin (2006) suggests that some organizations may be “pressured” into adopting new technologies and systems without fully comprehending the efficiency and effectiveness they may bring to their particular organizations. Some organizations may look for the adoption of an innovation by other organizations as a means to validate their own adoption.

Another relevant theory is the task-technology fit (TTF) theory. The TTF is defined as the degree to which a technology assists an individual in performing his or her portfolio of tasks (Goodhue and Thompson 1995). Employees initially appropriate a technology based on their prior understanding of it and the task. As employees work, they recognize outcomes from their work (e.g., effectiveness and efficiency) and develop perceptions of how well the technology fits the task. These perceptions may trigger changes in the way they subsequently appropriate the technology and define the task to produce new structures in which the technology is used (Dennis and Garfield 2003). Therein is an opportunity for organizations to encourage the use of UIS such as Weblogging to assist digital natives in their expected roles within organizations. Employees who already apply technologies such as Weblogs in their social environments require little or no training to use these technologies in organizational settings. Furthermore, the various features of the Weblogging tools familiar to digital natives might be leveraged by them to create knowledge-sharing communities within organizations (Kwai Fun 2008). As organizations attempt to balance the social needs of digital natives with business requirements for task completion, viewing task– technology fit in conjunction with people-technology fit as a driver of IT usage appears to be highly relevant and necessary.

Another theory that may be relevant to our second research question is structuration theory or adaptive structuration theory. Prior IS research has found that identical technologies do not provide identical results to groups and organizations (DeSanctis and Poole 1994). To explain divergent paths of evolution in use, DeSanctis and Poole (1994) developed the adaptive structuration theory (AST), which describes the intertwined structures of technology, human understanding, and action. AST illuminates how the appropriation of different technology and organizational features combine to structure the innovative adaptation, use, and ultimate outcomes of technologies in organizations. This theory may enhance our exploration of how certain aspects and elements of UIS are transforming the nature of organizational information systems. Moreover, AST describes how a particular user’s (or user group’s) experience with a new technology results in innovative adaptations and use. This theory might be particularly useful for exploring how the technological expertise of digital natives can be harnessed for the benefit of organizations.

With respect to research methods, these tools (exploratory surveys, field studies, case studies, and ethnographic approaches) could be used to help IS researchers answer our second research question. This question needs to be looked at both within and across organizations.

## Research Question 3: How Do We Design and Implement UIS for Digital Natives?

The recent interest in design science research in IS (Hevner et al. 2004) suggests that there is an opportunity for IS researchers to focus on design frameworks and models for UIS (Boyd and Ellison 2008). In order to guide the design of UIS for digital natives, we suggest a set of interrelated dimensions, namely, personalised, interactive, intuitive, attractive, and social (Figure 2). Angehrn and Lüthi (1990) discuss the tension between functionality and usability. In our opinion, traditional information systems design has for the most part focused on functionality at the expense of the five usability-oriented design criteria identified earlier. However, we believe that, though functionality is important, these five interrelated design dimensions are of paramount importance when we consider UIS and digital natives. We explore these dimensions in the context of a subset of UIS, namely, Web spaces on tabs and pads (though obviously these dimensions are equally important to other devices, environments, and interaction modalities) (Poslad 2009).

The personalisation dimension is primarily about giving digital natives ways to collaboratively change the design of a UIS. The UIS can be personalised in two ways: by automatically adapting to a user’s behavior and by being adaptable in terms of being customisable through direct configurations by users. Being adaptable means giving digital natives the opportunity to create customised start pages or dashboards. Examples of such functionality are the iGoogle start page or the homepage of bbc.co.uk. Personalisation helps to match user expectations and perception of the Web space. In terms of the flow aspect, personalisation allows users to adjust the difficulty of the interface according to their skills. Common examples of this are applications that offer an “expert mode” that enables more complex functions. Many blogging services such as wordpress.com allow users to change the appearance of their Weblogs by using templates.

Interactivity is another dimension that should be considered in the design of a UIS for digital natives. Terdiman (2005) reports on a study that observed American and Australian digital natives using different types of UIS for many different purposes. They found that the participants wanted to be active rather than reading or sitting quietly. Hughes-Hassell and Miller (2003) echo similar sentiments; their research suggests that the visual appeal of information systems, ease of navigation, currency, and accuracy of information are all key elements when it comes to creating an interactive UIS for digital natives.

The intuitive dimension is rooted in the requirement to allow users to navigate easily. Intuitive is an attribute for interfaces that can be used “without further explanation.” The button in Microsoft Word that is indicated by a “B” and allows one to make certain parts of text bold is an example of an intuitive interface element. UIS should be usable without the need to refer to manuals or tutorials—how to use them should be obvious “on the fly.” Yahoo mail, for instance, is a Web 2.0 application that is mostly intuitive for users with some basic computer knowledge. In Norman’s (1986) model, the concept “intuitive” can be reflected in making the task of specifying actions according to the user’s intentions as easy as possible.

The attractive dimension refers to the need to make the UIS specific to digital natives by incorporating “cool” and up-to-date designs. What these designs are, of course, is difficult to specify. One way to address this challenge is to “empower” the users to contribute to the design of the UIS just as they can contribute to the content. Elements of this dimension can be seen in the popular MySpace and Facebook sites.

The social dimension captures the requirements of showing who contributed what and allowing users to express their own virtual identities. Discussion boards are one illustration of this concept. In discussion boards, every “post” is attributed to a user and many platforms allow the maintenance of individual “user pages” or custom personal messages below each post. Users summarise their contributions in terms of their most popular “posts” or other resources they have created on the Web.

Although all five dimensions are just suggestions at this point, we tend to think that all five dimensions are interrelated. For instance, if a certain interactive element such as the ability to drag and drop items between two tables is enriched with functionality that allows one to personalise which items are displayed in the tables, this could make use of the tables more intuitive because they would only contain relevant items that the user understands.

We see the influence of interfaces for digital natives from two angles: the interface should meet the expectations of the users and, more specifically, the interface should enhance learning and creativity. Norman (1986) proposes an execution-evaluation cycle model to understand how a system meets the requirements of users. The two major phases in this model are execution and evaluation (Dix et al. 2004). The user first formulates a plan of action. This plan is then executed on the interface of a system. After the execution on the interface, the user observes the interface and takes further actions based on the observations. The effectiveness of the interaction is determined by the user in the evaluation phase (Dix et al. 2004). The more the system meets the requirements of the user, the smaller the gap between what the user has intended to happen on the system and what actually happens.

We cannot know the exact intentions of every digital native user of the UIS. A central element in the digital native perception of UIS is that it is “fun” to use. This requirement is reflected in the literature about “flow.” Flow represents a state of consciousness where a person is completely absorbed in an activity. Csikszentmihalyi (1990) describes this state as the “flow channel" where a balance between skills and challenges must exist. According to Pilke (2004), flow experiences can occur while working with information systems. Ghani (1995) developed a model to show the factors for flow in humancomputer interactions in which flow is measured in terms of enjoyment and concentration. In Ghani’s (1995) model, perceived task challenges, skills, and cognitive spontaneity (“playfulness”) are prerequisites of flow. When the system is too difficult to use, no flow state will occur. When the system is too “primitive” or simple for a very skilled user, boredom might prevent the flow state. In a way, flow can be understood as the “sweet spot” between challenge and boredom. For digital natives, it is of course desirable that they enjoy the experience of the information system and even better if this experience facilitates learning and creativity.

The design and implementation of UIS for digital natives would obviously benefit from some of the studies suggested for our first and second research questions. The problems, issues, and requirements identified by these studies would help us to design conceptual models, frameworks, and architectures for a UIS for digital natives. These, in turn, would be implemented using design science principles. The evaluation of the generated artifacts would be through a variety of methods such as field experiments, case studies, simulation, and laboratory experiments. These evaluation studies, in turn, will become the inputs to conceptual analysis and theory building and further refinement of the artifacts.

## Research Question 4: What Are the Positive and Negative Impacts of UIS on Digital Natives, Organizations, and Society?

Tapscott (2008) claims that the 80 million digital natives coming into the workplace will want to be part of an “engage and collaborate” model within organizations rather than a command and control model. This is because the workplace values and expectations of digital natives differ from those of older generations. For example, digital natives tend to have high expectations for rapid career growth, greater demands for work/life balance, and the need for clear and frequent performance feedback. This has important ramifications for management in organizations as well as the IS within these organizations.

However, the ease of use and familiarity with computers has also meant that digital natives are less cautious with their personal information and thus are potentially more vulnerable to threats and risks that the Internet and related technologies pose. Digital natives might not fully appreciate how their personal information could be misused. Proficiency with certain technologies does not necessarily translate into an understanding about their own personal security and privacy. Digital natives may take risks that their digital immigrant counterparts might well avoid. Also, the social and the personal uses of technology that digital natives take for granted in their social world may not necessarily be acceptable within organizations.

Simply blocking channels like YouTube and Facebook to address these governance and security concerns, as some organizations have done, is probably a poor solution. These organizations may be missing an opportunity to empower digital natives to connect and collaborate with one another. Organizations often take a conservative stance with any new technology that is introduced and is viewed as potentially causing a level of disruption. However, being connected is not only part of what digital natives do—it is who they are. They consider the digital world to be part of their personalities. Not being able to be constantly connected is the equivalent of telling digital immigrants that they cannot use the phone or talk to their peers during office hours. Digital natives have an expectation that at work they will continue to be connected; they will continue to collaborate, share, and have fun.

One aspect that can be considered in the context of the impact these systems have on the personal life of digital natives is on the development of selfidentity (Erikson 1980). Digital natives are in a period of their lives where they tend to experiment with a variety of identities (Cheng et al. 2002). The Internet and many UIS allow digital natives to “try on” different personas and determine which persona will gain the most approval and acceptance (McKenna and Bargh 1999). Another important influence in successful identity development is membership in social groups (Martin and Nakayama 1997). Group membership also enhances the need to belong, an innate human motivation (Brewer and Gardner 1996).

Another aspect that could be considered is adolescent cognitive development within the context of UIS. Johnson (2006) asserts that the Internet is a cultural tool that influences cognitive processes and an environmental stimulus that contributes to the formation of a specific cognitive architecture. Cognition is a general term encompassing mental processes such as attention, perception, comprehension, memory, and problem solving. Considering that the most common Internet activities for digital natives are playing games, navigating websites, and communicating with others (Roberts et al. 2004), Johnson (2006) suggests that metacognitive abilities are developed as a result of all these activities. For example, Johnson (2006) suggests that synchronous communication decreases cognitive processing speed (i.e., reaction time), playing video games requires simultaneous processing, and online communication (a different type of cognitive ability) requires successive processing.

Some of the main risks that digital natives expose themselves to in the new ubiquitous information society are exposure to pornography, online stalkers, and personal information theft. Filtering and blocking software is one of the most frequently publicized prevention devices. However, these types of tools only work on computers on which they have been specifically installed and even then they are not 100% effective in protecting digital natives from potential dangers. A recent survey by the American Pew Internet Group showed that digital natives access UIS from a variety of different places including school, the library, their friend’s houses, and their WiFi-capable mobile phones (Madden 2003). Berson and Berson (2003) suggests that empowering digital natives to critically evaluate their digital environments as well as encouraging their interests in producing their own digital content may mitigate many of the potential problems.

One theory that may be useful for exploring our fourth research question is developmental theory. Carr (2008) claims that the multiple sensory inputs and diverse sources of information offered up by the UIS can overwhelm the digital native’s capacity to engage in thoughtful decision making. The resulting brain overload can reduce the ability of digital natives to make sense of what is presented or to respond in a thoughtful manner (Carr 2008). Berson and Berson (2003) notes that images that pervade digital messages elicit a response from the limbic system, the part of the brain that tends to be impulsive and emotional. Digital natives may act or react without reflection on the implication for self and others. As a result, digital natives typically underestimate the influence of digital technologies on their behavior or the potential for risk (Berson and Berson 2003).

Another set of theories and concepts that may be relevant is in the area of corporate governance and the management and security of information systems. A successful governance framework for information systems requires the right mix of people, process, and technology (Swindle and Conner 2004). The formula for that mix begins with a clear understanding of governance goals and how they are to be achieved. One particular governance framework that could be considered in this regard is the information security governance (ISG) framework (Posthumus and Von Solms 2004). An ISG framework is an important step in ensuring the protection of business information through the protection of the information system as well as improving the efficiency of business operations. This framework addresses three central aspects of corporate governance (Swindle and Conner 2004), namely, people, processes, and technology.

There are many quantitative and qualitative methods that could be employed to answer this question. However, we tend to think that methods such as action research, case study research, field studies, grounded theory, and critical research may be more relevant to our fourth question. We also see the potential of using surveys as a mechanism to elicit information regarding impacts and governance issues related to UIS and digital natives. Perhaps a multimethodological approach would also be appropriate for exploring this research question.

## Conclusion

We have suggested that the rise of the digital native along with the growth of ubiquitous information systems have important implications for IS researchers. Until now, most information systems researchers have tended to focus on the use, design, and implementation of information systems for digital immigrants. However, there are significant differences between digital natives and digital immigrants. Traditional assumptions that were true for digital immigrants (such as “users always resist technology”) do not apply to digital natives. Also, the rise, popularity, and proliferation of UIS potentially changes the boundaries and scope of our research. Whereas traditional information systems research was focused primarily on information systems in organizational contexts, the use of UIS by digital natives blurs the boundary between organizational use at work and personal use at home. This suggests the need for a new IS research agenda, if not an entirely new paradigm for IS research. Hence, we need to figure out how digital natives are interacting with and transforming UIS, how UIS can be designed and implemented for digital natives, and what impacts and governance issues are related to the use of UIS by digital natives. It is our contention that IS researchers are uniquely positioned to understand and integrate the diverse technological, social, and managerial issues arising from the use of ubiquitous information systems by digital natives.
