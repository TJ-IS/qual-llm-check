# A for Effort? Using the Crowd to Identify Moral Hazard in New York City Restaurant Hygiene Inspections

Abstract. From an upset stomach to a life-threatening foodborne illness, getting sick is all too common after eating in restaurants. Although health inspection programs are designed to protect consumers, such inspections typically occur at wide intervals of time, allowing restaurant hygiene to remain unmonitored in the interim periods. Information provided in online reviews may be effectively used in these interim periods to gauge restaurant hygiene. In this paper, we use textual information from online reviews of restaurants to effectively identify cases of hygiene violations in restaurants, even after the restaurants have been inspected and certified, thereby identifying moral hazard. Using a data set of restaurant hygiene inspections in New York City from 2010 through 2016 and the associated set of online reviews for the same set of restaurants from Yelp, we use supervised machine learning techniques to develop a hygiene dictionary specifically crafted to identify hygiene-related concerns. The use of this dictionary and the related word counts in online reviews allows us to identify systematic instances of moral hazard, wherein restaurants with positive hygiene inspection scores are seen to regress in their hygiene maintenance within 90 days of receiving the inspection scores. To the extent that social media provide some visibility into the hygiene practices of restaurants, we argue that the effects of information asymmetry that lead to moral hazard may be partially mitigated in this context. Based on our work, we also provide strategies for how cities and policymakers may design effective restaurant inspection programs, through a combination of traditional inspections and the appropriate use of social media.

## Introduction

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) estimates that one in six Americans gets sick each year as a result of foodborne illness (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 2016a). Approximately 60% of such cases are estimated to be the result of inadequately managed hygiene-related practices at restaurants (Gould et al. 2013, Hedberg et al. 2006). Understandably, strictly adhering to hygiene standards requires significant and constant investment by restaurants in terms of training, additional staff, and equipment (Jin and Leslie 2009). Beyond providing standards for food safety and hygiene, regulators and public policymakers also work on hygiene inspection programs to ensure and certify the hygiene practices followed by restaurants. The potential benefits of such programs are clear and have been empirically shown to decrease foodborne illness outbreaks (Irwin et al. 1989), particularly in cities where the hygiene scores are made public (Jin and Leslie 2009, Jin and Lee 2014a). However, inspecting restaurants is a costly and time-consuming process, and real-time changes in hygiene quality are difficult to observe through infrequent inspections. Continuous monitoring is infeasible; New York City (NYC), for example, has over 20,000 restaurants, and inspections often take hours while incurring significant costs for the city. Thus, even with regulations in place, specific forms of noncompliance or gaps in the regulatory process can occur, leading to negative hygiene-related outcomes. Can the techniques of machine learning, applied to social media data, help by identifying these specific forms of noncompliance within the restaurant sector, within the broad context of hygiene inspections? Recent work in economics suggests that machine learning can be successfully combined with traditional economic models to augment their explana tory and predictive power (Mullainathan and Spiess 2017), especially with respect to text (Gentzkow et al. 2017). In this paper, we follow this strategy by using well-understood machine learning techniques to identify instances of moral hazard in the New York City Restaurant Inspection Program (RIP).

The NYC RIP, spearheaded by the Bloomberg administration in 2010 (Ho 2012, New York City Department of Health and Mental Hygiene 2016c) has widely been viewed as highly successful. The RIP was started in 2010 with three goals: give consumers easy access to information about the quality of hygiene of restaurants, improve restaurants’ hygiene practices, and reduce the amount of restaurant-related foodborne illness. The innovative aspect of the RIP program was a two-step inspection process (Ho 2012, New York City Department of Health and Mental Hygiene 2016c), wherein each food service establishment, a category that includes restaurants, coffee shops, bars, nightclubs, and most cafeterias (New York City Department of Health and Mental Hygiene 2016c), was inspected twice in an inspection cycle and given the chance to correct any hygiene-related problems identified in the first inspection. As will be described in the next section, this approach was designed to quickly identify problems and fix them without imposing a heavy regulatory footprint on the services industry (Ho 2012). Data released by the NYC Health Department indicate that the RIP has been highly effective. In terms of addressing restaurant hygiene practices, the number of restaurants that receive an A grade (the highest grade) at the end of an inspection cycle has increased significantly, from less than 30% in 2010, the first year of the program, to 42% in 2012 (Farley 2011), to 80% in 2014 (Farley 2016). More important, the rates of foodborne illness in NYC have declined significantly; the city had over 2.1 million foodborne illness episodes in 2009 but approximately 1 million in 2014 (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 2016b). Similarly, the reported cases of Salmonella, a dangerous foodborne bacteria, decreased by 14% between 2010 and 2013 (New York City Department of Health and Mental Hygiene 2016b).

These results notwithstanding, noncompliance with the RIP can still occur in selected cases. With any certification or inspection scheme, it is possible that the inspected entity will shirk on the required diligence needed to retain quality, once the certification process has been completed (Shapiro 1986). This is consistent with moral hazard, wherein firms or individuals change their behavior after acquiring risk protection or insurance (Holmstrom¨ 1979). In this particular context, it is possible that restaurants that acquire the highest hygiene certification will gradually discontinue their efforts to maintain their hygiene standards, given that the next inspection cycle is more than a year away and the cost of maintaining quality is not trivial (Starbird 2005, New York City Department of Health and Mental Hygiene 2016b). The problem is one of information asymmetry; that is, the regulator does not know which restaurants are likely to shirk on their hygiene practices after receiving certification. If restaurants appear to bypass these efforts to maintain hygiene (whether intentionally or otherwise), the resulting risk of disease outbreaks in the city increases significantly One solution to addressing moral hazard within the literature is to invest in information systems that pro vide continuous visibility and observability (Holmstrom¨ 1979). However, this would necessitate a system of continuous and ongoing hygiene inspections, an option that is clearly economically infeasible. To identify instances of moral hazard that can make the inspection program even more effective and create sustained improvements in hygiene, we use machine learning techniques applied to online review text, building on recent work arguing for how social media may help alleviate the problems of information asymmetry (Kang et al. 2013, Schomberg et al. 2016). Using a crowd-based “information system” created through the suitable extraction of information from online reviews, we show that incidents of moral hazard can be identified, thereby helping policymakers and regulators in their decision making.

Online reviews, provided by restaurant patrons and collated by firms such as Yelp and TripAdvisor, have been used in previous research to capture service quality and feedback and link these to economic outcomes such as sales and purchasing behavior (Lu et al. 2013a, Park and Allen 2013). We view these as potential information sources about a restaurant’s hygiene-related status that, when aggregated across multiple reviewers, can provide insight into whether the restaurant is shirking on maintaining its hygiene status. In effect, we allow consumers to act as hygiene inspectors, albeit unintentionally and in the aggregate, by sharing their content online without any forward-looking expectations of restaurant efforts or influence from restaurants that may potentially bias their responses. The advantage here is that online reviews of restaurants, when used through appropriate statistical techniques, can provide city regulators with information that can help identify, first, restaurants that are likely to be at risk for important hygiene violations even after receiving high hygiene grades (moral hazard) and, second, restaurants that are consistently diligent about their hygiene practices.

Specifically, we develop, test, and externally validate a social media–based hygiene dictionary that captures the observed counts of hygiene-related words within online reviews received by restaurants on an ongoing basis, using supervised machine learning techniques. Subsequently, we estimate regression models that correlate the actual hygiene scores from inspections to word counts generated through the hygiene dictionary to establish that the word counts are an accurate and reasonable proxy for hygiene scores. Having established this correlation, it is then possible to use word counts to gain visibility into restaurant behavior with respect to hygiene. We then use word counts to identify restaurants that display behavior consistent with moral hazard, as well as those that retain their hygiene status even after inspections. Our work extends recent work (Kang et al. 2013, Schomberg et al. 2016) in three distinct ways. First, rather than manually specifying the semantic terms that should be associated with disease outbreaks, we create our dictionary using a rigorous methodology that is based on the text present in online reviews. Using supervised machine learning techniques to generate the words and phrases for the dictionary helps generate terms that are more representative of the colloquial and informal ways in which consumers discuss hygienerelated issues at restaurants, while also leveraging the power of large corpuses of text data now available (Gentzkow et al. 2017). Second, rather than focusing on developing prediction models for hygiene inspection scores per se, we focus on identifying shirking behavior. Hygiene inspections are complex activities requiring experience and training; we argue that actual inspections cannot be fully substituted with data from online reviews, given the inherent limitations of what consumers may be able to observe in restaurants and the subjectivity of the inspection process. Rather, we argue that social media data, when appropriately used, can help improve the efficiency of such inspection programs by identifying noncompliance. Finally, adopting an exploratory approach, we isolate several sources of heterogeneity, such as restaurant characteristics, economic indicators, and the specific format of the RIP, that help provide a more complete picture of hygiene inspections and moral hazard in this context.

Our analysis provides several insights relevant to the RIP program as well as the use of social media. First, as a baseline, we find that restaurants do indeed respond to the threat of regulatory action by significantly improving their hygiene scores; restaurants that score badly on their initial hygiene inspections are able to generate much higher scores on reinspections within the same inspection cycle. This finding is important because it shows that hygiene problems are rarely structural and can be corrected by restaurants, given the right incentives. Equally important, it helps validate the assumption that maintaining hygiene is costly in terms of effort; on exerting such effort, hygiene improves for all restaurants. Second, based on the dictionary word counts, we find that roughly 30% of all restaurants in NYC deteriorate in terms of their hygiene into what would be considered appropriate for a lower grade (i.e., B or C grade) within 90 days of certification, that is, behavior consistent with moral hazard. More practically, we find that the effectiveness of the NYC RIP program is likely overstated and that there continue to be public health risks in the city arising from hygiene violations. From a policy perspective, augmenting the hygiene inspections regime with information from online reviews would enhance the effectiveness of the RIP program while also allowing it to be generalizable to other urban areas, with appropriate modifications to suit local inspection processes.

Our research contributes to the literature in several ways. First, we add to the small but influential literature in the restaurant sector that provides evidence for how social media can be used to tackle policyoriented or socially relevant problems, thus responding to the call by Aral et al. (2013) to look beyond the forprofit sector in the use of online reviews. Second, our work adds to the literature that combines machine learning and economics (Athey 2015, Athey and Imbens 2006) to address social problems. Applications of machine learning within economics have become increasingly prevalent as instruments to identify or measure economic activity that is otherwise hard to study, such as poverty (Jean et al. 2016), demand estimation (Bajari et al. 2015), and new entry threats (Pan et al. 2018). We add to this literature by using machine learning techniques to help identify moral hazard through the development of a generalizable hygiene dictionary. Third, we contribute to ongoing work in the public health domain, where tackling the risks of foodborne illnesses remains a hard problem within urban areas such as NYC (Harrison et al. 2014) Although inspection regimes and random checks are effective in general, there still remain inefficiencies in terms of the extent to which hygiene is maintained across the city. Our work provides an example of how social media content can be used to identify gaps in the inspection regime, potentially improving the efficiency of the process while also providing an alternative signal of hygiene (Schomberg et al. 2016).

Because our work is closely linked to the structure of the NYC RIP program, we describe this setting in some detail next. Subsequently, we briefly review the relevant literature in moral hazard, online reviews, and public health from which we draw, before describing the analysis conducted.

## The NYC Restaurant Inspection Program

To operate a food service establishment in NYC, owners must have their facilities inspected and graded by the NYC Department of Health and Mental Hygiene (DOHMH) as part of the RIP. Food service establishments are defined as fixed-site food vendors, a category that includes restaurants, coffee shops, bars, nightclubs, and most cafeterias (New York City Department of Health and Mental Hygiene 2016c). The program excludes mobile food vending units or temporary food service establishments, such as food trucks, correctional facilities, and charitable organizations. Every food service establishment receives an unannounced random inspection at least once every 365 days from the DOHMH, which inspects over 20,000 establishments each year (New York City Department of Health and Mental Hygiene 2016a). The visit may take place anytime the establishment is open to the public or preparing food.

For each inspection, the inspector follows an established procedure to record in a mobile device the observed violations to the health code. Lower inspection scores show better adherence to the city’s health code. Each violation is associated with a range of points, which depends on the type of violation and the risk it presents to the potential consumer. At the end of the inspection, the points are summed, and the total becomes the final inspection score, which is publicly available. Scores with 13 or fewer points, 14–27 points, and 28 or more points result in A, B, and C grades, respectively (see Figure A1 in Online Appendix A). The grade cards are required to be displayed within 5 feet of the entrance of the restaurant.

An important element that makes the RIP distinct from others in the United States is its two-step inspection process (Ho 2012, New York City Department of Health and Mental Hygiene 2016a). Each restaurant is inspected through an inspection cycle, which begins with an initial inspection, but which can also include a reinspection to ensure that any problems identified in the earlier inspection (within the same cycle) have been corrected. If the initial inspection yields an A hygiene score, the establishment posts an “A” sticker and is not subject to a reinspection until the next inspection cycle (roughly 12 months later). However, if it receives a B or C score, the establishment is scheduled for reinspection within the same inspection cycle, to be completed within 3 months approximately. Meanwhile, the restaurant posts a “grade pending,” or “P,” sticker. The score generated from the reinspection, which may be an A, B, or C grade, must be posted immediately unless the restaurant requests a hearing at the NYC Health Administrative Tribunal. The underlying rationale is that restaurants can improve their hygiene processes by exerting the appropriate effort, and having achieved the required level of hygiene, such practices can be institutionalized within the restaurants so that there is no return to the earlier state of lower hygiene. Implicit here is the recognition that ensuring hygiene requires ongoing effort, which restaurants may be unwilling to invest in unless needed (Jin and Leslie 2003). Apart from this two-step inspection design, the program in NYC classifies hygiene violations as critical and noncritical, grades different conditions depending on the severity of violation, and is tasked with posting public grades on restaurants (details on the RIP are available in Online Appendix A).

The NYC RIP is generally perceived as a success by the public (Farley 2011). Consumer surveys find that 89% of New Yorkers consider grades before dining out, 91% approve of publicizing grades, and 77% feel more confident dining in an A-grade restaurant (Farley 2012). Furthermore, the program is credited with increasing the average level of hygiene within NYC restaurants. The number of A-grade restaurants in the city has steadily increased, based on data released by the city, during the period of the program. By 2015, almost 90% of all restaurants in NYC received A grades, ranging from 87.7% in Queens to 90.2% in Manhattan. These improvements are unrelated to local poverty rates, based on NYC Open Data (City of New York 2016), showing that the program is successful across the NYC area. However, although many restaurants eventually receive an A grade each inspection cycle, approximately 54% of such restaurants actually achieve initial inspection scores within an inspection cycle that would have resulted in a B or C grade. Thus, at any point in time, there were roughly 4,000 restaurants operating with a P grade (New York City Department of Health and Mental Hygiene 2016b).

Despite the apparent success of the program, media reports indicate that the inspection program may not be as successful as the high compliance statistics would suggest (Buiso 2014). Indeed, the high frequencies of A grades and the two-inspection regime allow restaurants with low hygiene practices to temporarily improve their hygiene levels until they are reinspected, after which they can choose to not continue exerting this effort. Thus, given the protection offered by an A hygiene grade, such restaurants effectively undercut hygiene requirements and display risky behavior—by definition, this is behavior reflecting moral hazard (Holmstrom¨ 1979). The negative effects of moral hazard in the context of hygiene inspections of food, produce, and service establishments have been studied in some detail in past work (Irwin et al. 1989, Buchholz et al. 2002). However, the availability of crowd-sourced data, in the form of online reviews, as well as text analysis methods provides the opportunity to identify instances where such ex post shirking behavior can be isolated. We thus briefly review the streams of research in information asymmetry, public health surveillance, and online reviews below before moving on to the research methodology.

## Theoretical Background Information Asymmetry and Moral Hazard in Restaurant Services

Markets for services are prone to significant information asymmetry, which results in adverse selection and moral hazard; that is, it is hard to gauge the true quality of the counterparty ex ante as well as difficult to ensure compliant behavior ex post (Akerlof 1970, Holmstrom¨ 1979). These market limitations are particularly pressing in certain industries where vital information is needed for transactions to be conducted, verifiable information is hard to procure, and the costs of ex post renegotiation and opportunism are very high (Shavell 1979, Stiglitz 2002). Certain industries are prone to problems that arise from moral hazard in particular, such as insurance (Rubinstein and Yaari 1983), retail (Klein et al. 2016), and healthcare (Gaynor et al. 2000). One particular context where moral hazard remains a critical problem, relevant to our work here, is in public health surveillance of food safety (Starbird 2005). As food products travel through the supply chain from the source of production, there are various points where problems of food quality and hygiene can manifest. The traditional responses to these issues have been based on inspections, certifications, and labeling practices across the stages that food traverses within this supply chain (Crespi and Marette 2001, Starbird and Amanor-Boadu 2007). The final step in this supply chain is the food service establishment (Angulo and Jones 2006), that is, the restaurant. Significant information asymmetry exists here too, in terms of hygiene and food quality, with noncompliance potentially leading to the spread of foodborne illnesses.

The CDC estimates that as a result of foodborne illness, 3,000 Americans die each year (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 2016a), with approximately 60% of cases estimated to be the result of food prepared at restaurants (Hedberg et al. 2006, Gould et al. 2013). Most municipal and local authorities have thus instituted regulatory processes and programs for inspecting and certifying the hygiene of restaurants. From a policy evaluation perspective, the benefits of such programs are clear and have been empirically shown to decrease foodborne illness outbreaks, particularly in cities where hygiene scores are publicized, such as in the NYC RIP program (Jin and Leslie 2003). However, inspecting restaurants is a costly and timeconsuming process, and real-time changes in hygiene quality are difficult to observe through infrequent inspections, especially because maintaining hygiene quality requires constant effort on the part of restaurant managers. Thus, with any certification scheme with imperfect information on postcertification compliance (Shapiro 1986), there always remains the possibility of moral hazard and shirking.

Prior research has studied the issue of information asymmetry and moral hazard associated with restaurant hygiene inspections and letter grades. Jin and Leslie (2009) studied hygiene grades in Los Angeles County restaurants and found that franchises tended to free ride on the hygiene reputations of the brand owner. However, reputational incentives, that is, the need to retain reputation within local markets, served as motivators to maintain hygiene, especially because these scores were publicly available. In earlier work, the authors showed that the number of foodborne illness–related hospitalizations decreased after the Los Angeles County restaurant hygiene inspection program posted hygiene letter grades to the public, again driven by local reputational incentives (Jin and Leslie 2003). Hygiene inspection programs pose a two-sided moral hazard problem—whereas restaurants may violate due process with respect to hygiene, it is also possible that inspectors may shirk in their inspecting effort and not report or detect all violations, as evidenced by research showing that inspectors who are new to the particular restaurant find between 12% and 17% more violations than repeat inspectors, potentially because new inspectors may have “fresh eyes” (Jin and Lee 2014a). In this paper, in contrast, we study moral hazard from the perspective of the restaurant rather than the inspector. In order to identify sources of moral hazard within this context, we build on research showing how machine learning techniques can be effectively used in studying economic activity, especially in the case of large corpuses of text (Gentzkow et al. 2017). We briefly review this literature next.

## The Use of Machine Learning in Studying Hygiene Inspections

Recent work in economics has highlighted the opportunities provided by developments in machine learning and the availability of large volumes on data in studying aspects of the economy that have remained relatively understudied because of lack of appropriate data (Athey 2017). The availability of big data of diverse forms, including text, images, finegrained transaction data, and mobile device data, provides the means to study economic constructs that were hard to measure previously, thereby allowing existing economic models to be augmented with such new measures (Mullainathan and Spiess 2017). Concurrent developments in statistical and machine learning techniques that address key technical challenges arising in such large data sets, such as dealing with high dimensionality and unstructured data (Einav and Levin 2014, Varian 2014, Kleinberg et al. 2015), have helped give machine learning “its own place in the econometric toolbox” (Mullainathan and Spiess 2017, p. 87). Recent applications of machine learning in addressing economic or policy problems include studying demand estimation through the use of newer methods on scanner data (Bajari et al. 2015), using patent text to develop new patent taxonomies within the United States Patent and Trademark Office (Balsmeier et al. 2018), and the use of satellite data and image processing to study poverty (Jean et al. 2016). Within the information systems (IS) literature, combining machine learning with econometric models has led to recent work studying new entry threats faced by incumbent firms using text from 10-K filings (Pan et al. 2018) and the identification of copycat behavior on mobile app stores using text and network data (Wang et al. 2018).

In the specific context of public health surveillance, scholars and policymakers have tried to avail of data sources that are not typically associated with inspection regimes and regulations as a way to develop alternative sources of information on hygiene and food quality and thereby reduce foodborne illness (Chan et al. 2011). One such source of alternative data that is widely available now, particularly for the restaurant sector, is social media. Because social media often contain information not captured through traditional channels and created in an extremely decentralized manner, they are often useful to public health agencies for surveillance activities beyond traditional forms of inspection and certification (Brownstein et al. 2009). HealthMap (2017), for example, is an openly available public health surveillance system that includes data from disparate sources including social media, such as public health officials, clinicians, and travelers, to produce a global view of continuing disease threats. The CDC has a similar program (the Foodborne Outbreak Online Database) to make disease outbreak surveillance system data available to the public. In the specific case of restaurant hygiene, foodborne disease epidemiologists have used online reviews to study how this information may augment existing routines to identify disease outbreaks, finding that many such instances could be identified, although only 3% were reported to the authorities formally for follow-up (Harrison et al. 2014). The authors conclude that social media data had considerable promise in helping identify potential foodborne disease outbreaks, especially in cases where the official reporting mechanism may be inadequate.

Inasmuch as foodborne illnesses are preceded by unhygienic conditions at restaurants, it may be possible to use social media data in conjunction with machine learning to address the issue of restaurant hygiene directly. The closest analogues to our work are those by Kang et al. (2013) and Schomberg et al. (2016). Kang et al. (2013) use a set of data from Yelp reviews of restaurants in Seattle to predict the hygiene scores received by the same restaurants. They use a diverse set of input variables, including review volume and rating, restaurant metadata, and lexical cues (selected set of unigrams and bigrams), and show good accuracy in the prediction task using these variables. Although a full set of phrases used is not provided by the authors, the selection of terms is driven by a series of auxiliary regression analyses rooted in identifying the best predictors in a relatively theory-agnostic manner. In contrast, Schomberg et al. (2016) develop prediction models for hygiene scores of the most obvious offenders in San Francisco, based on previous history, using a combination of restaurant metadata and keywords in their model. However, th authors manually select specific keywords generated from a perusal of the major health code violations associated with the inspection process. Thus, the terms selected are not generated from the reviews themselves, a process that may miss colloquial and informal terms used to describe unhygienic conditions within restaurants. Interestingly, Schomberg et al. (2016, p. 6) use only the top-ranked reviews for each restaurant in the model and focus on “high risk populations” of restaurants. In order to ease interpretation, the keywords are further condensed into three dimensions using principal component analysis—“reviewer sentiment,” “physical environment and vermin,” and “foodborne illness–related symptoms.” The overall objective in both papers is to predict hygiene scores such that regulators use the models to help identify restaurants where hygiene is problematic, allowing further remedial action. Further details on these papers are available in Online Appendix F.

Our work here differs from these papers, both methodologically and conceptually, in important ways. First, we do not manually identify keywords to be used in extracting information; rather, we allow a classifier for identifying hygiene-related issues to generate the appropriate dictionary. Furthermore, we use all reviews on all restaurants to do so, thereby mitigating any potential biases that may emerge from the high-risk populations. To the extent that we consider a census of restaurants in NYC, our approach is more applicable for regulators, who bear responsibility for the hygiene of all restaurants. Second, we contend that the prediction per se of hygiene scores from social media data should be only the first step in using this form of crowd-sourced data. The inspection of restaurants is a complex task performed by trained inspectors and extends beyond what may be visible to the consumer, such as the state of the kitchen and food preparation processes. The inspection process also entails subjective assessments of conditions within restaurants that are unlikely to be observed by average consumers who then provide information on social media. Thus, a focus on developing prediction models of hygiene inspections from publicly available data is, we believe, relatively misguided. Rather, social media and machine learning techniques are better used to augment empirical models of economic behavior such that inefficiencies in such models can be reduced.

In this particular case, social media data, appropriately captured, can be used to help identify gaps or inefficiencies in the inspection process by identifying noncompliant behavior consistent with moral hazard. Such noncompliant behavior can be exhibited not only by repeat offenders but also by capable restaurateurs who may experience one-off hygiene problems. Therefore, machine learning applied to online review data can represent an independent information system that generates information to be used in conjunction with actual inspections, rather than simply achieve better predictions per se, thus addressing a social and policy problem effectively. To achieve this goal, we use a hybrid approach that combines elements of machine learning and prediction modeling but embeds these in a more familiar regression framework that allows us to establish the partial correlation between the machine learning variables and restaurant hygiene, after partialing out the effects of other covariates (Frisch and Waugh 1933, Filoso 2013).This approach thus builds on recent work in economics (Athey 2017, Brynjolfsson and Mitchell 2017, Gentzkow et al. 2017) as well as in IS (Pan et al. 2018, Wang et al. 2018). Because we build our econometric models on prior IS research studying online reviews in retail settings, we briefly review this literature next.

## Online Reviews as Sources of Text Data

Online reviews have long since been accepted as a strategic resource for firms in the services sector, capturing elements of quality, reputation, consumer feedback, and market power (Dellarocas 2003, Duan et al. 2008, Li and Hitt 2008). Online reviews have also been associated with a host of offline business outcomes such as offline revenues (Duan et al. 2008), firm survival (Mejia et al. 2015), and even critical decisions such as the choice of physicians (Gao et al. 2012). Viewed as a data source, online reviews typically contain multiple pieces of information that can be used by consumers. First, the aggregate or individual ratings provided by reviewers, typically on a one-tofive-star scale, are available. Researchers have also used measures built on the flow of reviews, representing traffic or interest in the product or service being reviewed, such as the number of reviews provided in each time period (Dellarocas and Narayan 2006). Extant literature has used these two sources of variation extensively in evaluating the firm-level impacts of online reviews. A comprehensive review of the online reviews literature is outside the scope of this study but is available (Cheung and Thadani 2012). We briefly review the more recent (and more relevant to our study) work extracting information from the actual text of online reviews.

Despite representing a collective corpus of opinions, judgments, evaluations, and suggestions from consumers that are of considerable utility, the text of online reviews has often been overlooked, with some notable exceptions. The first efforts to study text were based on explaining firm performance. This stream of research has focused on understanding the effect of the review text on firm performance outcomes (i.e., sales, product rankings, and revenues) using the number of repeated words in a review (Berger et al 2010) or content and sentiment (Godes and Mayzlin 2004, Dellarocas et al. 2007, Ghose et al. 2012, Ludwig et al. 2013). Alternatively, others have focused on inferring product-, brand-, and market-level attributes from the corpus of reviews. Decker and Trusov (2010), for example, suggest a methodology to enable the estimation of the relative effect of product attributes and brand names on the overall evaluation of products by using text analysis to classify the pros and cons of each product, whereas Netzer et al. (2012) propose a combination of text mining and semantic network analysis to understand consumers’ associative network of products and understand the implied market structure. Archak et al. (2011) are at the intersection of these streams of research and attemp to infer the economic value of online reviews by identifying the weight that consumers put on individual evaluations and product features, as well as estimating the overall impact of the product features discussed in the review text on sales. Moreover, a related research stream in this literature examines the features of the review text that increase the helpfulness of a review or maximize the impact of reading a review on consumers (Cao et al. 2011, Ghose and Ipeirotis 2011, Lee and Bradlow 2011). It is increasingly accepted that the text represents a vital and credible source of information for the service provider (Cao et al. 2011) that may even reduce bias in online review platforms (Lee et al. 2015). Building on this work, we use the review text to create a hy giene dictionary, which can then be used to identify cases of moral hazard with respect to restaurant hygiene.

## Research Methodology

## Data Set and Exploratory Analysis

We start by constructing a composite data set from two sources. First, data on the NYC RIP were gathered through the NYC Open Data program, which makes public data generated by various NYC agencies available for public use (City of New York 2016). The data set contains information on all NYC restaurant inspections since the beginning of the program in 2010 and is updated monthly. Our data sample includes restaurant-specific information, such as the restaurant’s address, phone number, and cuisine; inspectionspecific information, such as the inspection date, type, resulting grade, and inspector ID;<sup>1</sup> and violationspecific information, such as the violation code, type, severity, and points.

We supplement the NYC Open Data information on each restaurant with online review data from Yelp, the leading online review platform for restaurants. Restaurants in the two data sets were matched using the restaurant address and telephone number, with over 95% matching between the two data sets. The data in Yelp contain more detailed restaurant characteristics, such as price point, average consumer rating, hours, and payment options. In addition, the reviews are time stamped, thereby allowing us to partition the review data by time over the period of analysis. Although the hygiene inspection data start in 2010, when the new inspection regime was implemented, we collect review data for NYC restaurants from their first occurrence on the platform, dating back to 2004. The variable descriptions for the composite data set are shown in Table 1. The data set includes 24,625 restaurants in total across the five NYC boroughs, whereas the reviews data set contains approximately 1.3 million individual reviews. We exclude reviews that are flagged as fraudulent by Yelp.com. Yelp has its own algorithm for identifying fake reviews, which we implicitly use and which has also been used to conduct analysis on fraudulent reviews (Luca and Zervas 2016).

We observe, as the DOHMH and several media outlets have asserted (Barron 2015, Park 2015), that approximately 90% of restaurants in the city achieve an A grade in hygiene inspections. Recall that the twostep inspection approach allows restaurants scoring in the B or C range to first post a P grade and then be assigned a final grade after reinspection within the same cycle. Therefore, some A-grade restaurants achieve an A grade on the initial inspection, whereas others first receive a P and then an A on reinspection. Table 2 presents a distribution of grades from all inspection cycles in our data set and shows that these two subgroups, which we call A and PA, represent 41.0% and 48.7% of the data, respectively. Because they constitute the majority of the data and are used as an indicator that the NYC RIP is working successfully, they are the focus of our analysis.

We first examine the differences in initial hygiene inspection scores received by these two groups. This baseline analysis is useful in establishing the magnitude of hygiene improvement that is within the capacity of PA restaurants. Such improvements demonstrate that hygiene violations are not structural to the restaurant but can be rectified through effort and investment. This is an important assumption in establishing moral hazard because it implies the existence of agency on the part of the restaurateur (Jin and Leslie 2003). As expected, we see a substantial difference in initial inspection scores between the A and PA subgroups across all inspection cycles $( \mathrm { m e a n } _ { \mathrm { A } } = 8 . 7 1 ;$ $\bar { \mathrm { m e a n } _ { \mathrm { P A } } } = 2 1 . 5 1 ; \ p \ < \bar { 0 } . 0 0 1$ ; lower scores represent better hygiene). Notably, the median score for PA restaurants is 26.5, near the threshold for the C grade (28 points). Yet, upon reinspection, these restaurants achieve scores that are statistically indistinguishable from A-subgroup restaurants $\mathrm { ( m e a n _ { A } = 8 . 7 1 ; m e a n _ { P A } = }$ $9 . 1 8 ; p = 0 . { \check { 7 } } 6 )$ . We also study changes in hygiene scores before and after receiving a P grade using a longitudinal differences-in-differences model (Bertrand et al. 2002) This model is presented in Online Appendix B and shows that the opportunity for reinspection results in an estimated reduction of 6.25 points in hygiene scores, after controlling for restaurant and inspection characteristics. These restaurants are therefore able to achieve high levels of hygiene quality despite significant violations in their initial inspections. This shows that hygiene inspection scores are important enough to incentivize remedial action on the part of restaurants and that such action is indeed feasible (Jin and Leslie 2009).

Indeed, we observe a clear dichotomy between A and PA patterns over multiple inspection cycles. To illustrate this, we focus on restaurants in the A and PA subgroups in the second inspection cycle and observe their performance in their third inspection cycle. We avoid using the first inspection cycle because for many restaurants this occurred during the early, evolving days of the program and may also represent learning on the part of the restaurant. Looking at later cycles will exclude many newer restaurants that have undergone fewer inspections. Among those 70.3% of restaurants that have undergone at least three inspection cycles, 85.5% of restaurants that achieve an A on initial inspection in the second cycle do so again in the third cycle; we refer to this subgroup as the AA subset. In contrast, 90.4% of restaurants that exhibit the PA pattern in the second cycle do so again in next cycle; we refer to this group as the PAPA subset. Therefore, for the majority of A restaurants, the focus on maintaining hygiene remains in place even between inspection cycles, whereas the majority of PA restaurants appear to revert in terms of their hygiene to a lower level at some point in the period between inspection cycles. The behavior of restaurants in the PAPA subgroup illustrated in these figures is indicative of moral hazard; that is, their behavior after inspection is not compliant with hygiene expectations from the awarded A grade. The dramatic difference between reinspection scores and initial inspection scores of the next cycle for the PAPA subgroup shows the potential social cost associated with moral hazard (Stiglitz 2010). Although these restaurants post a grade of A for approximately one year after reinspection, their hygiene practices are inconsistent with that grade for at least some of that time. We observe similar patterns between future sequential inspection cycles. The high cost of monitoring for hygiene (Akerlof 1970, Starbird 2005) prevents continuous monitoring, and repeated inspections are expensive as well. It is here that an information system can help provide visibility into the restaurant’s hygiene-related behavior between inspection cycles, thereby improving the efficiency of the inspection program (Holmstrom¨ 1979). As a first step, we propose using text from online reviews to construct a hygiene-related dictionary that can provide such visibility.

## Creating a Social Media–Sourced Hygiene Dictionary

Online data sources include many different kinds of information pertaining to service quality and firm performance (Dellarocas 2003, Abbasi and Chen 2008, Tetlock et al. 2008). Issues relating to hygiene may be included in online reviews in various forms, but extracting and validating such information are not trivial (Schomberg et al. 2016). One option here is to manually use a small set of lexical cues that may signal when a hygiene-related issue is present at a restaurant, similar to the approach taken by Schomberg et al. (2016) and Kang et al. (2013). However, as the volume of reviews increases and increasingly colloquial references to hygiene are used in reviews, the use of relatively unsupervised techniques for extracting lexical cues and keywords of relevance can provide greater visibility into restaurant hygiene. We adopt such an approach to construct a social media–sourced hygiene (SMASH) dictionary from online review text, consistent with best practice in dictionary construction for use in policymaking (Loughran and Mcdonald 2011, Young and Soroka 2012, Liu 2015).

We follow well-established procedures in creating the dictionary. The primary process behind the dictionary is to use a thesaurus to repeatedly augment an initial seed list of hygiene words based on synonym relationships. We perform this in a series of steps. First, we identify an initial list of negative and hygienerelated words using the naive Bayes classifier on the text of online reviews.<sup>2</sup> In order to reduce the possibility of bias and allay any concerns about endogeneity of word usage relative to the start of the RIP program, we use only online reviews that were provided before the onset of the inspection program providing publicly visible hygiene grades, that is, prior to 2010, to create the seed list. Second, we recruit subjects in Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk) outside the state of New York (to minimize potential confounds) to perform the labeling of the reviews. Finally, the initial seed list of hygiene words is repeatedly augmented based on synonym relationships in WordNet (Miller 1995, Hornik et al. 2016), a popular online dictionary and thesaurus. The process is repeated until no newer synonyms can be found. The dictionary is then manually curated to remove clear errors owing to homonyms, ensuring that no errors propagate in the validation process. For example, “roach” is short for “cockroach” (relevant) and synonymous with “Mexican valium” (not relevant). We describe these steps in detail.

## Building the List of Seed Words with the Naive Bayes Classi<sup>fi</sup>er

Our procedure for creating the SMASH dictionary is based on prior work in the sentiment- and opinionmining literature (Feldman 2013, Tsai et al. 2013, Liu 2015) with one important extension. In sentiment mining, the initial set of seed words is often manually specified (Hu and Liu 2004, Valitutti et al. 2004). However, manually specifying words for the hygiene context is less preferable compared with tonal sentiment, given the wide range of issues that show up in restaurants, and may be unduly influenced by the researcher’s own vocabulary or lack thereof. Indeed, poorly created seed lists lead to less accurate dictionaries (Tang et al. 2009, Wallach et al. 2009). To mitigate this potential bias, we generate the initial word list through the naive Bayes classifier, a simple and effective machine learning technique to identify the initial word list in a data-driven manner (Li 2010 Liu 2015).

We first introduce some notation that will help facilitate discussion of the naive Bayes classifier (Hand et al. 2001, Tang et al. 2009). Suppose that we are given a training data set with n documents that are labeled by their hygiene polarity, or $d _ { j } ,$ defined as a binary variable denoting whether a review (docu ment) $j = 1 , . . . , n$ is discussing hygiene negatively. Let $w _ { j k }$ denote the number of times word $k \overset { \cdot } { = } 1 , . . . , p$ occurs in review $j ,$ where $p$ is the number of unique words appearing in all reviews. The naive Bayes classifier estimates the probability that each document discusses hygiene negatively based on the word occurrences, that is, $P ( d _ { j } = \bar { 1 } | w _ { j 1 } , w _ { j 2 } , . . . , w _ { j p } )$ . Through an application of Bayes rule,

$$
\begin{array}{c} {P (d _ {j} = 1 | w _ {j 1}, w _ {j 2}, \ldots , w _ {j p})} \\ {= \frac {P (w _ {j 1} , w _ {j 2} , \ldots , w _ {j p} | d _ {j} = 1) P (d _ {j} = 1)}{P (w _ {j 1} , w _ {j 2} , \ldots , w _ {j p})}.} \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

However, in practice, calculating the joint distributions requires an unrealistically large amount of data (Hand et al. 2001). To overcome this issue, the joint distribution is simplified under the assumption of conditional independence

$$
P (w _ {j 1}, w _ {j 2}, \ldots , w _ {j p} | d _ {j} = 1) = \prod_ {k = 1} ^ {p} P (w _ {j k} | d _ {j} = 1).\tag{2}
$$

By the law of total probability (Ross 1996), the probability that a document discusses hygiene negatively based on the word occurrences can be expressed as

$$
\begin{array}{l} P (d _ {j} = 1 | w _ {j 1}, w _ {j 2}, \ldots , w _ {j p}) \\ = \frac {\prod_ {k = 1} ^ {p} P (w _ {j k} | d _ {i} = 1) P (d _ {j} = 1)}{\prod_ {k = 1} ^ {p} P (w _ {j k} | d _ {i} = 1) P (d _ {j} = 1) + \prod_ {k = 1} ^ {p} P (w _ {j k} | d _ {i} = 0) P (d _ {j} = 0)}, \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

where $P ( w _ { j k } | d _ { j } = 1 )$ and $P ( w _ { j k } | d _ { j } = 0 )$ can easily be calculated by inspecting how often the kth word appears in documents that are labeled as discussing hygiene negatively $( d _ { j } = 1 )$ and those that are not $( d _ { j } = 0 ;$ Hand et al. 2001). Thus, the conditional independence assumption is a fundamental one that defines the naive Bayes classifier. Though from a probabilistic perspective the conditional independence assumption is usually not strictly valid, the performance of the naive Bayes classifier in various machine learning contexts, particularly with text data, has been widely demonstrated (McCallum and Nigam 1998, Sebastiani 2002, Go et al. 2009). We explore this issue in detail with a benchmarking study comparing the naive Bayes classifier with other machine learning methods, described in Online Appendix C. We find that the naive Bayes classifier is the most accurate on our data set among competing machine learning methods for text classification. After estimating the naive Bayes classifier (described in the next section), we sort all words by their estimated $P ( w _ { j k } | d _ { j } = 1 )$ and keep the top 5% as the initial seed list to ensure that seed words chosen are strongly associated with negative discussion of hygiene in the online reviews. The selection of the 5% threshold is based on best practice and is recommended in large data sets to maintain tractability (Loughran and Mcdonald 2011). Words outside the 5% level are typically phrases composed of root words that make up the seed list.

## Obtaining Training Data and Implementation

We begin with a data set containing the text of online reviews for NYC restaurants from Yelp, matched with those in the inspections data set based on name, address, and phone number, yielding a composite data set with inspection data, restaurant metadata, and reviews. We perform standard preprocessing of all review text, such as moving to lower case, removing stopwords, and stemming (Hornik et al. 2016). In order to identify documents in which hygiene is discussed negatively (i.e., lower hygiene ratings from consumers), we first randomly sample 1,200 restaurants with high inspection scores, which are most likely to have hygiene problems, and then randomly select one document (review) for each restaurant. The next task is to manually assign a label regarding the hygiene relation of each document, for which we recruited 1,200 subjects from MTurk for pay. MTurk subjects have been shown to represent the broader population (Buhrmester et al. 2011) and be reliable for experimental research (Goodman et al. 2013). Each subject was asked, “Given a restaurant review, answer questions about whether a review indicates problems related to hygiene,” and was then presented a single document (review). After reading the review, the subject indicated whether it was related to the hygiene of the restaurant using a sevenpoint scale adapted from previous work (Egan et al. 2007). Subjects were also asked to select the type(s) of hygiene problem(s) described (such as food preparation or cleanliness). Finally, to ensure high-quality responses, we discarded responses from subjects that took a large amount of time to finish to the task (>15 minutes), which has been shown to decrease subject’s attention span and the quality of responses (Buhrmester et al. 2011). Of 1,200 documents sampled, we received correct responses for 1,191 documents, of which 15% were labeled as relating to restaurant hygiene $( d _ { j } = 1 )$

These 1,191 training documents, their MTurkgenerated labels $d _ { j } ,$ , and the word occurrences $w _ { j k }$ allow us to calculate $P ( w _ { j k } | d _ { j } = 1 )$ , which forms the basis of the seed word list for the SMASH dictionary. By expanding the seed list using the synonym approach described earlier, we created a hygiene dictionary based on social media. The entire process was performed using single words, two-word phrases, and three-word phrases, that is, n-grams of up to order three (Lodhi et al. 2002), so the final dictionary contains single words along with two- and three-word phrases. For example, as a result of using n-grams, phrases such as “barely edible” were retained in our dictionary even though the individual words “barely” and “edible” were not included. This strategy is recommended in recent work in this literature, advising authors to move beyond word-level analysis, because this tends to oversimplify language (Cambria et al. 2013). Therefore, we extend the bulk of existing work that relies only on dictionaries based on individual words [see Liu (2015) and references therein]. Furthermore, compared with the set of keywords in Schomberg et al. (2016), our dictionary generated a richer and more varied set of terms associated with hygiene. Whereas some words are common, such as “vomit,” “diarrhea,” and “nausea,” our dictionary also included several phrases that, on first glance, may not appear relevant if manual curating of words associated with foodborne diseases were used, such as “pungency” and “wiping nose.” For simplicity, we refer to all phrases as “words.”

A subset of words in the final SMASH dictionary is shown in Table A10 in Online Appendix F. We chose not to display all the words from our dictionary because of space limitations and to avoid the strategic use of these words in reviews to create false or fraudulent impressions of hygiene concerns (Luca and Zervas 2016). Use of the dictionary to identify instances of hygiene violations relies on keeping the specific words within the dictionary private so that restaurants or individuals can rely on the unbiased use of these words and phrases in reviews. Further refinements to the dictionary, as well as systematically revalidating it at regular time intervals, would ensure that it is not used in fraudulent ways.

Once the directory has been created, we can use it to assign measures to each restaurant based on its reviews. For a given document (review), the SMASH score is defined as the total number of times words from the SMASH dictionary appear in the document. Letting $\delta _ { k } = 1$ if word k is included in the SMASH dictionary and 0 otherwise, the SMASH score for document j is $\begin{array} { r } { W C _ { j } = \sum _ { k } w _ { j k } \delta _ { k } , } \end{array}$ , where $w _ { j k }$ is the number of times word k appears in documen $j .$ The SMASH scores for a particular restaurant and time period can be created by summing over the reviews published in that time period for that restaurant. These scores effectively become alternate signals of restaurant hygiene. As a test of validity, we next check whether the SMASH scores developed in this way correlate well with the actual hygiene inspection scores.

## Regression Analysis

We present our statistical analyses in two stages. In the first stage, we validate the dictionary by studying the correlation between the word counts and actual observed inspection scores. Because the word counts and hygiene inspection scores are both reflective of the same underlying latent restaurant hygiene status that is not directly observable, it would follow that the two constructs should be positively correlated. We first examine the estimation of this correlation using multivariate regression analyses. In the second stage, we use the word counts to detect instances of moral hazard by identifying restaurants that appear to have regressed in their hygiene after receiving an A grade.

## Validating the SMASH Dictionary

A first step in validating the dictionary is to establish that the SMASH word counts are indeed correlated with the hygiene inspection score, even though these two variables are not directly related to each other. In order to calculate this correlation, we need the SMASH score for a restaurant in the time period immediately preceding the hygiene inspection. Therefore, we calculate the SMASH score for the restaurant in the month immediately prior to the date of the hygiene inspection. The univariate correlation between the SMASH word count and the hygiene score is 0.65, significant at $p <$ 0.05. However, there are several other sources of information about the restaurant that may also render significant correlations with the resulting hygiene score, and it is important to partial out the common correlational components that these other covariates have with the inspection score as well as the SMASH word count, as described by the Frisch–Waugh– Lovell theorem (Lovell 1963, Angrist and Pischke 2009). In other words, if the information present in the SMASH word counts is available in some other covariates that are easily observed, then the incremental signal value provided by the SMASH word counts is trivial. If this is the case, the correlation between the SMASH word count and inspection score, with other variables partialed ${ \mathrm { o u t } } ,$ should be no different from zero, based on recent work on regression anatomies by Angrist and Pischke (2009). In order to estimate this correlation where the explanatory power of other covariates has been fully captured, we use a regression framework, even though we do not posit a causal relationship in the regression specification per se. The value of the regression framework is to provide us with a simple and easy interpretable way to examine the correlation between the SMASH word counts and inspection score, net of the influence of other covariates.

More formally, we use a longitudinal mixed-effects regression model (Liang and Zeger 1986) to estimate the association between SMASH word counts and hygiene inspection scores. Because most restaurants in our sample are inspected multiple times over the study period, the unit of analysis is restaurant inspection. As described earlier, for each restaurant and inspection event, we use the SMASH score for that restaurant in the time period immediately preceding the inspection as the independent variable, whereas the dependent variable is the actual inspection score, using months as the unit of time.<sup>3</sup> Let t index monthyear time periods, and let $I N S _ { i , t }$ be the numerical hygiene inspection score for restaurant $i ,$ if that restaurant was inspected during time period t. If such an inspection is a reinspection within an inspection cycle, $R e I n s _ { i , t }$ equals 1, and it equals 0 otherwise. Let $W C _ { i , t - 1 }$ be the SMASH score for restaurant i based on reviews published on Yelp during time period $t - 1 ,$ the primary independent variable of interest. We estimate the following regression model:

$$
\begin{array}{r} I N S _ {i, t} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} W C _ {i, t - 1} + \beta_ {2} R a t i n g _ {i, t - 1} + \beta_ {3} R e v i e w s _ {i, t - 1} \\ + \beta_ {4} R e I n s _ {i, t} + \gamma R _ {i} + \delta I _ {i, t} + \alpha t + \varepsilon_ {i, t}, \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

where $R a t i n g _ { i , t - 1 }$ and $R e v i e w s _ { i , t - 1 }$ are the average numerical rating and number of reviews published on Yelp for restaurant i during time period $t - 1$ , and $R _ { i }$ and γ are restaurant-level indicator variables and fixed effects, respectively, which capture heterogeneity among restaurants.<sup>4</sup> Similarly, $I _ { i , t }$ and $\delta$ are inspector-level indicator variables and fixed effects, respectively, included to account for the influence of individual inspectors (Jin and Lee 2014b). Finally, we account for linear time trends.<sup>5</sup> The objective is to partial out the effects of these covariates, thereby establishing the correlation between the SMASH word counts and hygiene inspections. For estimation, we exclude restaurants that have undergone only one inspection, as well as inspections with fewer than five Yelp reviews for the same restaurant in the previous time period. The final data set contains 21,488 unique restaurants with 136,503 inspections in a 5-year period.

We first estimate a linear base model (Table 3, Model 1), including a number of covariates from prior research that have been shown to explain hygiene inspection scores (Kang et al. 2013). Our logic is that if all the information contained in the SMASH word counts was already captured by other factors, then our estimates would be close to zero. However, in our models, our coefficients are consistently far from zero. To emphasize the noncausal nature of our results, we report the coefficient estimate and the standard error in parenthesis in this section. Indeed, we observe that the coefficient for Rating is negative, <sup>−</sup>0.36 (0.14), indicating that negative online reviews are associated with higher (worse) inspection scores, as found previously by Schomberg et al. (2016). More pertinent to our analysis, the part correlation between SMASH word counts and the hygiene score is positive, 0.59 (0.21), after partialing out the effects of all the included covariates including the average rating and number of reviews, as well as restaurant-level fixed effects, inspector-level fixed effects, and linear time trends that could be associated with both the SMASH word counts and hygiene inspections (Filoso 2013). The resulting correlation we obtain indicates that there is a specific common component between inspection scores and the SMASH word counts, which is what we would expect because both signals are reflective of the underlying state of restaurant hygiene. We also see evidence for the argument that the SMASH dictionary is valid and captures elements of restaurant hygiene, despite the limited access that consumers have into many aspects of a restaurant’s operations relative to inspectors. Because hygiene inspection scores range from 0 to 90, we also estimate a Tobit regression of the model in Equation (4) to account for truncation in the inspection scores. ## Robustness Tests

We explore three alternative models to estimate the correlation between the SMASH word counts and hygiene inspectors as a way to establish the robustness of the estimated correlation. First, as Schomberg et al. (2016) point out, consumers may not be able to spot all types of hygiene violations. For example, consumers would not be expected to accurately report on the cleanliness of the kitchen, which could only be observed through full access to the restaurant premises. Generally, critical violations related to issues such as meat handling, kitchen cleanliness, and food preparation processes would not be directly observable by consumers, unlike noncritical violations, which include unclean bathrooms and dining areas. Each inspection has a score for critical violations and a score for noncritical violations, with the total score being the sum of both. This distinction provides us with a plausible test for falsifiability; that is, if the SMASH word counts are indeed reflective of restaurant hygiene as reported by average consumers, these word counts should have no systematic correlations with critical violation scores that are not observable by the same consumers. We therefore refit model (4) using critical violation scores as the dependent variable, and separately using noncritical violation scores, using a linear specification as before The results are presented in Table 4, with Model 1A based on critical violations and Model 2A based on noncritical violations. As expected, SMASH word counts are not strongly correlated with critical inspection scores, 0.94 (1.22), but remain correlated with noncritical violations, 0.43 (0.17). Tobit regression for the same models shows similar results. These results, beyond providing a falsifiability test, also emphasize the argument that although crowdsourced reviews are useful as complements to tra ditional inspections, they cannot and should not be viewed as substitutes. Furthermore, they have limited value in signaling the presence of critical violations within restaurants, which are more likely to be the drivers of foodborne illnesses. We depart from prior research in this domain (Kang et al. 2013, Schomberg et al. 2016) on this subtle but important point.

Second, thus far we have considered only a linear relationship between word counts and inspection scores. We now consider a possible nonlinear relationship between word counts and aggregate inspection scores (critical and noncritical) by including a squared term as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{r} I N S _ {i, t} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} W C _ {i, t - 1} + \beta_ {2} W C _ {i, t - 1} ^ {2} + \beta_ {3} R a t i n g _ {i, t - 1} \\ + \beta_ {4} R e v i e w s _ {i, t - 1} + \beta_ {5} R e I n s _ {i t} + \pmb {\gamma} \pmb {R} _ {i} + \pmb {\delta} \pmb {I} _ {i t} \\ + \alpha t + \varepsilon_ {i t}. \end{array}\tag{5}
$$

The results from this analysis, shown in Table 3, are similar to the results from above. The coefficient of the word counts is 0.43 (0.15) for the base ordinary least squares (OLS) model (Table 3, Model 3) and 0.58 (0.21) for the Tobit model (Table 3, Model 4). However, the squared term is near zero in both models, supporting the expectation of a linear correlation between SMASH word counts and inspection scores.

We also investigate whether the occurrence of SMASH words results from their being used in previously written reviews for the same restaurant by other consumers, because if such a pattern exists, the resulting SMASH word count may correspond to herding rather than actual observations of poor hygiene. To examine this issue statistically, for every restaurant and SMASH term combination, we test for potential serial correlation of each SMASH dictionary term at the individual review and daily resolutions; details of these tests are provided in Online Appendix E. Briefly, these tests show that any correlations of word usage across consumers for the same restaurant are not statistically distinguishable from pure chance.

Thus, we conclude that serial correlation and herding behavior of use of specific words across time is unlikely to be a significant issue in our data.

Finally, review sentiment may be associated with inspection scores, as observed by Kang et al. (2013). If sentiment is associated with SMASH word counts and is also correlated with hygiene scores, it is necessary to partial out the influence of sentiment on the correlation between SMASH word counts and inspection scores. We therefore add sentiment, averaged over reviews in time period t  1, as an additional covariate in the regression analysis.<sup>6</sup> We find that review sentiment is highly correlated with the average rating, as one might expect, raising the po tential issue of multicollinearity; therefore, we first estimate our model using sentiment but not numerical rating (Table 5, Model 1) and then both sentiment and numerical rating (Table 5, Model 3). We find that the correlation coefficient between SMASH word counts and inspection scores remains consistent even after including sentiment, indicating that the SMASH word counts do provide additional information not provided by sentiment.

To summarize, we find a robust and positive correlation between SMASH word counts and hygiene scores after partialing out the explanatory influence of restaurant-specific covariates as well as restaurant, inspector, and time effects, as would be theoretically warranted. We present these in an analysis-of-variance framework that typically assumes causal relationships between the independent and dependent variables. ## Using the SMASH Dictionary to Identify Moral Hazard

We use restaurant-specific SMASH word counts, aggregated by day, to track the hygiene of NYC restaurants in the time between hygiene inspection cycles, a period of approximately 12–15 months. Recall that, as illustrated in Figures 1 and 2, our exploratory analysis suggests that PAPA restaurants shirk on hygiene at some point during this period. The data used to generate Figures 1 and 2 were based on connecting inspection results for the same restaurant (or group of restaurants) across the inspection data set. In reality, the average customer or inspector cannot observe the actual hygiene within restaurants during the period between inspections. Here we propose to connect the dots between inspections by using the SMASH word counts as a proxy for the hygiene of restaurants between inspections.

## Discussion and Conclusion

In most marketplaces characterized by information asymmetry, the prevalence of moral hazard causes significant inefficiency. These pathologies are particularly problematic in contexts where the eventual result may be an increased incidence of foodborne illnesses, as in the food preparation industry, where maintaining hygiene is of particular importance (Hedberg et al. 2006, Gould et al. 2013). Traditiona techniques to detect and prevent moral hazard, such as rigorous inspections and a strong set of incentives, contribute toward decreasing these inefficiencies in the market. However, consistent with recent work, we believe that techniques of text analysis, within the domain of machine learning, alongside access to crowd-sourced data from online review platforms such as Yelp, can further enhance the efficacy of hygiene inspections (Schomberg et al. 2016, Athey 2017). In this paper, we present a hybrid approach, combining machine learning with analysis-of-variance techniques applied to online review text (Mullainathan and Spiess 2017), to analyze the effects of moral hazard in a restaurant hygiene inspection program in NYC which provides enough incentives for restaurants to improve their hygiene performance prior to receiving a hygiene sticker but appears limited in its ability to provide ongoing monitoring. To surmount these limitations, we devise a proxy “information system” for hygiene using online reviews provided by restaurant consumers over time, thereby allowing for a more continuous form of monitoring of restaurant hygiene Because foodborne illness remains one of the top public health challenges for the city, the state, and the entire country (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 2016b), our approach helps inform city authorities managing inspection programs on how they may improve the design and efficacy of such programs.

Our results indicate that the NYC RIP may be underperforming in the manner in which it communicates inspection results to consumers, and thereby ensures a socially optimal level of hygiene within the city, in several ways. First, by publicly posting a P grade without translating the current grade to a B or C that consumers can understand, the program allows for the presence of opportunism, whether intentional or otherwise. Short-term compliance is easy but allows relatively costless shirking behavior soon thereafter. Without a penalty for this initial lower performance, the incentives for moral hazard remain unchecked. Second, by only showing the most recent inspection cycle result instead of the trajectory (or previous history), the program reinforces a short-term perspective, as shown in our analysis. Indeed, certain municipal authorities are changing this approach; Louisville, Kentucky, for instance, requires restaurants to show the last three scores (regardless of the hygiene grade) in addition to the current one, thereby creating incentives to remain compliant over time and arguably translating inspection results more effectively to consumers.<sup>9</sup> Finally, by scheduling reinspection times for regular and critical offenders using the same system again reduces the incentive to comply.<sup>10</sup> Even in 90 days after inspection, we observe variations in the segments of restaurants that appear to reverse their hygiene standards. Thus, using prior scores in scheduling repeat inspections could be useful in incentivizing compliance. Furthermore, in the presence of critical violations, restaurants face an additional random check, beyond the regular schedule. To the extent that these additional steps are able to reduce the observed moral hazard, inspection effort and resources are likely to be more effectively utilized.

The increasing prevalence of social media data actually enhances the practical implications of this work for restaurants and for-profit firms. Investors and restaurant franchises may benefit by being able to design franchising contracts that potentially incorporate clauses based on social media discourse and online review data so as to avoid the typical ex post contracting problems that emerge in such settings (Lafontaine 1992). Finally, restaurants themselves would benefit from monitoring social media discourse. Although restaurants are likely to be intimately aware of their own hygiene issues, it is still useful for them to realize the extent to which such issues are discussed in the public domain. Interestingly, although our dictionary was crafted based on reviews in NYC, in robustness tests reported earlier, we show how our dictionary is successful in explaining hygiene inspections in Boston (see Online Appendix C), where a different inspection program is used, showing the generalizability of the approach.

Beyond the policy and managerial implications discussed earlier, our work adds to the current literature in IS research on the use of social media in part by responding to the sentiment expressed by Aral et al. (2013) that social media and user-generated content can, and should, be used in addressing pressing social problems. Whether it is the use of online reviews and social media data in medicine (Gao et al. 2012), the study of elections and politics (Wattal et al. 2010), or disaster recovery (Oh et al. 2010), we believe that the potential for crowd-sourced data from social media platforms within the nonprofit and policy sectors is enormous. Public health is one such context where social media can be particularly useful for two reasons. First, the use of online reviews and social media within the important restaurant sector is already the default, with most consumers not only consuming this information but also contributing (Lu et al. 2013b). Second, restaurants and other stakeholders have recognized the influence that social media discourse exerts, thereby creating a positive virtuous cycle that can be used for the greater good. Though our work here is focused on the NYC market, the larger contribution of our work is to provide a set of statistical and machine learning models that can be replicated elsewhere, as well as provide the dictionary of hygiene, which may be used in other policy and regulatory recommendations (Bichler et al. 2010). Although we focus on Yelp here, the described process can also easily be extended to data from other sources, such as Facebook and Twitter, as well as other review sites such as TripAdvisor and OpenTable, speaking to the generalizability of our work

Our work also responds to the call issued by Athey (2017) for more work at the intersection of machine learning and economics. Traditional economic modeling has postulated the effects of many factors that affect markets, such as moral hazard, adverse selection, and shirking (Akerlof 1970, Holmstrom¨ 1979), but empirical approaches to identify these have often been limited by the unavailability of data (Jean et al. 2016, Kleinberg et al. 2017). Even within the IS literature, machine learning applied to text and images from social media is leading to newer forms of measurement and testing (Pan et al. 2018, Wang et al. 2018). Interestingly, social media platforms have contributed to the enhanced efficiency of markets by explicitly addressing some of these traditional pathologies, for instance, by providing decentralized institutions that measure reputation (Dellarocas 2003, Moreno and Terwiesch 2014), improve transparency (Luca and Zervas 2016), and enhance trust (Duan et al. 2008). We see our work as continuing on this path.

Finally, we note that our approach based on using social media to identify instances of market inefficiency contributes to ongoing work fostering information technology innovations in the public sector (Kankanhalli et al. 2017), particularly in the public health domain, where tackling the risks of foodborne illnesses remains a hard problem within large urban areas such as NYC (Harrison et al. 2014). Inasmuch as such hybrid econometric approaches provide better outcomes for regulatory agencies and city authorities, we also believe that caution must be exercised in understanding how such hybrid methods may inform policy and the potential for unintended and negative consequences. We support the use of hybrid methods combining predictive and analysis-of-variance models in making the hygiene inspection process more efficient, as do Harrison et al. (2014), who propose incorporating online reviews directly into the scheduling of inspectors. However, such an approach would make online review platforms endogenous to the inspection process and likely incentivize a new breed of fraudulent reviews, effectively reducing the efficacy of online reviews as a monitoring tool. Our objective here is to show how such hybrid methods can be complements to traditional inspection methods, but a detailed analysis of the trade-offs inherent in including such methods into official processes is needed. Platform owners such as Yelp are important parts of this ecosystem and should be included in all such evaluations because they control the data and are accepted by consumers and merchants alike.

In summary, in this paper, we use a combination of supervised machine learning and analysis-of-variance techniques to devise a proxy information system for hygiene using online reviews provided by restaurant consumers over time. Through the creation of a hygiene-related dictionary, we show how a more continuous form of monitoring of restaurant hygiene may be achieved, thereby identifying, and hopefully reducing, behavior associated with moral hazard. We also show the efficacy of this approach in the context of the NYC RIP, providing evidence for how the success of the program may be overstated. Our work contributes to the emerging literature on how social media content and machine learning can be used in hybrid approaches to address persistent social issues in an effective yet scalable manner (Kang et al. 2013, Athey 2017).
