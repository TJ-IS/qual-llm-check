# Strategic scanning project failure and abandonment factors: lessons learned

Nicolas Lesca<sup>1,2</sup> and Marie-Laurence Caron-Fasan<sup>1</sup>

<sup>1</sup>IAE, Grenoble University, Grenoble, France; <sup>2</sup>Grenoble Business School, Grenoble, France

Correspondence: Nicolas Lesca, IAE, Grenoble University, BP 47, 38040 Grenoble Cedex 9, France. Tel: þ 33 (0)4 76 82 59 27; Fax: þ 33 (0)4 76 82 59 99

## Abstract

This article aims to explore factors contributing to the failure and abandonment of strategic scanning projects and operational systems. Thirty-nine projects that were studied using action research methods are analyzed. The results suggest that failure and abandonment are provoked by combinations of factors relating to stakeholders’ qualifications and experience, to the management and organization of the project system, as well as to strategic alignment and changes in the organization’s internal structure. These factors include poor project impetus, uninvolved management, unqualified people, inaccurate expectations, project mismanagement, strategy misalignment, poor participation, hostile culture, insufficient budget, conflating technical and managerial problems, previous project trauma and underestimated complexity. European Journal of Information Systems (2008) 17, 371–386. doi:10.1057/ejis.2008.21; Published online 2 September 2008

Keywords: strategic scanning; project failure factors; project abandonment factors; system abandonment factors; action research

## Introduction

A company’s ability to survive partly depends on its ability to anticipate external change and take it into account when defining the strategic targets it would like to pursue (Choo, 2001). It needs to be able to acquire, distribute and above all analyze information relating to the current state and evolution of its socio-economic environment. The implementation of an environmental scanning or strategic scanning (S.Scan) system is a response to that need. This calls for the organization to implement and operate a system for collecting, selecting and storing information, and then analyzing it for distribution.

S.Scan can serve different, complementary goals. Prior to constructing the strategy, it can be a means of keeping informed of market developments and trends (Hambrick, 1982), identifying and analyzing present and future constraints, threats and opportunities in the external environment (El Sawy, 1985; Hubert & Daft, 1987), understanding forces of change (Choo, 2001) and reducing risks linked to uncertainty (May et al., 2000). After the strategy has been constructed, S.Scan can also be a means of identifying changes in the environment, its new contingencies and possible discontinuities (Ansoff, 1975), developing an organization’s ability to react quickly, adapting its strategy at the right moment (Hambrick, 1981) and securing or improving its future position (Choo, 1997). More generally, S.Scan can be a support activity that serves the strategy and contributes to value creation and organization durability.

Research in the fields of strategic management and information systems stresses the importance of S.Scan for organizations whose environments are perceived as complex, dynamic and turbulent (El Sawy, 1985). Several studies have attempted to draw up a state-of-the-art on scanning practices and establish the existence of a link between scanning and strategy (Fahey & King, 1977; Jain, 1979; Thomas, 1980; Fahey et al., 1981; Choo, 1997; Julien et al., 1999). Several studies have reported a strong correlation between an organization’s performance and its ability to implement an environmental scanning system (Vandenbosh & Huff, 1997; Kumar et al., 2001). But S.Scan projects are seldom studied (Ghoshal & Westney, 1991).

In practice, implementing a S.Scan system raises difficulties (Diffenbach, 1983). Practitioners wishing to implement S.Scan often perceive the task as a difficult one (Gibbons & Prescott, 1996). Sometimes they do not even know how to go about it or where to start (Aaker, 1983; Hall, 2001; Albright, 2004). Many S.Scan projects fail in terms of deadline, cost, goal attainment and benefits for the organization. Some projects are abandoned along the way. S.Scan systems are also sometimes abandoned after being implemented. Being aware of potential failure factors from the outset could potentially help stakeholders anticipate project risks and increase the likelihood of success.

The aim of this exploratory research is to study factors contributing to the failure and abandonment of S.Scan projects and systems. First we outline characteristics of the S.Scan projects at the center of this study. Next we examine previous research on factors contributing to the failure and abandonment of information systems (IS) projects. We then present the methodology and context of our action research, which was based on data extracted from 39 S.Scan projects in which the authors intervened. Next we discuss the results, their implications and limitations. We hope this exploratory study will provide information that may help researchers and practitioners better understand why S.Scan projects fail. The study’s results can also make practitioners more aware of how they can avoid failure and improve S.Scan development productivity.

## Literature background

## Previous review on Strategic Scanning

This article deals with environmental scanning and strategic scanning. ‘Environmental scanning is the acquisition and use of information about events, trends, and relationships in an organization’s external environment, the knowledge of which would assist management in planning the organization’s future course of action’ (Aguilar, 1967, p. 1). To define strategic scanning, El Sawy (1985, p. 53) uses Aguilar’s definition of environmental scanning word for word. ‘Strategic scanning refers to the acquisition of information about events, trends and relationships in an organization’s environment, the knowledge of which would be of assistance to top executives in identifying and understanding strategic threats and opportunities.’ For the sake of simplicity, we will use the term strategic scanning (S.Scan) for the rest of the article.

There are several S.Scan information typologies in existence. For example, a distinction is made between retrospective, current and prospective information according to whether it relates to the past, present or future (Fahey et al., 1981). It is liable to draw managers’ and organizations’ attention to plausible change (the expression ‘early warning’ is used as well), and help them understand and anticipate these changes (El Sawy, 1985; Gilad, 2003).

The scanning is sometimes focused on a single facet of the organization’s external environment, as in the case of technological scanning, competitor intelligence, competitive intelligence, etc. Analyzing only one of these facets is not in itself enough to help management anticipate current and future change and direct the organization’s future course of action. S.Scan therefore involves monitoring the whole of the organization’s socio-economic environment (Choo, 2001).

S.Scan can be outsourced (Choudhury & Sampler, 1997). In that case, the organization purchases analyses and services, or subscribes to periodicals or newsletters that are more or less personalized according to its needs and/or profile (Ghoshal & Westney, 1991). S.Scan can be a personal, individual approach (El Sawy, 1985). In that case, a manager acquires information for his own needs. S.Scan can be an internal organizational process (Aguilar, 1967). In that case ‘it is an organized way of looking at the world outside the company so that planners can anticipate new opportunities and impending dangers soon enough to deal with them effectively [y] You might say it is the radar that informs the pilot of conditions which are likely to be encountered’ (Pearce et al., 1982, p. 27).

S.Scan is often an informal, unstructured activity; individuals acquire information in the course of their daily activities, and then communicate and discuss it informally (Aguilar, 1967). Many organizations that have chosen internal S.Scan create centralized Environmental Scanning Units made up of specialists in research, analysis and information distribution (Fahey & King, 1977; Thomas, 1980; Stubbart, 1982; Diffenbach, 1983; Engledow & Lenz, 1985). Other organizations choose to implement a collective, reticulated S.Scan system composed of a steering committee, a network of internal and/or external trackers tasked with collecting information targeted within the context of their activity, a facilitator and an ad hoc group responsible for analyzing the information (Aguilar, 1967; Keegan, 1974; Aaker, 1983; Jain, 1984).

Previous publications distinguish two primary modes of information collection (El Sawy & Pauchant, 1988; Choudhury & Sampler, 1997): the ‘reactive’ mode, in which information is acquired to resolve a specific problem or inform a decision (Fahey & King, 1977), and the ‘proactive’ mode, in which the organization explores or scans (Aguilar, 1967; Hambrick, 1982) its environment, on the lookout for potential problems or opportunities. From this perspective, ‘instead of seeing an organization as seeking information in order to choose among given alternatives in terms of prior preferences, we can see an organization as monitoring its environment for surprises (or for reassurances that there are none)’ (Feldman & March, 1981, p. 176).

This study focuses on projects in which S.Scan is conceived as a structured, reticulated and proactive internal system allowing the organization to explore its external environment in order to acquire, distribute and utilize prospective information, with the goal of anticipating plausible future change in the organization’s socio-economic environment and acting in advance. More precisely, this study focuses on factors that contribute to the failure and abandonment of S.Scan projects.

## Factors contributing to the failure and abandonment of IS projects

Failure factors are mentioned in a few publications on S.Scan (Diffenbach, 1983; Engledow & Lenz, 1985; Ghoshal & Kim, 1986; Ghoshal & Westney, 1991). However, until now the failure and abandonment of S.Scan projects have not been subjected to detailed study. The factors likely to contribute to the failure and abandonment of S.Scan projects are not really known. And yet, we can assume that knowing these factors would make it possible, when it is still feasible, to anticipate a S.Scan project’s risks and improve its chances of success. On the other hand, the failure (Hartman & Ashrafi, 2002; Kappelman et al., 2006; Robertson & Williams, 2006) and abandonment of IS projects (Ewusi-Mensah & Przasnyski, 1991, 1994; Oz & Sosik, 2000; Lam & Chua, 2005; Pan & Pan, 2006) have been the object of numerous studies. We will therefore rely on works relating to IS project failure and abandonment factors when constructing the theoretical framework of our study on S.Scan project failure and abandonment factors.

An IS project is said to have failed (1) either when unforeseen complications disrupt the smooth running of the project, bringing its deadlines, costs, objectives and benefits for the organization into question (Doherty & King, 2001; Robertson & Williams, 2006) or (2) when the project is abandoned before it is completed, including cases in which all or some parts of the IS that have been implemented are not being used (Oz & Sosik, 2000). An IS is said to have been abandoned when management decides, for whatever reasons, to discontinue temporarily or retire permanently (1) an uncompleted project or (2) a system in operation (Ewusi-Mensah & Przasnyski, 1991). Abandonment can be considered a special case of failure or one of its possible consequences (Ewusi-Mensah & Przasnyski, 1995).

An IS may be abandoned because it no longer meets the organization’s needs (Ewusi-Mensah & Przasnyski, 1991). It is then at the end of its life cycle. An IS may also be abandoned shortly after being implemented because the delivered system does not meet the organization’s needs.

Lam & Chua (2005) identify three main reasons for this: (1) the project was poorly managed at the implementation stage, (2) the operational system is out of line with the organization’s strategy, and (3) the IS’s contents are unsatisfactory.

Flowers (1996) distinguishes five IS project failure factor categories based on whether they stem from organizational, financial, technical, human or strategic issues. Doherty & King (1998) showed that only 10% of IS project failures are due to technical issues, with 90% attributed to social and organizational factors. Yeo (2002) divides Flowers’s five failure factor categories into two meta-categories based on whether they relate to the project’s organizational and managerial context or to how the project is managed. Doherty & King (2001) investigate the factors affecting the successful treatment of organizational issues in systems development projects. Hartman & Ashrafi (2002) identify seven failure factors that deal more precisely with management’s role in the failure of IS projects. Kappelman et al. (2006) specifically studied the warning signs that alert to the possibility of IS project failure. Fifty-three failure early warning signs are identified in their publications. Then 12 are singled out because the experts questioned and considered them the most important. A first group of six relates to human factors. A second group of six relates to organizational factors.

IS projects are above all abandoned due to organizational, behavioral or political factors. On the other hand, economic (exceeding budgets/deadlines) and technologi cal factors do not significantly contribute to the decision to abandon an IS project (Ewusi-Mensah & Przasnyski, 1991, 1994). Abandonment is often ultimately experienced as the consequence of a failure the stakeholders would rather conceal or forget. Some authors stress the need to draw lessons from abandoned projects in order to avoid repeating the same mistakes in the future (Ewusi-Mensah & Przasnyski, 1995). But abandonment can also represent a reasonable strategy for avoiding the escalation and failure of problematic IS projects (Pan, 2005) and projects that are showing early signs of failure and deterioration (Keil, 1995). Lastly, the decision can be taken to abandon an IS project without there really being any failure to speak of. This is particularly the case when the project’s initial objectives lose their importance, when the organization’s strategic priorities change and when an IS project is abandoned because it is no longer compatible with new priorities (Lam & Chua, 2005).

The rest of this article is organized as follows: the next part explains the action research method used and presents profiles of the organizations the study is based on. This is followed by a section studying the factors that contributed to the failure and abandonment of these organizations’ projects. The last section attempts to synthesize our understanding of this complex question and formulate a few basic recommendations for the practice of S.Scan projects.

## Research method

## Research objectives

The aim of this exploratory study is to shed light on the practice of certain types of S.Scan project. More precisely, this research attempts to answer the following two questions: What factors are likely to lead to the failure or abandonment of a S.Scan project? What factors cause a S.Scan system to be abandoned after the conclusion of the project? To explore these questions, we studied 39 projects in which we intervened within a multiview action research framework (Avison & Wood-Harper, 1990).

## Action research

The IS discipline seems to be a very appropriate field for the use of action research (Baskerville & Wood-Harper, 1998). ‘Only one research article was uncovered by Orlikowski & Baroudi (1991) between January 1983 and May 1988’ (Simon, 2000, p. 150). Since then, the work of David Avison, Richard Baskerville, Michael Myers and Trevor Wood-Harper has contributed to developing and structuring the action research methods’ area of application in the SI field (Baskerville & Wood-Harper, 1996, 1998; Avison et al., 2001; Baskerville & Myers, 2004).

Action research aims to solve current practical problems while expanding scientific knowledge. Unlike other research methods, where the researcher seeks to study organizational phenomena but not to change them, the action researcher is concerned to create organizational change and simultaneously to study the process. It is strongly oriented toward collaboration and change involving both researchers and subjects. Typically it is an iterative research process that capitalizes on learning by both researchers and subjects within the context of the subjects’ social system. It is a clinical method that puts IS researchers in a helping role with practitioners (Baskerville & Myers, 2004, pp. 329–330).

Action research has the following characteristics (Baskerville & Wood-Harper, 1998, p. 92):

\- A multivariate social setting.

\- Highly interpretive assumptions about observations.

\- Significant interaction between the field and the research team, in the course of which the researcher intervenes to help the organization he is observing.

\- The collection of data through participatory observation.

\- The study of change in the social setting.

\- Progress in resolving the company’s problem.

\- Reconsideration and enrichment of theoretical knowledge.

The researcher alternatively or simultaneously adopts the roles of observer–participant (in order to describe, understand and analyze the organization he is studying in its own environment) and actor (in order to participate, guide and influence the behavior, understanding and actions of the organization in whose environment he is intervening).

## Research design

Within the framework of this study, an action research methodology is used because

\- In all of the projects studied, the organizations requested the researchers’ help because they did not know how to start and conduct their projects. Without the researchers’ intervention, some of the organizations in question would have been unlikely to follow through with their projects. The researchers are expected to cooperate with the study subjects, but their task is distinctive from the subjects. ‘The burden of solving the immediate problem-setting rests with the study subjects. The task of the researchers is to facilitate or help the subjects with expert advice, technical knowledge or an independent viewpoint. However, the subjects are responsible for determining exactly what interventions will be created’ (Baskerville & Wood-Harper, 1998, p. 95).

\- The organizations that did not abandon their projects would have been unlikely to accept the presence of a non-participating observer after they had explicitly requested his participation and help.

\- Without knowing the organization from the inside, and without knowing its projects, it is very difficult to identify which organizations have a S.Scan project.

\- Managers are not inclined to welcome neutral observers on projects that, by nature, involve subjects and systems of strategic importance, even when the researchers guarantee discretion and confidentiality.

\- Given the state of current knowledge of S.Scan projects and their failure factors, a closed survey such as a questionnaire would not be an appropriate method for a thorough study of the subject.

\- Previous studies have shown that it is generally difficult to collect accurate primary data, particularly objective data, on IS project failure and abandonment factors, even when the anonymity of answers is guaranteed (Ewusi-Mensah & Przasnyski, 1991).

It is therefore an exploratory study to discover S.Scan failure factors and anticipate potential failure risks with the goal of improving S.Scan acceptability and feasibility conditions and formulating recommendations likely to be of help to organizations interested in conducting S.Scan projects.

Interventions all follow the same rigorous process:

\- A common S.Scan system of reference (the same concepts, same method and same tools) and the same intervention method are systematically used when assisting the organizations under study with their S.Scan projects.

\- The interventions are always carried out by the same three researchers, all of whom have experience with the S.Scan system of reference and intervention methods used.

Interventions all follow the same overall linear process:

\- One or more managers contact the researchers to give background information on their organization and their S.Scan project.

\- A presentation on S.Scan and S.Scan concepts is given to project stakeholders and/or a management committee that will decide whether or not to go ahead with the project.

\- The researchers and project managers jointly define a broad outline of the project and the terms of the researchers’ involvement so that the organizational context will be taken into account.

\- Members of the project team are chosen and one or more project managers are designated.

\- In the course of joint meetings, which vary in number according to the organizational context, the project team and project managers interact with researchers to learn the researchers’ system of reference and make it their own. Together they create a system that is adapted to the organizational context.

\- The researchers’ intervention finishes either because the project team is in a position to continue the project independently or because the project is postponed or abandoned.

\- After leaving the organization, the researchers are sometimes consulted on specific issues for which stakeholders require external expertise.

Table 1 sums up the main characteristics of the action research method adopted for this study, enabling it to be positioned in the framework of multiview action research (Avison & Wood-Harper, 1990; Baskerville & Wood-Harper, 1998).

As is the nature of an experimental process, in which the researcher attempts to control variables in order to neutralize their influence on the phenomena under study, by adopting one single S.Scan system of reference for all interventions, the influence of this system of reference on failure factors is partially neutralized, and the cases being studied are more or less comparable.

However, the results of this study are likely to be biased for several reasons:

\- The projects studied all relied on the same system of reference. They would not have been conducted the same way if the system of reference had not already existed. It is also likely that some of the projects studied would not have been conceived by the organization if the system of reference had not existed.

\- The researchers took part in constructing the object of their study. It is likely that the projects observed would not have existed if the researchers, whose assistance had been sought by the organizations, had not responded to their request.

\- As a participant in the project under observation, the researchers’ influence on the manifestation of failure factors is not neutral. If these organizations had run their S.Scan projects without the researchers’ intervention, it is possible that some of the failure factors observed would have manifested themselves differently or without the same frequency.

\- Observations strongly depend on the researchers’ implicit and tacit hypotheses. If other researchers had carried out this study, their observations and conclusions would probably have been different. The research data and results are therefore at least partially subjective.

## Profiles of the organizations and the life cycle of their S.Scan projects

Thirty-nine S.Scan projects were studied in 32 different organizations (see Table 2). In the case of four organizations, several projects with different stakeholders were studied in different locations and departments. Ninetyfive percent of the projects studied took place in francophone countries; the majority of them in France (see Table 3). The observation period covers 20 years, from 1988 to 2007, but 87% of the projects studied took place within the past 10 years (see Table 4).

In Table 5, the projects studied are grouped according to the industry sector of the companies under consideration. French activity nomenclature (Nomenclature des Activite´s Franc¸aises, or NAF) is used for these groupings. Abandoned projects are distinguished from those that are in progress or have been carried through to completion. Some projects were abandoned early, either because management decided not to implement them or because it did not come to the decision to get them underway. Other projects were abandoned while they were being implemented. We also single out completed projects whose implemented systems we know were abandoned after the conclusion of the projects. It is possible that other organizations abandoned their implemented S.Scan systems without our knowledge. Therefore the number of systems that were fully implemented but abandoned is likely greater than that indicated in Table 2, and this represents a limitation on our data. Nevertheless, since the aim of the study is to examine the projects’ failure factors, this limitation does not compromise the validity of our study.

The manufacturing industry represents about 50% of the projects studied. This could be explained by the importance placed on technology and innovation by the companies concerned with the projects under study, as well as their environments, which the managers in question considered very turbulent, full of changes, surprises and potential problems. All health sector projects were abandoned early. It is safe to assume that the S.Scan system of reference designed by the researchers was not relevant for this industry sector. However, due to the small number of projects conducted in this sector and the very particular context of the companies concerned at the time of our interventions, no definite conclusion can be drawn. In the public administration sector, three out of four projects were abandoned. These include two projects conducted in agglomeration communities, in the context of a participatory democracy, and another conducted at a mountain resort in a context reputed to be difficult owing to the total absence of governance cohesion. We could assume that the system of reference used was not suitable for the public administration sector. But once again, the small number of organizations observed and the specific nature of the organizational contexts in which the projects were conducted do not allow any conclusion to be drawn.

## Analysis of causal factors

Our results are presented in three parts: the first part studies S.Scan project failure factors; the second part deals with S.Scan project abandonment factors; and the third part examines S.Scan operational system abandonment factors.

## S.Scan project failure factors

Based on our field observations, we have inferred 38 S.Scan project failure factors. These factors and the number of cases in which we observed them appear in Table 6. By comparing them with failure factors identified in the literature we reviewed, we identified 12 failure meta-factors. detailed below and shown in Table 7.

Absence of the will necessary to get the project underway (factor 1) The stakeholders – primarily management and members of the project team – have trouble getting time away from the daily management of their operational activities to get a S.Scan project underway. If management does not make the decision to go ahead with the project (factors 25 and 28), and if no one takes the initiative to mobilize, assemble and involve all of the project’s stakeholders in defining a schedule and objectives, the project is usually postponed, sometimes for several years, and in some cases it is abandoned. In 33 of the 39 cases studied, the researchers were asked to help generate this impetus (factor 1), which raises questions about the companies’ ability to get S.Scan projects started by themselves, without external assistance.

Inappropriate management involvement (factor 2) We have shown that the S.Scan projects studied are of strategic importance. Management’s role is all the more important because it is directly involved in the project. If management does not support it (factor 15) or does not show enough interest in it (factor 5), its legitimacy and the project team’s involvement come into question. In that case, the project is likely to fail.

Extensive management involvement is also likely to inadvertently undermine other stakeholders’ initiative and interest in the project (factor 20). This is particularly the case when management assigns positions of authority to project team members who do not have a positive attitude toward the project or are not qualified for the assignment (factor 21).

This result does not appear to have been mentioned in previous studies on IS projects.

Project team leader unqualified (factor 3) We have shown that the organizations concerned with the projects under study often have no experience with networked S.Scan. They are not familiar with good practice that might provide them with inspiration for the project. They do not feel able to independently master the theoretical models after they have learned them, and are therefore tempted to seek the help of external expertise. This is the context in which our guidance is requested. If the project team is not in a position to bring in external expertise for this purpose, or if these external resources are difficult to mobilize, the project is likely to fail (factor 4). In the course of the project, certain abilities need to be developed in-house. This is particularly the case with regard to the collection of field information (factor 13). If stakeholders do not develop these abilities, the project is likely to fail.

Lack of in-house competence is also identified in previous studies as an IS project failure factor (Oz & Sosik, 2000; Hartman & Ashrafi, 2002; Yeo, 2002; Lam & Chua, 2005; Kappelman et al., 2006).

Poorly defined expectations and objectives (factor 4) If the project’s objectives, desired results (factor 2) and priorities (factor 3) are not clearly defined, explained and shared by all stakeholders (factor 10), these stakeholders are likely to have differing views and expectations of the project (factor 9). This can lead them to develop individual strategies that are counter-productive, and likely to cause the project to fail.

This failure factor is mentioned by several authors: for S.Scan projects (Herring, 1999) and for IS projects (Oz & Sosik, 2000; Hartman & Ashrafi, 2002; Kappelman et al., 2006).

Deficient management by project managers (factor 5) Although in 85% of the cases studied (Table 6), management instigates the S.Scan project, it is often confided to a third party (Table 8). If the project manager does not have the experience, ability or legitimacy necessary to motivate stakeholders (factor 19), the project is likely to fail, as Ghoshal & Kim (1986) showed during the implementation of a S.Scan system. This same factor is observed in IS project management (Oz & Sosik, 2000; Yeo, 2002; Kappelman et al., 2006).

The project is also likely to fail if the project manager does not have a positive attitude toward the project (factor 36), if no project manager has been designated (factor 7) and/or if several people with differing views of the project and different methods are designated as managers (factor 35).

Misalignment between the project and the strategy (factor 6) A project is likely to fail when another project is given higher priority (factor 24), when the strategy changes (factor 38) and when the organization undergoes internal restructuring (factor 12). More generally, business conditions and contexts change rapidly. In order to meet evolving environment contingencies, ‘organizations frequently change their business strategies, business processes, and organizational structures. These changes in the business context often lead to changes in user requirements of the system under development, including system objectives, system scope, input/output data, data structure, and user interface’ (Lee & Xia, 2005, p. 78). Thus, the project is likely to fail because it is no longer clearly in line with the way the organization’s strategy is evolving.

This is also demonstrated by Lam & Chua (2005) in their study of IS project failure factors.

A project is also likely to fail when a middle manager instigates the project, but senior management is neither informed nor involved. In that case, the project fails because it is not legitimate, and it does not match management’s expectations or strategy.

Weak stakeholder participation (factor 7) There can be many reasons for weak participation. It is particularly likely when the stakeholders do not feel that the project personally concerns them (factor 33), when their personal engagement in operational activities makes them unavailable for the project (factor 27), or when their geographical distance from the project’s main site makes it very difficult for them to participate in meetings and integrate into the project team (factor 8).

Even if the stakeholders are in principle available for the project, they are likely to become apathetic when new people enter the project and others leave before the project team has had the chance to stabilize (factor 23), when the behavior of one of the team members seriously disrupts the group’s work (factor 29) or when the project raises personal ethical issues (factor 37).

Weak participation on the part of project team members is a failure factor mentioned by Yeo (2002). Diffenbach (1983) mentions lack of motivation as something that hinders the implementation of a S.Scan activity.

A hostile organizational culture (factor 8) Stakeholders’ work habits and organizational culture can cause a project to fail (factor 6). In S.Scan projects, this is particularly the case when the stakeholders are not used to sharing information, or when they valorize figurebased information to the detriment of more qualitative and anticipative information (Engledow & Lenz, 1985; Ghoshal & Westney. 1991). Corporate culture can be either a hindrance or driving force in S.Scan activity (Diffenbach, 1983; Babbar & Rai, 1993).

In a broader sense, Tornatsky & Fleisher (1990) stress the role of corporate culture as a possible IS application failure factor.

An insufficient budget (factor 9) A S.Scan project’s budget is not necessarily of great importance, but neither is its importance negligible. If external resources and expertise are needed, a budget is necessary. Projects are likely to fail if they are not supported by a budget (factor 16), or if the budget is too small to provide for external help (factor 17) and to properly equip and sustain the S.Scan system (factor 22).

The role financial issues play in the failure of IS projects is also mentioned by Flowers (1996). More specifically, Ghoshal & Westney (1991) raise the problem of inadequate resources as a S.Scan failure factor.

Searching for a technical solution to managerial problems (factor 10) If stakeholders conceive of the S.Scan project as technological, and focus more attention on the choice and implementation of a technical solution than on common organizational issues and the development of their understanding and awareness of S.Scan practices (factors 14 and 26), the project is likely to fail.

This failure factor is also mentioned by Yeo (2002).

A ‘trauma’ provoked by the failure of a previous project (factor 11) This occurs when some of the stakeholders were at some time involved in other projects that failed. The memory of this failure can stimulate fears and frustrations likely to influence the stakeholders’ attitude toward the new project (factor 11). If these fears and frustrations are not identified, explained and discussed with the other people involved, the new project is likely to fail in its turn.

This result does not appear to have been mentioned in these terms in previous studies on IS project failure factors. There are studies that address the memory of previous IS projects and lessons learned from their success or failure (Walsh & Ungson, 1991; Ewusi-Mensah & Przasnyski, 1995). But they do not speak of possible ‘traumas’ likely to paralyze new projects.

Underestimation of the project’s complexity (factor 12) In certain cases, management hopes the S.Scan system will enable them to know everything that might be of concern to the organization’s environment, near and far (factor 18). In other cases, impelled by a sense of urgency, management wants the S.Scan system to be operational within such a short time frame, the project is not feasible (factor 32). If deadlines are too short, or the objectives too large, the project is not realistic and therefore likely to fail. This failure factor is also mentioned by Yeo (2002), Hartman & Ashrafi (2002) and Kappelman et al. (2006).

## S.Scan project abandonment factors

Of the 39 projects studied for this research, 12 were abandoned before a S.Scan operational system had been implemented. Nine of the 12 failure meta-factors discussed above appear to contribute directly to the abandonment of S.Scan projects (see Table 7, column A). It therefore appears that a S.Scan project has a strong chance of being abandoned when

\- There is no strong impetus to get the project underway.

\- Management’s involvement is inappropriate.

\- Project expectations and objectives are poorly defined.

\- Project managers poorly manage the project.

\- The project is not aligned with the organization’s strategy.

\- The budget is insufficient and does not provide for the necessary external expertise.

\- The project is perceived as a technical project rather than a management project.

\- ‘Traumas’ provoked by the failure of a previous project are not taken into account.

\- The project’s complexity is underestimated.

The failure factor results presented in Table 6 offer more precise information. It shows that S.Scan projects are always abandoned when

\- There is no clearly identified project manager.

\- The implementation of an almost exclusively computerized system is the project’s objective.

\- Management does not support the project.

\- The project does not meet management’s expectations.

\- There is an extremely urgent need for the project to produce results.

\- The stakeholders do not feel personally concerned with the project and do not see the need for it.

\- Project team members do not get involved in the project.

\- There is a change of management during the project.

The deterministic nature of the last five failure factors’ effect on S.Scan project abandonment is only a hypothesis, given the small number of abandonment cases observed. However, these observations on S.Scan project abandonment factors are consistent with those made by Ewusi-Mensah & Przasnyski (1991), who show that organizational, behavioral and strategic factors have a much stronger and more direct influence on the abandonment of IS projects than economic and technological factors.

## S.Scan operational system abandonment factors

Eleven of the 22 S.Scan operational systems implemented and studied in this article are still in place. Five of the 11 permanent projects have propagated within the organization. We have no follow-up information on seven of the 22 projects. But we know that four systems were abandoned in the months or years that followed their implementation. Table 9 lists the abandonment factors identified that concur with the abandonment of these operational systems. S.Scan operational systems get abandoned for three main reasons, which we will explain below.

The system facilitator makes a bad entrance (abandonment factor 1) When the facilitator is ‘parachuted’ in at the end of the project, he does not have sufficient knowledge of the project, its objectives, its contingencies and the decisions that went into creating the system he’s supposed to facilitate (factor 40). And he also does not know the members of the S.Scan network, who feel they have invested a lot of time in the project. A question of legitimacy thus arises. Even if the facilitator has participated in the project, if he does not have expertise in the company’s industry, or have the experience, know-how and approach necessary to manage such a system, he will not be able to motivate and coordinate the S.Scan network (factors 42 and 43). Therefore. if the facilitator does not have the qualifications and experience necessary to maintain and sustain the S.Scan system, it is likely to be quickly abandoned because it does not perform satisfactorily.

The facilitator moves (abandonment factor 2) The system facilitator gets a new post or leaves the organization (factor 41). Either no one is designated as a replacement or the person who has been designated has neither the management experience nor the qualifications necessary for facilitating such a system. Members of the S.Scan network might not recognize him as a legitimate choice for the role. Without a determined, competent and legitimate person to stimulate and coordinate the S.Scan network, the system will start to lose momentum, individuals will refocus attention on their own operational activities, and individual and collective good practice will suffer. In that case, the system is likely to survive for a time, but it is already in decline because members of the network are progressively disengaging.

Misalignment between the operational system and the organization’s strategy (abandonment factor 3) The organization’s strategy changes and/or the organization are restructured (factor 39). Changes in strategy bring into question the alignment between the S.Scan system and the organization’s strategy when members of the system are not able to evolve it in a way that matches new strategic priorities. The S.Scan system is abandoned when it is no longer able to inform the organization’s strategic planning, as also observed by Lam & Chua (2005). Internal restructuring can call into question the S.Scan network’s integrity and legitimacy for the new organization. In that case, if management does not explicitly express its willingness to maintain the S.Scan system, it is likely to be suddenly abandoned because members of the network disengage.

These observations on the abandonment of S.Scan operational systems suggest that the survival of such a system closely depends on the choice of facilitator, to his remaining in the role, and can also be easily affected by organizational restructuring and changes of strategy. Given the small number of operational system abandonment cases studied, these results are only hypotheses. Nevertheless, they enable us to advance three abandonment meta-factors. If a S.Scan operational system is to survive, careful attention must be paid (1) to the choice of facilitator, (2) keeping the facilitator in his role and (3) the system’s ability to develop in a way that keeps it in line with the organization’s strategic evolution.

## Discussion and conclusions

This study made it possible to construct a list of 38 S.Scan project failure factors, grouped into 12 meta-factors, and to reveal those that are most likely to provoke the abandonment of S.Scan projects. Also, five S.Scan operational system abandonment factors were identified and grouped into three meta-factors.

Some of the S.Scan project failure meta-factors (nos. 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 and 12) and an operational system abandonment meta-factor (no. 3) identified in this study are also mentioned in previous studies on IS projects. Thus, in all essentials, failure risks affecting S.Scan projects and operational systems are the same as those that apply to IS projects. On the other hand, a few S.Scan project failure meta-factors (nos. 1 and 11) and operational system abandonment meta-factors (nos. 1 and 2) are not, to our knowledge, mentioned in previous publications on IS project organizational system failure and abandonment factors. These four meta-factors perhaps apply more strictly to S.Scan projects.

A large number of S.Scan project and system failure and abandonment factors relate to the way personnel and their qualifications are managed. Stakeholders’ attitudes toward the proiect or system affect whether it will succeed or fail. Careful management of personnel would therefore appear to be vital. In-house, it starts with the nomination of a project manager, and then of a facilitator who can maintain and sustain the implemented S.Scan system. Both should be qualified for their roles, and legitimate choices in the eyes of the other stakeholders. It might also be necessary to give stakeholders’ access to resources that enable them to acquire the knowledge and qualifications necessary for their mission, or enable them to source external expertise, or the help and assistance necessary to start up and advance the S.Scan project.

The failure and abandonment of S.Scan projects and operational systems also depend on organizational contingencies. If management’s involvement is either inadequate or inappropriate, and if the project’s objectives are not clearly defined, these factors are likely to cause a S.Scan project to fail.

Conversely, material resources, particularly technology or tools likely to be installed and used in the context of a S.Scan system, are not significant project failure and abandonment factors. However, we sometimes observe abandonment cases in which stakeholders focus on technology from the very beginning of the project. This observation is consistent with previous research from the socio-technical perspective. IS project failure factors are more frequently due to social and organizational issues rather than technological ones (Doherty & King, 1998, 2001; Lyytinen et al., 1998; Schmidt et al., 2001; Lee & Xia, 2005; Luna-Reyes et al., 2005).

A final factor – a cultural one – seems capable of contributing to the failure of S.Scan projects. It would appear that an organization that is unable to get over ‘traumatic’ experiences with S.Scan or shake off old information management habits will have difficulty creating a new S.Scan system.

The meta-factors highlighted in this study offer precise information about S.Scan project failure and how management errors can be avoided. This constitutes actionable knowledge (Argyris, 1996). If stakeholders are made aware of it, it is likely to improve their ability: (1) to analyze failure risks; (2) detect failure early warning signs; (3) recognize and anticipate failure risks. On the whole, knowledge of S.Scan project and system failure and abandonment factors, and being vigilant of failure early warning signs, could improve chances of success. These meta-factors might help future S.Scan project managers improve their methodological approach to constructing projects.

But failure and abandonment also depend on S.Scan’s (perceived) potential value for management and their strategy. Results show that inappropriate management involvement can contribute to S.Scan project failure and abandonment. More precisely, a project can fail because management either loses interest or does not support it. Results also show that projects can fail, and S.Scan operational system can be abandoned, because they are misaligned or no longer aligned with the strategy. Misalignment between the project and the strategy, and a lack of management interest and support, can be perceived at any stage of a project’s development, from start to completion. Even after completion, when the S.Scan system is operational, the project can be abandoned at any time because it is not (or no longer) aligned with the organization’s strategy and priorities, or is perceived as such. Thus S.Scan projects and systems would appear to be very fragile. Their usefulness and viability seem to depend on many subjective fluctuating variables such as changes in business context and contingencies, changes in strategic and managerial interests, and changes in priorities and IS project team’s flexibility (Lee & Xia, 2005). Taken together, these results raise questions about the conditions required for S.Scan to be useful and viable for management in dynamic and changing organizations.

The validity of this research is conditioned by two criteria typical of action research.

One is an internal validity criterion according to which an immediate problem encountered in the social context needs to have been resolved during the course of the research (Baskerville & Wood-Harper, 1998). The researchers are sought by those in charge of the organization to help get their S.Scan projects underway. Of the 39 projects studied, 23 (59% of the cases studied) allowed the implementation of the S.Scan system to be both totally or partially completed, and 5% (13% of cases studied) are still in development. Eleven projects were abandoned (28% of the cases studied). However, in the case of the 11 abandoned projects, the researchers’ intervention helped companies better understand S.Scan or made them more conscious of the need to give their S.Scan projects time to mature.

The other is an external validity criterion according to which the research should be based on a theoretical framework that details how actions lead to favorable results or failure. The understanding or insights the researcher develops based on his results should be carefully generalized into a theory that could potentially be used in other contexts (Baskerville & Wood-Harper, 1998).
