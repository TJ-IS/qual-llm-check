# Why situational method engineering is useful to information systems development

Elizabeth White Baker

Department of Economics and Business, Virginia Military Institute, Lexington, VA, USA, email: bakerew@vmi.edu

Abstract. Effective methods and methodologies for building information systems (IS) remain crucial to building successful IS. This paper investigates the use of situational method engineering for IS development (ISD) projects in a professional bureaucracy. This specialist organization methodology (SOM) is built starting with the philosophy of ISD moving through to flexible, amethodical implementation methods, a proposed instance of situational method engineering that is targeted towards professional bureaucracies. An in-depth philosophical discussion of the ontological, epistemological, research methodological and ethical underpinnings of SOM are outlined, in addition to its limitations, to develop the building blocks of a methodology to implement within a professional bureaucracy organization ISD scenario. Amethodical ISD will be introduced as the appropriate way to implement this developed methodology to build a successful IS. The contribution of this paper is to detail how to develop a philosophically consistent methodology for a specific organizational environment (in this case, using the specific organizational example of a professional bureaucracy) that, when combined with developers and tools, can create a method to be applied to build an IS that has a greater possibility of successful organizational adoption than an IS built using a method devised using more common IS method engineering approaches.

Keywords: information systems development, ISD approach, philosophical foun dations of ISD, situational method engineering, amethodical systems development

## INTRODUCTION

During the last decade, software process improvement (SPI) has been the dominant approach to improving quality and productivity among software developers (Ngwenyama & Nielsen, 2003). Using its strictly technical perspective, the failure rate for SPI programs is high, at around 70% (Software Engineering Institute, 2002). Additional studies show the average cost overruns for systems development projects to be approximately 30–43% (Jørgensen & Moløkken-Østvold, 2006). Nielsen & Norbjerg (2001) argue that the SPI approach needs to espouse socially oriented theories in order to address organizational change issues, and othe researchers have posited that information systems development (ISD) should be seen as more than a technical activity and should be understood from the wider social and organizational context in which it takes place (Lederer & Nath, 1991; Cannon, 1994; Willcocks & Margetts, 1994).

Although up until recent years, the relationship between organizational culture and the deployment of ISD methodologies has been scarcely researched (Leidner & Kayworth, 2006); Iivari & Huisman (2007) investigate the relationship between organizational culture and ISD methodology deployment, and find that there is a perceptible impact of organizational culture on the successful deployment of these methodologies. Following Kautz (2004), in considering ISD as an innovative activity that can be better understood by its content, context and processes (Bucher et al., 2007; Kautz et al., 2007), this paper focuses on developing a comprehensive ISD methodology that is philosophically consistent and practically useful by ensuring that the ISD approach of systems developers, as defined by Hirschheim et al. (1995), is congruent with the organizational structure for which the developers are building a system. With this socially and technically congruent methodology, and by using situational method engineering (Kumar & Welke, 1992) to develop the eventual method, any organization can develop their information systems with ISD agility, defined as why and how organizations sense and respond swiftly as they develop and maintain information systems applications (Lyytinen & Rose, 2006).

System developers need to have a methodology when setting out to build an information system (even if they are not consciously aware of it), as the methodology provides an avenue of discourse on the ISD process and gives a framework and discipline to all involved to begin developing the system. By bringing this discipline and discourse to the whole ISD process, the methodology provides an opportunity for system development process discussion among all of the system developers, leading to higher quality information systems (Iivari et al., 2000). That all of the developers approach the development process with a single methodology will be important to the eventual successful construction of the information system itself. In addition to being crucial for system developers, other groups involved in the system development process also benefit from the existence of an explicit methodology. Wastell (1996) points out that methodologies provide an explicit structure for the ISD process visible to management and external control, and allows all involved to have insight into the methods of investigation being used to ultimately build the system. Should conflicts arise on the process of building the system among the different constituent groups involved in the ISD process, the methodology can provide a reference of common language and procedure in investigating the development situation to resolve the conflict.

In this research, what is being constructed is a methodology and not a method. The following definition of methodology is espoused: ‘An ISD methodology is an organized collection of concepts, methods, beliefs, values and normative principles supported by material resources (Hirschheim et al., 1995). Each ISD project begins with a method definition phase where the development method is constructed in situ, and any given ISD methodology can be used to develop a set of methods associated with it to assist the developer(s). Methodology applied in a particular development situation with a specific developer leads to a situational-engineered method, in the form of the specific approach adopted, and specific techniques and tools that the developer chooses to use in that development scenario to build an information system. If the methodology and its underlying assumptions are philosophically consistent, then a set of methods that can be and are successfully used in a situation will emerge over time as more developers utilize the methodology and construct those methods appropriate for their particular development situation.

The research contribution of this study demonstrates the effectiveness of context-aware situational method engineering in ISD using the example of an organizational structure-based ISD methodology, which can be used to make a company-specific method, thereby increasing the probability of successful ISD project outcomes within that organization. As Goulielmos (2004) points out, ISD’s ineffectiveness is in relation to the overarching methodology (Goul ielmos calls it an approach) adopted rather than the tools and process used. By looking at the company level of structure imposed on an ISD process, this work recognizes, in keeping with Kautz et al. (2007), that structure is influenced and shaped by market forces on the organization, the types of technology and applications involved, as well as the type of organization and its cultural and political situation. This paper expands the traditional idea of a methodology to include a process for analysing these organizational situations to achieve an enhanced methodology so that one can eventually create a method using situational method composition (Bucher et al., 2007) for that organization’s ISD projects, one that addresses persistent contextual issues hampering ISD (Curtis et al., 1988; Whyte & Bytheway, 1996; Goulielmos, 2004; Aydin, 2007).

This research will discuss the building blocks of a methodology to be implemented within a particular organizational development situation. As a case example, this research uses a philosophically grounded construction technique to build an ISD methodology to be utilized in a professional bureaucracy, an organizational structure that describes groups of professionals who work together in their chosen professional practice. This type of organization will be discussed as a representative organizational type that would benefit from an ISD methodology geared specifically for it. By intent, the specialist organization methodology (SOM) is built explicitly from the philosophy of ISD moving through to flexible, amethodical implementation methods, as opposed to built implicitly from tools and techniques common using method engineering (Odell, 1996). This is followed by an outline of the underlying assumptions necessary to construct an ISD methodology. By investigating the underlying philosophical reasoning behind the development process and constructing a methodology congruent with those philosophical underpinnings (Iivari et al., 1998), building a method using this approach alleviates the problem of possible paradigmatic dissonance among tools chosen by developers to build a system (Flynn & Hussain, 2003). This is more likely to result in a method with a congruent set of tools, techniques and methods of enquiry, which would lead to an information system with a higher probability of being built and implemented successfully. Each assumption of the methodology is subsequently discussed, as it would relate to being used in a professional bureaucracy/specialist organization. The conclusion will outline how to use this type of methodology without necessarily having to use a rigid, codified method. The idea of ‘amethodical ISD’ will be introduced as the appropriate way to implement this developed methodology to build a successful information system (Truex et al., 2000).

## BEGINNING WITH A PARADIGM AND AN ISD APPROACH

To be able to build a philosophically consistent methodology, there has to be an ISD approach and, ultimately, an ISD paradigm with which to start as an underpinning to the methodology. An ISD approach is defined as ‘a class of specific ISD methodologies that share a number of common features; a set of related features that drive interpretations and actions in ISD’ (Iivar et al., 2000). ISD approaches inherit the assumptions of the paradigm they represent. Similarly, any ISD methodology that is developed is going to be an instantiation of this more genera group of ISD assumptions. Hence, for any developed ISD methodology to be successful, the paradigm and ISD approach from which it develops must be philosophically consistent. Figure 1 below shows the relationship between ISD paradigms, approaches, methodologies and methods. While Iivari et al. (2000) use ISD approaches to classify methodologies that they deconstruct, ISD approaches can also serve as a template for building methodologies.

For a methodology to be most successfully used in our case example organization, a professional bureaucracy, the paradigm chosen is social relativism, which ‘seeks explanation within the realm of individual consciousness and subjectivity, and within the frame of reference of the social actor as opposed to the observer of the action’ (Hirschheim et al., 1995). The ISD approach that will be used as a template is the soft systems methodology (SSM) approach. (Note that SSM has developed as both an ISD approach and ISD methodology; in this research, it is strictly used as an ISD approach.) The goal of the SSM approach is to ‘provide a learning methodology to support debate on desirable and feasible changes’ (Iivari et al., 2000). The principles of the SSM approach include a stream of cultural analysis in the ISD process, concurrent with a stream of logic-based analysis. These are the choices of the ISD approach that the resultant SOM created would inherit, as SOM would be an instantiation of the SSM approach from the social relativism paradigm.

In developing a process to investigate building information systems, or developing a meth odology, there are two approaches that can be taken. The type of information system being built or the type of organization within which the system will be functioning can be the starting point. With the basis that all information systems are socio-technical systems, approaching methodology construction from either the technical perspective or the social perspective is certainly acceptable. The methodological approach with a technical starting point allows for broad application in ISD for specific information system types across organizations, aligning with the strategic use of information technology (IT), while the methodological approach with a social starting point allows for broad application for ISD within an organization type, aligning with matching IT to the organization (Baskerville, 1996).

In choosing to create a methodology for use in a professional bureaucracy, the approach of starting with the type of organization within which the system will be built has been taken to allow for a broad application of the methodology to any system development project within the professional bureaucracy itself. To successfully develop SOM, this research explores the social relativism paradigm in more detail, breaking it down into its fundamental components of ontology, epistemology, methodology and ethics. A brief discussion of the professional bureaucracy as a type of organization and the theory of organizations that best applies to a professional bureaucracy will begin our process of building SOM. Subsequently, the research will return to talk about the fundamental components of the social relativism paradigm, the role of system developers, as well as the implicit theory of systems and information systems engineering embedded in the resultant SOM.

## WHAT IS A PROFESSIONAL BUREAUCRACY?

In Mintzberg’s (1992) classic book on organizational structures, one of the five effective organizational structures he describes is that of a professional bureaucracy, a structure common in universities, hospitals, public accounting firms, law firms and social work agencies, among others. A professional bureaucracy hires field-specific specialists for its operating core and then gives them considerable control over their work. These professionals work relatively autonomously, achieving whatever coordination is necessary through the standardization of skills among themselves while simultaneously working closely with the clients being served (Mintzberg, 1992). The ISD implications of these organizational characteristics of this type of firm are twofold. First, the ISD process will be required to involve end-users of the systems. Any information system implemented in a professional bureaucracy would need to be developed in close collaboration with the organization’s professionals to ensure that the system facilitated (or at least did not interfere with) the necessary coordination of the administrative structure among the professionals themselves. Second, the environment is the chief situationa factor in a professional bureaucracy organization, far overshadowing the technical systems that might be in place. While the knowledge base of the group of professionals is very sophisticated (academics, medicine, law), the technical systems used to apply their knowledge are generally not. The most sophisticated technical systems that exist in a professiona bureaucracy are those that involve coordinating the individuals. The implications of this for information system developers in the professional bureaucracy environment are that they would need to account primarily for the environment of the organization in developing any system, for while the technical requirements might not be so demanding, the environmental requirements will be a fundamental determining factor in the success of an information system developed for this environment.

Although professional bureaucracies by nature are a relatively stable form of organization, no organization remains static, and these firms are constantly changing in response to externa environmental factors (changes in laws, advances in science) and internal turnover of professionals. What implications does the evolution of a professional bureaucracy over time have for ISD in that organizational environment? To answer, an investigation of the underlying theory of organizations to this methodology is in order.

## THEORY OF ORGANIZATIONS – THE EMERGENT ORGANIZATION

This ceaseless cycle of technological change and organizational adaptation means that any ISD project will face the daunting task of trying to capture information on changing organizational structures into which their information system will eventually be integrated (Brinckmann, 1989). Whether or not organizations are intentionally or unintentionally changing, the fact is that organizations are changing, and this change must be accounted for in the ISD process for a successful information system to be developed, regardless of the type of organization. As a beginning of this determination of the theory of organizations for SOM, this research looks to current thinking on the theory of organizations that is congruent with the social relativism paradigm. SOM espouses the theory of emergent organizations and autopoietic (selfreferential) organizational change to recognize the socio-technical nature of information systems and the constantly changing nature of organizations within which information systems must function. Emergent organizations, as described by Truex et al. (1999), represent a description of organizations that reflects the constant organizational change that has become so important to organization survival. Indeed, with organizations in a continuous state of adjustment, organizational artefacts (such as information systems) are constantly changing and always out of step with the reality of the organization (Baskerville, 1996). Therefore, information systems must incorporate continuous change. Later in the discussion of the assumptions of SOM, the impact that emergent organization theory has on the methodological assumption of the theory of information system engineering is discussed, namely, that con tinuous change implies replacement of traditional ISD values with amethodical ISD to build successful information systems (Wastell, 1996; Truex et al., 1999; 2000; Truex, 2001).

With organizations constantly changing, affecting the way that information systems are developed, determining the vector of organizational change becomes important in the ISD process. The degree of change over a unit of time involves self-reference and selfreproduction, and is called autopoiesis (Jantsch, 1980). Maturana and Varela’s description of autopoiesis argues that all organizations are closed-ended, autonomous systems of interaction that make reference when changing only to themselves (Maturana & Varela, 1980). This self-referential change process (Argyris et al., 1985) for emergent organizations has a profound impact on ISD, as ISD methodology must have a way of gathering information on how the organization perceives itself and how it sees its changing organizational culture (tracking the organization’s autopoiesis) to build an effective information system for the emergent organization. An appropriate emergent organization ISD strategy is to accommodate and learn to change with change itself, being sensitive to the idea that in changing times, new forms of system organization (and, therefore, new forms of information systems representing the organization) must be allowed to emerge.

## THE ONTOLOGY OF SOM

Having discussed the theory of organizations embedded in the ontology of SOM, the dimensions of its ontology are outlined. The view of information/data, the view of information systems, the view of human beings, the view of technology, the view of organizations and society, and a representation scheme for modelling concepts are all aspects of the ontology that must be made explicit in building SOM. All of these ontological dimensions must be philosophically consistent with the paradigm and ISD approach that are espoused in SOM, social relativism and the SSM approach, for the methodology to be able to be successfully applied. This work now returns to the discussion of the fundamental assumptions of the social relativism paradigm (and, hence, the basis of the SSM ISD approach) to uncover the onto logical assumptions as the next step in constructing SOM.

The social relativism paradigm has its basis in Burrell & Morgan’s (1979) interpretive paradigm, the subjective order paradigm in their framework. From this paradigmatic viewpoint the concern lies with understanding the essence of the world as it is experienced (Burrell & Morgan, 1979). As such, a systems developer using SOM would espouse interactionism and nominalism within their view of organizations. From such a perspective, organizations exist as an expression of the meanings that the social actors attach to their institution. All interests in the organization would be seen as legitimate in the SOM, appealing to all common interests through the search for agreed-upon norms and common understandings. The nature of information systems themselves in SOM outlines that an information system is a sociotechnical system with socio-technical implications, gathering information from both the socia environment and from the technical environment explicitly. Here, the concept of ‘joint optimization’ takes hold in SOM, the concept that states that when ‘achievement of an objective depends on independent but correlated systems, such as a technological and social system, it is impossible to optimize for overall performance without seeking to optimize these correlated systems jointly’ (Davis, 1972).

Another ontological building block concerns the view of information/data in an information system. In operationalizing SOM, the data would consist of descriptive facts and relative descriptive facts about the entities named, ‘reflecting’ reality, as opposed to constitutive meanings, ‘constructing’ reality (Iivari et al., 1998). Although this would seem contradictory to the idealist/constructivist interpretation predominant in the social relativism paradigm that information/data would consist of facts, this view would still be based on nominalism, where facts were named or described with a socially agreed-upon meaning. Once all of the interests involved in using SOM agreed and named the object, the object would then be able to be described with labels. This idea of data viewed as descriptive facts will become central when discussing how this methodology would construct a representation scheme for modelling concepts.

In philosophical congruence with the social relativism paradigm for ISD, the ontological view of human beings for SOM is voluntarist (Burrell & Morgan, 1979). Human beings are not subject to casual laws entirely or authoritarianism in a professional bureaucracy; instead, the professionals act to organize themselves with respect to each other in a largely democratic way (even if there is some form of hierarchy involved within the ranks). The confederated individuals in a professional bureaucracy use systems with consciousness and free will. This view of human beings (the social aspect of the information system) orients the view o technology (the technical aspect of the information system) in this ontological framework, as the view of technology is that of human choice to remain consistent with the paradigm. Autonomous human beings will use technology and organize information systems into systems to further their organizational objectives.

With all five key ontological viewpoints outlined, to complete the ontology, there needs to be a representation scheme for modelling the concepts, providing the basic constructs used to represent real-world phenomena in our conceptual models (Weber, 2002). Borrowing heavily from the view of object systems in the social relativism paradigm, what SOM information system developers concern themselves with is describing, designing, building and implementing systems that are nominal, therefore using symbols and language to elucidate socia cognitive processes and make distinctions (Hirschheim et al., 1995). This process-oriented view is based on social cognition as the lens into viewing how organizations work. By studying organizational social cognition, how organizational members develop consensual cognitive processes and act on them, system developers would get a sufficient understanding of the social dynamics that would be reflected in the information system. Finney & Mitroff (1986) offer organizational self-reflection (OSR) as a method for exploring organizational processes operating in an organization that can provide information for the design or correction of an organization’s strategy, including, but not limited to, its information systems. Having an accurate OSR, and having the system developers know that self-reflection explicitly, makes it possible to design a better-informed information system.

From a data-oriented perspective, the social relativism paradigm leads to two types of preferred object systems. One type views the object system as a socially constructed image, like an agreed-upon artwork. The second type of object system to which the social relativism paradigm leads focuses on the importance of language as the principle vehicle through which reality construction is mediated (Hirschheim et al., 1995). Methodologies that descend from the SSM ISD approach have embedded in them a sense-making approach (Weick, 1995) to developing information systems, and SOM would be no exception.

## THE EPISTEMOLOGY OF SOM

With the ontology of SOM made explicit, the next paradigmatic assumption to be explored is epistemology. From the perspective of ISD, epistemology addresses the assumptions asso ciated with what type of knowledge is important for ISD and with the way in which systems developers acquire knowledge needed to design the system (Hirschheim & Klein, 1989; Hirschheim et al., 1995). Explicit epistemological assumptions for SOM address the knowledge interest of the information system developer using SOM. SOM takes its epistemological stance from the social relativism paradigm, and the epistemology of social relativism (and, therefore, SOM) is decidedly anti-positivist, ‘firmly set against the utility of a search for laws or underlying regularities in the world of social affairs’ (Burrell & Morgan, 1979). Social reality is not an external entity waiting to be investigated, but rather, it is to be seen as continuously socially constructed and reconstructed by individuals and organized groups (Checkland, 1999). It is knowledge for action to develop an information system for a professional bureaucracy, indicating that the system developer is engaged in information system design science. The central problem in acquiring knowledge in ISD (and a very central problem to circumvent) is the problem of making the knowledge of the system explicit. In an attempt to overcome this problem, designers using SOM must try to convert tacit knowledge of professional bureaucracy users about social and technical system processes to explicit knowledge about those same system processes to enable the appropriate models (information systems) to be developed (Goldkuhl & Lyytinen, 1984).

## THE RESEARCH METHODOLOGY AND ETHICS OF SOM

Whereas epistemology is concerned with enquiry in general, the task of research methodology is to find or perfect optimal enquiry strategies (Bunge, 1983). In ISD, a research methodology outlines what the preferred research methods for developing and continuously improving the information system are, as well as the modes of evidence by which those preferred research methods are justified (Iivari et al., 1998). Burrell & Morgan (1979) distinguish between two types of research methods: nomothetic, relying on systematic protocols and quantitative techniques, and idiographic, focusing on first-hand, inside knowledge of the subject. Taking into account that ISD is an applied discipline, there is a third type of research methodology: constructive methods. Constructive research methods are concerned with the engineering of artefacts, either purely conceptual or more technical, physical artefacts (Simon, 1996). In SOM, constructive and idiographic methods are used, which are consistent with the socia relativism paradigm.

The view of constructive methods used in SOM includes modes of enquiry involving both conceptual development and technical development equally. Constructing both conceptua and technical artefacts are a necessary part of building an information system; therefore, having research methods to enquire into both of these aspects of the system in question are required to have a comprehensive and coherent prescription for the work of information systems analysis and design. The view of idiographic methods is focused on action research in SOM, as opposed to case studies, using detailed analysis of the subjective accounts that are generated by getting inside the situation and involving oneself in the flow of the system (Burrell & Morgan, 1979). This is consistent with the social relativism paradigm and the SSM ISD approach, which both render the distinction between practitioner and researcher moot to allow for the information system developer to enquire into the existing organizational scenario and simultaneously build the information system using the action research method of enquiry (Iivar et al., 1998; Checkland, 1999).

An important part of SOM’s research methodology is that it includes methods of enquiry into the social aspects of the information system, in addition to the technical aspects. Specifically, the OSR process (Finney & Mitroff, 1986) allows the reflecting of espoused organizational concepts vs. enacted organizational concepts, where, often, there is a discrepancy, and incorporates a double-loop organizational learning process for the organization facilitated by the system developers (Argyris, 1964; 1982). By bringing to light these discrepancies between the espoused and the enacted, this OSR method presents system developers with the cognitive processes operating in an organization to help them reconcile the discrepancies to mitigate impact on the information system before the information system is built. Whether or not the specific OSR method is used, SOM must include some method of enquiry into the social aspects of the information system incorporated into the action research plan.

The ethics of SOM refers to the assumptions about the responsibility of the information system researcher or practitioner for the consequences of his/her research approach and its results (Iivari et al., 1998). SOM uses action research as its primary idiographic method of enquiry into a system, with the developer taking on the role of information systems researcher and the role of information systems practitioner. Developers in SOM are primarily interpretive scientists who ‘enrich people’s understanding of their action’ and investigate ‘how social order is produced and reproduced’ (Chua, 1986), in addition to their technical role of engineering the artefact. Becoming part of the research scenario as action scientists do, the SOM developers would not provide knowledge about ways for achieving goals without questioning the legitimacy of the ends. This would turn information system developers using this methodology into potential critical scientists as well in the development scenario, should they identify any particular practice that needed to be eliminated from the system. The potential for harmful ideological practice exists in developing information systems for this organizational environment, and it would be the ethical responsibility of the SOM developer to identify and remove any that were discovered.

## SYSTEMS THEORY IN SOM

If one wishes to build real information systems, one must use specific system theories. In abstract terms, the behaviour of a system provides a description of what the system does, and the structure of a system provides a description of what the system is (Gaines, 1988). In creating an information system instantiation, the systems theory must encompass both perspectives. SOM espouses a constructivist model of people, akin to Habermas’, that sees them as anticipating the future through the development of personal construct systems that are always reconstructable (Kelly, 1955). Having this view, SOM developers would need to investigate all humans involved in the system, developers and users, and their interplay as users’ mental models may be influenced by developers’ intentions.

The soft systems theory, which underpins SOM, views the process of enquiry into the situation as systemic, organizing the exploration into the situation as a learning system. Developers would not be constrained to looking only at the parts of the organization that they perceive to directly relate to the information system; they can allow knowledge of the parts of the organization that affect the information system to emerge as a part of the systemic process of enquiry (the action research) to develop the information system. This soft systems theory relates directly to the theory of organizations as emergent. Developers would know that the systems being developed were always in flux, yet the process they used to investigate the system environment would be systemic, allowing the fullest set of requirements to be discov ered (even if those requirements are constantly morphing). Discovering how to approach gathering those system requirements, this research uses a branch of soft systems theory developed by Checkland & Casar (1986) from the corpus of writing of Vickers called appreciative systems theory. Appreciation perceives of some reality, makes judgements about it, contributes to the ideas stream and leads to actions that become part of the events stream. In an appreciative system, both reality and value judgements (the process of appreciation) stem from standards of both fact (ontology) and value (ethics): standards of what is good or bad, and acceptable or unacceptable. The dynamic form of an appreciative system is autopoietic (Maturana & Varela, 1980), which also underlies the theory of organizations as emergent. This underlying belief in systems theory and the theory of organizations will be the basis for the espoused theory of information systems engineering in SOM.

## THEORY OF INFORMATION SYSTEMS ENGINEERING IN SOM

The primary goal of a SOM information systems engineer is to build an information system to be used effectively in a professional bureaucracy organization. A SOM developer believes an organization itself to be a process, always in motion, in addition to a structure, so any information system as a representation of an organization would have to take into account the longitudina effects of the organization changing over time. The building of an information system is locking an organization into a present future, and developers must be mindful of this in the information systems engineering process, being careful to build into the system development process an ability to change the information system simultaneously as the organization changes.

SOM’s espoused theory of emergent organizations (organizational emergence) refers to a theory of social organization that does not assume that stable structures underpin organizations (Truex & Klein, 1991; Baskerville et al., 1992). With limited means to match ISD to a rapidly changing organization (Truex et al., 1999), if ISD espouses organizational emergence, ISD within an emergent organization becomes an activity facilitating high maintenance and adaptation of information systems to organizational needs (Truex et al., 1999; Truex, 2001). In this way, ISD works with the emergent organization by not idealizing low-maintenance, stable information systems as traditional ISD does but, instead, embracing continuously developed, low organizational drag information systems in the organization.

Truex et al. (1999) make a characterization of an emergent organization ISD methodology that closely resembles that of SOM from a paradigmatic perspective. The philosophical foundations of emergent organization ISD are mainly subjectivist. Its ontology is nominalist, and its epistemology is anti-positivist. It views human nature as voluntarist, espousing order with useful conflicts. This emergent organization ISD can fall into the social relativism or neohumanism paradigms; therefore, SOM can indeed espouse emergent organization information systems engineering theory. The eventual information system developed with emergent organization ISD needs to be significantly different than the product of traditional ISD; thus, the ISD goals and processes for emergent organizations are going to be different (Truex, 2001). The essential impact of the emergent goal set on ISD relates to the adaptability of information systems in the environment and its conduciveness to redevelopment (Truex et al., 1999). This is in keeping with researchers recommending the use of alternative approaches based on emergent and amethodical ISD (Baskerville & Pries-Heje, 2002).

Amethodical systems development, the application of this theory of information systems engineering, implies that the management and execution of systems development occur without a pre-defined space, control, rationality or even claims to universality. Although the process may reject codified structure, it does not imply chaos (Truex, 2001). It merely embraces the idea that the distinction between information systems and ISD disappears because the emergence of information systems is embodied by the goal set of emergent ISD. For SOM, the professional bureaucracy methodologists developing the system would espouse emergent organization ISD goals and build the system based on the developing situation. This returns to the idea that a method consists of a developer (user of the methodology), a development situation and a methodology (Checkland, 1999), and the resultant thinking of Kling’s (1987) Web model shows that the same methodology and the same information system (technical artefacts only) may produce very different effects in different organizations.

Although SOM rejects the use of a rigid, codified method to build an information system, there does have to be a way to develop a method. To address the issue of moving from using

SOM to gather knowledge to building a method to actually build the information system, this research introduces method engineering. Method engineering is the systematic discipline of designing, constructing and adapting ISD methods (Kumar & Welke, 1992), and is primarily focused on the relationship between methodologies and methods (Brinkkemper, 1995; Iivari et al., 2000). There is empirical evidence that method engineering has already had impact on methods, if informally, as existing research on method use in practice has consistently found that information systems methods, as far as they are used, are applied adaptively, customized to fit the organization and project in question without regard for the tools’ consistency or appropriateness with the methodology involved (Wynekoop & Russo, 1993; Hardy et al., 1995; Chatzoglou & Macaulay, 1996; Fitzgerald, 1996). Situational method engineering assumes that the developer can obtain detailed knowledge about the development situation (using a methodology) and make decisions about the applicable method. Accordingly, the key problem for information systems developers using SOM, who also in this case act as method engineers, would be to create the appropriate method that is philosophically consistent with SOM, rather than concerning themselves with how to adapt a pre-defined method to the development environment (Rossi et al., 2000). This is not to say that components of existing methods could not be used, merely that their combination and utilization would be considered with each ISD problem situation and should be constructed to meet a particular ISD situation’s needs. This situational method engineering approach is central to allow developers to apply amethodical ISD goals to emergent organizations to create an effective method for building a successful philosophically consistent information system.

From the perspective of SOM, the method would be emergent, factoring in the developer and the situation as investigated using SOM (the methodology for the organizational type). The methodology creates an organized learning system in which the appreciative settings of users in the organization, and the standards upon which they make judgements, are made explicit and debated. Developers and users together take action by building the information system to improve the problem situation by always considering the information system’s impact in terms of managing relationships based on explicit knowledge derived from users about the problem situation as the organization undergoes autopoiesis (Checkland, 1999). The emphasis on learning as a part of any method built upon SOM is important. Part of the knowledge gained in the ISD process using this methodology is based on reflection on the situations in which the developers find themselves as part of action research, rather than being bound solely by using pre-defined, immutable methods. The views of reflection-in-action (Schon, 1983) and technical rationality are complementary views of method development and use from this SOM methodology, as both explicit knowledge and tacit knowledge are necessary and useful for successful ISD (Rossi et al., 2000).

## RESEARCH IMPLICATIONS

In building a philosophically consistent methodology to develop information systems for specific organizational types, this research compliments the findings of Iivari & Huisman (2007), who developed a model for the relationship between organizational culture and the development of software development methodologies. These authors elucidate the possibility o engineering a software development methodology that is ‘a general approach that emphasizes goals, guiding principles, fundamental concepts, and principles of the design process may also make a software development methodology more useful’ (2007, p. 47). Indeed, the SOM developed in this paper is an instantiation of this type of software development methodology that is a general approach through which specific tools and techniques are tailored for a specific organizational structure and culture, the professional bureaucracy.

This research also outlines the importance of the integration of social and technical considerations for successfully building an information system. In amethodical systems development, there is a consistency with emergent organizations, where the information system evolves continually within the organization. This aligns with business process management and reengineering, where information systems are changed incrementally to support the evolution and provide flexibility for the organizational structure in which the information system resides. Investigating methodologies built using the framework presented in this paper, researchers can gain further insight into how socio-technical considerations in ISD can be effected to maintain successful information system development over time.

## PRACTICAL IMPLICATIONS

Researchers continue to recommend the use of methods based on incremental software process models, and emergent and amethodical ISD as opposed to rigid methods (Baskerville & Pries-Heje, 2002; 2004). Practically, ISD professionals have found that attempting to use rigid development methods in today’s dynamic organizational environments will invariably lead to a suboptimal information system, one not suitable for its intended environment. Evidence of this is found in the rapid adoption of agile programming methods among developers, such as extreme programming, SCRUM and feature driven development (FDD) (Swoyer, 2004). However, developers find that most organizations are not structured to be as flexible as agile development methods require to be successful, as these methods assume the organization to be already well suited to amethodical methods. As pointed out by this research, without the methodology being properly suited to the organizational structure of the firm as it is, the developers have scarce chance of building an information system that will be successfully implemented.

By developing a methodology that is philosophically consistent and consonant with the organizational structure, ISD managers and practitioners can tailor a development strategy that best suits their particular organization and can take advantage of amethodical development, evolving the information system continuously in the building process, possibly even finding it optimal to use unique, organic approaches. Subsequently, using situational method engineering, the in-house construction of an organization type-based, situation-specific method can be accomplished by selecting pieces of method, either method fragments (Punter & Lemmen, 1996; Harmsen, 1997; Brinkkemper et al., 1998) or method chunks (Rolland et al.,

1998; Ralyte & Rolland, 2001), that have already been created and stored in a method base (Henderson-Sellers, 2006; Henderson-Sellers et al., 2007). Starting from scratch with each new ISD project, this assembly-based method construction defines method requirements for the ISD situation, based on which the developer selects method components satisfying the particular development situation and assembles these chosen method components (Ralyté et al., 2004). What this framework for building methodologies also presents to practitioners is the explicit recognition that ISD approach and consistency in the development process rooted within that philosophical approach, in addition to integration within the organizational structure, are essential ingredients to ISD project success. Using a philosophically consistent method ology, the information systems developer has many relevant insights into the context of the development scenario that can be used to better inform the method construction efforts, ultimately leading to a more successful method being used. Indeed, the addition of creating philosophically consistent methods as a part of situational method engineering process preceding ISD further moves the steps of ISD from the realm of prescriptive workflows to more intentionally guided development steps.

## LIMITATIONS OF SOM

Despite the careful construction processes that are involved in constructing a methodology, every methodology has limitations inherent within it, and it is important for any methodologist to be aware of these limitations specific to SOM. Any methodology is limited in four key ways: practically, historically, functionally and paradigmatically. The first three threats are the most dangerous to the practitioner, and the fourth is most critical to this way of building philosophi cally consistent methodologies. To outline paradigmatic limitations, the methodology reformu lation technique presented by Hirschheim & Klein (1994), critical reformulation, is used. The process proceeds in two steps: (1) assumptions analysis, which identifies the basic building blocks of a methodology and reveals the dependence of their validity on the acceptance of underlying philosophical principles; and (2) the proposal of improvements for overcoming the limitations inherent in the assumptions (Hirschheim & Klein, 1994).

From a practical perspective, SOM has never been used in a real-world development situation, nor has it been critiqued by practising systems developers. This is a limitation in that there is truly no way to assess the effectiveness of the methodology without having an artefact (information system) to critique. Historically, it is often the case that even if the methodology is executed correctly, the resultant information system can fail because of a misapplied method. After all, there are a number of activities as a part of the ISD process that emerge outside of the scope of methodology and have significant impact (Goulielmos, 2004). To overcome this limitation, it is important that the developers remain engaged in the true, messy process of ISD and not let them invest too heavily in the tools or method itself.

Functionally, from an organizational perspective, the organizational processes might not be in place to support the methodology. Even though developers intend to utilize SOM in a professional bureaucracy to develop an information system, that particular professional bureaucracy might not have the processes in place to allow for a genuine dialogue between users and developers, which would hamper development efforts (Wastell, 1996). Should the organizational limits be surmounted, poor execution (or complete lack of execution) of the methodology can also be a problem. Baskerville (1991) states that one cannot simply assume that methodologies are applied as intended.

From a paradigmatic perspective, limitations are inherent in ontology and epistemology. There will be limitations on the representation of the system (ontological limitations) and limitations on getting the requirements outlined correctly (epistemological limitations). More dangerous than philosophical paradigmatic inconsistency is developer paradigmatic inconsistency (Flynn & Hussain, 2003). Often, a developer will make a methodology fit his or her own paradigmatic tendencies by reworking it subconsciously into some suitable framework that is based on his or her own preconceptions of the development situation. Recognizing these ‘embedded perspectives’ is crucial to determining the effectiveness of a methodology, as developers need to understand to what extent they are potentially sabotaging the methodology (by changing existing organizational practices and policies) with usage of their preferred methods and tools (Iivari et al., 2000). Throughout the entire life cycle of an ISD project utilizing SOM, the methodology would have to be consistent paradigmatically, from sense making and problem formulation through analysing and design, to the technical engineering, to achieving social acceptance and technical utility within the environment, for the methodology to be effective.

## CONCLUSION

The requirements of SOM follow the basic principles of a social relativism methodology, with organizational emergence, user participation in ISD processes and amethodical ISD as fundamental principles. For an ISD methodology to embrace social relativism ideals, and in particular, organizational emergence such as SOM does, it would have to allow users and developers together to build information systems that continually develop IT agility by adapting to the changing socio-technical organization (Lyytinen & Rose, 2006), both in the learning cycle of amethodical ISD and, subsequently, through information systems use. A critical aspect is that SOM and the resulting information system should help decrease system drag on the organization’s capability to emerge and decrease user frustration with the resultant information system by having users be involved in the continuous ISD process. How SOM as the basis for situational method engineering processes tailored for professional bureaucracies achieves these goals has been outlined throughout this research.

ISD remains a challenging and adapting area where the economic and organizational stakes are high. In light of significant numbers and the persistence of ISD project failures, the search for systems development practices that will result in more successful ISD projects remains strong. This work investigates how structure at the organizational level influences ISD and has a significant impact on how systems are developed by detailing how to develop a philosophically consistent methodology (in this research, using the specific organizational example of a professional bureaucracy) that, when combined with developers and tools, can create a method to be applied to build an information system that has a greater possibility of successful organizational adoption than an information system built using a method devised using more common information systems method engineering approaches. Future research would include studying how to develop a real-world example of SOM usage in an organization, as well as developing methodologies for other organizational structure targets, such as machine bureau cracies, division organizations, and operating or administrative adhocracies.
